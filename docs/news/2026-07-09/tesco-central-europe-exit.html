Tesco explores sale of over 500 Central Europe stores

A Tesco store in Balassagyarmat, Hungary — one of 561 outlets across Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia now under strategic review. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Tesco is working with bankers on a possible sale of its 561-store Hungary, Czech Republic and Slovakia business, ending a three-decade European push.

Tesco is exploring the sale of its supermarket operations across Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia, according to a Financial Times report — a move that would end Britain's biggest grocer's last significant foothold outside the UK and Ireland. The company is working with bankers on strategic options for a division that runs 561 stores and employs more than 22,000 people. Tesco's official line, when asked: "We never comment on rumour or speculation."

The numbers explain the interest without needing much interpretation. The Central European arm generated roughly £4.5 billion in revenue last year but contributed only about £115 million to group adjusted operating profit of £3.2 billion — call it 4% of profit for a division that has absorbed decades of capital and management attention. It also took a £53 million non-cash impairment charge in the most recent financial year, which Tesco attributed to intensifying competition in Slovakia and rising regulatory costs.

A three-decade experiment winding down

Tesco entered Hungary in 1994 through the acquisition of the S-Market chain, then expanded into the Czech Republic and Slovakia as part of a broader post-Soviet retail land grab that also took the company into Poland, Ireland, and eventually Thailand, Malaysia, South Korea and China. Most of that empire is already gone. Tesco sold its Asian operations in 2020 — the Thai and Malaysian business went to Thailand's CP Group for $10.6 billion, following an earlier exit from its China joint venture — and offloaded its Polish supermarkets the same year in a £181 million deal with Denmark's Salling Group, citing an inability to compete with discount chains after a Sunday trading ban hit footfall.

At the time, Tesco framed the Polish sale as sharpening focus on Hungary, the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Six years on, that residual business faces the same pressures that pushed it out of Poland: discounters Aldi and Lidl have kept gaining share, and a structural shift away from large out-of-town hypermarkets toward smaller-format and online shopping has eroded the economics of Tesco's Central European store estate, much of which was built for a shopping pattern that no longer holds.

The reversal is notable partly because of how recently Tesco was defending the opposite position. Chief executive Ken Murphy told investors in 2023 that the Central European business was "an integral part of the group and a successful part of the group" that "don't distract us very much from the core business." Markets exploring an exit less than three years later suggests either that the competitive picture has deteriorated faster than expected, or that the bar for what counts as an acceptable use of capital has risen — likely both.

Where the capital would go

A sale would free up capital at a company that has already been prioritising shareholder returns and its home market. Tesco's most recent preliminary results included a £750 million share buyback alongside record UK market share, and Murphy has spent his tenure since 2020 doubling the company's share price by concentrating on grocery execution in Britain and Ireland rather than international scale. Central Europe, however profitable in absolute terms, sits outside that story — it doesn't move UK market share, and its earnings are exposed to currency swings and regulatory regimes British investors have limited ability to underwrite.

The market's initial reaction was muted rather than euphoric: Tesco shares ticked up roughly 0.1% to around 470p on the report, part of a stock that has already climbed about 18% over twelve months. That is consistent with a deal that, if it happens, would be additive to the investment case without being transformative — the Central European unit's profit contribution is small enough that its removal, even at a decent multiple, changes the group's earnings mix more than its earnings level.

What a deal would need

No sale process has been confirmed, and Tesco's businesses in the three countries are large enough — 561 stores across three separate regulatory jurisdictions — that any transaction would likely need to be structured in pieces or find a buyer with existing regional infrastructure, such as a rival grocer or a private equity consortium comfortable with retail real estate. Precedent suggests price will be the sticking point: Tesco took years to find a buyer for Poland and eventually accepted £181 million, a fraction of what the business had been worth at its peak.

The next concrete marker will be whether Tesco confirms a formal process, likely alongside its next trading update, and whether any headline valuation or write-down accompanies it. Investors will also be watching whether proceeds are earmarked for further buybacks, debt reduction, or reinvestment in the UK estate — the choice will say as much about capital discipline at Tesco as the divestment itself.
