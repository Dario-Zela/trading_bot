IRAN · HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-07-09 · 5 min read

US strikes Iran again as Hormuz ceasefire collapses

A U.S. Navy aircraft carrier underway — American carrier strike groups have massed in the Middle East amid the renewed conflict with Iran. — U.S. Navy photo by MC2 Ridge Leoni / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
U.S. resumed strikes on Iran after tanker attacks shattered the Hormuz ceasefire, sending Brent crude up 5% and Treasury yields to multiweek highs.

The ceasefire that had quieted the Strait of Hormuz for the better part of three weeks did not survive contact with a burning tanker. On Tuesday, Iranian forces struck three commercial vessels transiting the strait — the Qatari-owned LNG carrier Al Rekayat, the Saudi-flagged supertanker Wedyan, and a third ship hit less than a day later. Al Rekayat's crew had to be evacuated after an engine-room fire raised the risk of explosion. By Wednesday, President Trump had told reporters at the NATO summit in Ankara that the truce with Iran was, in his words, over. U.S. Central Command's strikes followed within hours, and by Thursday morning Gulf time, CENTCOM said on X that American forces had "started conducting additional strikes against Iran to further degrade their ability to threaten freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz."

For traders, the mechanics of this are not abstract. Somewhere between a fifth and a third of the world's seaborne oil trade passes through a channel at its narrowest point barely two miles wide in each shipping lane. Every escalation cycle here reprices energy risk within hours, and this one has already done that: Brent crude jumped roughly 5% to settle near $78 a barrel on Wednesday, unwinding weeks of decline back toward pre-war levels. The question now is whether Washington is running a contained punitive campaign or sliding into something longer.

What CENTCOM actually hit

The first wave, completed Tuesday, was substantial by any recent standard: more than 80 targets, according to CENTCOM's own tally, including Iranian air defense systems, command-and-control networks, coastal radar installations, anti-ship missile batteries, and more than 60 small boats belonging to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps operating in and near the strait. The U.S. Treasury moved in parallel, reimposing sanctions on Iranian oil exports that had been lifted under the Islamabad Memorandum, the interim agreement that produced the ceasefire in the first place.

The second wave — the one CENTCOM announced on X, timed to Trump's overnight declaration that the truce was finished — was framed explicitly as a continuation, not a new front: additional strikes "to further degrade" Iran's capacity to threaten shipping, justified as accountability for what CENTCOM called "unjustified aggression against commercial shipping and civilian crews." Trump posted a photo he said was taken from strikes on Chabahar in southern Iran, describing the damage as "in retribution for yesterday's bombing of ships by Iran," and adding a line that reads as much as warning as threat: "If it happens again, it will get much worse!"

A president trying to have it both ways

What makes this episode harder to read than a simple escalation is that Trump has, in the same breath, tried to signal restraint. He told reporters he does not expect a full war with Iran to resume, that any renewed fighting would be "over very quickly," and that the underlying objective was to make oil markets "safer" rather than to pursue a long campaign. His formulation — "when Iran hits, we hit" — describes a tit-for-tat logic rather than an open-ended offensive, and he left the door open for talks to continue even as he called the current diplomatic track, by his own description, a "waste of time."

That ambiguity is precisely what markets are struggling to price. A one-off punitive strike that restores deterrence and holds oil flows steady is a very different risk than a rolling campaign that keeps insurers and shipowners rerouting around the strait for months. Iran has not made the calculation any easier: Tehran claimed retaliatory strikes on U.S. military sites in Kuwait and Bahrain, and Iranian state media reported explosions in a string of Persian Gulf port towns — Bandar Abbas, Sirik, Qeshm Island, Jask and Chabahar — some tied to the CENTCOM strikes and others still unconfirmed by independent sources at time of writing.

The chokepoint that sets the price

Bandar Abbas and Sirik are not incidental locations. Bandar Abbas is Iran's principal container and oil-handling port on the strait; Sirik and Qeshm Island sit directly on the shipping lanes that carry crude out of Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Iraq. State media reported roughly eight explosions in Bandar Abbas and ten in Sirik, alongside blasts near Jask and Mesen on Qeshm — a pattern consistent with CENTCOM's stated targets of coastal radar, surface-to-air missiles and anti-ship missile sites rather than a broader bombing campaign against the cities themselves. None of the port damage has been independently verified, and Iran's own reporting on casualties has been thin, which is its own kind of signal: neither side appears eager, yet, to publish evidence of a wider war.

The geography explains why traders reacted the way they did. Brent's 5% jump to $78.19 and WTI's climb to $73.52 were the immediate move; the more telling one came in rates markets. The 10-year Treasury yield rose more than four basis points to 4.571%, its highest level since May, as the same oil spike that unsettles energy desks feeds directly into inflation expectations. The 2-year climbed to 4.206% and the 30-year to 5.069%. By Asian trading hours yields had begun to stabilize, a sign that the initial shock is being digested rather than compounding — for now.

What determines the next move

The distance between "contained retaliation" and "sustained campaign" is the whole trade here. If the current wave of strikes proves to be the last word — Iran absorbs the losses to its coastal defenses, shipping resumes, and the Islamabad framework limps back into force — the oil and yield spike likely fades within days, much as it did after the initial round of attacks earlier this year. If instead Iran responds to Wednesday's strikes with further tanker attacks, the mechanism Trump described — "when Iran hits, we hit" — points toward another round of CENTCOM action and another leg up in Brent. Watch three things in the coming days: whether commercial shipping insurers reprice Hormuz transits, whether Iran claims further strikes on Gulf U.S. bases, and whether Washington reopens any channel to the diplomatic track Trump has already called a waste of time.
