Stocks to watch: Broadcom jumps on Apple's $30bn chip order

Broadcom's headquarters campus in San Jose, California. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Apple committed over $30 billion to buy U.S.-made chips from Broadcom through 2031, sending AVGO shares up 4.8%.

Apple said Wednesday it will spend more than $30 billion on U.S.-made chips from Broadcom under a multiyear agreement running through 2031, and the market's verdict arrived within minutes: Broadcom shares climbed 4.8% on the news, among the day's sharpest single-stock moves.

The deal, announced jointly by Apple chief executive Tim Cook and Broadcom chief executive Hock Tan, commits Apple to buying more than 15 billion U.S.-made chips over the life of the contract — radio-frequency components, including FBAR filters, and custom silicon that handle cellular, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth connectivity across Apple's device lineup. It is the largest single commitment yet under Apple's American Manufacturing Program, the sourcing initiative the company launched last year as part of a pledge to invest $600 billion in the U.S. economy over four years.

Fort Collins gets a bigger footprint

A sizable piece of the money is concrete and steel: Broadcom will put $1.5 billion into expanding its manufacturing plant in Fort Collins, Colorado, where it already makes the RF filters that go into iPhones. "The cutting-edge components built in Fort Collins are essential to delivering the incredible performance and connectivity our customers expect, and we're proud to deepen our investments in U.S.-based suppliers that share our commitment to excellence and innovation," Cook said in a statement. Tan, in his own remarks, called the expansion a continuation of "decades of success together" between the two companies and said the work in Colorado supports hundreds of American jobs.

Why the stock moved

The rally is less about the dollar figure — Apple has made bigger domestic pledges before, in aggregate — and more about what it resolves. Broadcom's stock has carried a persistent overhang: the fear that Apple, which by analyst estimates accounts for roughly a fifth of Broadcom's annual revenue, would eventually design its own RF and connectivity components in-house, the same path it took with modems and chip cores over the past decade. Locking in supply through 2031 pushes that risk out by years and reframes Apple from a potential in-sourcing threat into a committed, multi-generation customer. That is the kind of revenue visibility that tends to move a stock more than the headline number attached to it.

The agreement also folds into Apple's broader AI-silicon relationship with Broadcom, which already co-designs custom networking chips for Apple's server infrastructure — giving investors a second reason to read the deal as strategic rather than incremental.

What to watch

The practical test comes at Broadcom's next earnings call, when analysts will press for how the incremental Apple volume flows into revenue guidance and whether the Fort Collins expansion changes the company's capital-spending plans for the year. More broadly, the deal adds to a run of reshoring commitments from major tech buyers this year, and rival chipmakers with exposure to Apple's supply chain — and to the same onshoring push in Washington — are likely to face questions about whether they can strike comparable arrangements.
