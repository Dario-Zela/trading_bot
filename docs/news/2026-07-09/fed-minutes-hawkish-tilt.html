Fed minutes show inflation fears rising, growth outlook trimmed

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, ran his first FOMC meeting in June with a shortened policy statement and no personal rate projection. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (official portrait)
In one sentence
Fed minutes show staff raising inflation forecasts and cutting growth projections, with AI demand joining tariffs and the Middle East war as inflation drivers.

The Federal Reserve entered the summer with a problem that doesn't have a clean textbook answer: inflation is going the wrong way and growth is slowing at the same time. Minutes from the June 16–17 meeting, released Wednesday, showed staff economists marking up their inflation forecasts for both 2026 and 2027 while marking down the growth outlook — the kind of combination that leaves a central bank with fewer good options and more uncomfortable trade-offs.

The committee itself voted unanimously to hold its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75%, extending a pause that has now outlasted most forecasters' expectations from earlier in the year. But the minutes make clear the doves are losing ground inside the room. "A few participants saw a case for raising rates," the record shows, even as they ultimately "supported maintaining the current range at this meeting." That's a notable shift in tone from a committee that spent much of the past two years debating how fast to cut.

Three sources of heat, one inflation problem

The staff's updated projections tell the story in numbers. Core PCE inflation for 2026 was revised up to 3.3%, from 2.7% in the April forecast; total inflation was running near 4.1% in the most recent reading, up from 3.8% the month before. Real GDP growth, by contrast, was nudged down to 2.2% on a fourth-quarter basis, from 2.4% previously.

Participants pointed to three overlapping causes. Tariff pass-through continues to work its way into consumer prices. The conflict in the Middle East — including the closure of the Strait of Hormuz — has pushed up energy and input costs, with "price pressures had become more broad based," according to the minutes. And a newer factor has joined the list: artificial intelligence. Participants noted that "ongoing strong demand for AI infrastructure would likely sustain upward pressure on prices for technology products and electricity." Some officials argued that AI-driven productivity gains would eventually pull costs back down, but flagged that "this effect would likely take time to materialize" — cold comfort for a committee trying to set policy in real time rather than on a multi-year horizon.

Staff economists explicitly cited the AI buildout, alongside the Middle East war, as reasons their inflation forecast moved higher than in April. That's a meaningful marker: the Fed is now treating capital spending on data centers and chips not just as a growth story for markets, but as a demand shock large enough to show up in its own inflation math. For portfolios leaning heavily into the AI trade, that cuts both ways — the same spending boosting equipment and power demand is also the thing complicating the rate outlook.

On the other side of the mandate, the committee saw modest relief. Downside risks to employment "had moderated a bit," with the labor market described as broadly balanced despite pockets of "relatively low dynamism." That gave the committee room to keep rates on hold rather than easing preemptively, but it did not settle the underlying disagreement about which way the next move should go. The minutes note that "many participants" saw the appropriate year-end rate as within or slightly below the current range, while "many other participants" judged it should be above — a split about as close to fifty-fifty as the Fed's language gets.

A statement built to say less

The policy debate wasn't confined to rates. A majority of participants favored shortening the FOMC's post-meeting statement, and most preferred dropping language that had signaled an "easing bias regarding the likely direction of the Committee's future interest rate decisions." Some welcomed a broader review of the Fed's communications tools — the kind of housekeeping that sounds procedural until you notice what it's for: removing forward guidance that no longer matches the committee's actual thinking.

The push traces back to the new chair. Kevin Warsh, sworn in on May 22 after the narrowest confirmation vote for the position in the Fed's history, ran his first meeting as chair in June — and his statement came in at roughly half the length of his predecessor's. Warsh also declined to submit a rate projection to the dot plot at all, telling reporters "I did not submit a dot for me" because it's "not helpful in the conduct of policy." That left the minutes carrying unusual weight: with the chair withholding his own signal and the committee's forward guidance deliberately thinned out, the written record of the debate is close to the only on-the-record account of where policy might head next.

What the dot plot did show was hawkish. Nine of eighteen participants projected at least one rate hike before year-end, with six projecting two. Markets read the combination of a stripped-down statement and a hawkish dot plot as a signal that Warsh intends to run a more disciplined, less accommodative Fed than the one that spent 2024 and 2025 debating the pace of cuts.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the three threads describe a Fed narrowing its own room to maneuver. Inflation forecasts are rising on three fronts that have little to do with each other — trade policy, a shooting war, and a capital-spending boom — which makes the price problem harder to solve with a single tool. Growth is softening, which argues against tightening further. And the institution itself is signaling, through the length of its statements as much as their content, that it no longer wants to promise markets an easing path it isn't sure it can deliver.

Rate futures moved accordingly: the implied probability of a hike by the September meeting rose to roughly 69% after the minutes were published, up from about 62% the day before, according to CME FedWatch data. The next real test comes with the July 28–29 meeting and the September Summary of Economic Projections, when the committee will have another two months of inflation and employment data — and, potentially, more clarity on the Middle East and tariff fronts — to decide whether "policy firming" moves from a scenario in the minutes to an action on the statement.
