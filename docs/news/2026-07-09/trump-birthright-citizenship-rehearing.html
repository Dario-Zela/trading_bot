Trump seeks Supreme Court rehearing on birthright citizenship

The U.S. Supreme Court, which ruled 6-3 in June to strike down Trump's executive order on birthright citizenship. — Jarek Tuszyński / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Trump says he will ask the Supreme Court to rehear its June 30 ruling striking down his birthright citizenship order — a request the Court has not granted in an argued case since 1965.

President Trump said on Wednesday that he will ask the Supreme Court to rehear the case that struck down his executive order restricting birthright citizenship, a long-shot move that legal scholars say has almost no precedent of succeeding.

"AMERICAN CITIZENSHIP IS NOT FOR SALE! In fact, that is a crime, and therefore, the Supreme Court's ruling is wrong," Trump wrote on Truth Social. "I will be asking for a Rehearing by the United States Supreme Court, IMMEDIATELY."

The post came a little over a week after the Court, in Trump v. Barbara, ruled 6-3 that children born in the United States to parents who are undocumented or only temporarily present remain citizens at birth under the 14th Amendment's Citizenship Clause. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority, though the ruling was narrower than the 6-3 tally suggests: only five justices signed onto Roberts' full constitutional reasoning, while Justice Brett Kavanaugh concurred in the result on statutory grounds without endorsing the broader rule. Justices Samuel Alito, Clarence Thomas and Neil Gorsuch dissented, with Alito calling the decision "a serious mistake" and Thomas describing the majority's historical account as inaccurate.

The ruling upheld the reading of the 14th Amendment set out in United States v. Wong Kim Ark in 1898, which has governed birthright citizenship for 128 years. Trump's order, Executive Order 14160, was one of the first acts of his second term, signed on January 20, 2025, and was blocked by lower courts before it ever took effect.

A procedural long shot

Supreme Court Rule 44 allows a rehearing petition within 25 days of a judgment — a window that runs to late July in this case — but requires the support of a majority of justices, including at least one from the original majority. The Court has not granted a rehearing of an argued case since 1965, and the last time it reversed itself on rehearing a merits decision was 1956. Petitions typically succeed only when a party can point to a change in controlling law or evidence the Court was misled, neither of which applies here; Trump's objection is that the ruling itself was wrong, not that new facts have emerged.

Cecillia Wang, the ACLU lawyer who argued against the administration in the case, said after the original ruling that it "reaffirms a fundamental American promise — if you are born here, you are a citizen."

The rehearing bid is unlikely to alter the legal landscape for immigrants and their U.S.-born children in the near term, and any economic effects of a citizenship policy shift — on labor supply or migration patterns — would only show up over years, not the current quarter. But the announcement signals that Trump intends to keep birthright citizenship as a live political fight rather than treat the June ruling as final. The Court has 25 days from the June 30 judgment to act on any formal petition; unless a justice from the majority breaks ranks, the case is expected to end where it stands.
