Airbus sees 90% Middle East travel recovery, backs long-term growth

An Emirates Airbus A380 departs Dubai International Airport — Emirates remains the world's largest A380 operator with 118 in service. — Steven Byles, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Airbus says Middle East travel has recovered to 90% of pre-conflict levels and expects Gulf carriers to keep expanding fleets, even after trimming its global 20-year forecast by 1%.

Airbus says Middle East air travel has climbed back to roughly 90% of levels seen before the region's recent conflict, and the planemaker is betting that Gulf carriers will keep buying jets rather than pull back, even as it trims its overall 20-year demand outlook.

The company's latest Global Market Forecast, released this week, still projects 42,060 aircraft deliveries worldwide between 2026 and 2045 — a figure Airbus itself describes as a 1% cut from last year's edition, with the reduction attributed to the Iran war and escalating trade tariffs. Of that total, 33,920 are expected to be single-aisle jets and 8,140 widebodies, the larger, longer-range aircraft that carriers such as Emirates and Qatar Airways favour for their hub-and-spoke networks through the Gulf.

"We've seen strong connectivity through the Middle East despite the crisis," said Joost van der Heijden, head of marketing at Airbus's commercial aircraft division. Antonio Da Costa, the company's head of market analysis and forecasting, was more circumspect on the broader trend, noting that global recovery has "flattened after a tariff crisis and the Middle East crisis."

Parked fleets coming back online

The operational data backs up the recovery claim. Qatar Airways had 181 aircraft sitting idle at the peak of the disruption in March; by June that had fallen to 45. Emirates' parked fleet shrank over the same stretch from 44 aircraft to 28. Traffic at the region's hub airports has moved in step: daily flights at Dubai International rose from 499 in March to 844 in June, Abu Dhabi's went from 174 to 462, and Doha's climbed from 42 to 570, according to flight-tracking data reviewed alongside the Airbus outlook.

Not everything has snapped back cleanly. Average Europe-to-Asia flight times were still running roughly 47 minutes longer in June than in February, Airbus noted, as carriers continue routing around restricted airspace rather than flying the shortest path.

The fleet math underlines why Airbus is willing to look past the near-term noise. Emirates carries a firm order for 270 Boeing 777X widebodies and remains the largest A380 operator in the world with 118 of the superjumbos still flying. Qatar Airways, which has been openly critical of the A380, has instead leaned into 777X and A350 orders — one of the largest widebody commitments in commercial aviation history — as it modernises around twin-engine, longer-range types. Both carriers reducing parked aircraft rather than deferring deliveries is, in Airbus's reading, a vote that the disruption is cyclical rather than structural.

That reading will be tested quickly. A ceasefire covering the Strait of Hormuz has already come under strain, with fresh US strikes on Iran reported this week — the kind of escalation that could reverse the very traffic and flight-time numbers Airbus is citing as evidence of recovery. The next checkpoint is Airbus's delivery tally for the third quarter, due in October, which will show whether Gulf carriers' order books are translating into aircraft on the ramp or merely into forecasts on a slide deck.
