Trump says Lockheed building five new plants to quadruple defense output

A MIM-104 Patriot interceptor during a test launch. Lockheed Martin builds the PAC-3 variant at its Camden, Arkansas plant. — Bernd vdB / U.S. Army, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump said Lockheed is building five plants to quadruple output; Lockheed's own filings show a $8-9bn, 20-site build-out on a slower, less uniform timeline.

President Trump told reporters at the NATO summit in Ankara on Wednesday that Lockheed Martin is building five new plants that will "quadruple the output" of American munitions — Patriot interceptors and Tomahawk cruise missiles among them. It was a throwaway line in a wide-ranging press conference, delivered without a dollar figure, a completion date, or a specific list of sites. It also was not new: Trump and his defense contractors have been using some version of the word "quadruple" since January.

The claim landed alongside two other announcements from the same appearance — that Lockheed will build a Patriot sustainment facility in Europe, incentivized by European governments, and that it will partner with Germany's Rheinmetall to produce Army Tactical Missile Systems. None of the three came with financial terms attached. Lockheed Martin has not issued a statement confirming or clarifying the "five plants" figure specifically.

What Lockheed has actually disclosed

Separate from Trump's remarks, Lockheed's own public filings and press releases describe a real, if differently shaped, expansion. The company has a long-term modernization program worth $8 billion to $9 billion running through 2030, spread across more than 20 facilities in Arkansas, Alabama, Florida, Massachusetts and Texas — roughly $1.25 billion of it already committed. Two groundbreakings stand out: a Munitions Acceleration Center in Camden, Arkansas, broken ground in January, and an 87,000-square-foot addition — Building 47 — at the Troy, Alabama missile plant, broken ground on May 21, which the company says will nearly double that site's production floor for THAAD interceptor and Next Generation Interceptor work.

The one place a specific "quadruple" figure does show up in writing is THAAD. In a January 29 framework agreement with the Department of War, Lockheed committed to lifting THAAD interceptor production from 96 units a year to 400 — a 4.2x increase, close enough to justify the word. Patriot's PAC-3 line is a different story: Lockheed's plan is to move from roughly 650 missiles a year to 2,000 over three to four years, which is closer to triple than quadruple. Whether Trump's Wednesday comment referred to THAAD, Patriot, the whole missile portfolio, or all three interchangeably is not clear from the transcript, and the White House has not elaborated since.

The paper trail behind the number

The "quadruple" language traces to a March 6 meeting at the White House, where Trump summoned executives from Lockheed, RTX, Boeing, Northrop Grumman, BAE Systems, L3Harris and Honeywell and announced that six contractors had agreed to quadruple production of what he termed "Exquisite Class Weaponry." That meeting, too, produced no published dollar total. What followed over the next four months was a series of individually documented, smaller-scale actions — the THAAD framework agreement, the Arkansas and Alabama groundbreakings, a roughly $7 billion quarter of PAC-3 orders including a $4.7 billion undefinitized contract disclosed with first-quarter results in April. Lockheed's backlog stood at $186.4 billion at the end of the first quarter, down from $193.6 billion at year-end 2025 — still enormous, but not obviously accelerating in a way that lines up with a fresh five-plant, output-quadrupling commitment.

What the market is pricing

Lockheed shares have not moved as though a step-change in federal contracting is underway. The stock trades around $534, down roughly 20% from its March 2 closing high of $672.30, even as NATO members formalized a 5%-of-GDP defense-spending target at this week's summit and pledged a further €70 billion ($80 billion) in aid to Ukraine. That gap — rising alliance-wide spending commitments against a falling share price — is the more concrete market signal this week than the plant count. Investors appear to be weighing execution risk (three-to-four-year ramp timelines, workforce hiring, supply-chain constraints) more heavily than the announcement effect of Trump naming a number at a podium.

The next hard data point is Lockheed's second-quarter earnings report, expected in the coming weeks, which will show whether order intake and backlog reflect the acceleration Trump described or the more incremental, facility-by-facility buildout the company has been disclosing since January. Until then, "five plants" and "quadruple output" remain a presidential characterization of a program whose specifics — cost, sites, and completion dates — Lockheed itself has yet to confirm in those terms.
