Coty cedes Gucci Beauty license to L'Oréal in cash-for-litigation trade

A Gucci storefront in Hong Kong; Gucci Beauty's fragrance and cosmetics license moves from Coty to L'Oréal on July 1, 2027. — Maizeam / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Coty will take roughly $400m and a litigation truce from Kering and L'Oréal to give up the Gucci Beauty license a year early, but analysts say it still leaves an EBITDA hole to fill.

Coty is giving up one of the few luxury names left in its portfolio, and it is doing so a year sooner than its contract required. The company announced on July 7 that it has agreed with Kering to hand the Gucci Beauty license to L'Oréal effective July 1, 2027 — roughly twelve months ahead of the license's scheduled 2028 expiry — in exchange for approximately $400 million and a mutual resolution of pending litigation between the two companies over the license's terms.

The market's verdict arrived within a day. Coty shares fell 4% to $2.14 on the news, a stock now down 60% over the past year and trading 65% below its 52-week high of $5.33. Investors, it seems, were less interested in the cash than in what it is replacing.

The mechanics of the exit

Coty picked up the Gucci fragrance and cosmetics license in 2016, and by its own account grew the business more than 60% since 2019 — a rare bright spot in a portfolio otherwise weighed down by mass-market brands like CoverGirl and Rimmel London. That run ends earlier than planned because L'Oréal, having paid Kering roughly €4 billion last year for Kering Beauté, wanted the Gucci license folded into the same 50-year agreement sooner rather than waiting out Coty's contract.

The termination payment breaks down plainly: $250 million in cash landed at signing, with a further $150 million due by September 30, 2027, of which up to $30 million is contingent on unspecified conditions. L'Oréal is covering about 70% of the associated transition and inventory costs. Coty expects to owe roughly $30 million in cash taxes on the total, trimming the net proceeds but still leaving a meaningful sum. Markus Strobel, the Procter & Gamble veteran who took over as Coty's executive chairman and interim chief executive on January 1 after Sue Nabi's departure, called it "a favorable outcome to conclude the Gucci Beauty license, enabling Coty to redeploy capital and focus on our priority brands." The litigation the deal resolves — a dispute over license terms that had been running alongside the transition talks — disappears as part of the same package, clearing the path for what both sides describe as an orderly handover. Coty will keep operating Gucci Beauty until the July 2027 cutover.

Where the math gets uncomfortable

The cash is real. The EBITDA hole it leaves behind is the part analysts are stuck on. Jefferies' Sydney Wagner called the early termination favorable on balance — it converts an uncertain future into cash today and takes the litigation off the table — but added that "the loss of Gucci Beauty remains a meaningful long-term portfolio headwind." Barclays' Lauren Lieberman was blunter, writing that the agreement "does not solve for the issue of Coty being able to fill the gap from lost EBITDA." Barclays now models Coty's fiscal 2028 sales 4% below its October estimates and adjusted EBITDA down 32% over the same comparison, translating to roughly $115 million in lost adjusted EBITDA by fiscal 2028 — about 15% of the company's total profit base — if Gucci's trajectory tracks the broader prestige beauty slowdown.

That is the crux of the trade Coty has made: it swapped a recurring, high-margin royalty stream tied to someone else's brand equity for a lump sum and a cleaner balance sheet, on the bet that its own brands can grow into the space Gucci leaves behind.

The bet on what's left

Coty says the proceeds will go toward debt reduction, reinvestment in its core prestige fragrance and beauty portfolio, and organizational streamlining to match the company's now-smaller scope — all under the "Coty:Curated" turnaround plan that interim CEO Strobel introduced earlier this year after a disappointing second quarter. That plan leans on prestige names Coty still owns or fully controls: Kylie Cosmetics, which along with other prestige brands made up 68% of second-quarter sales, a forthcoming Calvin Klein fragrance launch, Marc Jacobs Beauty makeup arriving later this year, and continued expansion of Adidas fragrances. On the mass side, Coty is trying to stabilize CoverGirl and Rimmel London while reviewing whether to keep its mass color-cosmetics business and Brazilian operations at all.

Whether that portfolio can replace $115 million or more in annual EBITDA is an open question the market has already started pricing skeptically — the stock's post-announcement drop suggests investors read this less as balance-sheet relief and more as confirmation that Coty's growth engine has shrunk. The company's next quarterly report, and any update on the mass-brand portfolio review, will be the first real test of whether Coty:Curated brands can grow fast enough to offset what Gucci Beauty is about to take with it when it leaves in mid-2027.
