API data shows crude stockpiles fell 6.07 million barrels

Storage tanks at a petroleum refinery — the kind of infrastructure whose weekly fill levels drive the API and EIA inventory reports. — Carl Young / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
API data shows U.S. crude stockpiles fell 6.07 million barrels last week — nearly eight times the prior week's 765,000-barrel draw.

Crude oil pulled out of U.S. storage tanks at nearly eight times last week's pace, according to industry data released Tuesday — a jolt that cuts against the prevailing story of a market awash in supply.

The American Petroleum Institute's weekly count showed commercial crude stockpiles fell 6.072 million barrels in the week to June 26. The week before, the draw was a modest 765,000 barrels. Put the two side by side and the swing looks less like a trend than a snap-back: inventories had been falling hard through most of June — 7.2 million barrels the week of June 5, 8.3 million barrels the week of June 12 — before nearly stalling out at the 19th. Tuesday's number resumes the steeper pace.

API figures are a private, voluntary survey and routinely diverge from the Energy Information Administration's official tally, released the following morning. Traders treat API as a same-week preview rather than gospel, and Wednesday's EIA report — due out later today — is what will either confirm the draw or complicate it. Still, a swing of this size rarely passes without comment on trading desks, if only because it forces a question: is the physical market tighter than the price action suggests?

Barrels tightening, prices falling

That question has some bite because the two signals have been pointing in opposite directions. Crude prices fell roughly 30% over the second quarter, the steepest quarterly drop since 2020, as shipping through the Strait of Hormuz resumed following progress in U.S.-Iran talks and oil that had been bottled up in the Gulf worked its way back onto the water. WTI closed Tuesday near $70 a barrel, Brent near $73 — both well off where they stood when Cushing, Oklahoma's storage hub, was reporting levels near its lowest since 2014 earlier in June.

A market flooded with supply optimism does not usually keep drawing down inventories at this clip. One read is that the price relief is ahead of the physical reality — that whatever crude was freed up by calmer shipping lanes hasn't yet reached refiners and storage tanks in volume, while refinery runs into the early-summer driving season keep pulling barrels out faster than they come in. Another is simpler: weekly API prints are noisy, and one outsized draw after a near-flat week is as easily mean reversion as it is a signal.

What's missing from Tuesday's release is a clean read on what analysts had penciled in before the number landed — without that consensus figure, it's hard to say whether the market was surprised or merely watching a familiar pattern repeat. That gap should close within hours.

What to watch

The EIA's Weekly Petroleum Status Report, due Wednesday morning, is the next data point, and it carries more weight with traders than the API count alone. Beyond that, Cushing storage levels and refinery utilization rates will say more about whether this is a demand story or a supply one — and whether the crude market's summer has more surprises left in it than the quarter's price chart suggests.
