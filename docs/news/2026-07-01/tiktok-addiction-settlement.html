TikTok nears settlement of addiction lawsuit to avoid trial

TikTok has now settled two bellwether cases in the coordinated social media addiction litigation rather than let either reach a jury. — Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
TikTok settled a Florida teen's addiction lawsuit to dodge a July 27 jury trial, its second such settlement this year, leaving Meta and Snap to face the jury alone.

TikTok has agreed to settle a lawsuit brought by a Florida teenager who says the app's design fed his anxiety, depression and suicidal thoughts — a deal that pulls the company out of a jury trial set to open July 27 in Los Angeles County Superior Court. The terms are confidential. The company did not admit wrongdoing.

The plaintiff, identified in court filings by his initials, R.K.C., says he started using TikTok at age eight and was diagnosed with generalized anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder years later, entering therapy in 2023. His suit named TikTok alongside Meta, Snap and Google's YouTube, arguing the platforms engineered features — infinite scroll, autoplay, algorithmic recommendation feeds — to keep young users hooked regardless of the psychological cost. YouTube settled with him earlier this month. Meta and Snap have not, and are expected to face a jury on schedule, with Snap chief executive Evan Spiegel reportedly among those who could testify for the first time in the litigation.

A pattern, not a one-off

This is the second time TikTok has bought its way out of a bellwether trial rather than let a jury hear it. In January, the company settled with a California plaintiff, K.G.M., days after Snap did the same, avoiding what became the first trial in the coordinated litigation. That case still went ahead against the remaining defendants: in March, a jury found Meta and YouTube negligent in how they designed their platforms and awarded $6 million in damages — the first verdict of its kind against a social media company on addiction claims.

Bellwether trials exist to test how juries respond to representative cases before thousands of similar claims are resolved, in settlement talks or otherwise. More than 10,000 individual suits and nearly 800 school-district claims are pending nationally, coordinated through multidistrict litigation that lawyers and reporters have taken to comparing with the tobacco litigation of the 1990s. Settling early, twice, lets TikTok avoid setting a courtroom precedent — and keeps its engagement metrics, internal design documents and executive testimony out of public view, at least for now.

Why settle now

A jury verdict against TikTok would do more than cost money. It would hand plaintiffs' lawyers a template for the thousands of cases still in the pipeline, and hand regulators a factual record to point to. That risk is already visible in Europe, where the European Commission said in February that TikTok may have breached the Digital Services Act by failing to address addictive design risks, after leaked internal material suggested the company knew some of its own screen-time tools barely worked. Settling in Los Angeles doesn't make that scrutiny go away, but it keeps a Los Angeles jury from adding to it.

The July trial proceeds against Meta and Snap regardless, and its outcome — a second verdict, another settlement, or something in between — will shape how the remaining social media addiction claims get priced. Watch for TikTok's next move once that verdict lands: a pattern of pre-trial settlements only holds up as a strategy if the juries who do hear these cases keep coming back with numbers the company would rather not pay twice.
