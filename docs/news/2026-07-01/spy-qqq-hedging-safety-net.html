Put buying on SPY and QQQ builds a hidden safety net under record-high stocks

In one sentence
Record options volume and an elevated SKEW index show institutions quietly buying downside protection even as the S&P 500 sets fresh highs.

The S&P 500 closed out June with its ninth straight weekly gain and its 23rd record close of the year, briefly trading above 7,600. The Nasdaq-100 has kept pace. By the standard measures of investor mood, nothing is wrong: the VIX, Wall Street's so-called fear gauge, sat at a placid 17.65 as the quarter ended.

But a second, less-watched gauge tells a different story. The Cboe SKEW Index — which prices in the odds of a sharp, unexpected drop in the S&P 500 — has been running around 140 to 144 through June, well above its neutral baseline of 100. SKEW does not predict a crash. It measures how much traders are willing to pay to insure against one. Right now, they're paying up.

The plumbing is running hot

The clearest signal isn't sentiment — it's volume. Cboe's multi-listed options hit an all-time record average daily volume of 22 million contracts in May, up more than 25% from a year earlier. SPY and QQQ, the two most liquid equity ETFs in the world, are the primary vehicles for that flow, and traders have been using them less for outright bets and more for structural insurance.

There was a brief, telling wrinkle in that calm mid-June: the 9-day VIX, which tracks very short-dated volatility, spiked nearly two points above the standard 30-day VIX — an inversion that typically shows up only when a specific date on the calendar worries traders more than the months around it. The date in question was the mid-June Fed meeting and the inflation data released alongside it. Once that risk passed, the term structure normalized.

What the hedge actually looks like

Few institutions are buying plain protective puts anymore — an option that decays to zero if the market simply grinds higher, as it has for nine straight weeks, is an expensive habit. Instead, desks are building put-spread collars: buying a near-the-money put on SPY or the S&P 500 index, then selling a second put further out of the money to offset the cost. The JPMorgan Hedged Equity Laddered Overlay ETF (HELO) is a retail-accessible version of the same idea — it buys puts struck 5% below the market and finances them by selling puts struck 20% out of the money, layering three-month hedges so a third of the book resets every month. The design covers a 5%-to-20% drawdown; below that, the fund starts losing money again, same as everyone else.

A signal, not an alarm

Worth some caution here: the SPX put-call ratio, a simpler and longer-running gauge, read 1.23 on June 25 — elevated, but inside the 1.0-to-1.4 band it has occupied for years. Institutions buy index puts as routine portfolio insurance whether they're bullish or not, so a reading in that range is not by itself a bearish tell. What is unusual is the combination: record options volume, a persistently rich SKEW reading, and a term-structure inversion around a known event, all arriving while the index sets fresh highs. That pattern points to hedging against a specific, if unspecified, tail risk rather than a broad loss of conviction.

The next test of that thesis lands with the July jobs report and the following Fed meeting, the kind of data points that produced June's brief volatility spike. If SKEW keeps climbing while the index keeps setting records, the options market will be saying more about what institutions fear than what they're buying.
