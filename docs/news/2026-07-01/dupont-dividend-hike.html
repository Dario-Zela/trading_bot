DuPont triples its dividend to $0.60 a share, betting on cash generation through the cycle

The DuPont Building in Wilmington, Delaware, a former headquarters and a landmark of the company's 224-year history. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
DuPont's 200% dividend hike to $0.60 a share lines up almost exactly with a same-day 1-for-3 reverse stock split, leaving the real payout ratio roughly unchanged.

DuPont de Nemours told shareholders last week that its quarterly dividend is tripling — from $0.20 a share to $0.60, a 200% increase the company is happy to have written up as a vote of confidence in its cash generation. Read the filing closely, though, and the number tells a smaller story than the headline suggests.

The board declared the new $0.60 payout on June 24, the same day a 1-for-3 reverse stock split took effect, collapsing roughly 405 million shares outstanding into about 135 million. Multiply the old $0.20 dividend by three and the answer is $0.60, to the penny. A shareholder who held DuPont stock through the split is set to collect, in total dollars, almost exactly what they collected the previous quarter — just spread across a third as many, three-times-pricier shares. The "tripling" is largely an artifact of arithmetic, not a new commitment of cash.

The mechanics behind the number

DuPont's board and shareholders approved the reverse split at the May 21 annual meeting, and it became effective at 12:01 a.m. Eastern on June 24 — the same day the new dividend rate was announced. Outstanding shares fell from roughly 405 million to about 135 million; authorized shares dropped from 1.67 billion to 555.6 million. The stock, which had traded in the teens and twenties for months, now trades around $75, and DuPont carries a market capitalization near $30.9 billion.

The reverse split briefly scrambled analyst screens, too: price targets set before June 24 needed to be multiplied by three to mean anything, and for several days trackers were quoting stale, pre-split numbers against a post-split price — a minor but telling illustration of how much of this episode is capital-structure housekeeping rather than a fundamental re-rating.

None of that makes the dividend fake. It means the real question is whether the underlying payout ratio — what fraction of earnings DuPont commits to dividends — actually moved. It didn't, much. When DuPont set its first post-spinoff dividend at $0.20 a share in November 2025, the company said the rate was "in line with new DuPont's targeted 35%-45% payout ratio." On a split-adjusted basis, the new $2.40 annualized dividend against DuPont's own full-year adjusted earnings guidance of $7.02 to $7.16 a share works out to roughly 34% — inside the same band the company set for itself eight months ago.

What's actually driving the confidence

Set the split aside and DuPont's underlying numbers have genuinely improved. First-quarter 2026 net sales rose 4% to $1.7 billion, with adjusted earnings per share of $0.55, up 53% from $0.36 a year earlier and ahead of Wall Street's estimate. Operating cash flow from continuing operations jumped to $232 million from $77 million, though free cash flow conversion was a comparatively modest 65% — DuPont is guiding to greater than 90% conversion for the full year, weighted toward the second half.

CEO Lori Koch called it "a strong start to the year, exceeding our financial guidance through disciplined commercial and operational execution." The company also closed the sale of its Aramids business on April 1 for roughly $1.2 billion in pretax proceeds, immediately funding a $275 million accelerated share buyback — bringing cumulative repurchases to $775 million of a $2 billion authorization. CFO Antonella Franzen framed the moment as one of unusual flexibility: "We do have a lot of flexibility relative to the cash in the door related to the Aramids transaction as well as the balance sheet we have." Management is targeting roughly 2x leverage and reaffirmed full-year sales guidance of $7.155-$7.215 billion.

The Aramids sale is the latest step in a longer unwinding: DuPont spun off its electronics business as Qnity Electronics in November 2025, collecting a roughly $4.1 billion special distribution in the process, and now describes itself simply as focused on healthcare, water, construction and industrial markets. Each divestiture has both simplified the portfolio and refilled the cash pile funding buybacks and, now, the dividend reset.

The overhang that didn't go away

Wall Street's reaction has been positive but not uniform. Deutsche Bank and RBC Capital both raised price targets recently, citing improving EBITDA trends, while Goldman Sachs initiated coverage at Neutral, arguing the growth case leans heavily on the Healthcare & Water Technologies segment carrying the rest of the portfolio. Across 17 analysts tracked by one service, ratings skew heavily toward Buy, with a small Hold minority and no Sell ratings.

The unresolved risk sitting underneath all of it is PFAS. DuPont shed most of its historic "forever chemicals" liability to Chemours in a 2015 spinoff, but cost-sharing arrangements still link the companies: a roughly $875 million settlement with New Jersey, covering Chemours, DuPont and Corteva jointly, begins payments this year, and Chemours separately settled EPA claims in June tied to its Washington Works, Fayetteville Works and Chambers Works sites. None of that lands directly on DuPont's income statement today, but it is the kind of contingent liability that dividend models tend to underweight until it isn't contingent anymore.

The next concrete test comes when DuPont reports second-quarter results, expected in early August, and again when the first $0.60 payment goes out on September 15 to holders of record August 31. If free cash flow conversion climbs toward the guided 90%-plus in the back half of the year as promised, the payout ratio holds where management says it wants it. If it doesn't, a dividend that looks tripled on paper — but isn't, in substance — will be the first place analysts look for cracks.
