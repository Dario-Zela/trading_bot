HSBC raises its Micron price target for the fifth time

A patterned silicon wafer — the substrate memory chips like Micron's DRAM and HBM are cut from. — Inductiveload / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
HSBC has raised its Micron price target five times in 2026, from $350 to $1,700, as AI-driven memory shortages outpace even bullish forecasts.

Nobody argues about memory chips at dinner parties. They argue about Nvidia, about who's buying the most GPUs, about whether the AI trade has run too far. Micron Technology — which doesn't make processors at all, just the memory those processors depend on — has spent 2026 quietly becoming one of the best-performing large-cap stocks in the world, and HSBC's research desk has been playing catch-up with it in real time.

On June 25, HSBC analyst Ricky Seo raised his price target on Micron to $1,700 a share, up from $1,100. It was the fifth increase this year. In January, Seo's target was $350. It went to $500, then $750, then $1,100 in May, and now $1,700 — a nearly fivefold climb in six months, on the same stock, from the same analyst, without a rating change. He has kept it at Buy throughout.

Why memory, not chips

Seo's argument hasn't changed shape, only scale: memory demand is running so far ahead of supply that prices are holding even as production expands, and the shortage isn't a blip. He's framed it as a four-to-five-year upswing, longer than the two-to-three-year boom-bust cycles that have historically defined the memory business. Part of the reason is architectural. Nvidia's upcoming Rubin Ultra chip is expected to need roughly 3.5 times more DRAM than today's models, and Seo projects the overall DRAM market to grow 69% in 2026, with NAND flash up 62%. Every new generation of AI accelerator needs disproportionately more memory bolted onto it, and there are only three companies in the world — Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix — that make the high-bandwidth memory those chips require.

The latest upgrade landed days after Micron's fiscal third-quarter results gave Seo's thesis hard numbers to point to. Revenue came in at $41.46 billion against Wall Street's roughly $35.7 billion estimate; adjusted earnings hit $25.11 a share versus consensus near $20.49. Gross margin reached 84.9%, a company record, and Micron told investors its high-bandwidth memory is sold out for all of 2026. Guidance for the current quarter calls for revenue of $50 billion, plus or minus $1 billion, at roughly 86% margin.

A consensus still catching up

Even at $1,700, HSBC isn't out on a limb the way it might sound. Micron shares themselves touched a record near $1,089 in late June, and the Street's average target across all analysts now sits around $1,404 — meaning HSBC has gone from broadly in line with peers in January to the most bullish house on the stock, five revisions later. That pattern, one analyst structurally behind the curve and forced into repeated catch-up upgrades, is a reasonable proxy for how the market as a whole has treated memory relative to the GPU names it fixates on. The component nobody discusses over dinner turned out to be the one the AI buildout can't function without.

Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results in late September; that print, against a guide already calling for a record quarter, is the next test of whether Seo's supercycle call still has room to run — or whether the target chasing finally catches the stock.
