Director sentenced for using Netflix funds to trade crypto

Netflix's headquarters in Los Gatos, California. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Director Carl Rinsch got 30 months in prison for diverting $11 million in Netflix production funds into failed options bets and Dogecoin.

The pitch was science fiction. The accounting turned out to be, too.

Carl Erik Rinsch, the director best known for the 2013 Keanu Reeves samurai film "47 Ronin," was sentenced Monday to 30 months in federal prison for diverting $11 million in Netflix production financing into his own brokerage and crypto accounts. U.S. District Judge Jed Rakoff also ordered three years of supervised release, a $700 fine, and full restitution of the $11 million to Netflix.

Netflix had given Rinsch roughly $44 million between 2018 and 2019 to write and direct "White Horse," a sprawling sci-fi series later retitled "Conquest." When he came back asking for another $11 million to finish it, prosecutors say he had no intention of spending it on the show. Instead he moved the money into a personal account, then into speculative stock options — losing about $5.9 million within months — before shifting roughly $4 million of what remained onto the Kraken exchange to buy Dogecoin. The series was never completed.

Rolls-Royces and a $638,000 mattress

What survived the trading losses went to a spending spree that made the eventual court filings read like a lifestyle-magazine parody: five Rolls-Royces, a red Ferrari, $652,000 on watches and clothes, $295,000 on luxury linens, and — the detail that stuck in headlines — $638,000 on two mattresses. Prosecutor David Markewitz told the court Rinsch "had every possible advantage" and that his motive was "naked greed."

Rinsch was convicted in December of wire fraud, money laundering and illegal monetary transactions. His defense leaned on mental health struggles, including what his attorneys described as improperly managed medication, and drew a supportive letter from Keanu Reeves, who called Rinsch capable of bringing "exceptional joy and warmth to people around him" while conceding he could "self-sabotage by amplifying the scale" of his ambitions. Rakoff was unmoved on the core question of intent. "The perhaps improper medication Mr. Rinsch received may have played a role," he said, "but he was determined to lie to get substantial moneys from Netflix, cover it up through money laundering and lies." He added that Rinsch "had no reason to do this. He chose to do this and covered it up for years."

There is nothing here about crypto markets misbehaving or algorithms gone wrong — Dogecoin was simply where the money went after the options bets failed. The lesson sits upstream of trading entirely: a studio wired eight figures to one man on the strength of a pitch deck, with control failures that let him treat production financing as a personal trading account for the better part of two years. Netflix has not said publicly whether it has changed how it disburses money to unfinished productions; the restitution order gives the company a decade or more to collect, assuming Rinsch, now headed to prison, ever has $11 million to give back.
