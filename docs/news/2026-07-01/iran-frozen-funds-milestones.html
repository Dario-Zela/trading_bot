Iran still hasn't received its $6 billion in frozen funds, US official says

The Strait of Hormuz, a condition Washington has tied to releasing Iran's frozen funds, seen from NASA's Terra satellite. — MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A US official says Iran still hasn't received any of the $6B frozen in Qatar and won't until Tehran clears unspecified 'milestones' — despite Iran's own claims of a release.

Tehran has not received a single dollar of the $6 billion in frozen funds it has spent the past week telling its own public was on its way home. A senior US official, speaking to the New York Post, put it more bluntly than Washington usually does on Iran policy: "None of this money is ever going to touch Iran."

The gap between those two statements is the story. On June 29, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told state media the $6 billion — held in Qatar since a 2023 prisoner-swap deal brokered under the Biden administration — would be released and returned to Iran, calling it "a great victory for the Iranian people." He said it represented half of roughly $12 billion Tehran believes it is owed from frozen accounts. Qatari and US officials have not confirmed anything close to that.

What the US official described instead is a conditional, phased arrangement: the funds would not go to the Iranian treasury at all, but would be released — if released — for humanitarian purchases like food and medicine, under a "dual-key" mechanism requiring sign-off from both the Qatari central bank and the US Treasury. And release depends on Tehran hitting a set of "milestones" that Washington has declined to spell out in full, though officials have pointed to reopening and de-mining the Strait of Hormuz as one of them.

A ceasefire that keeps slipping

The money sits inside a larger, shakier framework. Trump and Pezeshkian signed the so-called Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding earlier in June, brokered by Pakistan, setting a 60-day ceasefire clock meant to end the brief war between the two countries and open the door to a fuller peace deal — one that could eventually touch as much as $25 billion in frozen Iranian assets. That framework didn't hold cleanly: on June 26, US Central Command struck Iranian missile and drone storage sites and coastal radar after President Trump accused Tehran of violating the ceasefire with a drone attack on shipping in the Strait of Hormuz. Talks resumed anyway, which is roughly where things stand now — a fragile truce, an unsigned final deal, and $6 billion sitting untouched in a Doha account.

Iran's Foreign Ministry has made clear it isn't inclined to accept dictated terms. Spokesman Esmail Baghaei said this week that Iran "said goodbye to the language of 'must' 47 years ago" and would make decisions "based on the interests and rights of the Iranian people" — a pointed rejection of Washington's benchmark-first approach, even as Tehran's own president was publicly claiming victory on the money.

Why traders are watching

None of this has produced a clean market signal yet — no single equity or futures move traces directly back to the Post's report. But the ambiguity itself is the mechanism. Every unresolved condition on the $6 billion, every contradicted claim about the Strait of Hormuz, keeps a geopolitical risk premium priced into crude and into sanctions-exposed names, without giving traders a date or a threshold to underwrite. Markets can price a war or price a peace; what's harder to price is a ceasefire that could re-escalate over a shipment of frozen cash whose release conditions neither side has fully published.

The near-term marker is the 60-day window set by the Islamabad MOU, which runs into late summer. Whether the Strait of Hormuz stays open to commercial traffic — and whether Washington and Tehran ever agree publicly on what the "milestones" actually are — will do more to move oil risk premia than anything either government says about the $6 billion itself.
