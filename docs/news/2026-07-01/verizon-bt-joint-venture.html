Verizon and BT Group agree international joint venture

In one sentence
Verizon and BT are merging their international enterprise units into a $4B, 50:50 joint venture — a balance-sheet tidy-up more than a growth bet.

Verizon Communications and BT Group are combining their international enterprise-connectivity businesses into a jointly owned company, the two carriers announced Monday — a deal that hands Verizon a stake in a $4 billion-revenue platform serving multinational clients in more than 180 countries, while letting both firms offload units neither could scale alone.

The new venture will be split 50:50, with equal voting rights, and will combine BT International — which sells secure network services to multinational corporations — with Verizon's international enterprise wireline business. Verizon will make an “equalization payment” of $625 million to BT to balance the value of assets each side is contributing. The combined entity, still unnamed, will be incorporated in Jersey and headquartered in the UK. Martijn Blanken, a telecom veteran with stints at Telstra and KPN, is set to take over as chief executive when the deal closes, expected sometime in 2027 pending regulatory clearance.

"Customers will benefit from new, secure and resilient connectivity platforms designed for the age of AI," BT chief executive Allison Kirkby said. Verizon's Dan Schulman called it "a cutting-edge, AI-ready and secure platform serving global customers."

A tidy-up, not a land grab

Strip away the AI framing and the deal reads as a consolidation of two subscale businesses rather than a bid to reshape the global telecom order. Neither BT's international arm nor Verizon's enterprise wireline unit commands anywhere near the market position each company holds domestically — a mismatch analysts have flagged for years. Jitesh Bhayani, a telecom analyst tracking the sector, described the move as letting both companies "shed units that were subscale, low-margin and strategically peripheral to their core businesses."

The prize for Verizon appears to be BT's Global Fabric, a network-as-a-service platform for managing enterprise connectivity across cloud providers — something Verizon lacked a comparable answer to. The combined company will compete for multinational contracts against Orange Business, NTT, Vodafone, Tata Communications, Telefónica and Colt, a crowded field where scale matters more than brand.

The costs land first, the benefits later

For Verizon shareholders, the immediate effect has been financial pain, not strategic clarity. The company disclosed it will book $700 million to $800 million in non-cash losses this quarter from reclassifying the international assets as held-for-sale, on top of up to $750 million in restructuring and severance charges tied to the transaction and a separate acquisition, Carolina West Wireless. Verizon shares fell more than 5% in the days around the announcement, compounding a separate blow: the stock was removed from the Dow Jones Industrial Average, replaced by Alphabet, after 22 years in the index. VZ is trading near $42, close to its 52-week low of $38.39 and well off its high above $51.

Whether those charges are a one-time cost of tidying the balance sheet or a sign of a business under more sustained pressure is the real question the market is pricing. Verizon has framed the divestiture as freeing up management attention and capital for its domestic priorities — integrating the Frontier Communications acquisition and building out fiber — rather than as a pivot toward international growth. That framing matters: Verizon isn't buying its way into new markets here, it's consolidating a business it already had, with a partner doing the same on its side of the Atlantic. The JV's success will hinge less on strategic vision than on governance — 50:50 joint ventures are notoriously prone to decision-making gridlock — and on whether the merged sales force can actually win multinational contracts against larger rivals.

The deal still needs antitrust clearance across the jurisdictions where the two companies overlap, and employee consultations in markets requiring them, pushing completion into 2027. Verizon's second-quarter earnings report will be the next checkpoint for investors trying to separate the one-off accounting charges from any sign of deeper strain in its enterprise business.
