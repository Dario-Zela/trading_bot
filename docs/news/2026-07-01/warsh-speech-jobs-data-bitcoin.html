Warsh speech and jobs data loom as some traders abandon the never-sell-bitcoin playbook

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh at his swearing-in ceremony at the White House, May 22, 2026. — Daniel Torok / The White House
In one sentence
A hawkish Fed chair's first overseas speech, an early jobs report and bitcoin's broken 'never sell' pledge collide in a thin, holiday-shortened trading week.

Three things are colliding this week that don't usually share a headline: a Federal Reserve chair's first big overseas speech, a jobs report moved up two days early, and a bitcoin bull finally admitting he might sell. None of them is decisive on its own. Together, they are enough to make traders who spent the spring buying every dip start asking what happens if the dip doesn't end.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in on May 22, shares a stage with Christine Lagarde, Andrew Bailey and Tiff Macklem on Wednesday at the European Central Bank's forum in Sintra, Portugal — his first appearance outside the United States since taking the gavel. It follows a hawkish debut: at his first FOMC meeting on June 17, Warsh held the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% and used the word "unambiguous" to describe the Fed's commitment to bringing inflation down, a tone markets read as unwilling to rush toward cuts.

In one sentence: A hawkish Fed chair's first overseas speech, an early jobs report and bitcoin's broken "never sell" pledge are converging in a holiday week thin enough to amplify all three.

A jobs report with nowhere to hide

The June employment report lands Thursday instead of the customary Friday, pulled forward because markets close for Independence Day. Economists expect around 100,000 to 115,000 new jobs and an unemployment rate holding at 4.3%, after May's payrolls came in stronger than forecast at 172,000. The catch is that recent prints have swung well outside consensus in both directions, and this one arrives a day after Warsh speaks — meaning traders get a hawkish-leaning Fed chair and a jobs number that could either validate or undercut him within about 24 hours, then face a shortened, thinly traded session before the long weekend with the bond market itself closed Friday.

Light holiday liquidity cuts both ways. Momentum desks have historically pushed prices higher into the break, and Wall Street has tended to buy ahead of index-fund flows rather than against them. But the same thin order books that let buyers run prices up let sellers do the same on the way down — a market that "can easily be pushed around in either direction," as one options desk put it this week. The S&P 500, which closed at a record 7,599.96 on May 31 and touched an intraday high above 7,609 in early June, has already given some of that back, closing at 7,440.43 on June 29.

Bitcoin's broken vow

The more symbolic crack is in crypto. Michael Saylor's Strategy — the largest corporate bitcoin holder, with roughly 843,700 coins — spent four years insisting it would never sell a single one. In May the company approved a bitcoin monetization program allowing sales to fund its cash reserve, cover preferred-stock dividends and interest, and support buybacks. Late that month it sold 32 BTC for about $2.5 million to pay the dividend on its STRC preferred shares — a trivial 0.004% of its roughly $60 billion treasury, but the first disclosed sale in the company's history as a bitcoin buyer. Strategy's own shares fell nearly 6% on the news.

It was not an isolated case. Nakamoto Holdings sold bitcoin at roughly a 40% realized loss to keep operating, and Marathon Digital (MARA) liquidated more than 15,000 BTC to retire convertible debt, as the wider cohort of bitcoin-treasury companies now trades below the value of the coins they hold. Bitcoin itself has fallen from a record above $123,000 last July to roughly $58,000 on Wednesday, down more than 2.5% on the day, with spot bitcoin ETFs bleeding an estimated $2.8 billion to $3.5 billion over ten-plus straight days of outflows — the longest redemption streak since the products launched in January 2024.

Reading the hedges

Equity investors are not waiting to find out how the week resolves. The Cboe's S&P 500 put/call ratio jumped more than 10% even as the index notched its record close in late May, and multi-listed options volume hit an all-time monthly high in May at 22 million contracts a day, up more than 25% year-over-year. Rather than complacency at the highs, desks are running put spreads and collars — buying near-the-money protection and selling further out-of-the-money puts to offset the cost — a sign that professional money is quietly insuring against the same scenario retail bitcoin holders are now confronting directly: that a long-standing "just hold" thesis can break precisely when liquidity is thinnest.

What's next

Warsh speaks in Sintra on Wednesday at 13:00 GMT; the June jobs report follows Thursday morning at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, a day early and a day before markets close for the holiday. Any hint from Warsh that rate cuts are further off than priced, paired with a jobs number that misses to the downside, would hand momentum traders a genuine test of conviction in a week when there may not be enough liquidity to absorb the answer gracefully.
