Ipsen's $450 million bet on Kartos deepens biotech's myelofibrosis consolidation

A bone marrow harvest procedure. Myelofibrosis scars the marrow, the tissue at the center of the disease Ipsen is now betting $450 million on treating. — Photographer's Mate 2nd Class Chad McNeeley / U.S. Navy, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Ipsen will pay $450M upfront — up to $1.75B with milestones — for Kartos's myelofibrosis drug, as Sobi absorbs an FDA rejection and BeOne posts strong Brukinsa data.

Ipsen agreed on June 29 to buy Kartos Therapeutics, a privately held California biotech, for $450 million upfront — with as much as $1.75 billion in total consideration once regulatory and sales milestones are counted. The prize is navtemadlin, an experimental pill for myelofibrosis, a rare bone marrow cancer that scars marrow tissue and starves the body of healthy blood cells. The deal is expected to close in the third quarter of 2026, subject to customary regulatory clearances.[^1]

On its own, a $450 million check is not a large biotech transaction — plenty of Phase 1 assets have fetched more. What makes the Kartos deal worth a second look is the gap it is meant to fill in a drug class that already generates billions in annual sales, and the company it sits alongside in a single day's blood-cancer news: Sobi's regulatory stumble in gout, and BeOne Medicines' encouraging new data in lymphoma. None of the three companies are related to one another. What connects them is a sector where JAK-inhibitor economics, FDA manufacturing scrutiny and an unusually crowded oncology pipeline are all colliding at once.

The drug and the gap it's built for

Myelofibrosis is treated today mostly with JAK inhibitors — Incyte's Jakafi (ruxolitinib) and GSK's newer rival Ojjaara (momelotinib) — which shrink enlarged spleens and ease symptoms but do not fix the underlying disease. Jakafi alone generates more than $2 billion a year for Incyte, and the wider disease category is estimated at close to $1.7 billion in annual U.S. sales, according to market-research firm DelveInsight.[^2] The problem, and the opening Ipsen is buying into, is durability: an estimated 50% to 75% of patients discontinue ruxolitinib within three years, often because their response fades, and median survival after stopping the drug runs only one to two years.[^1]

Navtemadlin is designed as an add-on rather than a replacement. It inhibits a protein called MDM2, which in myelofibrosis cells suppresses p53 — the tumor-suppressor gene sometimes called the "guardian of the genome." By blocking MDM2, the drug is intended to restore p53's activity and push patients who are responding poorly to ruxolitinib back into remission, rather than switching them to an entirely new regimen.[^1] The approach is now in the pivotal Phase III POIESIS trial, which aims to enroll more than 600 patients at upwards of 250 sites worldwide, with top-line results expected in 2027.[^1]

The closest precedent is instructive on price. In 2022, GSK paid $1.9 billion in cash for Sierra Oncology to acquire momelotinib, which was then also in late-stage testing for myelofibrosis and went on to become Ojjaara.[^2] Ipsen's $450 million upfront looks modest by comparison, though the milestone-heavy structure — with the bulk of the eventual $1.75 billion contingent on approval and sales thresholds — reflects that navtemadlin, unlike momelotinib at the time of the Sierra deal, has not yet finished its pivotal trial. Ipsen is effectively pricing in the risk that POIESIS could still disappoint.

A rockier day elsewhere in blood cancer

The same week brought a reminder that the FDA's bar has not gotten any lower. Sobi, the Swedish rare-disease specialist, disclosed it had received a complete response letter for NASP (nanoencapsulated sirolimus plus pegadricase), its infused therapy for uncontrolled gout — a different disease area, but one the company had positioned as a major growth driver. The agency's objections were narrow: it flagged manufacturing control data and deficiencies at contract manufacturing facilities, and explicitly said it found no problems with NASP's clinical efficacy or safety.[^1] Sobi said it would meet with the FDA to map out a resubmission, but the setback delays a launch some analysts had modeled in the billions of Swedish kronor.[^3] It is a useful counterweight to the Ipsen news: a late-stage biologic can clear every clinical hurdle and still stall on a factory inspection.

BeOne Medicines, meanwhile, reported that Brukinsa (zanubrutinib), its BTK inhibitor, combined with Rituxan significantly delayed disease progression or death in treatment-naive patients with mantle cell lymphoma, cutting the risk of progression or death by 43% versus comparison therapy, with a trend toward better overall survival that did not yet reach statistical significance.[^1] BeOne plans to submit the data to regulators by the end of 2026. Brukinsa is already approved for several blood cancers, so this is less a binary catalyst than a steady accumulation of evidence supporting broader use — the kind of incremental data package that, over several cycles, has turned zanubrutinib into a genuine commercial rival to AbbVie's Imbruzena and Johnson & Johnson's Calquence.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the three items describe a hematology-oncology market that is consolidating unevenly. Large and mid-cap companies are still willing to pay for late-stage myelofibrosis and lymphoma assets — Ipsen's move follows GSK's Sierra purchase and continues a pattern of Big Pharma outsourcing early risk to smaller biotechs and buying in once Phase III data de-risks the story. But the FDA's manufacturing scrutiny, on display in Sobi's letter, is a live variable that dealmakers cannot fully underwrite at signing; a clean trial readout is necessary but not sufficient. For traders, the more consequential number to watch is not this week's $450 million check but whatever POIESIS reports in 2027 — that trial, not the acquisition price, will determine whether Ipsen's bet on plugging the ruxolitinib-resistance gap was worth the premium.

The next concrete markers: Sobi's FDA meeting on a NASP resubmission path, expected in the coming months; BeOne's regulatory filing for the expanded Brukinsa indication before year-end; and the Ipsen-Kartos deal's expected close in the third quarter of 2026, ahead of POIESIS data due in 2027.
