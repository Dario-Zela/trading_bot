Imviva Biotech wins FDA authorization for blood cancer trial device

CAR-T cell therapy involves extracting a patient's T-cells, engineering them to target cancer cells, and reinfusing them — the process at the center of Imviva's CTD402 trial. — Reyasingh56 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Imviva Biotech got FDA clearance to use Adaptive Biotechnologies' clonoSEQ test in a blood-cancer CAR-T trial — a step toward, not proof of, an approved therapy.

Two biotechnology companies said the Food and Drug Administration has cleared a path for them to test a diagnostic-guided approach to treating aggressive blood cancers — a regulatory step that is easy to overstate and worth being precise about.

Imviva Biotech, a clinical-stage firm developing off-the-shelf CAR-T cell therapies, said the FDA authorized its Investigational Device Exemption (IDE) application to incorporate Adaptive Biotechnologies' clonoSEQ assay into its ongoing TENACITY-01 trial. The authorization, announced June 30, does not approve any drug or device for sale. It permits Imviva to use clonoSEQ — a next-generation sequencing test that can find one cancer cell among a million healthy ones — to help select trial patients and track how they respond to treatment.

"FDA authorization of our IDE is a significant step forward as we advance the TENACITY-01 clinical trial," said Jan Davidson-Moncada, Imviva's chief medical officer, adding that the assay would let the company "more accurately monitor" residual disease and the durability of its therapy.

What's actually being tested

TENACITY-01 (NCT07070219) is a global Phase 1b/2 trial of CTD402, Imviva's investigational allogeneic anti-CD7 CAR-T therapy, in patients 12 and older with relapsed or refractory T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia and lymphoblastic lymphoma. A second arm enrolls patients already in remission but who test positive for minimal residual disease (MRD) — cancer cells that persist below the threshold of conventional detection and often presage relapse. The trial plans to enroll up to 120 patients combined across both arms.

That second cohort is where clonoSEQ does its work. Adaptive Biotechnologies' test is already FDA-cleared for MRD detection in multiple myeloma and certain leukemias, and available as a lab-validated test for T-cell cancers, detecting disease down to a 0.01% threshold. Mary Pat Lancelotta, an Adaptive senior vice president, called the assay "uniquely positioned to support these next-generation trial designs," citing its clinical track record in blood cancers. The stakes of getting MRD status right are real: published data on pediatric leukemia patients show roughly 77% ten-year event-free survival for those who test MRD-negative after treatment, versus about 32% for those who remain MRD-positive.

CTD402 has already picked up FDA orphan drug, rare pediatric disease, and Regenerative Medicine Advanced Therapy designations — signals of regulatory interest, not endorsements of efficacy. Imviva, which has raised more than $170 million and dosed over 300 patients across its pipeline in China, reports early data showing a 64.1% complete remission rate and 91.7% MRD-negative rate in the relapsed/refractory cohort, though those figures come from the company itself and predate any FDA review.

The companies expect Phase 1b interim data by mid-2026 and full study completion by late 2028 — the point at which the clonoSEQ integration, and CTD402 itself, will actually be judged.
