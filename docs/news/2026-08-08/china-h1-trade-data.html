China's H1 goods trade rises 17.3% to $4.46tn despite US tensions

The Yangshan Deep-Water Harbour Zone, part of the Port of Shanghai, one of the hubs handling China's surging export volumes — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's trade grew 17.3% to $4.46tn in the first seven months of 2026, powered by ASEAN, EU, Latin America and Belt-and-Road partners rather than the US.

China's goods trade with the rest of the world grew 17.3% year-on-year in the first seven months of 2026, reaching 30.13 trillion yuan — about $4.46 trillion — customs data released this week show. It is the first time the seven-month total has crossed 30 trillion yuan, and the growth is not evenly spread: it is concentrated almost entirely in markets that are not the United States.

Exports rose 14% to 17.44 trillion yuan; imports climbed faster, up 22% to 12.69 trillion yuan, a gap officials have pointed to as evidence that domestic demand, not just factory output, is doing some of the work. Machinery and electrical goods — the backbone of Chinese exports — rose 21.2% to 11.12 trillion yuan. Beneath that headline, the mix is telling: electric-vehicle exports jumped 71.2%, lithium-ion battery shipments 35.8%, wind-turbine equipment 34.8%. This is the green-tech and advanced-manufacturing push showing up directly in the trade ledger, not just in policy speeches.

The pivot away from Washington

The regional breakdown is where the diversification story sharpens. Trade with ASEAN rose 20% to 5.14 trillion yuan, making Southeast Asia collectively China's largest trading partner bloc by a wide margin. Trade with Belt and Road Initiative partners rose 15.5% to 15.36 trillion yuan — roughly half of China's total trade now runs through that network. Latin America grew 15.4%, Africa 18.9%, and trade with the European Union, the slowest-growing of the major blocs, still managed 9.5% growth to 3.67 trillion yuan.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Washington's effective tariff on Chinese goods remains high — a blended rate near 33% once the fentanyl-related IEEPA tariff, Section 301 duties and the reciprocal tariff are stacked together, and the US Trade Representative added a further 12.5% Section 301 tariff in July over forced-labor enforcement. Yet China-US trade has actually risen for four straight months, according to the customs bureau, a reminder that decoupling and diversification are running in parallel rather than one replacing the other. The US relationship has not collapsed; it has simply stopped being the fastest-growing part of China's trade book.

For traders, the pattern matters more than any single monthly print. Sustained double-digit growth with ASEAN and Belt and Road partners points to durable shifts in commodity demand — more manufacturing inputs flowing into Vietnam, Indonesia and Malaysia, more Chinese capital goods and green-tech exports flowing out — with knock-on effects for regional currencies and shipping rates that a US-focused reading of the trade relationship would miss entirely.

The next test comes fast: the current US-China tariff truce, holding the reciprocal rate at 10%, runs only through the end of August, and Beijing's full-month July data — released alongside the seven-month figures — will be parsed for early signs of whether that truce holds or lapses back into escalation.
