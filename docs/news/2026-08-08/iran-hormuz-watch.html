STANDING WATCH · IRAN/HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-08-08 · 3 min read

Iran talks 'progressing' toward Hormuz shipping deal, US officials say

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow chokepoint between Iran and Oman that carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil and LNG exports. — CIA / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
US officials call Oman-mediated Iran talks on reopening the Strait of Hormuz 'progressing,' but a nearly identical deal collapsed in July.

The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to normal commercial traffic since Iran's Revolutionary Guard declared it shut in the wake of the February 28 US-Israeli strikes on Iranian nuclear and military sites — a war that is now into its sixth month with no formal end. On Thursday, US officials speaking on background described talks to reopen the waterway, mediated by Oman, as "progressing," with a deal characterised as close. They did not put a name to the claim, a date on the timeline, or terms on the record.

That caution is earned. A near-identical framework — a June 17 memorandum signed by President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian to end hostilities and reopen shipping — collapsed on July 8 after Israeli strikes in Lebanon prompted Iran to reimpose restrictions and hit several commercial vessels. Roughly 20 to 25% of the world's seaborne oil and a fifth of its LNG normally pass through the strait; when it shut in the spring, Brent crude spiked to $126 a barrel in the sharpest monthly jump on record, and prices have stayed elevated since.

What's actually on the table

The emerging arrangement, as described by officials on both sides this week, is narrower than "reopening." Iran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, called it a "temporary route" lasting two to four months, adding pointedly that "this understanding does not mean the full reopening of the Strait of Hormuz." The plan reportedly routes inbound vessels through an Iranian-controlled lane and outbound traffic through a corridor overseen by Oman, which would issue exit clearance after notifying Tehran.

The sides remain apart on money and access. Iran has floated a transit toll of 5–7% of cargo value; Oman has proposed 3%; Washington says any temporary route must carry "no approvals or permissions and no tolls or charges," according to a US official cited by ABC News. Tehran also wants US and Israeli-flagged vessels barred outright and compensation from what it calls hostile states before they may transit — terms Washington has publicly rejected. President Trump told reporters talks are "moving along very nicely" and that "we'll know in 48 hours," a phrase he has used before without a deal materialising. Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei called the negotiations "forward-moving," conditioned on whether "certain third parties" — a likely reference to Israel — "obstruct this process."

Vice President JD Vance has pointed to divisions inside Iran's government — moderates seeking an exit from the war against hardliners who, in his telling, "want the war to continue" — as a reason talks keep stalling short of signature.

The practical test is not the rhetoric but the paperwork: whether Tehran and Muscat finalise and publish the joint statement now said to be under final review, and whether it survives contact with the next flashpoint in Lebanon or elsewhere, as June's did not. Energy markets, and a US administration watching gas prices ahead of November's midterms, are waiting on the same signal.
