Conservatives pledge to ban foreign nationals from social housing

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch, whose party unveiled the social housing policy this week — ©House of Commons / Roger Harris (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
The Conservatives have pledged to bar foreign nationals from social housing in England, a plan critics call divisive and Labour calls unworkable.

The Conservative Party has pledged that, if returned to power at the next general election, it would strip foreign nationals of eligibility for council and housing-association homes in England — a policy aimed squarely at the acute shortage of social housing and, just as plainly, at the voters Reform UK has been peeling away from the Tories.

Shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately unveiled the plan this week, arguing that settling in Britain "should be earned by working, paying tax and integrating" rather than treated as an automatic entitlement. Under the proposal, new tenancies would go only to UK and Irish nationals and those with EU settled status. Existing tenants who fall outside that definition — a single person without UK or EU citizenship, or a couple where neither partner holds it — would have six months to leave and find a home in the private rental market.

The numbers involved are not trivial. Official data show 88.5% of social housing main tenants are UK or Irish nationals, leaving roughly 470,000 people — some 11.5% of tenancies — who are neither. The Conservatives estimate around 228,000 homes could eventually be freed up for British families, though that figure assumes near-total turnover among affected households, something six months' notice does not guarantee in a market already this tight.

Who would actually be affected

The policy's fine print matters more than its headline. Asylum seekers are already ineligible for social housing except in exceptional cases such as domestic abuse, so the change bites hardest on people with settled status, refugee status or humanitarian protection who currently qualify and, in many cases, have lived in Britain for years. That distinction is central to the criticism from Shelter chief executive Sarah Elliott, who called the plans "divisive" and warned they would "kick people out of their homes," and from Labour's Angela Rayner, who argued the policy would hit refugees and long-settled residents while doing nothing to address the underlying shortage of new-builds. Even Reform UK found an angle of attack: chairman Zia Yusuf said the Tories were still too generous, since EU nationals with settled status would keep their eligibility, and pledged that social housing under Reform would be "strictly for British citizens."

What the announcement does not settle is how the policy would work in practice — whether local authorities would face legal challenge over the six-month eviction clock, how displaced tenants' housing costs would be met in a private rental market already under strain, and what happens to children whose only home is being reallocated. Those questions, rather than the headline pledge, are likely to shape whether this becomes durable Conservative policy or another pre-election marker overtaken by events before it reaches a manifesto.
