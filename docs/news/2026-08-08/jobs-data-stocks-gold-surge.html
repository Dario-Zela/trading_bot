S&P 500 hits records and gold posts rare surge as weak jobs data guts rate-hike bets

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor on Wall Street, where the S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64 on Friday. — Carol M. Highsmith / Library of Congress via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A shock July jobs slump — payrolls down 23,000 vs. +80,000 expected — erased Fed rate-hike bets, sending the S&P 500 to a record and gold to its biggest weekly surge in years.

Wall Street closed out the week the way traders like best: with a record on the board and a clean story to explain it. The S&P 500 rose 0.6% on Friday to finish at 7,757.64, a fresh all-time high, capping its best week since April after a July jobs report landed so far below forecasts that it undid weeks of market chatter about the Federal Reserve raising interest rates.

The Nasdaq Composite climbed 1.3% to 26,690.62 and the Dow Jones Industrial Average added 151.83 points, or 0.28%, to 54,036.93. Over the full week, the S&P 500 and Dow each gained more than 3% and the Nasdaq rose roughly 5%, powered by a rebound in semiconductor stocks that had spent much of the summer lagging the broader market.

Gold moved just as sharply, and on a scale that has almost no recent precedent. Spot prices traded near $4,350 an ounce on Friday, up about 8% for the week — a weekly gain matched only seven other times in the past 40 years, according to an analysis of pricing data back to 1986. Three of those seven came during genuine financial-crisis stress — the Lehman Brothers collapse, the Fed's move to zero rates in December 2008, and its unlimited bond-buying pledge in March 2020. This was not that kind of week. It was one jobs report.

A jobs report that broke the hiking narrative

To understand why a single data release moved two very different asset classes in tandem, it helps to know what markets had priced in beforehand. The Fed's July meeting had ended in its most divided vote since 2016 — a 9-to-3 decision to hold the benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75%, with three dissenters pushing for a hike rather than a cut. Governor Lisa Cook had said days earlier she was prepared to support raising rates if inflation didn't resume its decline. By late July, rate-cut talk had essentially vanished from major Wall Street desks; the live debate was whether the Fed would hold steady through year-end or be forced to tighten further.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics report released Friday reversed that in a single morning. Employers cut 23,000 jobs in July, against forecasts for a gain of roughly 80,000. May and June payrolls were revised down by a combined 103,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1% from 4.2% — but for the wrong reason, reflecting people leaving the labor force rather than finding work. Wage growth slowed to 3.2%, below expectations. Fed funds futures traders cut the odds of a September rate hike from 57% to 44% within hours, and most now expect the central bank to hold its range through the next meeting rather than tighten further.

For equities, that was straightforwardly good news: a weaker labor market lowers the odds that the Fed leans harder on borrowing costs, and growth and technology stocks — the most rate-sensitive corner of the market — led the rally. A resilient earnings season, which had already been calming worries about heavy AI-related capital spending, gave the move a second engine. Global semiconductor sales hit a record $120.6 billion in May, up more than 100% year over year, and chip stocks posted their strongest week in two months as the rally broadened beyond the handful of mega-cap names that have carried the index for most of 2026.

Gold's rally is a different animal

For gold, the same jobs report was read through a different lens — not as a reprieve from tightening, but as the possible start of an easing cycle further out, plus a hedge against a labor market that is now visibly cracking. That alone explains part of an 8% weekly move. It doesn't explain all of it.

China has been the more persistent force underneath the price for months. Institutional investors there have been building long positions in gold-backed assets as a hedge against volatility in domestic technology stocks, while the People's Bank of China has extended an 18-month streak of reserve purchases, adding to holdings that now sit near 2,322 tonnes — about 9% of the country's foreign-exchange reserves. Chinese customs data has shown import volumes climbing toward multi-year highs as banks work through quotas and restock bullion to meet retail demand. That is a demand pattern built on diversification away from the dollar and hedging against a domestic equity bubble — not on the same fear of imminent U.S. recession that would typically explain a move this size.

That divergence is the paradox in this week's rally. A move this rare, historically, has usually meant acute financial stress. This one arrived with the S&P 500 simultaneously hitting a record high — a combination with no real analogue in the seven prior episodes. Either gold is pricing something equities haven't caught up to yet, or a China-driven bid, layered onto one soft jobs print, produced statistically the effect without the underlying substance.

What it says about the market's dependence on one data point

The more uncomfortable reading of the week sits underneath both moves. Stocks rallied because the labor market got worse. That is not a new phenomenon — markets have priced central-bank reaction functions for years — but it is worth stating plainly: a jobs report showing outright payroll losses and a two-month downward revision of 103,000 was, on Friday, treated as good news for equity holders. That works cleanly in one direction. If July's weakness turns out to be a blip and hiring rebounds in August, the hawks on the Fed's committee — the same three who dissented in July — regain the argument, and both rate expectations and Friday's gains could reverse quickly. If instead the weakness compounds into something closer to a genuine slowdown, the calculus changes again, and equity markets that just celebrated a soft labor report may find themselves repricing for a harder landing instead.

The next nonfarm payrolls report, due the first Friday of September, arrives days before the Fed's next policy meeting and will carry outsized weight for exactly that reason. Between now and then, investors are left holding a rally in stocks and a rally in gold that were driven by the same headline for two different reasons — one a straightforward rates trade, the other a bet on China's continued appetite for bullion that has little to do with the Fed at all.
