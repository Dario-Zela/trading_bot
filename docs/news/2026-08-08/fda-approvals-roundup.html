FDA clears new melanoma therapy, narcolepsy drug and mRNA flu vaccine in approval flurry

The FDA's headquarters at White Oak, Maryland, where the agency cleared three novel treatments within a single week. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
FDA cleared a $450,000 melanoma virus therapy, the first narcolepsy orexin drug, and the first mRNA flu shot within days of each other.

The Food and Drug Administration cleared three treatments in the space of a week — a genetically engineered virus for melanoma, the first drug that treats narcolepsy at its biological root, and the country's first mRNA flu shot. Individually, none is a household name yet. Together they say something about where the FDA's risk appetite sits in August 2026: still willing to move fast on thin data, still willing to bless genuinely new mechanisms, and still leaving plenty of unresolved questions for the companies now selling them.

A virus, injected, against melanoma

The headline approval went to Tudriqev (vusolimogene oderparepvec-wtpg), made by Replimune, a Massachusetts biotech that had been rejected by the FDA twice before this month's clearance[^1]. The drug is a modified herpes simplex virus, engineered to replicate inside tumors and provoke an immune response, injected directly into melanoma lesions every two weeks alongside intravenous nivolumab — Bristol Myers Squibb's Opdivo. It is approved for patients with advanced, unresectable cutaneous melanoma whose disease progressed after standard anti-PD-1 therapy.

The approval rests on accelerated-pathway data from the IGNYTE trial: a 24.2% objective response rate and a median response duration of 14.1 months among 91 efficacy-evaluable patients, a small single-arm dataset rather than a randomized comparison[^1][^2]. That is the trade the FDA keeps making in oncology — approve on a response-rate signal, require a confirmatory trial afterward to prove the drug extends survival. Replimune must now run that follow-up study to keep the approval.

Replimune has set a list price of $450,000 per treatment course before rebates[^3]. Shares rose more than 8% on the approval before slipping back in after-hours trading, as investors weighed the confirmatory-trial contingency against the novelty of the mechanism; Wedbush responded by upgrading the stock to Outperform with a price target of $19, up from $12[^4]. Because nivolumab is a Bristol Myers drug, a genuine expansion of Tudriqev's use would flow partly into the Opdivo franchise, though nothing in the approval commits BMS to anything beyond continuing to supply an existing, already-approved drug.

Narcolepsy and flu, cleared the same week

Two unrelated approvals landed alongside it. Orzeyful (oveporexton), from Takeda, is the first orexin receptor agonist approved for narcolepsy type 1 — a disorder caused by loss of orexin-producing neurons — and the first drug to target that underlying deficiency rather than just manage symptoms like excessive daytime sleepiness and cataplexy[^5]. Two Phase 3 trials, FirstLight and RadiantLight, met their primary endpoints.

Separately, the FDA approved Moderna's mFlusiva, the first mRNA-based influenza vaccine, for adults 50 and older, on condition that Moderna run an additional trial in patients 65 and up[^6]. In company trials of more than 40,000 people, it showed roughly 27% greater relative efficacy than a standard flu shot — a meaningful number if it holds up once the vaccine reaches wider use, expected for the 2026–2027 season.

What connects the three is less the science than the regulatory posture: each approval leans on accelerated or conditional pathways, betting that real-world use and required follow-up trials will validate a decision made on incomplete data. The confirmatory trials for Tudriqev and mFlusiva, and next year's flu season data, are where that bet gets tested.
