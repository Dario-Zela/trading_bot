North Sea shutdown could leave Treasury £13bn out of pocket in tax rebates

An oil platform in the North Sea, where operators are accelerating decommissioning as tax and licensing pressure mounts. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Operators quitting UK waters early are triggering North Sea decommissioning tax rebates faster than modelled, a hit the Treasury could feel to the tune of £13bn by 2035.

The UK government is facing a fiscal headache it did not model: a wave of North Sea decommissioning claims arriving years earlier than planned, as oil and gas operators accelerate their exit from British waters. The bill, according to industry estimates, could reach roughly £13bn by 2035.

The mechanics are straightforward but counterintuitive. When operators dismantle platforms and plug wells, they can claim back a share of the cost — worth roughly 25% — against tax paid on past profits. That relief was always going to fall due eventually. What has changed is the timing: rebates crystallise the moment a field is decommissioned, not when the pipes are finally cut and the platform towed away. The North Sea Transition Authority has warned that accelerated shutdowns could cost the Treasury £10.8bn in lost tax, with roughly half of that in direct rebates.

Annual decommissioning spending has already risen from £2bn in 2024 to a projected £3bn a year through 2030, with around £28bn expected to be spent by 2035. The NSTA puts the eventual full decommissioning bill for the whole basin at £48bn, with a final Treasury rebate liability approaching £30bn over the long run — the £13bn figure is the near-term slice landing inside the current fiscal forecasting window.

Why operators are leaving early

The proximate driver is tax, not geology. The Energy Profits Levy, raised by Chancellor Rachel Reeves to 38% and extended to March 2030, pushes the total marginal rate on North Sea profits to 78%. Combined with a ban on new drilling licences, that has left operators with more incentive to wind down productive assets and bank the rebate than to keep investing. BP's decision on 31 July to put its entire North Sea business up for sale — ending 60 years of production in the basin it helped pioneer at Forties in 1970 — is the clearest signal yet that the economics have turned.

"Forcing fields to close prematurely does not simply switch off future tax receipts; it also brings forward the point at which those decommissioning tax reliefs crystallise, increasing the near-term pressure on the public finances."

That warning comes from Brian Gilvary, the former BP chief financial officer who now chairs Sir Jim Ratcliffe's Ineos Energy — hardly a disinterested observer, but one with a clear view of the mechanics. Ashley Kelty, an analyst at Panmure Liberum, was blunter: the acceleration, he said, "shows the folly of the drive to shut down the North Sea."

For the Treasury, the awkward part is that this liability sits outside the normal tax-and-spend cycle politicians campaign on — it is a legacy cost, triggered by private investment decisions, that shows up in the fiscal arithmetic regardless of who is in office. Rachel Reeves has already delayed plans to scrap the windfall tax once; the next test comes when the Office for Budget Responsibility next updates its energy-receipts forecasts, and finds out how much of that £13bn has moved from a modelling assumption into a cash claim.
