Thai PM vows stricter gun laws after school shooting kills eight

Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul, who pledged new restrictions on public firearm carrying after the Nonthaburi school shooting. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Thai PM Anutin Charnvirakul vowed new gun-carrying restrictions after a 14-year-old killed eight and wounded 22 at a Bangkok-area school.

The gunman was fourteen. Before dawn broke over Bang Kruai district on Friday, he shot his grandparents dead in their home in Bang Bua Thong, took his grandfather's registered CZ pistol and two boxes of ammunition, and made his way to Debsirin Nonthaburi School, where he was a student. Between roughly 9:30 and 9:50 that morning, he opened fire on teachers and staff before turning the gun on himself. By the time police secured the campus, eight people were dead and 22 more wounded — one of the deadliest school shootings in Thailand's history.

Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul visited the scene and, hours later, did what Thai leaders have done before after mass shootings: promised a new law.

"The new law will only allow government officials on duty to carry guns," Anutin told reporters, adding that neither ordinary citizens nor off-duty officials should be permitted to carry firearms in public. "This is horrible, it shouldn't have happened."

A familiar playbook

This is not new territory for Anutin. As interior minister after the October 2023 Siam Paragon mall shooting, he ordered a temporary freeze on new gun licences, tightened import rules and barred under-20s from shooting ranges, alongside longer-term proposals for mandatory psychological screening of gun owners. Those measures did little to change the underlying picture: Thailand remains Southeast Asia's most heavily armed civilian population by a wide margin, with an estimated 10.3 million guns in private hands — more than the rest of the ASEAN bloc combined — and a legal ownership regime that, on paper, is already fairly restrictive.

That is the paradox at the centre of this story. The weapon used on Friday was not illegally acquired; it was a registered handgun, lawfully owned by the shooter's grandfather and kept, evidently insecurely, inside the family home. "The gun was a registered firearm and was kept at a house, but somehow he gained access to it," Anutin acknowledged. A law restricting who may carry a gun in public does not address how a legally owned weapon ends up in a child's hands at home — the same gap that ran through the 2023 mall shooting and the 2022 Nong Bua Lamphu nursery attack that killed 36 people, most of them children.

Grief, not policy risk

Investigators say the boy had been bullied at school, including being locked in a bathroom the day before the attack, and had reportedly viewed footage of American school shootings in the days prior. Five of the dead were teachers; the ninth-grade student and his grandparents make up the rest of the toll announced so far, with several of the wounded still listed in critical condition.

For readers tracking market-relevant risk, there is little here to reassess. Thailand's baht, equities and bond markets showed no reaction to the news, and firearm restrictions of the kind Anutin is proposing carry no read-through for regional trade, investment flows or political stability — this is a domestic policy response to a national tragedy, not a signal of institutional stress. The more durable story is one of repetition: a mass shooting, a promised law, and a gun-ownership rate that has not meaningfully moved in years.

Watch for the draft legislation itself, which Anutin has not yet detailed — including whether it touches home storage and licensing renewal, the actual mechanism by which Friday's weapon reached a fourteen-year-old.
