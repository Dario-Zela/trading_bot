Trump touts $3bn in critical minerals deals to counter China

Mountain Pass in California remains the only active rare-earth mining and processing facility in the United States. — Tmy350 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Washington committed over $2bn in loans to battery and minerals firms like Sila Nanotechnologies and Sunrise Energy Metals to chip at China's dominance of the supply chain.

The Trump administration on Friday said it would channel more than $2 billion into American battery and critical-minerals companies, part of a broader $3 billion package of deals and financing unveiled as the president hosted roughly 200 mining executives at the State Department. The pitch, delivered alongside Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, was blunt: China controls the processing of the materials that power everything from fighter jets to smartphones, and Washington intends to buy its way out of that dependence.

The two largest checks went to firms trying to build supply chains that barely exist outside China. The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital committed $1.4 billion in conditional loans to Sila Nanotechnologies, a California-based maker of silicon-carbon battery anodes, to expand its Moses Lake, Washington plant and build a new lithium-ion cell factory. A further $400 million goes to Sunrise Energy Metals, an Australian company developing the Syerston project in New South Wales — pitched as the world's first primary mine dedicated to scandium, a metal used in the heat-resistant aluminum alloys found in fighter aircraft and spacecraft.

Smaller sums rounded out the package: $150 million to Niron Magnetics for rare-earth-free permanent magnets, $85 million to Standard Bauxite, and awards through the Export-Import Bank to Westwater Resources, Global Advanced Materials and 5E Advanced Materials for graphite, tantalum and boron projects. The Development Finance Corporation added $4.8 million for a rare-earth mining venture in Madagascar. Roughly $180 million more is earmarked for training programs at mining schools, an acknowledgment that the sector's bottleneck isn't only capital — it's geologists and metallurgists.

The scale of the gap

The numbers explain the urgency. China refines something like 95% of the world's rare earths and, as of a few years ago, produced 85% of battery anodes and 70% of cathodes. Sila's loan is meant to chip at that anode figure directly; Sunrise's at a scandium supply chain that is essentially non-existent outside China today. Neither company will move that needle quickly — Sila's Moses Lake plant only began operating last September, and Syerston hasn't started construction.

Friday's announcement builds on a program that has grown fast this year. The Office of Strategic Capital, created to funnel Pentagon-backed credit into mineral supply chains, signed a $725 million loan with uranium and rare-earth processor Energy Fuels in June and a combined $1.2 billion with Energy Fuels and Phoenix Tailings the same month. In July it launched a mechanism to route government-backed loans through private fund managers, aiming to mobilize up to $100 billion in total lending capacity — a sign the administration wants private capital, not just federal balance sheets, doing the heavy lifting eventually.

What the bet actually is

For investors, the interesting part isn't the headline total — it's the shape of the wager. These are conditional loans, not grants, aimed at commodity and materials businesses whose economics have historically lost to Chinese state-subsidized competitors on cost alone. Federal backing lowers the execution risk that has sunk earlier Western critical-minerals ventures — cheaper capital, a buyer of last resort in the Pentagon, political cover against future policy reversal. Shares in companies that land these loans, including Sunrise Energy Metals on the ASX, have moved on the news before.

What federal money can't do is compress the years-long lag between a loan commitment and a functioning mine or a cost-competitive plant, nor guarantee Beijing won't respond by loosening or tightening its own export controls in ways that change the payoff calculus entirely. Mountain Pass in California remains the only operating rare-earth mine and processing site in the US, more than a decade after the strategic vulnerability became obvious to policymakers.

What's next

The next data point worth watching is construction progress at Sunrise's Syerston site and utilization rates at Sila's Moses Lake plant, both of which will show whether these loans translate into actual output rather than announcements. Also worth tracking: whether the National Security Fund Finance program attracts the private capital it needs to reach anywhere near its stated $100 billion ceiling, and how Beijing's rare-earth export licensing regime — tightened repeatedly over the past two years — responds to Washington's buildout.
