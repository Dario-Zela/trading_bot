Trump revives bid to fire Fed Governor Lisa Cook after Supreme Court setback

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook, whose removal the White House is again pursuing over mortgage-fraud allegations she denies. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The White House gave Fed Governor Lisa Cook until August 26 to answer mortgage-fraud claims, restarting a removal bid the Supreme Court blocked in June.

President Trump's second attempt to remove Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook began, like the first, with a letter. This one is dated August 5, signed by White House deputy chief of staff Dan Scavino, and gives Cook until August 26 — 21 days — to respond to allegations that she committed mortgage fraud before joining the Fed's board. It is the administration's answer to a Supreme Court ruling five weeks ago that stopped the first removal attempt cold, and it is written, almost pointedly, in the language the justices themselves used to describe what a lawful attempt would require.

A letter written to the Court's own instructions

The new notice does not accuse Cook of anything she has not already denied. It restates the core claim — that she misrepresented two properties, in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and Atlanta, Georgia, as each being her "primary residence" on mortgage paperwork — and tells her the president is "considering removing" her from the Board of Governors. What is new is the framing: the letter says it is being issued "pursuant to the Supreme Court's opinion," a direct nod to the notice-and-response procedure the Court said Trump would have to follow if he wanted another run at firing her.

Cook's attorney, Abbe Lowell, was unmoved. "These allegations are as baseless now as they were a year ago," he said, "when President Trump tried to remove Governor Cook and interfere with the independence of the Federal Reserve." Cook has said publicly that the first attempt came "on a manufactured pretext because I refused to bow to political pressure and continued to set interest rates based only on what would best serve the American people."

What the justices actually decided

In June, the Supreme Court ruled 5-4 that Trump could not remove Cook while her legal challenge was pending — a narrow, procedural win for Cook rather than a ruling on the merits of the fraud claims themselves. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion, joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, Brett Kavanaugh and Ketanji Brown Jackson. Buried in a footnote, though, was the line the White House is now leaning on: nothing stops the president from "trying again," Roberts wrote, provided Cook is given proper notice and a real chance to contest the allegations before any removal takes effect.

That sentence is doing a lot of work now. The administration is treating it as a roadmap rather than a rebuke, and the August 5 letter reads like an attempt to check every procedural box the Court flagged — dated notice, a specific deadline, an explicit citation to the ruling itself. Whether that satisfies "proper process" in a way that survives a second court challenge is, at this point, untested. Cook is still litigating the underlying case in the D.C. Circuit, where the administration is appealing a lower-court ruling that had gone in her favor.

The paperwork behind the fight

The allegations trace back to a criminal referral filed roughly a year ago by Bill Pulte, the Federal Housing Finance Agency director and a Trump ally, who accused Cook of designating both her Michigan and Georgia properties as primary residences to obtain more favorable mortgage terms. Cook has never been charged with a crime. Her lawyers say she has lived primarily in Ann Arbor since 2005 and that a 2021 refinancing application there was accurate; they describe the Atlanta paperwork, which some documents reviewed by news organizations list as a "vacation home" rather than a primary residence, as an isolated notation rather than evidence of fraud.

None of that has been resolved by a court or a regulator. What has happened is that the mortgage dispute has become the vehicle for a much larger question: whether a president can remove a sitting Fed governor — nominally protected by statute from removal except "for cause" — over conduct that predates her appointment and has nothing to do with her conduct in office.

A board already being reshaped

Cook is not fighting from a position of institutional strength. In May, the Senate confirmed Kevin Warsh, Trump's pick, as the Fed's 17th chair in a 54-45 vote, ending Jerome Powell's eight years at the helm. Powell remains on the Board of Governors — his term there runs to 2028 — but he no longer sets the agenda, and he has said he intends to stay only until a Justice Department inquiry into the Fed's headquarters renovation concludes. Trump has separately pushed a criminal referral against Powell himself. Cook, confirmed in 2022 as the first Black woman to sit on the Board and holding a seat that runs until 2038, is now the most prominent remaining obstacle to a board fully aligned with the White House's preferences on interest rates.

The stakes sharpened last week when the July jobs report landed far short of forecasts — roughly 22,000 new positions against expectations near 75,000, with the unemployment rate ticking up to 4.3% — and erased what had been building odds of a rate hike at the Fed's September 15-16 meeting in favor of a possible cut. A Fed board with one fewer governor, or one whose independence has been visibly tested by a removal fight, enters that meeting under a different kind of scrutiny than a board operating at full, undisputed strength.

What comes next

Cook's deadline to respond falls on August 26. If she does not resign or satisfy the White House, Trump could issue a formal removal order shortly after, which would almost certainly trigger another emergency court filing from Cook's legal team and, potentially, another trip to the Supreme Court — this time on a fuller record than the emergency posture of June's ruling. Markets will be watching two dates that converge awkwardly: Cook's response deadline on August 26, and the FOMC's rate decision three weeks later, on September 16, the meeting at which her vote — and her seat — may or may not still be in question.
