Spain imposes Schengen border checks on Italy as Ceuta migrant crisis escalates

The border fence separating Spain's North African enclave of Ceuta from Morocco, the site of a record migrant surge in late July 2026. — Mario Sánchez Bueno / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Spain matched Italy's Schengen border checks with its own after tens of thousands crossed into Ceuta and a court barred summary returns to Morocco.

The Schengen Agreement has no army, no court of its own, and — as it turns out — no real defence against two governments determined to defy it in public. This week Spain and Italy did just that, trading passport checks across a border they do not even share, in a dispute that started with tens of thousands of people swimming into a five-square-mile Spanish enclave on the coast of Morocco.

From midnight on Saturday, Spain began checking passports, nationality and visa status of travellers arriving from Italy — a direct, mirrored response to controls Rome imposed on Spanish arrivals a week earlier. The measures run until September 7. Both governments are invoking a clause in the Schengen framework that allows temporary internal border checks in exceptional circumstances — a provision written for crises like terrorist attacks or major security threats, now being used by two founding EU members against each other over a migration event that, geographically, could not touch either of their shared borders. Spain and Italy do not adjoin one another. No migrant walks from Ceuta to Rome.

How a court ruling became a stampede

The chain of events starts on June 29, when Spain's Supreme Court ruled that migrants intercepted while swimming into Ceuta and Melilla — the country's two North African exclaves — could not be summarily deported to Morocco without individual due process, because they had not crossed the physical fence infrastructure that Spanish law treats as the actual border. It was a narrow, technical judgment about swimmers versus fence-climbers. Within days it had been rewritten online, by smuggling networks and social-media accounts, as an announcement that Spain had effectively opened Ceuta to anyone who could reach it by sea.

The result was one of the largest single migration surges Europe has recorded. Beginning July 30, an estimated 80,000 people crossed from Morocco into Ceuta, many in wetsuits or on improvised flotation devices, with roughly 49,000 arriving in a single 24-hour period on July 31. At least 100 people died in the crossing attempts, with a further 11 deaths recorded on the Moroccan side at Fnideq. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez called it "an attack" and "a violation of Spain's territorial integrity," vowing to "use every available means to restore control of the border." Morocco's government denied encouraging the surge, blaming smuggling networks and the online distortion of the court ruling. By early August, roughly 70,000 of those who had crossed had returned to Morocco voluntarily or under Spanish pressure, leaving a residual population in Ceuta's already overwhelmed reception centres.

Rome moves first, Madrid answers

Italy acted within a day of the crisis peaking. On July 31, Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi ordered a suspension of Schengen free movement with Spain, introducing "targeted and selective" checks on non-EU travellers arriving by air and sea — a measure Rome said would run through August, later extended to at least August 15. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's government framed it as pre-emptive: Deputy Prime Minister Antonio Tajani called it "a necessary choice to safeguard the security of our citizens and defend the European borders... made unavoidable today." France, more quietly, reimposed its own checks at the Spanish border the same day, wary that migrants might transit onward through the peninsula.

Madrid's response sharpened over the following week. Spanish officials gave Rome a two-day ultimatum to "rectify, put an end to the checks and treat Spaniards like other European citizens," warning of "proportional measures" if Italy did not comply. Sánchez took the argument public, posting that "solidarity and empathy are optional. Respect for European treaties and the facts is not" — pointedly citing Frontex data showing Italy has recorded more irregular border crossings than Spain in every year since 2021. Italy rejected the ultimatum outright, with officials saying Rome would not bow to "ultimatums or impositions," and held its checks in place. Spain's countermeasures followed on schedule.

Several migration analysts have noted the asymmetry underlying the dispute: Italy's original checks were, in practical terms, largely symbolic, given that no Ceuta migrant was ever going to transit through Spain to Italy by land. What both sides are actually contesting is something closer to reputational fairness — who bears the political cost of an EU migration failure, and who gets to claim the moral high ground while doing so.

What the fence-mending will cost

Schengen's internal-border exception was designed to be an emergency valve, used sparingly and lifted quickly — a handful of member states invoke it in any given year, usually over a specific security event with a defined endpoint. Two founding members using it against each other, in a tit-for-tat sequence with no shared border and no realistic security rationale, is a different kind of event. It signals that when domestic political pressure over migration reaches a certain pitch, the treaty's guardrails bend easily, and neighbours reach for border controls as a message to voters rather than a tool against an actual threat.

The European Commission has held emergency talks on the Ceuta crisis but has not moved to formally challenge either country's use of the Schengen exception, leaving the dispute to run its course bilaterally. Spain's checks on Italy are due to lapse on September 7; Italy's are set to run at least to August 15, with Rome's willingness to extend them an open question. Whichever government backs down first will effectively concede the argument over who was in the wrong — which is exactly why neither has shown any early sign of doing so. The Commission's next scheduled migration ministers' meeting will be the first real test of whether Brussels intends to draw a line under the precedent, or let it stand as the new normal for how EU states handle a crisis at any one member's external border.
