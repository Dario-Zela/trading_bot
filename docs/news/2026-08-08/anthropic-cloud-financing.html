Anthropic commits $10bn to week-old cloud startup in novel financing structure

In one sentence
Anthropic committed $10bn to a five-day-old cloud startup, with Google guaranteeing data centres and private credit funds owning the chips, because Anthropic itself has no credit rating.

Anthropic wants to be the company that runs the world's most capable AI model. It does not, on its own, have the balance sheet to prove it can pay for the computers that would make that true. That gap is the real story behind the $10 billion deal announced this week with Volta Infra Holdings — a cloud-capacity provider that did not exist, in any public sense, until the day the deal was announced.

Volta emerged from stealth on August 4th with $300 million in seed and Series A funding, co-led by Andreessen Horowitz and Altimeter Capital, with Nvidia and Michael Dell's family office also putting money in. That funding round valued the company at $2.4 billion. On the same day, Volta unveiled its marquee contract: six years, $10 billion, for 133 megawatts of AI computing capacity running out of a Bitdeer-operated site in Tydal, Norway, stocked with Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin chips. The customer has not been named outright, but reporting from Bloomberg and others points squarely at Anthropic.

A company that can't borrow on its own name

What makes the deal notable isn't the number — Anthropic has signed far larger commitments this year, including a roughly $200 billion web of contracts with Google and Broadcom for custom chips. It's the plumbing. According to the Financial Times, Anthropic "carries no credit rating," so it cannot walk into debt markets and borrow against its own name the way an established company would. Instead, Google guarantees the underlying data-center leases, Broadcom commits to buying and part-financing the chip hardware, and private credit funds — chiefly Apollo Global Management and Blackstone, in a related $35 billion facility struck earlier this year — buy the physical equipment outright and lease it back to Anthropic.

That earlier Apollo-Blackstone package illustrates the mechanics well: the debt is split into tranches, with the top layers priced closer to Broadcom's investment-grade credit than to Anthropic's own, unrated profile. In effect, other companies' balance sheets are being rented out so that Anthropic's compute bill gets paid.

Why a five-day-old startup got $10 billion

Volta's pitch is that it serves what its founders — both former Brookfield Asset Management infrastructure executives — call "Little Tech": AI labs too large for a credit card and too small to cut their own multiyear deal with Microsoft or Amazon. Anthropic, despite a $65 billion Series H raise this year that valued it at $965 billion, evidently still counts as needing that kind of intermediary. It has now assembled roughly a dozen compute suppliers, including Amazon (up to $25 billion), SpaceX, AMD and Akamai, alongside separate talks with Meta.

What it signals

The pattern across these deals is consistent: Anthropic supplies demand and a name investors want exposure to; other, better-rated balance sheets — Google's, Broadcom's, a consortium of private credit funds — supply the actual creditworthiness. That works as long as Anthropic's revenue growth outruns its compute bill. It becomes a liability the moment growth slows, since the guarantees and leaseback structures were built precisely because markets won't take that bet on Anthropic alone.

The next marker to watch is whether Anthropic's rumored push toward a public listing brings a formal credit rating — the plainest test of whether investors believe the underlying business, rather than its guarantors, can carry this debt.
