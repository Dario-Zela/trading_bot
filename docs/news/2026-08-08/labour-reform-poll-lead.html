Labour edges ahead of Reform UK in first poll lead in over a year

Andy Burnham's official portrait as Prime Minister, taken shortly after he entered Downing Street in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street, Open Government Licence v3.0
In one sentence
A JL Partners poll put Labour ahead of Reform for the first time in over a year, but one poll isn't yet a market-moving trend.

For the first time since November 2024, more Britons say they'd vote Labour than Reform UK. It isn't a large gap — 27% to 25%, according to a JL Partners poll published August 4 — but after fifteen months in which Nigel Farage's party led almost every survey published in Britain, a two-point Labour edge counts as news.

The shift has a name in Westminster now: the "Burnham Bounce." Andy Burnham, the former mayor of Greater Manchester, became prime minister on July 20 after Keir Starmer resigned in the wake of catastrophic May local election losses — Labour shed more than 1,400 councillors, its worst result in a single contest on record. Burnham took the leadership unopposed, with 94% of Labour MPs backing his nomination, a level of support that exceeded Gordon Brown's 2007 record.

A pattern, not a one-off

What lends the JL Partners number weight is that it isn't isolated. More in Common's July 27 poll put Labour at 28%, up four points, with Reform down two to 24% — the first time that pollster had shown a Labour lead since March 2025. A third survey around the same period showed a similar pattern. Reform's slide has been just as notable as Labour's climb: Farage's personal approval rating has dropped to around -32%, roughly ten points lower than a fortnight earlier, while Burnham's stands at +19 after what allies describe as a deliberate two-week policy blitz since taking office.

Internally, the reaction has been just as pointed: Labour's own July polling of party members found 56% believe the party now has a much better chance of winning the next general election with Burnham in charge, versus Starmer.

Why markets are watching, cautiously

The irony is that when Burnham's leadership bid first gathered momentum in May, it was gilts and sterling that flinched, not Reform's polling. Investors read a Burnham premiership as a tilt toward higher spending and looser fiscal discipline, and 10-year gilt yields pushed above 5% while 30-year yields hit their highest since 1998. Traders began pricing what some strategists dubbed a "Burnham premium" into UK assets — a risk spread tied specifically to expectations of weaker borrowing discipline, distinct from the broader rate and inflation story.

That premium has since eased alongside other developments — gilt yields touched a one-month low this week as falling oil prices dampened Bank of England hike bets — but the underlying question for currency and rates desks hasn't gone away: does a sustained Labour recovery in the polls reduce political risk (a government with a clearer mandate, less likely to be blown off course by Reform-driven pressure), or does it validate the spending instincts that spooked the gilt market in the first place? A single poll doesn't resolve that. It takes a trend across pollsters, holding for weeks rather than days, before macro desks start rewriting their UK forecasts around it.

What's next

The test is whether the Burnham Bounce survives contact with governing. His policy blitz has bought him a honeymoon; the next scheduled fiscal event — and any further gilt-market reaction to it — will show whether investors read a more popular Labour government as stability or as a green light for the borrowing they priced in back in May. Until several more pollsters confirm the same Labour-over-Reform pattern, most trading desks are likely to treat this as noise worth watching rather than a signal worth acting on.
