Canada and US hold daily talks to avert new tariffs before Aug 19 deadline

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, whose government is negotiating tariff relief with Washington ahead of the August 19 deadline. — World Economic Forum / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Ottawa and Washington are negotiating daily on alcohol, dairy, auto and metals tariffs, with no deal terms disclosed ahead of the August 19 deadline for 50% US levies.

Canadian and American negotiators are meeting daily in Washington, with eleven days left before a threatened round of tariffs takes effect — and Ottawa is signalling it will bend on alcohol, dairy and autos to avoid it.

The deadline is August 19. On that date, absent a deal, the United States is set to impose 50% tariffs on a swath of Canadian goods, from wine and cheese to hockey sticks, cement and Christmas ornaments — an unusual grab-bag list built around three separate presidential proclamations issued in July. Each invoked Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act, a Depression-era provision no US administration had used before this year.

Three grievances, one deadline

The White House has tied the tariffs to three specific complaints. On alcohol: all but two Canadian provinces halted purchases of American liquor beginning in March 2025, a boycott the US says cut its alcohol exports to Canada by roughly 81%. On autos: Canada's 25% retaliatory tariff on US-made vehicles, imposed after Washington's own auto tariffs, coincided with a $5.6bn drop in American car exports north of the border over the year to March. On dairy: Canada extends the European Union duty-free cheese quotas it withholds from the US under comparable trade terms.

Ottawa disputes the framing but is negotiating on the substance. Canadian officials have told reporters — first CBC, then confirmed elsewhere — that the government is prepared to end the provincial alcohol bans, ease its retaliatory auto tariffs, and adjust some dairy quota terms, in exchange for the US dropping the new 50% levy and offering relief on the separate sectoral tariffs already in place on steel and aluminum. Dairy farm groups have pushed back publicly against any quota concessions, calling the supply-management system a red line.

Daily meetings, no numbers yet

Dominic LeBlanc, Canada's minister responsible for US trade, has been in Washington with chief negotiator Janice Charette for a run of high-level sessions; both governments have agreed to meet daily at various levels through the deadline. Prime Minister Mark Carney described the current tone as "quite firm" while calling the talks themselves "constructive," and has said Canada is prepared to "get tougher" if no deal materializes in time.

What's missing from the public record is any sense of where the numbers land. No draft tariff schedule, no percentage relief figure, no timeline for steel and aluminum has been disclosed by either side. Canada is also pushing for a joint commitment to resume broader CUSMA renewal talks this fall — a signal that whatever emerges by August 19 is likely to be an interim arrangement rather than a settled trade architecture, with the harder structural questions pushed past the deadline.

What moves markets

Of the four sectors in play, autos and metals carry the larger balance-sheet exposure for cross-border manufacturers and their suppliers; alcohol and dairy are politically charged but comparatively small in trade-flow terms. A deal that lifts the 50% threat while leaving steel and aluminum tariffs intact — which US negotiators have reportedly signaled is likely — would be a partial win Ottawa can present domestically, but it would leave the metals tariffs, and the uncertainty they create for automakers and builders on both sides of the border, unresolved into the fall CUSMA talks.

The next fixed point is August 19 itself. Absent an announced deal before then, the 50% tariffs take effect automatically under the July proclamations, and the question shifts from negotiation to how — or whether — Canada retaliates.
