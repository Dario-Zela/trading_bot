Counter-terror police reopen 2025 burglary probe tied to Widdecombe murder

Nigel Farage, whose London property is at the centre of the reopened burglary case — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Counter-terror police reopened a 2025 burglary case at a Farage-linked property and referred themselves to the IOPC after finding a missed lead tied to the Widdecombe murder probe.

Counter Terrorism Policing has reopened an attempted burglary investigation from April 2025 at a London property linked to Nigel Farage, and referred itself to the police watchdog over how the original case was handled — the latest twist in the month-long inquiry into the murder of Ann Widdecombe.

The reopened case, confirmed by police on August 7, centres on a break-in reported to the Metropolitan Police at a Greater London address more than a year before Widdecombe's death. At the time, officers — supported by Counter Terrorism Policing London — investigated, made no arrests, and closed the file. It sat untouched until detectives working the Widdecombe murder came across it again.

A file reopened, a line missed

Vicki Evans, senior national coordinator for Counter Terrorism Policing, said in a statement that officers had identified "a line of enquiry which was not identified and pursued by them at the time, which may have been relevant." Police have not said publicly what that line of enquiry is, nor precisely how the 2025 break-in connects to the killing — only that the connection was significant enough to justify reopening a dormant case and briefing it publicly, mid-investigation, rather than waiting for trial.

That omission has done most of the work in this story. A burglary with no arrests, closed and filed away, does not normally resurface unless something in a live murder inquiry points back at it. Counter Terrorism Policing's own self-referral to the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC) — confirming a member of CTP London staff is now under independent investigation for allegedly failing to pursue that line of enquiry in 2025 — is itself a tacit admission that the missed thread mattered.

The Widdecombe case

Ann Widdecombe, 78, a former Conservative minister who in recent years became a prominent figure in Reform UK, was found dead at her home near Haytor, on Dartmoor, on July 9, a day after she was attacked. Prosecutors say she was struck around the head with a hammer in what the Crown Prosecution Service described as a "planned and targeted" assault, carried out while she was at home for lunch.

Joshua Kerry, 28, from Rotherham, was arrested at his home on July 11 on suspicion of murder, then rearrested two days later under the Terrorism Act as new evidence emerged. He was charged with murder on July 20 and appeared at Westminster Magistrates' Court the following day; a provisional trial date has been set for June 8, 2027. Frank Ferguson, the CPS's chief prosecutor for special crime and counter-terrorism, said at the time that "police enquiries into the defendant's motives are ongoing, including a potential political or terrorist connection" — Kerry has not been charged with a terrorism offence, and the CPS has stressed a charge is not a finding of guilt. Detectives have said they are working through more than 800 lines of enquiry.

Farage himself has publicly pushed back on one narrative already: he has said he was initially told the killing looked like "a burglary that's gone wrong," a version he found hard to square with the circumstances — "a burglar," he said, "does not park his car on your drive and walk into the house." That account concerned the murder scene in Devon, not the London property now under fresh scrutiny, but it underscores how quickly a burglary framing has recurred in this case, and how sceptical Farage has been of official explanations offered to him along the way.

What's still unknown

Police have declined to spell out the nature of the London property's link to Farage, whether he or anyone connected to him was a target of the 2025 break-in, or how directly the missed line of enquiry bears on the motive investigators are now pursuing. The IOPC's independent probe into the CTP staff member's conduct will run alongside the wider murder investigation, adding a second, procedural inquiry to a case that already spans a possible terrorism motive.

The near-term markers are the IOPC's initial findings on the conduct referral, and whatever further disclosures Counter Terrorism Policing makes about the reopened burglary as its 800-plus lines of enquiry narrow ahead of Kerry's trial in June 2027.
