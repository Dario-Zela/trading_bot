Reform UK unveils GB News presenter as by-election candidate

Matt Goodwin, the former academic turned GB News presenter selected as Reform UK's by-election candidate. — Elekes Andor / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK drafted GB News presenter Matt Goodwin as a by-election candidate amid a Labour row over blocking Andy Burnham — a preview of both parties' current shape.

Reform UK's approach to candidate selection has a recurring pattern: when a seat comes open, look for a familiar face from the party's own media ecosystem. The clearest recent example is Matt Goodwin, the GB News presenter and former Kent University politics professor who was unveiled as the party's candidate for the Gorton and Denton by-election, called after Labour's Andrew Gwynne stood down citing 21 years of declining health, worsened by the WhatsApp scandal that had already cost him his ministerial job and his party whip.

Goodwin framed his candidacy as anti-establishment insurgency, telling reporters he was "not part of the establishment" or the "Westminster blob," and casting the contest as "a referendum on Keir Starmer." The reaction from his opponents was pointed. Green leader Zack Polanski called it "a straight contest between hope and hate" and accused Goodwin of a "track record of anti-Muslim bigotry"; Labour's Lucy Powell said he "represents the kind of politics that will drive a wedge between communities in Manchester." Goodwin denies the bigotry charge, though his 2025 book drew separate ridicule after critics found footnotes citing ChatGPT.

A parallel Labour rift

The Reform selection landed awkwardly for Labour, which was fighting its own battle over the same seat. Andy Burnham, then Greater Manchester's mayor, wanted to stand, but the party's National Executive Committee blocked him — a decision roughly 50 Labour MPs publicly urged the NEC to reverse. The episode became an early marker of the discontent that, by June, had pushed more than 95 Labour MPs to call for Keir Starmer's resignation, and by July had put Burnham himself in Downing Street as prime minister after he won a separate seat at the Makerfield by-election.

Gorton and Denton itself went to neither man's party: the Greens' Hannah Spencer took it with 40.7% of the vote, with Goodwin's Reform finishing second on 28.7%. But the episode is worth revisiting now less for its result than for what it previewed — a party willing to draft campaigning broadcasters directly into frontline politics, and a Labour Party whose internal management of star candidates would keep resurfacing as a liability.

The irony is that Burnham has since turned unity into his signature pitch, promising a "broad church" cabinet after taking over from Starmer. It appears to be working, for now: a JL Partners poll this week put Labour on 27% against Reform's 25%, the first Labour lead over Farage's party in more than a year. Whether Reform's celebrity-recruitment strategy — pulling candidates straight from its allied broadcaster's studios — can outlast a single news cycle, or convert media name-recognition into durable vote share, remains the open question as both parties start positioning for the next general election.
