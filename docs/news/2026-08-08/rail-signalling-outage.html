90-second power outage exposes fragility in Britain's rail signalling network

Manchester Piccadilly station, one of the stations hit by cascading delays after a power failure knocked out the nearby Rail Operating Centre — Peter Hyde / Geograph / CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
A 90-second power blip at Manchester's rail signalling hub triggered a day-long cascade of cancellations, reviving a decade-old warning about single points of failure.

It took ninety seconds for the power to fail across a stretch of east Manchester on Thursday afternoon, and considerably longer for the railway to recover from it. A supply fault in the Harpurhey area cut electricity to more than 400 postcodes at 13:41, according to SP Energy Networks, the local distribution operator. Among the properties briefly in the dark was Network Rail's Manchester Rail Operating Centre — one of twelve control hubs that between them signal most of Britain's mainline railway.

The substation itself was back up within a minute and a half. The signalling centre's own backup power was not. Engineers have not said publicly why the standby generator failed to pick up the load in time, but the practical effect was that safety systems dropped out across the routes the centre controls, and trains already in motion were required to stop.

No services could run between Manchester Piccadilly, Manchester Victoria and Bolton for the rest of the afternoon. The knock-on reached across Greater Manchester, into the Midlands and along trans-Pennine and West Coast corridors as far as London Euston and Scotland, with seven operators — Avanti West Coast, Northern, TransPennine Express, East Midlands Railway, Transport for Wales, CrossCountry and London Northwestern Railway — all reporting delays or cancellations. Some issued "do not travel" notices for the evening peak.

A known vulnerability

Chris Wright, Network Rail's North West route director, said engineers "worked through the night to restore signalling systems and recover the railway," and that most services were running again by Friday morning. But the disruption lingered: trains and crews had been displaced from their scheduled starting points during Thursday's chaos, and operators warned of continued delays into Friday afternoon as stock and staff were repositioned.

The episode revives a warning that is nearly a decade old. In 2017, Ian Prosser, then chief inspector of railways at the Office of Rail and Road, told the Transport Select Committee that not enough had been done to stop Rail Operating Centres becoming single points of failure, citing both the heavier workload piled onto signallers in centralised hubs and the lack of contingency if one centre were disabled. The rollout of ROCs was meant to cut cost and improve coordination by consolidating dozens of local signal boxes into a handful of large control rooms. Thursday's outage is a small-scale demonstration of the trade-off: a fault that would once have knocked out a single junction instead rippled across three train operating regions.

Network Rail has not said whether it will review backup power provision at its ROCs, nor has the ORR indicated it will investigate. Neither Network Rail nor the affected operators have published passenger numbers, cancellation counts or compensation costs for the incident — the kind of figures that would let the disruption be weighed against the price of hardening a control centre that, this week, went dark for ninety seconds and took the best part of a day to recover.
