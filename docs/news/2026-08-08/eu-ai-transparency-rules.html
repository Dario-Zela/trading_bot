EU AI transparency rules take effect as bloc launches AI Gigafactory tenders

Henna Virkkunen, the European Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, who called gigafactory-scale compute 'a strategic necessity for Europe.' — European Union / European Parliament, 2024 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
EU AI content-disclosure rules took effect August 2, even as Brussels opened a €30bn tender for seven AI 'Gigafactories' to close the compute gap with the US and China.

Brussels spent the first week of August doing two things that, on paper, pull in opposite directions. On August 2, new EU rules requiring companies to disclose when content is AI-generated came into force. Three days earlier, the European Commission opened a tender worth more than €30bn to build up to seven giant AI data centres on European soil. One move tightens the leash on AI. The other pours public money into growing more of it. Both are part of the same strategy.

What the transparency rules actually require

Article 50 of the EU's AI Act is the operative provision, and it now applies to any business operating in the bloc that touches generative AI, not just the large model-makers. The rule covers four situations: when someone is talking to a chatbot rather than a person, when they're looking at emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation software, when they're viewing synthetic audio, image, video or text, and specifically when that synthetic content touches a matter of public interest — a deepfake of a politician, for instance, or an AI-written news article.

The mechanics are stricter than a disclaimer in small print. Outputs from generative systems must carry a machine-readable mark — metadata embedded in the file — and, in many cases, a visible label as well. The Commission has published a standard set of icons for this. Providers whose generative systems were already on the market before August 2 get a grace period on the machine-readable marking specifically, running to December 2. Everything else applies immediately, retroactively, to systems already in use.

The penalties sit at the upper end of what EU digital regulation imposes: up to €15m or 3% of a company's global annual turnover, whichever is larger — the same tier as the bloc's toughest GDPR fines. The Commission's AI Office has also published a voluntary Code of Practice on the same subject, and firms that sign on get a more lenient enforcement posture, a familiar EU pattern of using the threat of hard law to extract voluntary compliance. Industry groups have pushed back, arguing the rules add friction at a moment when European AI adoption already trails the US and China. That complaint is the hinge for the second half of the story.

The gigafactory bet

On July 30, the EuroHPC Joint Undertaking — the body that runs Europe's supercomputing programme — opened a formal call for consortia to build and operate up to seven "AI Gigafactories" across at least seven member states. Each facility is specified to house at least 100,000 state-of-the-art AI chips, roughly four times the capacity of anything currently running in the EU.

The funding structure follows the pattern the EU has used for other big infrastructure pushes: public money as a lever for much larger private capital. Brussels and national governments are putting up to €10bn on the table; the Commission wants that to pull in at least €20bn more from private investors, cloud providers and technology firms, taking total investment past €30bn. The bloc has also signed letters of intent with chipmakers AMD, Nvidia and Qualcomm to help secure the hardware.

"Access to the raw scale of computing power within AI gigafactories is a strategic necessity for Europe as AI development accelerates," said Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, security and democracy, framing the call as "a milestone in our AI Continent ambitions." The submission deadline for consortia is November 12, with winners chosen in early 2027 and facilities expected to be operational within 18 months of selection — meaning the first gigafactory compute likely doesn't come online before late 2028.

Regulate and subsidise at once

Seventy-six potential consortia have already expressed preliminary interest, according to EuroHPC, spanning cloud providers, chipmakers, public research bodies and investors. That is a reasonable proxy for appetite, though expressions of interest are cheap; the real test comes when firms have to commit capital against a project that won't generate revenue for years.

The underlying logic, stated plainly by EU officials, is that Europe currently rents most of its AI compute from American hyperscalers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google — and wants to own more of the physical infrastructure itself, both for economic reasons and to reduce exposure to US export controls or policy shifts. That's a separate goal from the transparency rules, which are about content provenance and consumer protection, not compute ownership. But the two policies share a premise: that AI is now large enough, and load-bearing enough, that the EU can't treat it as a purely private-sector matter to be regulated lightly and left alone.

For markets, the split matters more than the philosophy. Tighter labelling and disclosure rules raise compliance costs disproportionately for smaller AI startups that lack in-house legal teams, while the gigafactory money is a multi-year tailwind for European cloud and data-centre operators, and for chipmakers with EU supply agreements already in hand. It also formalises a fragmentation that's been building for a while: American, Chinese and now European AI infrastructure increasingly running on separate rails, under separate rules.

The next marker is November 12, when the gigafactory bids close and the market gets its first real read on how much private capital is actually willing to follow the EU's €10bn in. Enforcement of the transparency rules, meanwhile, is already live — the first fines, if they come, will show whether Brussels intends to use the full €15m stick or lean on the softer Code of Practice route instead.
