CLIMATE · WILDFIRES · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-08 · 4 min read

France and Spain battle worst wildfire season on record amid fresh heatwave

Marseille naval firefighters battle the wildfire near Biscarrosse in France's Gironde region, July 2026. — Mikani / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 300,000 hectares have burned and 330,000 people been displaced in France and Spain's worst wildfire season on record, as a new 40-45°C heatwave builds.

It is starting to look less like a bad summer and more like a new normal. Wildfires have burned more than 300,000 hectares across France and Spain so far this year — Europe's worst fire season since records began — and a fresh heatwave pushing temperatures to 40–45°C is now settling over the same scorched ground. Roughly 330,000 people have been forced from their homes since July, most of them in France's Gironde region and central Spain, in what French officials are calling the country's largest civilian displacement outside wartime.

The two crises are feeding each other. Heat dries out vegetation and hardens the ground; dry ground burns faster and hotter; the smoke and disrupted farmland then complicate the response to the next heat spike. Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, touring fire-hit regions in late July, called the blazes "the most painful expression of a climate emergency." Eric Brocardi of the French firefighters' federation put it more starkly as crews battled the Gironde fire: "We are at a point of fragility."

A season without precedent

By August 6, Spain had lost 217,609 hectares to fire this year, more than five times the 41,903 hectares burned by the same date in 2025. France had lost 91,057 hectares, roughly four times last year's pace. Across the European Union as a whole — adding in Greece, Portugal, Turkey and Romania — the burned area has passed 500,000 hectares, ahead of both last year's record and the 20-year average.

The individual fires have set their own grim records. The Gironde blaze west of Bordeaux, which alone consumed more than 42,000 hectares, is France's largest since 1949; it grew large enough to generate its own lightning through pyrocumulonimbus clouds, a fire-weather phenomenon documented before in North America and Australia but never previously recorded in France. In Spain, a fire in Ávila province burned some 44,000 hectares to become the largest in the country's history, and a separate blaze near Los Gallardos in Almería killed 14 people, the deadliest single fire of the season. Across the continent, the death toll from this year's fires stands at roughly 17 civilians, six firefighters and two helicopter crew.

The climate signature

A rapid attribution study published by World Weather Attribution in early August found that human-caused warming made the hot, dry, windy conditions behind the Spanish fires roughly 20 times more likely than in a pre-industrial climate, and doubled the likelihood of the conditions that drove the French fires. The researchers point to a mechanism they call weather whiplash: an unusually wet spring produced dense vegetation growth across northwestern Iberia and southwestern France, which a subsequent record-hot, rainless summer then turned into an enormous reservoir of fuel. Their measure of fire intensity, the seven-day Daily Severity Rating, ran 42–46% higher than it would have without the warming trend. "Severe fire weather is no longer rare under today's climate," the study concludes — a finding at odds with the once-a-decade framing still common in insurance and agricultural risk models.

The bill comes due

The economic exposure is starting to show up beyond the fire lines. Restoration costs across the five hardest-hit eurozone countries — France, Spain, Portugal, Greece and Romania — already total an estimated €3.1bn, above the European Commission's usual annual average of €2.5bn for wildfire damage across the entire bloc, and that figure excludes destroyed homes, tourism losses and long-term health costs from smoke exposure. Bordeaux recorded hourly PM2.5 readings of 341 micrograms per cubic metre during the worst of the Gironde fire, roughly 20 times the World Health Organization's air-quality threshold, prompting France's first-ever release of its strategic reserve of FFP2 masks to pharmacies.

Agriculture is absorbing a parallel hit. Spain's agriculture ministry has cut its 2026/27 grain harvest forecast to 18.5m tonnes from 24m tonnes the previous season, and the European Commission has trimmed EU-wide projections for sunflower, corn, potato and soybean output. Olive groves damaged by fire and drought across Spain, Greece and Portugal are expected to push olive oil prices higher into the autumn, with wine, coffee and cocoa also flagged by analysts as vulnerable to weather-driven volatility. On the energy side, elevated river temperatures have forced EDF to curtail output at its Golfech nuclear plant and warn of further cuts along the Rhône and Garonne, while Hungary's Paks plant and Romania's Cernavoda reactor have both faced drought-driven shutdown risk on the Danube — pressure that has kept French and German day-ahead power prices elevated through the summer.

The next test arrives with the heatwave itself, expected to peak over the coming days as temperatures climb toward 45°C across inland Spain and southern France, precisely the combination of heat and dry fuel that produced July's worst fires. Beyond that, the European Commission's autumn crop report and EDF's nuclear-output guidance will show whether this season's damage is a one-off shock or the start of a structural repricing of European agricultural and power risk.
