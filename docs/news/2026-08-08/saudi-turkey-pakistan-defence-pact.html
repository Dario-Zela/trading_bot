Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan sign Makkah mutual defence pact

Masjid al-Haram in Mecca, where the trilateral defence pact was signed on 7 August 2026. — Bluemangoa2z / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Pakistan signed a NATO-style mutual defence pact in Mecca, pledging that an attack on one is an attack on all three.

The three men stood shoulder to shoulder in Mecca on Friday and put their names to a document that, on paper at least, redraws the security map of the Middle East and South Asia. Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdogan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed what is now being called the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement — a pact stating plainly that an armed attack on any one of the three countries will be treated as an attack on all three.

It is the kind of language usually associated with NATO's Article 5, and the comparison has been made instantly and often, including by the officials themselves. Coming from three Sunni Muslim-majority states with a combined population above 500 million and a combined economic output near $2 trillion, the pact formalises an alignment that has been building quietly for months, and does so in the holiest city in Islam — a choice of venue meant to signal permanence rather than a passing communiqué.

From bilateral pact to triangle

The agreement did not appear from nothing. Saudi Arabia and Pakistan signed a bilateral Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement in September 2025, a document reported to cover "all military means" and widely read — though never officially confirmed — as extending an informal nuclear umbrella over the kingdom, given Pakistan's status as the only nuclear-armed state in the Muslim world. In the months since, Pakistan has moved thousands of troops, fighter squadrons and air-defence units into Saudi territory, according to regional reporting, turning a paper commitment into a physical one.

Turkey's addition completes a deliberate division of labour. Turkey brings NATO's second-largest standing military and a defence-industrial base that has become a significant regional arms exporter in its own right. Saudi Arabia brings capital and the world's largest oil-export capacity. Pakistan brings a battle-tested army and nuclear weapons. Burcu Ozcelik, an analyst at the Royal United Services Institute, described the arrangement as creating "significant opportunities to deepen defence-industrial footprint through joint production [and] technology partnerships" among the three — a reading that treats the pact as much as an industrial alliance as a military one.

Notably, unlike the earlier Saudi-Pakistan text, the trilateral agreement's public language does not explicitly mention nuclear weapons or sharing, leaving the extent of any nuclear guarantee to Saudi Arabia deliberately unstated. Analysts including Javed Hassan, a Pakistani geopolitical commentator, have flagged this as the central unresolved question: whether the pact contains a genuine "one for all" military commitment or is, for now, a looser statement of intent dressed in NATO's vocabulary.

Signed in the shadow of a live war

The timing explains the urgency. The pact was struck five months into a regional war that began on 28 February 2026, when the United States and Israel launched large-scale strikes on Iran after Tehran refused to halt uranium enrichment — strikes that killed Iran's supreme leader and, in retaliation, brought Iranian missile and drone fire down on every member of the Gulf Cooperation Council for the first time in the bloc's history. Saudi energy and civilian infrastructure was hit directly. Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and the UAE, all hosts to US military bases, absorbed the heaviest early barrages.

A ceasefire and a US-Iran memorandum on reopening the Strait of Hormuz to prewar shipping levels have since pulled oil prices down roughly 20% from their 2026 peak, but the peace remains thin — an attack on a cargo vessel near the Omani coast in June was enough to halt an IMO evacuation plan and send prices climbing again. Against that backdrop, a formal collective-defence pact functions as an insurance policy: it raises the cost of any renewed strike on Saudi or Gulf infrastructure by making clear that Turkey's military and Pakistan's nuclear deterrent would, in some fashion, be implicated in the response.

Saudi Foreign Minister Faisal bin Farhan called it a "milestone agreement which aims to unify efforts to confront the challenges and threats" facing the region, while Pakistan's foreign ministry framed it as reflecting a "shared commitment to further strengthening... collective security and to promoting peace, security, and stability." Turkish Vice President Cevdet Yilmaz was careful to add that the pact targets no specific country — a formulation that fooled no one given the circumstances of its signing.

An open question over Iran and Israel

Tehran's public reaction has been dismissive rather than alarmed. Iranian MP Ebrahim Rezaei told local media that "a paper agreement... will not bring them security" absent broader policy change, and Iranian state commentary on the earlier bilateral pact last year described it as non-threatening. Israel, which some officials have previously cast the emerging alliance as implicitly directed against, has not issued an official response through the prime minister's office or foreign ministry, though the pact's timing — and Pakistan's nuclear status — will not have gone unnoticed in Jerusalem.

The bigger unresolved tension sits with Turkey's existing NATO membership. Ankara is bound by its Article 5 commitments to the alliance while now also carrying a parallel mutual-defence obligation to two non-NATO states, one of them nuclear-armed and outside the Non-Proliferation Treaty. How those overlapping commitments would interact in a real crisis — say, a renewed Iranian strike on Saudi oil infrastructure — has not been addressed in anything made public so far, and the full treaty text has not been released.

For oil markets, the pact reads as a stabilising signal rather than a new source of risk: a credible deterrent against isolated strikes on Gulf energy infrastructure, layered on top of the fragile Hormuz shipping arrangement already easing prices. What would test that reading is the next incident — a strike, intercepted or not, on Saudi, Turkish or Pakistani territory or assets, which would be the first real occasion to see whether "an attack on one" produces anything beyond a strongly worded statement.
