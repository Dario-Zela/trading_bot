Gilt yields slide to one-month low as falling oil eases BoE hike bets

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the MPC voted 6–3 to hold rates on July 29. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields hit a one-month low near 4.9% as tumbling oil prices undercut the case three BoE hawks made for a rate hike.

UK government bond yields slipped to their lowest level in a month this week, as a sharp drop in oil prices took the edge off fears that the Bank of England might need to raise interest rates again. The move looks technical on the surface — a few basis points here, a barrel price there — but it marks a real shift in how traders are pricing the Bank's next move, after a decision that revealed a central bank more split than it has been in months.

The 10-year gilt yield fell below 4.9% on August 4, its lowest since July 10, according to Trading Economics data, before ticking back up to around 4.95% by August 7 as crude found a partial floor. The proximate cause was oil: Brent crude dropped roughly 10% over the course of the week on growing optimism that Washington and Tehran are closing in on an arrangement to end their five-month standoff and reopen the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes. President Trump described the talks as "very good discussions."

Three votes for a hike, not a cut

That matters to gilt investors because of what happened at the Bank's July 29 meeting. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6–3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% — the fifth straight hold — but the dissent ran in the hawkish direction. Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann all voted to raise the rate a quarter point, to 4%, arguing that Middle East-driven energy volatility risked reigniting the inflation the Bank has spent two years grinding down.

Governor Andrew Bailey's own reading was more sanguine. UK inflation had fallen faster than expected, to 2.6%, and he said the disinflation process "remains on track." But he did not dismiss the hawks' case outright, warning that "the possibility of repeated resumptions of conflict, combined with lower than usual European gas stock levels and a fall in global refining output, mean that risks to energy prices lie to the upside." It was, in other words, a live argument inside the committee — three policymakers were prepared to tighten policy specifically because they did not trust oil to behave.

A week and a half later, oil has behaved rather better than the hawks feared. Every dollar Brent gives back weakens the specific inflation channel — pricier fuel and pricier gas passing through into headline CPI — that Pill, Greene and Mann cited as their reason to vote for a hike. Gilt yields, which move inversely to the odds of tighter policy, have adjusted accordingly. The Bank's own minutes noted that some policymakers indicated rate cuts could return to the agenda if the conflict were to end altogether — a marker worth watching if the Hormuz talks progress from optimism to an actual deal.

The next scheduled test is the Bank's September 17 meeting, by which point another CPI print and, potentially, more clarity on the Iran-US negotiations will have landed. Until then, gilt pricing is likely to keep tracking the oil tape as closely as it tracks the Bank itself.
