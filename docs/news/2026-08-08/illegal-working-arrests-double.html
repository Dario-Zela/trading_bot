Illegal working arrests double in two years, Home Office says

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, who says illegal workers and rogue employers have "nowhere to hide" as enforcement activity hits record levels. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Home Office data show illegal working arrests have more than doubled in two years, but the release omits the labour-market and business-cost side of the crackdown.

Arrests for illegal working in the United Kingdom have more than doubled over the past two years, according to Home Office figures released this week, as the government leans harder on workplace raids as a lever of immigration policy.

More than 17,000 people have been arrested for illegal working since July 2024 — a 122% rise compared with the equivalent two-year period before Labour took office — while enforcement visits to employers climbed 111% to almost 25,000. In the first six months of 2026 alone, Immigration Enforcement carried out 7,270 visits, up 31% year-on-year, resulting in 4,756 arrests, a 20% increase. London, the West Midlands and South West England recorded the sharpest rises, with arrests up 22% and 44% respectively in the latter two regions.

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood framed the numbers as evidence of intent rather than accident. "Illegal workers and rogue employers have nowhere to hide," she said, pointing to plans to double the Immigration Enforcement budget and expand staff numbers by 60% by 2029. Sectors singled out for targeted visits — nail bars, car washes, food delivery platforms, construction and warehousing — have seen enforcement activity in some categories rise by close to half in the past six months.

A shift from the border to the workplace

The emphasis marks a tactical pivot. Under the previous government, illegal migration policy was dominated by Channel crossings and the now-abandoned Rwanda removal scheme. Since 2024, ministers have redirected resources toward the demand side of the equation: the businesses that hire undocumented workers, rather than only the routes migrants use to arrive. Civil penalties for employers have risen sharply too — up to £45,000 per illegal worker for a first offence and £60,000 for repeat breaches — and more than 3,100 sponsor licences were revoked in 2025, the most since records began in 2012.

That combination of higher fines, more visits and tighter monitoring — the Home Office now cross-references HMRC, PAYE and Companies House data to flag suspect employers without needing to send officers to a site at all — suggests the doubling in arrests reflects deliberate capacity-building as much as any single policy announcement. It is not simply that more illegal workers exist; it is that the state built more machinery to find them.

What the figures leave out

What the Home Office release does not address is the effect on the businesses and workers caught in the wider net. Employment status disputes are common in sectors like delivery and courier work, where riders are often classified as self-employed contractors rather than direct employees, complicating right-to-work checks. Deliveroo said this week it already runs right-to-work checks on all riders and uses fraud-detection technology, a sign that platform companies are treating enforcement risk as a compliance cost rather than a marginal issue.

Nor do the figures separate the effect of stricter sponsor licence rules — including a requirement, from April, that sponsored skilled workers be paid at or above the minimum salary threshold in every pay period rather than as an annual average — from the effect of workplace raids proper. Employer groups have not yet published a consolidated estimate of compliance costs, and the Home Office statistics offer arrest and visit counts without any parallel data on vacancy rates, sponsor licence appeals, or sectors reporting staffing shortfalls as a result.

The politics have moved quickly around the numbers. Reform UK's Zia Yusuf drew ridicule this week for proposing a "Deliveroo law" against illegal working that largely duplicates penalties already on the books, carrying prison terms of up to five years. Labour's chair, Bridget Phillipson, cast the episode as a deflection from Reform's own troubles, while pointing to the enforcement statistics as evidence the government is already delivering.

The next Home Office transparency release, due in the autumn, will show whether the first-half momentum — a 20% arrest increase against a 31% rise in visits — continues to hold, or whether the easiest targets have already been found. A fuller picture would also need labour-market data the Home Office does not currently publish alongside its enforcement figures: whether sectors facing the heaviest crackdowns are reporting worker shortages, and how many of the revoked sponsor licences involved genuine fraud versus paperwork breaches by otherwise compliant employers.
