Meta ordered to pay $567m in largest-ever child-safety ruling

The entrance sign at Meta's headquarters complex in Menlo Park, California. — Nokia621 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A New Mexico judge branded Meta a 'public nuisance,' ordering $567m more — $942m total — and new safety limits for under-18 users.

A state judge in Santa Fe has done something no court had done before: put a dollar figure on the idea that a social media platform's design, not just its failure to police bad content, can constitute a public nuisance.

On August 6, Judge Bryan Biedscheid ordered Meta to pay $567 million into a fund for treating and preventing harm to children from its platforms. That's on top of $375 million in civil penalties a jury awarded in March, bringing what Meta owes New Mexico to $942 million — the largest total penalty ever levied against a social media company in a single case.

From decoy accounts to a landmark verdict

The case began in 2023, when New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez sued Meta and Mark Zuckerberg directly, accusing the company of turning Facebook and Instagram into what his office called a "breeding ground" for predators. The evidence wasn't abstract. Investigators built decoy accounts posing as children as young as 12 and documented, in Torrez's telling, a platform that proactively pushed sexually explicit content to minors and let adults contact them with little friction. A related sting, Operation MetaPhile, led to the arrest of men who allegedly believed they were meeting children after grooming them through the platform.

In March, a jury sided with the state, finding Meta had violated New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act by deceiving teenagers and parents about the risks its products posed. That produced the $375 million penalty. The August ruling was the remedy phase — the part where the judge decides what actually has to change.

Why "public nuisance" is the word that matters

Most tech litigation runs on a narrower theory: did the company break a specific rule, fail a specific disclosure, violate a specific statute? Public nuisance is a different animal. It's the doctrine states used against tobacco companies in the 1990s and opioid manufacturers more recently — not "you broke the law" but "the way your product operates is itself the harm, at a scale that affects the public." Biedscheid's ruling found that Meta's platforms are, in his words, "a cause of and have substantially contributed to a public nuisance in New Mexico," citing features built to "optimize engagement" that the court found harmful to teenagers, paired with inadequate warning to users about the risk.

That framing is what makes this ruling more consequential than its dollar figure suggests. A compliance fine is a cost Meta can model, budget for, and absorb. A nuisance finding aimed at the underlying architecture — the feed, the notifications, the metrics — is not something a legal team can just write a check to settle. It invites a court to rewrite how the product works.

Which is exactly what happened next. Biedscheid's order, in effect for five years, requires Meta to cap teen usage at roughly 90 hours a month, pause push notifications to minors between 10 p.m. and 7 a.m., hide Like counts from under-18 users unless a parent approves otherwise, tighten controls on how adults can contact minors, add safeguards to AI chatbots, and improve how the company reviews reports of child sexual abuse material. None of that is a fine. It's a specification document for a different product.

Meta says it will appeal. Spokesperson Andy Stone posted that the company "disagree[s] with the ruling" and remains "confident in our record" on teen safety, noting it has been "transparent about the challenges of identifying and removing bad actors." Investors, notably, shrugged — Meta's stock showed little reaction to a ruling worth nearly a billion dollars, a sign that markets still treat this as a legal cost rather than a business-model threat. That gap between market indifference and the ruling's actual reach may not last.

What comes next

New Mexico was the first state to take a major platform to trial over child safety and win, but it isn't the only one trying. More than 40 state attorneys general have active claims against Meta over youth mental health, and a consolidated multidistrict case involving roughly 1,300 school districts is proceeding separately in Oakland federal court. Legal analysts have started using the phrase other industries dread: a "Big Tobacco moment," referencing the 1998 settlement that eventually cost cigarette makers more than $200 billion.

Whether that comparison holds depends on whether other judges adopt Biedscheid's reasoning — that a recommendation algorithm and an engagement-optimized notification system can, on their own, amount to a public nuisance, independent of any single failure to remove a predator or a post. If more courts agree, social platforms may be negotiating product specifications with judges as often as they negotiate fines with regulators. The next marker will be Meta's appeal, and whether the Oakland MDL judge cites New Mexico's theory when the first bellwether trials there get underway.
