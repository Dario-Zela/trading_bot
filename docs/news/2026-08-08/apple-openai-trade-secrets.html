Apple's trade-secrets case against OpenAI widens to 11 more ex-employees

Apple Park, the company's Cupertino headquarters, where the trade-secrets dispute originates. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Apple named 11 more ex-employees and sought an emergency injunction against OpenAI in its trade-secrets suit; OpenAI calls the case "careless" and moved to dismiss it.

Apple's trade-secrets fight with OpenAI has stopped looking like a two-person personnel dispute and started looking like a full-blown corporate espionage case. In a court filing this week, Apple said its internal investigation has turned up 11 more former employees who may have helped move confidential Apple information to OpenAI or to Jony Ive's hardware startup io — on top of the two ex-employees already named as defendants. At the same time, Apple asked the court for a preliminary injunction, the legal equivalent of an emergency brake, to stop OpenAI from touching whatever it has before the case is even decided.

OpenAI's reply was blunt. The company published a blog post calling Apple's suit "careless, aggressive and oddly personal," and separately filed a motion to have the whole thing thrown out.

From two defendants to thirteen

Apple filed the original complaint on July 10 in federal court in the Northern District of California, naming OpenAI, its foundation, io Products, and two individuals: Chang Liu, a senior systems engineer who left Apple after eight years to join OpenAI in January, and Tang Yew Tan, Apple's former vice president of product design for iPhone and Apple Watch, who left in 2024 to co-found io with Ive and now serves as OpenAI's chief hardware officer. OpenAI bought io for $6.5 billion last year, folding its device ambitions into the AI lab.

Apple's core claim is that this was not garden-variety poaching but a coordinated campaign — its complaint says the alleged scheme ran "at every level, from members of its Technical Staff to its Chief Hardware Officer." One detail that has stuck: Liu allegedly messaged a colleague, Yu-Ting "Alyssa" Peng, saying "LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny," after exploiting what Apple describes as an authentication bug to reach Apple's systems from Peng's work computer.

The new filing widens the net. Apple says one of the 11 additional former employees met with Liu and Peng before Peng's own OpenAI interview and discussed proprietary information about unannounced products, and that another took screenshots of confidential documents relating to an unreleased Apple product shortly before interviewing at OpenAI. Apple also says that after it filed suit, several former staff now at OpenAI contacted the company to return Apple-issued devices they had held onto after leaving — which Apple is treating as evidence of exactly the kind of retained access it's suing over, not as a sign of a company cleaning house.

An emergency ask

A preliminary injunction is a big ask in civil litigation — it requires convincing a judge that a plaintiff will suffer irreparable harm before trial can run its course, not merely that it might eventually win. Apple's filing argues exactly that: "The harm is happening now — every day that passes without an injunction allows OpenAI to embed their knowledge of Apple's stolen information into its hardware development efforts."

Apple says it first sent OpenAI a letter in February proposing five remedies. OpenAI agreed to three — stop accessing Apple information, halt any use or disclosure of it, and preserve evidence — but the two sides couldn't agree on the other two: forensic inspection of OpenAI's devices, cloud storage, email and Slack and Teams accounts, and testimony from relevant witnesses. That impasse is what pushed Apple to ask a judge to order it. OpenAI has until August 17 to respond formally to the injunction request, with a hearing scheduled for October 1.

OpenAI pushes back

OpenAI's countersuit strategy has two parts: discredit the specific allegations, and argue Apple hasn't even cleared the legal bar for a trade-secrets claim. In its motion to dismiss, filed August 6, OpenAI's lawyers argue Apple "never properly identified what information actually counts as a protectable trade secret," and that the company "should not be permitted to use a baseless and pretextual lawsuit" against a competitor. On Tan, OpenAI says he "followed normal industry recruiting practices when interviewing Apple staff"; on Liu, that he was "simply helping a former Apple colleague find information for their own work."

OpenAI has also gone further than most defendants do in a pending case, publishing what it says are iMessage exchanges showing Apple employees repeatedly asking Liu for help locating files and technical information after his departure — an attempt to flip the narrative from Liu extracting Apple's secrets to Apple's own staff pulling him back in. The company also points to Apple's own security practices, including tolerance for personal iCloud logins on work devices, as the more likely explanation for any residual access.

The bigger stakes

Whatever a judge makes of the underlying facts, the shape of this fight matters beyond Apple and OpenAI. Big tech companies have generally handled talent raids with recruiting, retention bonuses and, occasionally, gentleman's-agreement lawsuits that settle quietly. Apple is instead running the full trade-secrets playbook — expedited discovery, forensic device inspection, a preliminary injunction, an expanding list of named individuals — against a company it is also racing in AI hardware. That's a template other firms losing engineers to well-funded AI labs will be watching closely, since a court win (or even a costly settlement) for Apple would make trade-secrets litigation a more attractive tool for slowing employee mobility across the industry, at a moment when California law otherwise bars most non-compete agreements outright.

The next concrete marker is OpenAI's August 17 response to the injunction motion, followed by the October 1 hearing where a judge will decide whether to grant Apple's emergency request before the underlying case — which could take years — is resolved.
