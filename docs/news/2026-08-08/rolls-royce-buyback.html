Rolls-Royce tracks toward £2.5bn 2026 buyback after strong H1

A Rolls-Royce Trent 1000 turbofan, the engine family that powers the Boeing 787 and anchors the civil aerospace division driving the buyback. — Jorge Láscar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Rolls-Royce has completed £1.4bn of a planned £2.5bn 2026 buyback and confirmed a 6.0p interim dividend, backed by a £7-9bn multi-year return programme.

Rolls-Royce has bought back £1.4bn of its own shares so far this year, putting the aerospace and defence group on track to complete a £2.5bn buyback by the end of 2026 — the opening instalment of a capital return programme worth £7bn-£9bn out to 2028. Alongside the buyback update, the company confirmed a 6.0p interim dividend, up a third on the equivalent payout a year ago, to be paid on 18 September to shareholders on the register on 7 August.

The numbers behind the return of cash are the more interesting story. Half-year revenue rose 26% to £11.3bn, underlying operating profit jumped 46% to £2.5bn, and the operating margin widened to 22.5% from 19.1% a year earlier. Rolls-Royce raised its full-year guidance accordingly, now expecting underlying operating profit of £4.7bn-£4.9bn, up from a prior range of £4.0bn-£4.2bn, with free cash flow guided to £3.8bn-£4.0bn. Cash on hand stood at £6.5bn at the end of June, against total liquidity of £9.0bn.

Flying hours, not just financial engineering

What separates this buyback from a simple balance-sheet exercise is the operating data underneath it. Large engine flying hours — the metric that determines how often Rolls-Royce's civil aerospace division gets paid to service its engines — grew 4% year-on-year in the first half, and the company expects to land toward the lower end of a range equivalent to 115%-120% of 2019 levels for the full year. Original equipment deliveries are guided at 550-600 units. Both figures point to demand that has not just recovered from the pandemic collapse in air travel but settled durably above it, which is the basis for the argument that this cycle of buybacks reflects structural confidence rather than a one-off cash windfall. Chief executive Tufan Erginbilgić put it plainly: "Our transformation continues to deliver, and we are demonstrating that Rolls-Royce is now a very different company."

The caveats analysts are flagging

The market's reaction has been broadly favourable. Russ Mould, investment director at AJ Bell, said the results showed "the narrative has shifted to growth," with the company no longer merely stabilising after the near-collapse of 2020-21. But the caution worth noting sits in the valuation math: Simply Wall St flags that Rolls-Royce shares now trade at a level some analysts consider rich relative to a consensus forecast of declining earnings over the next three years, and the dividend yield, at roughly 0.7%, remains well below the 1.4% sector average even after this increase — the payout is still dwarfed by the buyback as the preferred route for returning cash. Combined, the dividend and 2026 buyback represent close to 90% of this year's projected free cash flow, a level that leaves little room for the flying-hours recovery to stall.

The next checkpoint is the September dividend payment date, followed by third-quarter trading commentary that should show whether flying hours are holding near the top or bottom of the guided range — the single number most likely to validate or unwind the durable-recovery thesis underpinning the buyback.
