Warsh kills the easing bias in hawkish Fed debut; October rate hike fully priced in

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh at his first FOMC press conference, June 18, 2026 — Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System
In one sentence
Warsh held rates at 3.50%-3.75%, stripped the easing bias, and flipped the dot plot from a cut to a hike — markets fully priced an October rate increase by session's close.

Kevin Warsh chaired his first Federal Reserve policy meeting on Wednesday and left little ambiguity about the institution he intends to run. The Federal Open Market Committee held its benchmark rate steady at 3.50%–3.75% — a unanimous 12-to-zero vote, itself a notable contrast to the fractured 8-to-4 split that accompanied the last decision carrying an easing bias — but the surrounding architecture of the decision made plain that the next move is more likely up than down.

Markets did not need much time to work through the implications. Two-year Treasury yields jumped 14 basis points to 4.19%, their highest in more than a year. The S&P 500 fell 1.2% to 7,440, the Russell 2000 lost 2%, and gold dropped 3.4% to around $4,229 an ounce — a rapid reversal of the haven bids that had accumulated on expectations of easier monetary policy. By the close, fed funds futures had fully priced a rate increase by October.

A Dot Plot That Points the Other Way

The clearest statement came from the Summary of Economic Projections. The median forecast for the federal funds rate at year-end 2026 moved to 3.8% from 3.4% in the March projections — a quarter-point above the current rate rather than below it. Nine of 18 participating officials projected at least one hike this year; six of those expected two or more. Wall Street had entered the session pricing a coin-flip on a cut by mid-year. That trade is now inverted.

Inflation forecasts were revised substantially higher. Personal consumption expenditure inflation for 2026 was lifted to 3.6% from 2.7%. Core PCE moved to 3.3% from 2.7%. These are not rounding errors; they represent a formal acknowledgment that the disinflationary progress assumed in earlier projections has stalled, and that the committee no longer expects it to resume on its own.

Warsh declined to add his own dot. He had long criticized the dot plot as a tool that constrains rather than clarifies policy, and he carried that skepticism directly into the round: "Submitting a dot for myself would not be helpful in the conduct of policy," he said. He offered a colloquial summary of how much conviction attaches to anyone else's submission: "Pencils with big erasers. Policymakers don't feel bound by their dots." The plot survives through year-end under a prior commitment, Warsh confirmed, but a broader communications review — including the SEP's structure, press conference cadence, and the dot format itself — is already underway and expected to conclude by December.

Scrapping the Roadmap

Warsh's signature departure from recent practice was the elimination of forward guidance. The decision, he said, "was not well-suited to the current policy conjuncture." Where the Powell-era Fed anchored markets to explicit rate-path language — often at the cost of credibility when conditions shifted — Warsh made clear that decisions would be made meeting by meeting on what the committee sees at the time. "We've dropped forward guidance," he said flatly. "I cannot give any forward guidance about what we will do next."

The commitment he did make was harder-edged. "This committee will deliver price stability," Warsh said at his post-decision press conference. "The commitment to deliver is strong, unanimous, and unambiguous." He was direct about the record the new leadership inherits: "We've missed on inflation for five years and we're going to fix that." He characterized inflation as fundamentally a policy choice — "Inflation is a choice. You bet it is" — framing it as a problem with a solution, not a structural condition to be managed around.

On the degree of restriction already embedded in current policy, Warsh offered a pointed nuance: the Fed's rate level bites mainly in housing and not broadly across financial markets. "It is hard to say policy is restrictive anywhere but in housing," he said — a candid concession that the 3.50%–3.75% range has not yet cooled the wider economy. He also rejected the idea of a forced trade-off: "I don't believe we have a cruel choice between full employment and stable prices." All 19 members at the table felt no need to tighten at Wednesday's meeting, he noted — though the dots reveal many expect tightening soon.

Warsh disclosed that he meets weekly with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, having spoken with him three times since taking office, calling the coordination "very useful." He acknowledged Middle East tensions as a factor that affects the Fed's "day job," even if geopolitical developments fall outside its mandate. The committee is focused, he said, on preventing commodity-specific price increases — oil, eggs, beef — from broadening into the wider economy rather than using them as grounds for a blanket monetary response.

Four Task Forces and a Year-End Deadline

Alongside the rate decision, Warsh announced four internal task forces designed to modernize how the Fed gathers and interprets the data it acts on. A Balance Sheet task force will review the ample-reserves operating regime. A Data task force will consider new sources and methodologies; Warsh expressed openness to private-sector data and acknowledged that most central bank data still relies on "old-fashioned surveys." A Productivity and Jobs task force will survey the reach of AI and other general-purpose technologies — the committee discussed AI at length on Wednesday, Warsh said, describing it as "filled with opportunity and risks" and noting more certainty about demand-side AI investment than about supply-side capacity. An Inflation Frameworks task force will examine the drivers of inflation; the 2% target itself is explicitly outside scope.

All four begin work in coming weeks, with results expected by fall and final conclusions by year-end. Warsh committed to reporting back, and said he "would not be surprised" if a new communications framework and changes to the SEP follow. He framed it in terms of credibility: "Our credibility requires delivering on promises in all areas."

The internal divisions on rates are real, and Warsh was candid about them. His read on the 19 participants: roughly half felt rates should be lower and half felt they should be higher. That even split, set against a dot plot that now leans toward a hike, suggests the path forward hinges on incoming inflation data rather than on built-in consensus. There was one policy proposal on the table at Wednesday's meeting, he said — discussed briefly, reached after what he called "a good family fight for a couple of days" — and the task forces will make recommendations, not decisions. "We are not outsourcing decisions."

The October Clock

For fixed-income traders, the session compressed months of rate repricing into a single afternoon. The two-year yield at 4.19% is pricing a world in which the Fed moves at least once more before year-end. The gap between two-year and ten-year yields narrowed to within 30 basis points — the flattest the curve has been in over a year — as the front end bore the brunt of the shift. The dollar index climbed 0.8% past 100, and the Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index rose 0.7% against major peers.

"Half the committee is expecting rate hikes this year," said JPMorgan Asset Management's chief investment officer. "That's a real shot across the bow at the market."

Warsh signaled he would not be moved by the reaction: "I won't be particularly intrigued by how markets react in the first minutes, days." That stance, whatever its intent, amounts to a withdrawal of the implicit Fed stabilizer that equity investors have leaned on for years. The so-called Fed put — the market expectation that sufficiently large equity losses would prompt a dovish pivot — is absent from the new playbook. "We've given markets a new chapter for the central bank," Warsh said. "This is a lot of change for financial markets to digest."

Six weeks separate this meeting from the next FOMC gathering. Between now and then, two CPI prints, a payrolls report, and ongoing developments in Middle East energy markets will either validate or complicate the hawkish tilt. The task force results arrive in fall; the potential SEP overhaul in December. Warsh's own communications innovations — new formats, ad hoc press conferences, whatever he described as "some new and interesting things" — remain unspecified but promised. The clearest signal he gave was that the Fed would let the data speak, and would follow financial market prices as its most important source of guidance. How much more change is coming will depend on what both tell him by July.
