Lenovo raises $2 billion from seven-year convertible bond offering

Lenovo's Beijing R&D campus, where the company is headquartered alongside its global operations. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lenovo sold $2B in zero-coupon seven-year converts to retire in-the-money 2029 debt at 293% of par and fund buybacks — its second $2B raise in two years.

Lenovo Group raised $2 billion through a sale of seven-year zero-coupon convertible bonds this week, the Beijing-based computer and AI server maker's second capital raise of identical size in as many years — and a sign that the company has found a reliable valve in the convertible debt market even as its equity tells a different story.

The bonds, due 2033 and marketed to investors at a conversion premium of 40 to 50 percent above Lenovo's Wednesday close, priced at the tight end: 47.5 percent above the prior closing price, implying a conversion price of HK$36.70 per share. They carry no coupon — meaning Lenovo pays nothing in interest over the seven-year term — and include an investor put option after four years. The bonds are intended to list in Vienna; any shares issued on conversion would list in Hong Kong. JPMorgan, HSBC, BNP Paribas, and DBS Group arranged the deal, which drew orders from more than 160 investors, with allocations heavily weighted toward the top ten.

Retiring the old debt first

The use of proceeds sharpens the picture. A portion of the new $2 billion will go toward repurchasing up to $675 million of Lenovo's outstanding 2029 convertible bonds — debt the company originally issued at a 2.50 percent coupon. Those bonds are being bought back at 293.2 percent of par, a premium that reflects how deeply in the money they have become: Lenovo's Hong Kong-listed shares have surged roughly 165 percent this year, hitting a 26-year high after full-year revenue rose 20 percent to $83.1 billion and adjusted net profit climbed 42 percent to $2 billion. The company has already secured agreements from holders of about $225 million in principal; approximately $450 million remains outstanding.

Rather than let those bonds convert — which would flood the market with new shares — Lenovo is paying cash to retire them, funded by what amounts to free money: a zero-coupon instrument that costs nothing until 2033. The remainder of the proceeds will go toward on-market share repurchases, subject to shareholder approval at the upcoming annual meeting, a precaution designed to let the company buy back stock without triggering a mandatory general offer under Hong Kong takeover rules.

The prior $2 billion raise, completed in January 2025, went to Alat, a subsidiary of Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund. That deal was structured as a three-year zero-coupon convertible and came packaged with a commitment to build a regional headquarters and manufacturing facility in Riyadh. The current transaction has no such bilateral arrangement — it was placed into the broader institutional market and met with multiple times oversubscription.

Lenovo enters this financing with a $21 billion AI server order pipeline and a stated ambition to reach $100 billion in annual revenue within two years. Investors who received allocations are now effectively betting that the stock will be worth at least 47.5 percent more than today's close by 2033 — a threshold that looks less exotic given the pace of the past twelve months.

The next marker to watch is the annual general meeting, where shareholders will be asked to authorize the buyback mandate. How much of the 2029 bond stock Lenovo ultimately retires — and at what total cost — will be disclosed in the next results announcement.
