Trump pledges Intel support and tariff shield for domestic chip manufacturing

Intel's Fab 12, Fab 22, and Fab 32 facilities in Chandler, Arizona — among the domestic manufacturing sites at the center of Trump's semiconductor push. — neepster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Trump's Truth Social post touts an Apple-Intel chip deal and tariff protection for US fabs, but names no rates, timeline, or support mechanism traders can price.

On Thursday, President Trump posted on Truth Social that Apple has agreed to work with Intel to design and manufacture chips in the United States — an announcement wrapped in the by-now-familiar charge that Taiwan and others "stole our Semiconductor Factories" while past administrations looked away. "The Technology the World relies on was invented in America," the post read. "We all remember 'Intel Inside.'" Tariffs, Trump said, were the mechanism to fix it.

No tariff rate, no timeline, and no support mechanism for Intel were specified.

What the policy record actually shows

The tariff architecture is real, if incomplete. In January, the administration issued a Section 232 proclamation putting a 25% levy on specified advanced chips — Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X among them — while carving out imports destined for domestic manufacturing buildout. The exemption was deliberate: the policy is meant to pressure foreign foundries to build on US soil, not to penalise the TSMC fab expansions in Arizona that the same administration championed.

A broader 100% tariff on foreign-made chips has been floated since August 2025. It remains unscheduled. Commerce is running a Section 232 investigation into the broader semiconductor trade picture; a formal finding and accompanying rate schedule have not been published.

On the Intel side, the government already holds roughly a 10% equity stake in the company and has committed approximately $10 billion to domestic factory development. Intel has entered risk production on its 18A process node — the technology it is counting on to regain parity with TSMC and Samsung, and the foundation any Apple manufacturing deal would require.

The Apple-Intel partnership itself is not new news. The Wall Street Journal reported a preliminary deal in May, following more than a year of discussions. What Thursday's post adds is a presidential endorsement on Truth Social — not a signed contract, not a tariff proclamation, not a CHIPS Act disbursement.

Market reaction

Intel shares opened Thursday at $125.05, climbed to an intraday high of $128.68 — roughly 3.5% above Wednesday's close of $124.34. A real move, but well below the May 11 all-time high of $132.75, which was driven by the 18A risk-production confirmation rather than any executive post.

For traders trying to price the tariff component specifically, the post offers little purchase. The 25% advanced-chip rate is enacted; the 100% threat is still floating. Without a formal proclamation that maps rates to specific Harmonized Tariff Schedule codes, the supply-chain repricing that would move the broader semiconductor sector stays theoretical.

The actionable catalyst to watch: a Commerce Department Section 232 report on broader chip imports, which trade lawyers and industry groups have been tracking. Until that document lands with concrete rates attached, the gap between White House rhetoric and implementable policy remains open.
