RAF pauses fighter jet training after two pilots eject before crash

A BAE Systems Hawk T2 of the Royal Air Force, the type flown from RAF Valley that crashed near Anglesey on 23 September 2026. — MilborneOne / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Two RAF pilots ejected safely before their Hawk T2 crashed near Anglesey, prompting a fleet-wide pause of the jet used to train Typhoon and F-35 pilots.

Two Royal Air Force pilots ejected from their Hawk T2 training jet moments after take-off from RAF Valley on Wednesday afternoon, parachuting to safety as the aircraft crashed and burst into flames in a field near Pencarnisiog on Anglesey, narrowly clearing a row of houses. Both pilots suffered only minor injuries. The RAF has since paused all Hawk T1 and T2 flying operations while it investigates — a decision that grounds a significant slice of Britain's fast-jet training pipeline.

Footage filmed by residents shows the jet "porpoising" — pitching nose-up and nose-down at low speed and a steep angle of attack — shortly after departure, with flames visible before the crew punched out. Mikey Kay, a former RAF pilot who now works as a security analyst, described the ejection as "quite remarkable," noting that modern Martin-Baker seats are designed to work at zero speed and zero height and "were one of the big lifesavers in this whole story." The RAF's statement was terse: "An incident involving an RAF T2 Hawk aircraft, flying out of RAF Valley, occurred near Anglesey. An investigation is under way and we will not comment further at this time."

Why the Hawk matters

The Hawk T2 is not a peripheral training type — it is the last step before RAF and Royal Navy pilots convert onto the Eurofighter Typhoon or the F-35 Lightning II. Flown by IV and XXV (Fighter) Squadrons as part of No. 4 Flying Training School at RAF Valley, the jet was introduced in 2009 with a digital cockpit deliberately built to mirror the Typhoon's, so that students arrive at their operational conversion units needing a shorter, cheaper course. Pausing the fleet does not just cancel a few sorties; it stalls the one aircraft the RAF uses to certify pilots as ready for frontline jets.

That makes the "precautionary" pause, announced alongside the investigation, a bigger operational headache than the language suggests. The RAF has not said how long it expects the suspension to last, and with a limited pool of instructor aircraft and airframes already old — the Hawk fleet is due to be retired by 2040 but has flown since the mid-2000s — any lengthy grounding risks backing up the training pipeline for months.

Not the fleet's first stumble

Wednesday's incident is not an isolated blemish on the Hawk's safety record. The fleet had engines pulled in 2022 over concerns about in-flight failures, and was briefly grounded again in January 2023 after a separate engine-related incident. In 2024, the RAF's own Air Chief Marshal signalled plans to eventually replace the T2, citing exactly these engine reliability and support concerns. None of that proves Wednesday's crash shares a cause with the earlier episodes — investigators have given no indication yet of what went wrong — but it means the RAF is pausing a training type with a recent history of unscheduled stand-downs, not a spotless one.

What happens next will hinge on how quickly investigators can identify a cause and whether it points to something fleet-wide — an engine or systems fault — or an isolated failure specific to this airframe or sortie. The RAF has given no timeline for resuming flights; a swift, narrow finding would let training resume within weeks, while anything that implicates the Hawk's ageing engines would revive the debate the Air Chief Marshal opened in 2024 about how much longer the type can safely bridge Britain's pilots to the Typhoon and F-35.
