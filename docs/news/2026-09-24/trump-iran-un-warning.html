Trump warns UN he could 'annihilate' Iran without a deal; Tehran defiant

President Trump addressing the United Nations General Assembly, where he has now threatened Iran three Septembers running. — Daniel Torok / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Trump told the UN he'd deal with Iran after the midterms or 'annihilate' it; Tehran's defiant reply and inconclusive UN talks leave oil's war premium intact.

The choice, President Trump told the assembled diplomats of the world on Tuesday, was his alone to make. Either Iran signs a deal that lets the Islamic Republic "rebuild," or the United States finishes what it started in February. "Do I annihilate the Islamic Republic, and do it quickly," he asked the hall, "or do I drive them into hell with no chance of survival and no hope of future greatness or generations?" He said he expected the former — but not before Americans vote in November.

That framing, delivered from the same podium where Trump has now addressed the UN General Assembly for the third consecutive September, is the clearest signal yet that Washington's Iran policy runs on an electoral clock as much as a diplomatic one. "I believe we'll make a deal, right after the election," he said, adding that Tehran was "waiting to see how I do on the midterm election." For an energy market that has spent seven months pricing war risk into every barrel, a president openly tying the end of a shooting conflict to a vote count is not reassurance — it is a new kind of uncertainty.

A war that didn't end when the ceasefire said it did

Context matters here, and it is easy to lose amid the rhetoric: this is not a hypothetical confrontation. American and Israeli strikes on February 28 killed Iran's supreme leader, Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, in a bombardment Israeli officials said hit roughly 500 sites across the country. A ceasefire in April gave way to a June memorandum of understanding that Washington and Tehran signed to wind the conflict down — and that collapsed within weeks, formally expiring in August. Since then the two sides have traded intermittent strikes; the Congressional Budget Office estimated in September that U.S. military operations tied to the war had cost $38 billion through August 1. Iran, for its part, has used control over shipping through the Strait of Hormuz — the channel carrying roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil — as leverage, and insurers have at various points pulled war-risk coverage for tankers transiting it.

So when Trump raises the prospect of annihilation, he is describing an escalation of an active war, not the opening of a new one. That is precisely why traders treated Tuesday's speech as market-moving rather than as theater. Brent crude, which had fallen for five straight sessions as diplomatic signals softened, snapped back 3.9% to $103.08 a barrel; U.S. crude gained 1.8% to settle at $92.16. JPMorgan has pegged oil's underlying "fair value" closer to $90, meaning something like $10-13 a barrel is pure geopolitical premium — a premium that inflates or deflates with each UN speech, each Hormuz incident, each hint of a Witkoff meeting.

Talks, but no breakthrough

The speech was not the only Iran news out of New York this week. Hours after Trump's remarks, his envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner sat down with Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi for roughly three hours of Qatari-mediated, indirect talks — the first direct engagement between the two governments since the June ceasefire fell apart. Trump called the session "very productive" and said another meeting was planned "in the very near future." No breakthrough was announced. Iran's conditions, conveyed through the talks, were unchanged from before the summer: an end to fighting on every front, the lifting of the U.S. naval blockade, and relief from sanctions, in exchange for fully reopening Hormuz to normal commercial traffic.

That a channel exists at all — even one running through intermediaries rather than a direct line — is more than existed a month ago. But it sits awkwardly next to the maximalist language used at the podium the same week, and markets are left triangulating between the two signals rather than reading one clean narrative.

Tehran holds its ground

Iran's answer came the next day, and it was not conciliatory. President Masoud Pezeshkian, addressing the Assembly in what UN correspondents described as a rare wartime speech from an Iranian head of state, opened by pushing back on Trump directly: "The United States president described us as terrorists. We have been the victims of terrorism." He held up a portrait of Khamenei, killed seven months earlier, and photographs he said showed children killed in a U.S. strike on an Iranian school. "It must be understood by Mr. Trump and those who seek to bully us," he said, "that we are ready for dialogue and diplomacy and negotiations without accepting the language of force."

Pezeshkian also turned the nuclear argument back on Washington and Israel — "the nuclear weapon is in Israel, but the inspectors are in Iran," he said, and "Israel kills, but Iran is subject to sanctions" — while denying Iran seeks a weapon of its own and refusing to accept limits on what he called its civilian nuclear program. The U.S. delegation walked out during the speech, a now-familiar diplomatic gesture that underscored how far apart the two governments remain even as their negotiators were meeting a floor below.

What the calendar actually says

Strip away the podium theatrics and the dispute reduces to a genuinely simple structure: Washington has set an implicit deadline — after the U.S. midterm elections on November 3 — for either a deal or an escalation, while Tehran has offered a conditional negotiating path that neither side has yet walked down. The mechanics that would need to resolve first are concrete — the naval blockade, the sanctions regime, the fate of Hormuz shipping — and none of them have moved this week, talks notwithstanding.

For markets, the takeaway is less about the annihilation threat, which is difficult to price as a discrete probability, and more about the November 3 marker Trump himself supplied. Between now and then, the likeliest path is more of what has defined the past two weeks: intermittent strikes, a wobbling risk premium, and diplomatic contacts that produce statements of good faith but no signed agreement. The next test comes quickly — Witkoff and Kushner's follow-up session with Araghchi's team, which Trump suggested could happen within days, will show whether Tuesday's three hours were a genuine opening or another data point in a war that has already outlasted two ceasefires.
