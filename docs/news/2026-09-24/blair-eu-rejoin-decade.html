Blair says Britain should work towards rejoining the EU within a decade

Tony Blair, whose institute has urged the government to set a course back towards EU membership. — World Affairs Council / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Tony Blair's institute has urged PM Andy Burnham to set a public goal of rejoining the EU within a decade — Burnham says it's a question for another day.

Tony Blair has never much liked staying out of a debate he once shaped, and Brexit is the one that made him. Ten years on from the referendum he lost, the former prime minister has used his eponymous institute to tell his party's latest leader exactly what to do about it: set a public target to rejoin the European Union within a decade.

The vehicle is a paper from the Tony Blair Institute, titled Beyond Leave and Remain: A Path to Rejoining a Reformed Europe, published this week. In his foreword, Blair writes that Britain "should signal an intention if the terms are right to enter into a structured formal relationship with Europe and initiate a process of negotiation where everything is on the table but nothing agreed until everything is agreed." It is careful diplomatic language for a blunt proposition: scrap the red lines, and put rejoining back on the table.

The pitch to Burnham

The timing is deliberate. Andy Burnham, who became prime minister in July after Keir Starmer's resignation, is due to publish a ten-year governing plan in November, and Blair wants EU membership written into it. The institute's paper goes further than rhetoric, urging Burnham to drop the government's existing commitments not to rejoin, not to accept free movement of people, and not to enter the single market or a customs union — replacing them with a single, looser test: sign up to whatever EU rules or initiatives prove of economic or strategic benefit to the UK.

That would mean accepting free movement and a financial contribution as the price of entry, terms the paper treats as unavoidable rather than negotiable. It frames the prize as bigger than trade friction: a reformed EU, it argues, could become a geopolitical counterweight to the United States and China, with Britain inside rather than outside it.

A door Burnham hasn't shut — or opened

Burnham has so far declined to rule out an EU membership pledge in Labour's next manifesto, but he has been equally careful not to embrace one. Asked this week whether rejoining could feature, he said it was a question "for another day." That non-answer is itself the story: a prime minister seven months into the job, with a fragile economic backdrop and Reform UK still eating into Labour's polling, has no appetite to reopen the argument that broke his predecessors.

Blair's intervention doesn't change that political arithmetic. It is aimed less at Downing Street this week than at shifting the terms of what becomes thinkable by the time Burnham's ten-year plan is drafted — a long game from a former leader with no seat, no vote, and considerable practice at being heard anyway.

What's next

Burnham's ten-year plan lands in November; whether it contains so much as a nod to EU membership will be the first real test of how far this argument has actually moved inside government. Until then, expect the paper to circulate mainly among people who already have a strong view on Brexit — which, a decade on, is still most of the country.
