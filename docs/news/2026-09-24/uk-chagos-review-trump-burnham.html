UK to review Chagos deal after Trump calls it 'terrible' in first Burnham meeting

Diego Garcia, the Indian Ocean atoll hosting a major US military base at the centre of the UK-Mauritius sovereignty dispute — U.S. Navy photo, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump called the UK's Chagos handover to Mauritius 'terrible' at his first Burnham meeting; Britain has suspended payments and reopened the deal.

Andy Burnham had been prime minister for barely nine weeks when he sat down with Donald Trump for the first time. The two men met on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York on Tuesday, for longer than the 30 minutes originally scheduled, and by most accounts it was a warm encounter — Trump called Burnham "a natural business person" he could work with, a notably softer line than the one he took with Burnham's predecessor. Then he turned to the Chagos Islands.

"I don't support it. I think it's terrible. It was a terrible deal," Trump said, with Burnham sitting beside him. He did not name a deadline, and Burnham did not concede the point on camera. But by Wednesday morning, Defence Secretary Wes Streeting was on the BBC confirming that Britain would reopen an agreement its own government had signed and ratified little more than a year ago.

A base, an island chain, and a bill

The arrangement Trump was describing dates to May 2025, when Keir Starmer's government reached a deal to transfer sovereignty of the Chagos Archipelago to Mauritius while retaining a 99-year lease — with an option for a 40-year extension — over Diego Garcia, the atoll that hosts one of the United States' most important military installations. The base sits roughly equidistant between the east coast of Africa and Indonesia, giving Washington a logistics and surveillance hub that serves CENTCOM, AFRICOM and INDOPACOM alike; it has hosted B-2 bombers and served as a forward node for Indian Ocean operations since the 1970s. Starmer called the deal "absolutely vital" for British and American security.

It was also expensive. Britain agreed to pay Mauritius £165 million a year for the first three years of the lease, dropping to £120 million a year from year four — an annual average of £101 million in 2025-26 prices over 99 years, totalling around £3.4 billion. Chagossians would be free to resettle on the outer islands, though not Diego Garcia itself, and Mauritius would establish a UK-backed marine protected area.

Trump's relationship with the deal has been inconsistent from the start. He once called it "a big mistake" and, in January, "an act of great stupidity" — before nonetheless allowing it to proceed under the Biden-negotiated terms he inherited. Plans were quietly paused in April. What changed this week is that the pause has become an open, public reconsideration, with the president delivering his verdict directly to the new prime minister rather than through aides or statements.

Streeting: "not news to us", but no longer deniable

Streeting was careful on Wednesday not to frame the review as a surprise. "What President Trump said yesterday wasn't new," he told BBC Breakfast, "but I think it does provide absolute clarity that his position on this Chagos deal as currently drafted is not changing." Pressed on why the government was acting now, he was blunter: the deal "can't go ahead" without American support, because "the United States doesn't support it."

The practical step is a suspension, not a cancellation. Britain has stopped making its lease payments — worth at least £120 million a year — to Mauritius while the review proceeds, though officials have avoided saying whether the treaty itself will be renegotiated, replaced, or abandoned. Opposition Conservatives want it scrapped outright: shadow defence secretary James Cartlidge argued the agreement "should be binned entirely." Streeting rejected that as an "ostrich strategy" of burying the government's head in the sand, insisting London still needs a durable arrangement with Mauritius to keep the base secure — just not this one, and not without Washington's sign-off.

What Burnham's first test reveals

Burnham took office on 20 July, Labour's seventh prime minister of the last decade to face the modern British premiership's compressed timelines, and Chagos was not a policy he wrote. That may be precisely the point of Trump's timing: a first meeting is when a foreign leader's deference — or lack of it — gets calibrated for everything that follows. Trump praised Burnham personally while publicly overruling him on substance, a combination that leaves the new prime minister little room to look either weak or combative.

The episode fits a wider pattern this autumn of Trump asserting direct control over allies' strategic choices where they touch US military assets or trade terms, rather than working them through traditional diplomatic channels — visible elsewhere in his approach to trade partners and, more dramatically, in his UN warning to Iran. For the UK, the immediate costs are financial and reputational: a suspended payment stream, a treaty in limbo, and a reminder that decisions over British sovereign territory now run partly through Washington's approval rather than around it. Sterling and UK defence contractors are unlikely to move much on this alone, but it adds one more data point to a year in which US policy signals have repeatedly reshaped how London plans.

What happens next depends on a negotiation that has, by Streeting's own account, barely begun. The UK has given no timeline for a revised proposal, and Mauritius — which has previously threatened legal action over delays — has yet to respond to the payment suspension. The next marker to watch is whether London brings a redrafted Diego Garcia arrangement back to Washington before the funding freeze starts to strain relations with Port Louis as much as the original deal strained them with Washington.
