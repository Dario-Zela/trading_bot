Reform UK narrows gap on Labour in September polling

Andy Burnham, who became prime minister in August 2026 after Keir Starmer's resignation. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
UK polling has narrowed to a three-way contest — Labour 25-27%, Reform UK 23-24%, Tories near 20% — as the 'Burnham Bounce' fades but Reform's slide stalls.

Three weeks ago, Labour activists were bracing for a wipeout. Reform UK had spent the best part of a year at or near the top of the polls, riding voter anger over immigration and a Conservative Party in visible disrepair. Now, as party conference season closes out, the picture looks different: Andy Burnham's Labour is back in front, Reform has slipped from the low 30s of midsummer into the low-to-mid 20s, and the contest has narrowed into something closer to a three-way scrap.

The latest averages tell the story. PollCheck's rolling tracker puts Labour on 26.7%, Reform UK on 22.9% and the Conservatives on 20.1%, with the Greens on 10.9% and the Liberal Democrats on 10%. Britpolls' own aggregate has it tighter still — Labour 26%, Reform 22.6%, Tories 20% — while Election Maps UK's poll of polls shows the closest race of the three, Labour 25.6% to Reform's 24.3%. Take the range across trackers and the shape is consistent: Labour ahead by two to four points, Reform second, the Conservatives a close third and effectively flatlined since the last election.

From ten-point deficit to conference-season lead

The swing is steep by recent standards. At the start of 2026, Reform led Labour by around ten points nationally, and by July — with Keir Starmer's approval rating sunk to -23.8% — the two parties were essentially level. Starmer's resignation and Burnham's arrival in Downing Street in early August triggered what pollsters have taken to calling the "Burnham Bounce": Ipsos fieldwork from 30 July to 4 August showed the public preferring a Burnham-led Labour government to a Farage-led Reform one by 50% to 29% in a straight head-to-head, and Burnham's net approval has held at roughly +15%, against Starmer's final score of -24%. By late August, Ipsos had Labour four points clear of Reform heading into conference — Labour's first outright lead of the parliament.

September's numbers show that lead holding rather than growing further, and on some trackers narrowing back toward three points. That is the detail worth sitting with: this is not Reform in freefall, but a party that has stopped gaining and given back some of the ground it built through 2025, without the underlying complaint driving its support — immigration — going away. Ipsos found 48% of the public still favour strict immigration limits even where they carry an economic cost, and net migration running at 728,000 in 2025 remains the reference point Reform campaigners return to.

Why the floor holds even as the ceiling drops

Farage's own position has taken hits that would sink most opposition leaders. He is under investigation over an undeclared £5 million cryptocurrency gift from the Thailand-based businessman Christopher Harborne, and resigned his Clacton seat to force a by-election rather than fight the allegations from the backbenches — a contest in which his most prominent challenger is the satirical candidate Count Binface, after Labour and the Conservatives both declined to field candidates. Ipsos data shows the toll: only 21% of voters think Reform is ready to govern, 65% don't rate Farage as prime-ministerial material, and his personal approval, at 24%, trails Burnham's 50%.

And yet Reform's floor has proved sturdier than its recent slide in vote share might suggest. Burnham's own ratings are unremarkable by historical standards — just 29% are satisfied with his performance as prime minister, worse than Starmer's first-month score of 37% and well short of the honeymoon periods enjoyed by Tony Blair (65%), David Cameron (57%) or Theresa May (54%). Seven in ten voters remain dissatisfied with the government generally, and 58% still describe Reform as "extreme." Ipsos's own read is blunt: Labour's lead looks "less like overwhelming enthusiasm and more like the benefit of the doubt."

What's next

The Clacton by-election, expected within days, is the nearest test of whether Farage's legal troubles are actually costing Reform votes on the ground rather than just in the topline numbers. Beyond that, Reform's own conference — its first since the polling slippage began — is the moment the party has flagged as a chance to broaden its platform past immigration and answer the "readiness to govern" question that Ipsos finds hanging over it. For markets, the more direct catalyst sits elsewhere: Chancellor Rachel Reeves's November Budget, where a government defending a fragile lead against a resurgent opposition on its right will have to weigh fiscal credibility against the political cost of tightening further.
