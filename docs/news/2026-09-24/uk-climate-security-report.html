UK shared suppressed climate security report with allies at UN summit

Ed Miliband, who as UK Foreign Secretary briefed allied ministers in New York on a report his own government had shelved. — Shaun Curry / Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Miliband briefed EU ministers in New York on a JIC climate security report Downing Street suppressed last October.

Ed Miliband spent last October fending off questions about why his own government had pulled the plug, at the eleventh hour, on a Joint Intelligence Committee assessment of climate-driven security risk. This week, in a closed-door room on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York, the foreign secretary was the one presenting its findings — to ministers from the EU and beyond.

The private briefing on Wednesday night, described by attendees to the Guardian, drew on the same JIC-backed report that Downing Street shelved on the eve of its scheduled publication last October, reportedly over concern that its conclusions were too alarming. A heavily redacted version only surfaced in January, after the think tank Green Alliance forced disclosure through a Freedom of Information request — and ministers have resisted calls to release the document in full ever since.

The report assesses that the Amazon, the Congo Basin, the Himalayan biome and the coral reefs of Southeast Asia and the South Pacific could each cross critical ecological thresholds between 2030 and 2050.

What the report says

The 14-page assessment, titled Global Biodiversity Loss, Ecosystem Collapse and National Security, warns of "cascading risks" as major ecosystems buckle under climate stress — mass migration, food shortages, resource competition and, in its starkest passages, an elevated risk of conflict between nuclear-armed states. It singles out the drying of rivers fed by Himalayan glaciers and the degradation of the Congo rainforest as pressures that could push migration toward Europe, noting the UK's large South Asian diaspora would make it "an attractive destination" — with knock-on effects for domestic politics that the report describes as "more polarised and populist."

The New York briefing

Miliband's private session followed a public address at the UN General Assembly in which he urged other governments to fold climate risk into defence and intelligence planning. Alongside the briefing, the UK announced £331m for the Global Environment Facility to shore up food and water security and protect the Amazon and Congo basins, and said it would appoint a special representative for climate security and a new ministerial taskforce on the national security risks of climate and nature loss.

The contrast was not lost on those in the room, nor on MPs who had spent months pressing for the full report's release. A government that judged the analysis too sensitive for British voters was, within the same year, using it to shape the private thinking of allied foreign ministries.

Why the suppression matters now

Downing Street's rationale for holding back the report last autumn was never fully explained, but reporting at the time pointed to concern within Number 10 that its findings — particularly the reference to nuclear conflict risk — were too bleak to publish alongside the government's broader climate messaging. The episode drew criticism in the House of Lords in February over the lack of transparency, and campaigners have argued the pattern fits a wider tendency among governments to soften or delay unwelcome climate intelligence rather than confront it publicly.

That tension is now harder to avoid. A report judged unfit for public consumption has effectively been validated as sound enough to share with allied governments — raising the question of who British climate risk assessments are ultimately produced for, and who gets to see them first.

What's next

Pressure is likely to build on Downing Street to publish the assessment in full, rather than the redacted January version, particularly as Miliband's new climate security taskforce begins its work. The Foreign Office has not said when — or whether — that will happen.
