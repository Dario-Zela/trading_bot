Prison governors warned against unsafe cell doubling as overcrowding worsens

A cell fitted with two beds — the kind of doubling-up governors have been warned to handle carefully. — Bill Nicholls / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Leaked Prison Governors' Association guidance shows staff doubling up inmates in unsafe single cells as England and Wales jails hit 98.5% capacity.

England and Wales's adult male prison estate is running so close to full that staff are increasingly being told to put two people in cells built for one — including in prefabricated units designed from the outset as single occupancy. Leaked documents seen by the BBC show the Prison Governors' Association (PGA) has warned its members to be careful how they do it, or risk breaking the law.

The six-page PGA letter, described as "Operational Capacity Guidance," reminds governors of a basic legal threshold: no cell can be used for confinement unless it is certified adequate for health and allows a prisoner to communicate with officers. That reminder exists because, according to staff who spoke to the BBC, it is no longer being reliably met. "Keeping two prisoners in cells built for one isn't ideal, but the pressure is on to maintain operational capacity," one prison worker said. Another put it more starkly: "We don't have enough staff to monitor the extra prisoners. We don't have the infrastructure to house."

A system with nowhere left to put people

The numbers explain the pressure. The male adult estate is operating at roughly 98.5% capacity, with just over 1,200 spare spaces across the country and a total population near 87,000 as of early September. The Ministry of Justice's own definition of "safe and decent" capacity puts the ceiling closer to 81,600 — meaning the system has, by its own metric, already run past the point it considers acceptable.

Into that gap has come a specific and contentious fix: rapid deployment cells, steel-framed prefab units measuring 2.9 by 3.4 metres, originally built as a stopgap and fitted with a bed, toilet and shower for one occupant. Leaked internal plans show governors at ten category C prisons have been asked to approve fitting second beds into these units, adding 476 spaces to the estate at a stroke. No final decision has been confirmed publicly, but the request alone signals how thin the margin has become.

Governors and officers push back

The reaction from the people who would have to run doubled-up units has been blunt. Mark Fairhurst, national chair of the Prison Officers Association, said the numbers being proposed by Whitehall could breach health and safety law and warned of consequences: "By law our members cannot strike, but I know they would walk out on health and safety grounds if asked to oversee prisons which cannot cope with the numbers being proposed." Tom Wheatley, president of the Prison Governors Association, said any doubling up would need a full, prison-by-prison risk assessment before it could be considered safe for staff, prisoners and the public.

Andrea Coomber, chief executive of the Howard League for Penal Reform, called the plan "an act of desperation," noting that rapid deployment units had, somewhat unexpectedly, become one of the calmer parts of the estate — associated with less violence, debt and drug misuse than older wings. "The risk now is that all this will be undermined as more and more people get crammed in," she said. Former governor John Podmore was equally direct: "You can put a second bed in a cell and call it capacity but that doesn't make it sensible or safe."

The Ministry of Justice's position is narrower than the criticism aimed at it. Its statement said only that "cells are only doubled up where it is safe to do so" — a formulation that leaves the judgment of safety inside the system it is trying to relieve.

What the government is banking on

Ministers are not relying on doubling up alone. An early release scheme is due to begin in October, releasing around 4,500 offenders over ten months in an effort to buy the estate breathing room. That follows the Sentencing Act 2026, which received royal assent in January and was designed by Shabana Mahmood, Justice Secretary at the time, specifically to reduce the flow of people into custody. David Lammy, who has since taken over the justice brief, inherits a system where the legislative fix and the emergency release programme are both running against a population that keeps climbing — official projections put it at 100,000 by March 2030.

That a professional association representing the people who run prisons felt the need to leak its own internal guidance says something about how confident governors are in the chain of command above them. It is the kind of internal friction that has, in other parts of the public sector, preceded either a change in ministerial policy or a costlier reckoning — inquest, inspection report, or legal challenge — when an unsafe practice becomes an incident. The next test comes in October, when the early release scheme starts and the government finds out whether releasing 4,500 people is enough to make the doubling-up guidance moot, or merely delay the argument.
