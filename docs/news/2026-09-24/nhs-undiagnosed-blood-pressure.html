Up to 5 million people in England unaware they have high blood pressure

A blood pressure reading being taken with a manual sphygmomanometer. — YVSREDDY / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
New Oxford data show 59% of English adults with hypertension are undiagnosed, but neither the NHS nor the BHF has put a cost figure on it.

Somewhere in England today, roughly five million people are walking around with dangerously elevated blood pressure and no idea it's there. That figure — drawn from British Heart Foundation and Blood Pressure UK analysis released around this year's Know Your Numbers! Week[^1] — sits alongside a starker one published just this week: a study of 1.4 million adults found that 37% of adults in England have hypertension, and 59% of them don't know it.[^2]

The newer number comes from Oxford Population Health researchers working with Our Future Health, the UK's largest health research programme, using clinic blood pressure readings from participants enrolled between 2022 and 2025.[^2] Even people who are diagnosed and medicated fare only modestly better: half still have readings above the 140/90 mmHg threshold that defines hypertension, meaning their treatment isn't working as intended.[^2]

"The current approach to detecting and treating high blood pressure is not fit for purpose," said Wenyu Liu, a medical statistician at Oxford Population Health and one of the study's authors.[^2] Professor Bryan Williams, chief scientific and medical officer at the British Heart Foundation, called hypertension "the leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease" and, separately, "the silent killer" — a label earned because it typically produces no symptoms until it causes one: a stroke, a heart attack, kidney failure.[^2] [^3]

The response so far

The NHS's answer, for now, is testing capacity rather than new money. Community pharmacies have run a hypertension case-finding service since October 2021, offering free checks and, where warranted, a 24-hour ambulatory monitor to adults aged 40 and over who haven't had a reading taken in five years.[^4] An updated specification took effect on 3 September, tightening eligibility so the service focuses on genuinely undiagnosed cases rather than people already being treated.[^4] NHS England also backed this September's Know Your Numbers! Week, urging the public to get checked at a pharmacy or GP surgery.[^5]

What neither the BHF's campaign material nor the Oxford study offers is a current price tag. Older government estimates put the annual NHS cost of conditions attributable to high blood pressure — stroke, coronary heart disease, chronic kidney disease, dementia — above £2 billion, with the wider economic burden estimated near £15.8 billion a year;[^6] those figures predate this week's findings and have not, as far as this paper can establish, been updated to reflect the scale the new research describes. Nor has NHS England published projections of how much expanding case-finding might cost, or save.

What's missing from the picture

That absence matters for anyone trying to gauge the practical weight of this story. A campaign urging more testing is, on its own terms, uncontroversial — catching hypertension early is cheap and treating it is far cheaper than treating the stroke it causes. But without a funded target, a timeline for the case-finding service's expansion, or a modelled effect on GP and pharmacy workload, it is difficult to say whether this is a meaningful shift in NHS prevention strategy or a routine awareness push dressed in fresh data.

The next marker to watch is whether NHS England or the Department of Health and Social Care attaches figures to the case-finding service ahead of the November Budget, when health spending typically comes under scrutiny alongside the Chancellor's broader fiscal position.[^7]
