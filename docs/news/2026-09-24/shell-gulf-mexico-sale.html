Shell completes $840m Gulf of Mexico asset sale

Shell's Perdido platform, one of the deepest floating oil platforms in the world, in the U.S. Gulf of Mexico. — Mike Duhon / Shell, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell banked $840m exiting two ageing, non-operated Gulf of Mexico fields while completing a $16.4bn Montney buy in Canada — a swap of asset quality, not a regional retreat.

Shell has closed a sale it announced back in June, collecting $840 million in cash for its remaining stake in two aging U.S. Gulf of Mexico assets — even as it presses ahead with the largest Canadian oil and gas acquisition in its history. Taken together, the two moves outline where the supermajor thinks the next decade of North American energy returns will actually come from.

The divested assets are Shell's 50% non-operated interest in the BP-run Na Kika platform and its 100% stake in the Coulomb subsea tieback, both in the Gulf of Mexico — a region the Trump administration has rebranded the Gulf of America in federal usage. Talos Energy and an affiliate of Ridgewood Energy were the buyers. Talos took a 25% slice of Na Kika plus half of Coulomb and its operatorship, paying $420 million including a $42.5 million deposit already lodged; Ridgewood picked up the rest. The transaction, first struck in June at a headline value of $1.7 billion, closed at $840 million once adjustments and Shell's retained payments were netted out.

It is not a clean exit. Shell keeps contingent payments through 2027, royalty rights on any new wells tied back into Na Kika, and offtake agreements for future output. Both fields are also in decline — they produced a combined 37,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day for Shell in 2025 but hold only about 13.5 million boe of remaining proved reserves between them, and Shell's own modelling has them contributing little to production by 2030.

Pruning, not retreating

"The transaction supports Shell's efforts to actively shape its portfolio to ensure a resilient and increasingly competitive upstream business," the company said in confirming the close. Chief executive Wael Sawan had framed the broader push in blunter terms on the second-quarter earnings call in July, describing the disposal programme as "high grading in action, releasing value from assets where we are no longer the natural owner and reinvesting it in the next generation of competitively positioned supply."

That framing matters for how the Gulf sale should be read. Shell is separately reported to be in advanced talks to buy LLOG Exploration Offshore, a privately held Gulf operator, in a deal that could exceed $3 billion — a far larger bet on the same basin it just trimmed. The pattern is less a regional retreat than a swap: mature, non-operated, tail-end production out, higher-working-interest assets with longer runway in. The sale also sits inside a wider cost programme, a $5-7 billion structural reduction target Shell has set for 2028.

The Canadian side of the ledger

The contrast that matters more for positioning is Canada. Shell completed its $16.4 billion all-cash-and-stock acquisition of ARC Resources on September 2, taking full ownership of one of the largest independent producers in the Montney shale basin spanning British Columbia and Alberta. The deal adds roughly 370,000 boe/d of production immediately and about two billion boe of combined proved-plus-probable reserves, moving Shell from the seventh-largest Montney producer by gross operated volumes to second, behind only Ovintiv. Shell says the combined position lifts its targeted annual production growth to around 4% through 2030 — up from the 1% guidance given at its 2025 Capital Markets Day.

The Montney gas underpins a second Canadian commitment: LNG Canada, the Kitimat export terminal Shell leads alongside Petronas, PetroChina, Mitsubishi and Kogas. Partners could reach a final investment decision on a Phase 2 expansion — doubling capacity to 28 million tonnes a year — as soon as early October, with Asian buyers pushing for supply security amid Middle East tensions and Red Sea shipping disruption. ARC's acreage is explicitly positioned as feed gas for that expansion.

What it signals

For traders parsing capital allocation across the majors, the read is not "Shell exits U.S. offshore" but "Shell is repricing operatorship and asset life, wherever it finds them." Non-operated, declining Gulf interests get sold at a premium to a buyer better suited to running them out; operated, long-dated Gulf and Montney positions get bought. The Gulf remains part of the portfolio — Shell still runs assets like the Perdido spar — but the bar for what Shell itself operates there has risen.

The next marker is the LNG Canada Phase 2 investment decision, expected within weeks. A green light would confirm the Montney bet is being built out on schedule; a delay would raise questions about how quickly the ARC production actually finds a route to Asian buyers.
