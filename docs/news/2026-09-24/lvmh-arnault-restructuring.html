Arnault family moves to simplify LVMH holding structure

Bernard Arnault, chairman and CEO of LVMH, whose family is consolidating its holding structure to cement control of the luxury conglomerate. — Jérémy Barande / École polytechnique Université Paris-Saclay, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The Arnaults are merging their holding companies into a renamed Agache SCA, spending ~€1.63bn to buy out Dior's minority shareholders and simplify LVMH control ahead of succession.

The Arnault family filed plans on September 23 to collapse the layered network of holding companies sitting atop LVMH into a single listed entity, ending a governance structure that has shadowed the world's largest luxury group for years with questions about what happens when Bernard Arnault, 77, eventually steps back.

Under the plan, Financière Agache will merge into Agache, which will then merge into Christian Dior SE — the entity that already holds the bulk of the family's LVMH stake. Christian Dior will be converted into a limited partnership (société en commandite par actions) and renamed Agache SCA. Bernard Arnault keeps his role as general partner and managing partner of the new entity. To clear out the remaining public shareholders of Christian Dior, the family will launch a cash tender offer for the 2.44% of Dior it doesn't already own, at €469.05 a share — a premium of 21.6% to the one-month average price — worth roughly €1.63 billion.

One holding company, one set of votes

The mechanics matter because LVMH's control chain has long run through two intermediate layers. Agache owns Financière Agache outright; Financière Agache in turn holds 96% of Christian Dior's capital and 97.1% of its voting rights; and Christian Dior holds the Arnault family's direct stake in LVMH — currently 6.77% of the capital and 8.49% of the votes, according to the filing. Layer that against the family's other direct and indirect holdings and the group controls 50.33% of LVMH's share capital and 66.27% of its voting rights, a gap driven by the double-voting rights French law grants to shares held in registered form for more than three years.

Once the merger completes, that chain collapses to one: Agache SCA will hold a direct 49.76% of LVMH's capital and 65.55% of the votes, folding in "substantially all" of the family's LVMH stake, per the company's filing on Euronext Paris. The arithmetic changes only slightly — the family gives up a fraction of a percentage point of voting power by absorbing minority shareholders along the way — but the structure goes from three public and private entities stacked on top of each other to one.

Minority shareholders in Christian Dior aren't being squeezed out entirely. They can sell into the tender offer or hold their shares and become shareholders of Agache SCA once the conversion is complete — an unusual choice for a family that could, in principle, have forced them out. The extraordinary general meetings to approve the conversion and merger are scheduled for December, with the transactions expected to close before year-end and the tender offer opening in the first quarter of 2027, subject to sign-off from France's AMF markets regulator.

Why now

The timing lands amid two separate pressures on the Arnault empire. The first is succession. Arnault has five children — Delphine, Antoine, Alexandre, Frédéric and Jean — each holding a 20% stake in Agache Commandite SAS, the vehicle created to eventually take over the top of the LVMH control chain once their father steps down. Absent specific instructions from Arnault himself, major decisions in that vehicle require the assent of at least three of the five. A Le Monde investigation this summer reported tension among the siblings over the succession pecking order, which Arnault publicly rejected, writing in an open letter that his children "run Houses, build teams, make decisions, and — sacrilege — call each other on Sundays." Simplifying the holding structure now, while Arnault remains in the general-partner seat, reduces the number of moving parts his heirs will eventually have to navigate and removes one layer of complexity investors have cited as a source of succession-related uncertainty.

The second pressure is the business itself. LVMH's market capitalization has fallen to roughly €200 billion, down from a peak above €430 billion in 2023, pushing the group out of Europe's ten most valuable listed companies for the first time in years. First-half 2026 revenue slipped 3% to €38.6 billion and recurring operating profit fell 4% to €8.7 billion, as softer Asian demand, a run of price increases that has tested customer loyalty, and a slump in Hennessy cognac sales weigh on the group. A cleaner ownership structure does nothing to fix weak Chinese demand or cognac volumes, but it does remove a discrete source of valuation uncertainty — the risk premium investors sometimes attach to companies with opaque or contested control — at a moment when the group can ill afford additional doubt.

What the buyout signals

The €1.63 billion price tag for the Dior minority stake is small next to LVMH's market value, but it is a real cash outlay at a moment when the family is also managing a weaker earnings base. That the Arnaults are willing to pay a premium — 21.6% above the one-month average, 12.5% above the three-month average — to clear out a 2.44% stake rather than wait for a cheaper moment suggests the family places a higher priority on structural clarity than on near-term capital discipline. It is also a bet that a single, transparent holding vehicle, listed on Euronext Paris as Dior has been for more than three decades, will be easier for both minority investors and the next generation of Arnaults to work with than the current three-tier arrangement.

What's next

The December shareholder votes are the first checkpoint — Dior's minority holders, not just the family, need to approve the merger and conversion terms. After that, the AMF's review of the tender offer document will set the actual timeline for the 2027 buyout window. Longer term, the more consequential date has no fixed number attached to it: when Bernard Arnault eventually cedes the general-partner role, Agache SCA — and the five-way voting arrangement among his children — will be tested for the first time without him at the top of the chain.
