Refiners lobby against reported White House diesel export ban plan

US refiners warn that export restrictions could force them to cut production runs rather than redirect diesel to the domestic market. — JPxG / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Over 30 oil and business groups are lobbying Trump against a reported 90-day diesel export ban that his own energy and interior secretaries have also pushed back on.

Diesel is trading at record levels — AAA put the national average above $6.50 a gallon this week, up roughly 90 cents in a month and more than $2.80 above a year ago — and the White House is weighing whether to do something markets have not seen in years: temporarily stop US refiners from selling the fuel abroad.

The idea, first reported by Politico citing five people familiar with the deliberations, is a 90-day ban on diesel exports, with President Trump said to be inclined to announce it as soon as this week. It set off a scramble. Refiner and product-futures prices fell on the report, and by Wednesday more than 30 of the country's largest energy and business groups — the American Petroleum Institute, the American Fuel & Petrochemical Manufacturers, the US Chamber of Commerce, the National Association of Manufacturers, the Business Roundtable and others — had sent Trump a letter urging him to drop it.

"Export bans would lead to less fuel production, tighter supplies, and rising costs for American families, farmers, and truckers," the groups wrote, warning also of "retaliatory actions from other countries."

A White House at odds with itself

What makes this dispute unusual is that the administration seems to be arguing with itself. A White House official told Politico the administration was "not considering an export ban or export restrictions at this time." Energy Secretary Chris Wright has been more emphatic, telling reporters the "blunt tool of banning diesel exports definitely doesn't work" and that the real conversation is about getting more diesel into the domestic market while keeping gasoline and jet fuel flowing at full tilt. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum said he was "not at all confident" a ban would actually lower prices and warned it could "hurt Americans" if trading partners retaliated by curbing their own exports to the US. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, by contrast, has said only that officials are studying whether a full or partial ban is feasible given current refining capacity — leaving the door open rather than shutting it.

That spread of positions — from flat denial to open study — is the "partly denied" that makes this story hard to pin down. It reads less like a coordinated policy signal and more like a White House testing a politically appealing idea against a cabinet that thinks it would backfire, five weeks before the November 3 midterms.

Why refiners are alarmed

The price spike has a clear external trigger: Russia, hit by Ukrainian drone strikes on its refineries, has restricted its own diesel exports, removing the second-largest source of the fuel from the global market. US refiners argue that cutting off American exports in response would make the underlying supply problem worse, not better. Their logic is that a chunk of US refining capacity is built to serve export markets in Latin America and Europe; wall those customers off and refiners may simply run their plants less, shrinking output of diesel, gasoline and jet fuel together rather than redirecting diesel barrels home. API chief executive Mike Sommers put the industry's preferred fix bluntly: "The answer is more supply and more flexibility — not new restrictions that risk making a difficult situation worse."

It is also, more plainly, a margin question. Export bans depress the price refiners can charge for the barrels they can no longer sell overseas, even if domestic diesel prices ease — a trade the industry has no interest in making voluntarily, midterms or not.

What's next

Trump has given no public timeline beyond the reported end-of-week target, and nothing in the cabinet's public statements suggests the internal disagreement has been resolved. The near-term catalyst is simple: either an executive action materializes in the coming days, in which case refiner stocks and diesel futures move on the specifics of scope and duration, or the White House lets the idea lapse quietly, as it has with past export-restriction trial balloons. Diesel's price trajectory into the AAA's weekly reading will be the more immediate tell.
