Trump's Board of Peace unveils $2.45bn Gaza reconstruction plan

Destroyed buildings along Gaza's coast near the Beach refugee camp, illustrating the scale of the reconstruction challenge. — Abedallah Alhaj / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO)
In one sentence
The Board of Peace unveiled a $2.45bn, six-month Gaza rebuilding blueprint — just 3.4% of the $71.4bn decade-long cost, with most prior pledges still undisbursed.

The scene, on paper, had the trappings of a donor summit: a New York ballroom on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly, a stack of glossy project sheets, executives from the World Bank and Gulf sovereign funds nodding along. What the Board of Peace presented on Wednesday was, in substance, a plea. Trump's postwar governing body for Gaza unveiled a 66-project blueprint to begin reconstruction — priced at $2.45 billion for the first six months — while conceding that the territory's full rebuilding will cost roughly $71.4 billion over the coming decade. The initial ask covers barely one-thirtieth of that.

"The plan shows how to stand up a government from nothing, an economy from shambles, and a territory from disaster."

That framing, from a Board of Peace official, captures both the ambition and the problem. Eleven months after the ceasefire took effect, the group tasked with turning Trump's 20-point peace plan into a functioning Gaza is still, by its own admission, at the blueprint stage.

Sixty-six projects, six months

The blueprint, presented by Board of Peace high representative Nickolay Mladenov, breaks the initial phase into sector-specific spending: roughly $1 billion for infrastructure alone, including about $400 million for temporary shelters, $275 million to clear rubble and unexploded ordnance, $50 million for hospital rehabilitation and $30 million to repair Gaza's shattered road network. The remainder covers water and electricity restoration, education, health services and the deployment costs of the International Stabilization Force, the multinational security mission meant to underwrite the whole effort.

Implementation is designed to move geographically, sector by sector, starting in Rafah in the south and working north through Khan Younis, the central camps, Gaza City and finally the north — trailing, in theory, behind Hamas's disarmament and phased Israeli withdrawals. Jared Kushner, the president's son-in-law and an executive board member, described the effort as a platform for ordinary life to resume. "We're building something that is really a platform to enable the Palestinian people in Gaza to finally focus on improving their livelihoods," he said. Ali Shaath, chief commissioner of the newly formed National Committee for the Administration of Gaza — the technocratic body meant to eventually govern the strip — put it more plainly: "We want them to stay and live in Gaza, with hope and prosperity."

The arithmetic problem

The scale gap is the story. A World Bank-style damage assessment behind the plan puts medium-term reconstruction of Gaza's physical infrastructure at $35.2 billion, rising to $71.4 billion across a full decade once housing, utilities, health and education systems are rebuilt to something resembling pre-war capacity. Against that, $2.45 billion is a rounding error — 3.4% of the ten-year total, and less still of the true cost, since 92% of housing units in Gaza are destroyed or damaged and an estimated 42 million tonnes of rubble sit where neighbourhoods used to be.

The credibility problem runs deeper than the new ask. At a donor conference in February, roughly a dozen countries pledged some $17 billion toward Gaza's recovery — the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait and Saudi Arabia each committing $1 billion or more, alongside a larger U.S. contribution. By this summer, reporting on the fund's status found that almost none of it had actually moved: out of $17 billion promised, only around $280 million had been transferred. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, appearing alongside the new blueprint, was candid about the mismatch between ambition and timeline. "It's not something that can happen in six months," he said. "But at least this gives people a hope."

Facts on the ground haven't caught up

The reconstruction plan is also racing ahead of the political and security conditions it depends on. Israeli forces control an estimated 60% of Gaza territory and have not completed the withdrawals envisioned under the ceasefire. Hamas has not disarmed. The International Stabilization Force — meant to secure the territory for reconstruction crews — has moved slowly: Israel approved an initial deployment of only around 200 troops, and contributing countries have trickled in one at a time, with Kosovo signing on earlier this month and Albania in discussions to follow. Airstrikes continued even as the New York meeting took place. Humanitarian indicators remain grim — roughly 12% of children under five suffer chronic malnutrition, and a quarter of Gaza households are now headed by women, many in tents or the ruins of homes.

French President Emmanuel Macron, addressing the broader failure to move faster on Gaza's recovery at the UN this week, asked: "Are we simply to stand by and watch this spectacle that should shame us all?" It is a question the Board of Peace's own numbers seem to answer. A blueprint for 66 projects is a real planning achievement — the kind of document donors, engineers and technocrats need before a single shovel goes into the ground. But it is not money, and the group's own experience since February shows how far pledges can drift from disbursement.

The next marker will be whether the $2.45 billion for the six-month phase actually clears into accounts, rather than joining the $17 billion still parked in promises. Mladenov himself framed the moment as transitional rather than triumphant: "Our goal is to get through the next stage of actually moving from planning to implementation." Until pledges convert into wired funds and the stabilization force reaches meaningful strength, Gaza's reconstruction remains a plan for a plan.
