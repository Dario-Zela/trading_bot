UK losing up to £6.5bn a year in EU trade to mismatched product rules

Dover's Eastern Docks, the main crossing point for UK-EU freight, where post-Brexit certification requirements add cost and delay. — DeFacto / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
IPPR analysis finds UK exporters lost up to £6.5bn a year since Brexit from duplicate EU product testing, concentrated in autos, electronics and pharma.

British exporters have lost as much as £6.5bn a year in sales to the European Union since Brexit because UK and EU regulators no longer accept each other's product testing, according to new analysis from the Institute for Public Policy Research.

The think tank's report, published Thursday, puts the annual toll at between £3.7bn and £6.5bn for 2021 to 2024 — the years since Britain's exit from the single market took full effect. At the top end, that is roughly 0.18% of GDP, more than a tenth of the growth the Office for Budget Responsibility currently expects for a full year.

"Removing duplicate testing requirements could have increased exports by up to £6.5 billion a year while reducing unnecessary costs and delays for businesses."

That's Joseph Sassoon, an IPPR economist and co-author of the report, describing the gap left by the absence of a mutual recognition agreement (MRA) — a deal under which each side's regulators would accept the other's conformity testing rather than requiring goods to be certified twice.

Where the losses concentrate

The IPPR used a gravity-model, triple-difference design — comparing UK exports to the EU against rest-of-world exports to the EU as a control group — to isolate the effect of duplicate testing from other post-2020 shocks: Covid, supply-chain reshuffling, and the sanctions-driven repricing of energy and freight following Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Three sectors account for most of the gap:

  • Motor vehicles and parts: £2.48bn–£3.42bn a year
  • Electronics: £1.17bn–£1.67bn a year
  • Pharmaceuticals: £0.74bn–£0.82bn a year

These are sectors where a single product line often needs distinct safety, emissions or efficacy certification for the UK and EU markets even when the underlying manufacturing process is identical — a cost that scales with volume and hits mid-cap exporters, who lack the balance sheet to run parallel EU subsidiaries, hardest.

Why a fix hasn't landed

The mechanism to close the gap already has a name and a rough shape: an MRA built on "dynamic alignment," where the UK commits to tracking EU standards over time in exchange for reciprocal recognition of its testing regime. Keir Starmer's government pushed for exactly this in EU-UK talks, but EU diplomats say France rejected the demand outright, with the bloc broadly treating it as a non-starter — Brussels has been wary of any arrangement that lets the UK access single-market benefits without also accepting European Court of Justice oversight.

The issue hasn't gone away under Andy Burnham's government, which has signalled interest in a deeper UK-EU relationship on services and regulation, even as the more politically charged question of EU membership — floated this month by Tony Blair, who suggested Britain should aim to rejoin within a decade — dominates the wider debate. A standards-only MRA is a narrower, more technical ask than rejoining, but Paris's earlier rejection suggests it still needs bigger diplomatic cover than a bilateral customs fix implies.

For markets, the number gives a first concrete anchor to a cost that has mostly been argued about in adjectives. An annual £6.5bn drag, concentrated in autos, electronics and pharma, is a modest but persistent subtraction from UK export earnings — and, absent a negotiated fix, one that compounds each year regulatory regimes continue to drift apart rather than converge. The next marker to watch is whether standards alignment resurfaces as a formal agenda item at the next UK-EU summit, expected in the coming months.
