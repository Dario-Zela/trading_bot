Quantum computing stocks rally on IonQ breakthrough claim

Trapped-ion technology underpins IonQ's quantum processors, the architecture at the center of the company's error-correction claim. — Y. Colombe / NIST, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
IonQ jumped 11% and dragged quantum peers higher on an error-correction breakthrough claim, in a sector that has rallied and crashed on similar news twice before.

IonQ shares jumped as much as 12% intraday on Wednesday and closed roughly 11% higher, near $45, after the trapped-ion quantum computing company said it had cracked a problem that has dogged the industry for years: correcting errors fast enough, on ordinary hardware, to keep a quantum computer running.

The move dragged the rest of the sector with it. Rigetti Computing rose about 6%, D-Wave Quantum added 4%, and Quantum Computing Inc. gained roughly 3%. It was the kind of broad, sympathetic rally that has become a familiar pattern in this corner of the market — one stock's announcement lifting four or five others that had nothing to do with it.

What IonQ actually claimed

Quantum bits are notoriously fragile, prone to errors from heat, vibration and electromagnetic noise, and fixing those errors in real time — without pausing the machine — has been one of the field's harder engineering problems. IonQ says it built and tested a decoder that runs on a conventional CPU, works continuously in the background, and can catch and correct errors as they happen rather than after the fact.

The company put numbers behind the claim: the decoder was validated on benchmark circuits simulating 408 logical qubits and more than 31.5 million quantum operations, adding just 0.02% processing overhead. In plain terms, IonQ argues it can now manage error correction at scale without slowing the computer down or requiring specialized decoding hardware — a claim that, if it holds up in independent testing, would remove one of the standing objections to near-term commercial quantum computing.

The announcement landed alongside a separate deal: IonQ's Superion 256 system will be the first on-premise deployment at Nvidia's Accelerated Quantum Research Center, a facility built to pair quantum processors with classical GPU infrastructure. Two pieces of good news on the same day gave the rally extra fuel — B. Riley set a $100 price target on IonQ and pointed to a path toward $1 billion in annualized revenue within four to six quarters; Jefferies raised its target from $75 to $80.

A sector that has done this before

The harder question is whether this rally has more staying power than the last few. Quantum computing stocks have already been through two boom-and-bust cycles in roughly the past two years: a run that began in late 2024 collapsed in January 2025, and a second wave built through the year before fading again in the autumn. Since October 2024, IonQ, Rigetti, D-Wave and Quantum Computing Inc. have all posted quadruple-digit or near-quadruple-digit gains at their peaks — yet as of last week, IonQ, Rigetti and D-Wave were all sitting 60–76% below their 52-week highs. IonQ's own range this year has spanned $25.89 to $84.64, a spread that says more about sentiment than about the underlying business.

That history is why Wednesday's move is worth treating as a data point rather than a verdict. None of the quantum companies generate meaningful revenue from fault-tolerant, commercially useful quantum computing today; the investment case still rests on a technology roadmap measured in years. A decoder that performs well in benchmark simulations is a genuine technical result, but it is not the same as a customer paying for compute time on a machine that outperforms classical alternatives at a real task. B. Riley's $1 billion revenue timeline, in particular, is a projection, not a booking.

What would make this one stick

The test for whether Wednesday's rally is different from the last three is whether IonQ's decoder gets validated outside the company's own benchmarks, and whether the Nvidia partnership produces something more concrete than a hosting arrangement. Analyst price-target revisions are a useful signal of sentiment shift but not proof of it; the more telling marker will be IonQ's next earnings call and any independent peer review of the error-correction results. Until then, the rally reads as consistent with the sector's established pattern — real technical progress, an enthusiastic market reaction, and a valuation still well ahead of revenue.
