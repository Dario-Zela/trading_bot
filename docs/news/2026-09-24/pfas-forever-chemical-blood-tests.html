Blood tests find high levels of cancer-linked 'forever chemical' near UK factory

An industrial plant on the Hillhouse site near Thornton-Cleveleys, Lancashire, where chemical manufacturing has continued since the 1950s. — Bob Jenkins / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Blood tests on 49 Lancashire residents found alarming PFOA levels near a chemical plant, adding fuel to tightening EU and UK PFAS rules.

Blood tests carried out on 49 residents of Thornton-Cleveleys, a small town on the Lancashire coast, have found levels of a cancer-linked chemical high enough to put roughly seven in ten of them at increased risk of serious illness, according to results released on Wednesday. One resident, a child, recorded a reading described by the scientist who ran the tests as "ridiculously off the scale."

The chemical is PFOA — perfluorooctanoic acid — a member of the PFAS family of synthetic compounds known as "forever chemicals" because they resist breakdown in the environment and the body. The World Health Organization classifies PFOA as a Group 1 carcinogen, with links to kidney and testicular cancer, reduced thyroid function and pre-eclampsia. It has been banned in the UK since 2020, but no legal limit exists for how much can safely sit in a person's blood — Dr David Megson of Manchester Metropolitan University, who ran the analysis, compared the readings against guidelines used in the US and Germany instead. "Something seriously wrong has happened to that community," he said.

Decades of emissions, one closing plant

The likely source sits a short distance from the tested homes: the Hillhouse site, a former ICI chemical works taken over by AGC Chemicals in 1999. AGC used PFOA to manufacture non-stick coatings until 2012. An Environment Agency and Wyre Council investigation running since 2024 has traced roughly 49 tonnes of the chemical released from the site between the 1950s and 2012, contaminating soil, eggs and home-grown vegetables closely enough that two nearby allotments have been closed and classified as contaminated land.

AGC announced in July that it plans to close the plant, citing four consecutive years of financial losses and putting around 190 jobs at risk. More than 100 residents are weighing group legal action against the company through the law firm Leigh Day, seeking compensation for property devaluation and health risks — a path with an expensive precedent: DuPont paid $671m to settle more than 3,500 similar claims in West Virginia over PFOA contamination from a Teflon plant.

The wider tail risk

The findings land as PFAS regulation tightens on both sides of the Channel. The EU's Risk Assessment Committee concluded in March that existing rules are insufficient and backed a broader restriction; from August, its Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation bans PFAS above set thresholds in food packaging, forcing reformulation across fast-food wrappers and similar products. Britain's own draft rules tightening PFOS and long-chain PFCA limits are due in force by mid-December, and the EU's recast Product Liability Directive — which member states must transpose by the same month — widens the grounds on which PFAS makers and users can be sued.

For an industry embedded in textiles, firefighting foam, food packaging and industrial coatings, cases like Thornton-Cleveleys function less as isolated scandals than as regulatory previews. The next marker to watch is the EU's formal restriction proposal, expected to follow the RAC opinion, alongside whatever compensation claim Leigh Day's clients ultimately file against AGC.
