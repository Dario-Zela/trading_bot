FBI says hackers stole sensitive data on employees' intelligence work

The J. Edgar Hoover FBI Building in Washington, D.C., the bureau's headquarters. — Rob Young / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Hackers claim to have stolen terabytes of FBI employee data, including assignments to counterintelligence work against China and Russia, in a breach the bureau is still investigating.

The FBI confirmed this week that it is investigating a breach of its own job-recruitment systems, after a cybercriminal group claimed to have stolen terabytes of data including sensitive details about employees' intelligence work — the kind of information that, in the wrong hands, can turn agents and their families into targets.

The hacking group ShinyHunters says it broke into an Oracle PeopleSoft server that the FBI uses for HR and recruitment, then pivoted into an Amazon-hosted government cloud environment holding data on agents and job applicants. The bureau's public-facing recruitment site, FBIjobs.gov, went offline and stayed down through Wednesday. In a statement, the FBI said it was "aware of claims regarding unauthorized activity affecting FBIjobs.gov and is currently investigating," adding it was doing so "actively and aggressively."

What makes this breach different from a typical HR leak is the granularity. According to Reuters, which reviewed a 5,000-line spreadsheet the hackers say is a small sample of a much larger haul, the data includes names, home addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth, Social Security numbers and emergency contacts for what the group claims are thousands of current and former employees. Critically, it also maps individuals to their specific field offices and, in some cases, to units working counterespionage cases against Chinese and Russian intelligence services and drug cartels. Reuters said it independently verified details on more than 22 people by cross-referencing the data against credit records and earlier leaks. ShinyHunters claims the full trove runs to two or three terabytes.

Why the assignment data matters more than the addresses

Stolen names and addresses are a nuisance — stolen job assignments are a counterintelligence problem. Knowing which FBI employees are working against a specific foreign intelligence service, and where they're based, gives an adversary a target list for surveillance, recruitment attempts or coercion, particularly against family members whose details were also swept up. That risk is why security researchers contacted about the breach described the assignment-level detail as far more damaging than a standard payroll leak, even before any use of the data has been confirmed.

ShinyHunters is not a new name. It's one strand of a loose alliance — sometimes called Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters — that also includes Scattered Spider and remnants of Lapsus$, groups previously tied to breaches at Salesforce, Snowflake, Google and Workday. The FBI itself called the group out by name in a May advisory warning victims not to pay ransoms. ShinyHunters says the FBI breach is retaliation for that statement and is demanding the bureau retract it — not, notably, asking for money, which the group says isn't the motive here.

A pattern, not an isolated incident

This is the bureau's second acknowledged system compromise this year, following a breach of an internal wiretap-related surveillance system in March, the same month a hacking group linked to Iran claimed to have accessed FBI Director Kash Patel's personal accounts. It also lands amid a longer run of intrusions into federal networks: Salt Typhoon, a Chinese state-linked group, was found last year to have penetrated Treasury Department systems — reaching workstations used by then-Secretary Janet Yellen and other senior officials — after burrowing into telecom carriers including AT&T and Verizon. Washington sanctioned a Shanghai-based hacker and a Chinese cybersecurity firm over that campaign in January 2025.

What ties these episodes together is not a single point of failure but a recurring one: third-party and cloud-hosted systems sitting adjacent to core government networks, often with less scrutiny than the crown-jewel databases everyone assumes are locked down. The Treasury breach ran through a vendor. The FBI breach, if ShinyHunters' account holds up, ran through an HR platform. Investing in intrusion detection for classified systems does little if the door left ajar is a recruitment portal.

The FBI has not confirmed the scope or authenticity of the stolen data, and its investigation is ongoing. The near-term marker to watch is whether the bureau verifies the counterintelligence-related assignment data as genuine — that would elevate this from an embarrassing HR breach to an active operational security incident, and would likely sharpen congressional pressure for the kind of contractor-accountability and cloud-security spending that has followed prior federal breaches.
