GSK bets on long-acting biologics and AI to reshape respiratory pipeline

In one sentence
GSK is betting twice-yearly biologics and a 150-engineer AI team can defend its respiratory franchise against rivals writing multi-billion-dollar AI checks.

GSK used a Wall Street investor forum this week to make its case that respiratory disease — one of pharma's largest and most contested markets — is still winnable, provided the company can out-engineer rivals on drug design rather than out-spend them.

Speaking at Bernstein's healthcare forum in New York on Wednesday, Kaivan Khavandi, GSK's global head of translational and development sciences for respiratory, immunology and inflammation, laid out a pipeline built around long-acting biologics — drugs dosed twice a year or quarterly instead of weekly or monthly — paired with an internal AI effort GSK says helps it pick the right drug target for the right patient before a trial ever starts.

The centrepiece is depemokimab, sold as Exdensur, an anti-IL-5 antibody approved this year in the US, EU, UK, Japan and China for severe eosinophilic asthma. In the pivotal SWIFT-1 and SWIFT-2 trials it cut annualised exacerbation rates by 58% and 48% against placebo, with just two injections a year. GSK has since pushed it into COPD, running three further phase 3 trials — ENDURA-1, ENDURA-2 and VIGILANT — in patients with uncontrolled disease and type-2 inflammation.

Behind it sits a TSLP antibody inherited from the $1.4bn Aiolos Bio acquisition, now in pivotal asthma trials after supportive phase 2 data from the NAZARE study, and an ultra-long-acting IL-33 programme dosed quarterly, due to enter a third pivotal COPD trial next year tracking cardiovascular and respiratory hospitalisations and mortality — a harder endpoint than exacerbation counts alone. Earlier-stage bets include an activin-trap antibody for pulmonary arterial hypertension picked up via the 35Pharma deal, and a monthly FGF21 analogue for MASH from the Boston Pharmaceuticals acquisition.

Bigger checks are landing elsewhere

On AI, GSK points to a roughly 150-strong internal engineering group applying machine learning to target selection, trial enrolment and regulatory filings, plus a research tie-up with the AI biologics-design startup Chai Discovery. Company-wide, GSK has committed $30bn to US R&D and manufacturing over five years, including a $1.2bn slice for AI and digital infrastructure tied to a new biologics plant in Upper Merion, Pennsylvania.

That looks modest next to what competitors are now committing. Novo Nordisk struck a company-wide AI partnership with OpenAI in April. Eli Lilly has a $1bn joint lab with Nvidia and a deal worth up to $2.75bn with Insilico Medicine, whose lead asset, rentosertib, is a lung-fibrosis drug already through a phase 2a study in China — a direct shot at the respiratory territory GSK is trying to defend. Amgen, too, is running workflows through OpenAI's research tools. None of those figures are directly comparable to GSK's spending, since the companies disclose different things, but the gap in headline deal size is real, and GSK has offered no equivalent number of its own.

The company also isn't fighting in an empty field. Sanofi and Regeneron's Dupixent won FDA and EU approval in COPD last year, giving the IL-4 pathway a head start in a disease area GSK is only now entering with late-stage biologics. GSK's argument is that trial design and patient selection — not the antibody target itself — will decide who wins; whether that holds will show up in ENDURA-1 and ENDURA-2 data and in how quickly its AI-derived patient-selection claims translate into faster, cheaper trials rather than just a good line at an investor conference.
