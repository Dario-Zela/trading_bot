US lawmakers move to fast-track Chinese vehicle ban and overhaul FCC telecom review

A BYD Han EV — the kind of Chinese-made connected vehicle the Senate bill would permanently bar from US roads. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Senators are fast-tracking a permanent Chinese vehicle ban via unanimous consent, while House lawmakers separately unveil a bill giving Congress veto power over FCC telecom-gear blacklisting — both timed to the Trump-Xi summit.

The timing was not subtle. On the same week Xi Jinping arrived in Washington for talks with Donald Trump, a bipartisan group of senators told colleagues they intended to force a vote on permanently banning Chinese-made connected vehicles from American roads — using a procedural shortcut that needs no debate, no committee markup, and no dissent. A separate pair of House lawmakers, on the same day, unveiled a bill to rewrite how the Federal Communications Commission decides which Chinese telecom equipment gets blacklisted. Neither bill is law yet. Both are aimed squarely at the same idea: that the tools Washington already has for policing Chinese technology in critical infrastructure are too slow, too narrow, or too easily reversed.

The vehicle ban: fast enough to beat the summit

The push is led by an odd-couple pairing — Senator Elissa Slotkin, a Michigan Democrat, and Senator Bernie Moreno, an Ohio Republican — whose states between them build a large share of America's cars. Their bill, the Connected Vehicle Security Act, cleared the Senate Commerce Committee unanimously back in July. Getting a floor vote is a different matter, so Moreno notified Democratic offices this week that he intends to seek passage by unanimous consent — the mechanism senators use to skip debate when nobody formally objects. It only takes one senator saying no to sink it.

"Whether you are a Democrat or Republican, no one wants Chinese cars in America," Slotkin said. It is a tidy line, and it undersells how much the bill actually does. It would permanently bar the import, manufacture and sale of vehicles built in China (or Russia, Iran, North Korea) and strip the White House of the power to grant waivers — closing a door the Commerce Department had already started to shut administratively. The bill defines a "connected vehicle" broadly: any car built to communicate over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, cellular or satellite links, whether or not that capability is switched on when the car lands in a US port. The ban would bite in phases — vehicles and software from 2027, hardware components like modems, telematics units and even some safety electronics from 2030.

That is a wide net, and it explains why the bill already has 51 Senate co-sponsors and a House companion past 100. It also explains who is cheering loudest: a coalition of automakers — GM, Ford, Tesla, Toyota, Volkswagen, Stellantis, Hyundai — wrote to Congress urging exactly this, warning that letting Chinese manufacturers in would "shift jobs away" from their US operations. That is a real national-security argument about supply-chain surveillance and remote-kill risk in networked cars. It is also, transparently, an ask from companies that would otherwise have to compete on price with electric vehicles China builds more cheaply than almost anyone. Both things can be true, and the bill's backers have made only the first argument out loud.

The FCC bill: reining in the regulator, not just China

The second bill is less about China directly and more about who gets to decide. Representatives Brett Guthrie and Frank Pallone — the Republican chair and top Democrat on the House Energy and Commerce Committee, a pairing that rarely agrees on much — unveiled legislation to overhaul the FCC's "Covered List," the mechanism the agency has used since 2019 to blacklist foreign telecom gear (mostly from Huawei and ZTE) from US networks.

The bill would require the FCC to consult other agencies, chiefly Commerce, before adding anything to the list; force the agency to notify Congress at least seven days before doing so; and — the sharpest change — give Congress the power to overturn an FCC listing decision. It would also let the FCC decline to retroactively strip equipment authorizations issued before a product was blacklisted, easing the compliance burden that fell on carriers like AT&T and Verizon under the current rules. Guthrie's office framed it as a fix, not a rollback: "The current process for identifying and barring malicious foreign technology has worked in many cases, but lacks the transparency, consistency, and clarity needed to meet our long-term national security needs." Note what that sentence does not say — it does not accuse the FCC of being too lenient on China. It accuses the FCC of being too unaccountable, full stop. Giving Congress veto power over an independent agency's national-security calls is as much a separation-of-powers move as it is a China move.

Two bills, one instinct

Neither piece of legislation, taken alone, tells a complete story. The vehicle ban is a hard, absolute prohibition with phased deadlines already written into it. The FCC bill is procedural plumbing — it doesn't ban anything itself, it just changes who has to sign off before something gets banned, and hands Congress a check it didn't have before. But stacked together during the same week Xi sat down with Trump, they read as a single message from Capitol Hill: whatever comes out of the summit on trade, the legislative branch wants its own, harder-to-reverse guardrails around Chinese technology in American infrastructure — cars, networks, or whatever comes next.

That is also where both bills are weakest. "Fast-track" for the vehicle ban means unanimous consent, which collapses the moment one senator objects — and holdouts within the GOP have not been ruled out. The FCC bill is only just unveiled, with no committee date set and Chair Brendan Carr's office not yet on record. The bipartisan consensus is real. Whether it survives contact with amendments, industry lobbying, and the actual mechanics of getting anything through a divided Congress before the session ends is the part nobody in Washington is promising this week.

Watch for whether Moreno's unanimous-consent request actually gets a floor reading in the coming days — that is the first real test of whether this bipartisan energy converts into an enacted law rather than a press release.
