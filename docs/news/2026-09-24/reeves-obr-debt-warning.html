Reeves faces fresh OBR debt warnings ahead of November Budget

Chancellor John Healey, who replaced Rachel Reeves at the Treasury in July, faces his first Budget on 28 October with fiscal headroom cut by rising gilt yields. — Lauren Hurley / No. 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Chancellor John Healey faces a fiscal headroom nearly halved by surging gilt yields ahead of the 28 October Budget, with the IFS pricing a £25bn gap.

The government minister who will stand at the despatch box on 28 October to deliver Britain's next Budget is not the one this newsroom, and most of Westminster, spent the summer expecting. Rachel Reeves left the Treasury on 20 July, when Keir Starmer's resignation as prime minister handed the keys of Downing Street to Andy Burnham. Her successor, John Healey, inherits her fiscal rules, her in-tray, and a bond market that has grown considerably less forgiving since March.

At the Spring Statement, the Office for Budget Responsibility pencilled in £23.6bn of headroom against the government's current-budget rule — the margin by which day-to-day spending must sit in surplus by 2029-30. That cushion has since been cut roughly in half. A sell-off in gilts, aggravated by a spike in oil prices past $100 a barrel amid the conflict with Iran, pushed 10-year yields to around 5.3% in September, their highest since 2008, while 30-year yields edged toward 6%, a level last seen in 1998. Analysts at Pantheon Macroeconomics put the resulting hit to headroom at roughly £9bn-£10bn, with weaker growth accounting for a further £2bn or so — leaving Healey with something closer to £13bn, before he has committed to a single pound of new spending.

The arithmetic is unforgiving at the margin: every quarter-point rise in gilt yields adds about £2.5bn a year to the government's debt-interest bill. August alone saw the Treasury pay out £8.8bn in interest, the highest figure for that month on record, while public borrowing came in £3.5bn above the OBR's own forecast. National debt stood at £2.985 trillion in July, 94.1% of GDP.

The gap between what's needed and what's ruled out

The Institute for Fiscal Studies estimates the government may need to find upwards of £25bn in tax rises or spending cuts to rebuild a working buffer against fiscal-rule breach — a figure that sits well above the narrower £10bn-£13bn some market economists see as the bare minimum. Healey has so far ruled out following Reeves's playbook of raising income tax, employee National Insurance or VAT, the three taxes Labour's manifesto explicitly protected. That leaves thinner, more contested options — property, dividends, pensions relief — to do the heavy lifting, with KPMG forecasting UK growth of just 1.3% this year, not enough to grow the country out of the problem.

Healey has pledged the Budget will be "built on fiscal discipline," language chosen to reassure a gilt market still pricing in the risk of slippage. The Bank of England's decision to slow quantitative tightening and pause further gilt sales until April 2027 has taken some pressure off the long end, but the underlying tension — a chancellor needing to tighten policy into a slowing economy while debt-servicing costs climb — remains exactly the one Reeves faced a year earlier, now under a new name.

The next hard data point is the OBR's pre-Budget forecast, expected in the fortnight before 28 October, which will fix the fiscal window the Treasury has to work with. Gilt auctions between now and then will tell markets how much patience is left.
