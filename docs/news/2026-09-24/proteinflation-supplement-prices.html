'Proteinflation': shoppers paying up to £100 for a bag of protein powder

Whey protein powder, the raw ingredient behind the 'proteinflation' squeeze — Adrem68 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Whey protein prices have surged up to 130% in a year, with some UK shoppers now paying £100–£170 for a single bag.

The gym bag has a new grievance. Ask around a British fitness class this autumn and someone will bring up "proteinflation" — the gym-goers' nickname for the price of whey protein powder, which has climbed so fast that a weekly shake habit now competes with the weekly shop.

The numbers are startling for a category once associated with bulk tubs and bargain multibuys. One London banking worker told the BBC his usual 4.5kg bag of whey rose from around £60 in 2020 to £120 last year, then £170 this year — prompting him to trade down to a cheaper brand that still costs roughly £100 a bag. A 19-year-old Manchester student said she now rations her £32.99 tub, scooping less than the label recommends to make it last. Across the market, according to the commodities intelligence firm Expana, European wholesale prices for a widely used high-protein whey ingredient were 130% higher in late August than a year earlier — and more than four times their level three years ago.

Why the powder costs more

Whey is not manufactured on demand; it is skimmed off as a byproduct of cheesemaking, which means supply cannot simply be scaled up when appetite grows. And appetite has grown a great deal. High-protein eating has moved from gym culture into the mainstream, with food companies now stretching the label onto bagels, yoghurts, crisps and bottled water. Layered on top is the GLP-1 effect: roughly 5% of UK adults are now thought to be on weight-loss drugs such as Mounjaro, Wegovy or Ozempic, and patients are routinely advised to raise protein intake to preserve muscle mass while they lose weight quickly. That adds a new, non-discretionary source of demand onto a supply chain built around a cheese industry that was never sized for it.

Suppliers are absorbing some of the hit rather than passing it all on. One UK sports nutrition founder told the BBC his firm raised its retail price from £12.50 to £17.99 last year but is still subsidising the true cost of whey from elsewhere in the business — a sign that margins, not just shelf prices, are being squeezed.

The narrower story behind the headline number

What makes this useful beyond the gym-locker anecdote is what it says about where inflation is actually landing. Headline UK inflation has been comparatively tame this year, but that average obscures pockets of much sharper price growth in categories where demand has outrun a slow-moving supply chain — exactly the dynamic here. Protein powder is a small line in most households' budgets, but the fact that shoppers are absorbing triple-digit price rises rather than walking away says something about how sticky discretionary wellness spending has become. Fitness and health categories, once the first casualties of a squeezed budget, are proving unusually resilient.

The next data point worth watching is whether whey prices ease as new dairy processing capacity comes online in Europe and the US over the coming year, or whether GLP-1 prescription growth keeps demand structurally higher than the cheese industry can supply.
