BEYOND THE TAPE · INCOME · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-06-08 · 3 min read

Why passive-income investors are looking abroad as US yields fail to clear 5.4%

The London Stock Exchange at Paternoster Square — home to several of the international dividend payers now filling the income gap that US markets can no longer close — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
S&P 500 yields at 1.07% make a 5.4% blended-yield target impossible on domestic equities alone — the math for $19k income on $350k points firmly abroad.

For a generation of income investors who built strategies around dividend reinvestment and passive cash flow, the arithmetic has quietly turned hostile. Generating $19,000 a year from a $350,000 portfolio requires a blended yield of 5.4%. The S&P 500, now yielding around 1.07% — a level not seen since the nineteenth century — offers no route to that number on its own.

The solution that a growing number of systematic income strategies have landed on is uncomfortable but logical: go international.

The Numbers Demand It

The math is unforgiving. An equal-weighted basket of five heavily traded international names — British American Tobacco, HSBC, Shell, Novartis, and BHP — produces a blended yield of roughly 3.8%, generating about $13,300 on that same $350,000. Better than the S&P 500 by a multiple, but still $5,700 short of the target.

To close the gap, allocation has to do some work. Tilting 35% of the portfolio toward British American Tobacco (BTI), which currently yields 5.5% on an annualised payout of $3.34, and 25% toward HSBC — which paid $3.30 in dividends in 2025 and has guided to a 50% payout ratio through 2028 — gets the blended rate past 5.4%. Shell (3.6%) and Novartis (3.3%), which has raised its annual dividend from $3.38 in 2021 to $4.77 this year, fill out the growth component. BHP (2.9%) brings commodity exposure but drags the average; position sizing matters here.

The contrast with home markets is stark. Per Siblis Research, US equities yield 1.18% against 3.10% in the UK, 2.94% in France, and 4.45% in Italy. JPMorgan estimates US equities carry a 34% valuation premium over international peers — against a 19% long-run average — while the dollar sits roughly 10% above fair value. Both factors argue for the rotation.

There is a tax wrinkle worth naming. Foreign dividends typically attract a 15% withholding at source. In taxable accounts, the shortfall is reclaimable via IRS Form 1116's foreign tax credit; in tax-deferred accounts — IRAs and 401(k)s — the credit is forfeit. The after-tax yield in sheltered accounts is therefore lower than the headline rate, which matters when the target is already a squeeze.

What changes the calculus from here is currency. The yield advantage from international holdings comes partly from the weak dollar and partly from genuine payout culture differences — UK, Australian, and continental European companies have historically distributed higher fractions of earnings than their US counterparts. If the dollar claws back its overvaluation, some of the income gains get eaten at the exchange desk. The cleaner long-term watch is whether US large-cap payout ratios respond to any rotation out of buybacks — but there is no near-term catalyst. Until then, the 5.4% target points overseas.
