Hallador commits $350m to new coal turbines, betting Merom can outlast the energy transition

The Merom Generating Station, Sullivan County, Indiana — a 1,080 MW coal-fired plant that Hallador plans to expand with 460 MW of natural gas turbines. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Hallador Energy committed $350M to natural gas turbines for Merom as hedge funds holding $118M in HNRG bet on capacity-starved grid demand over coal's long-term future.

Hallador Energy, operator of a 1,080-megawatt coal-fired station on the Indiana side of the Wabash River, has agreed to pay $350 million for 460 megawatts of Siemens natural gas turbines it intends to install at the same site. The purchase agreement, announced June 1, marks the largest single capital commitment in the company's history and locks in equipment that management says gives the project its first realistic chance of advancing.

The turbines — acquired from Australia-based Energy World Corporation, never previously fired — carry a headline price of roughly $760 per kilowatt. Delivering them to the Merom site in Sullivan County adds approximately $100 million in transportation, refurbishment, and logistics, pushing the total delivered cost to around $450 million. That figure represents more than half of the estimated total project cost. CEO Brent Bilsland was direct about why the company moved when it did. "Until you have equipment, you don't have a project," he said at the announcement. "We now have Siemens equipment — at what we believe is the right price, at the right time."

The pricing rationale is straightforward: new turbine order books at major manufacturers currently stretch years into the future. Acquiring never-fired units on the secondary market short-circuits that queue, and Hallador argues it did so at a discount to new-build alternatives.

Not quite the coal bet it looks like

Hallador sits awkwardly in the coal-stock column. It owns the Oaktown underground mines in Knox and Sullivan counties, and Merom's two generating units still burn bituminous coal. But the turbine purchase is for a proposed natural gas combustion turbine project at the site — additional capacity alongside the existing coal plant, not a coal retrofit. Hedge funds holding approximately $118 million in HNRG as of the first quarter of 2026 are, in effect, making a bet on gas-fired capacity demand as much as on coal's staying power.

What keeps the stock interesting to those funds is Hallador's contracted position. The company's sales book exceeded $2.1 billion in 2026, anchored by a 12-year capacity agreement running 2028 to 2040 and valued at over $1 billion — priced at roughly $470 per megawatt-day, more than double the most recent capacity market clearing price of around $230 per megawatt-day. Jefferies upgraded the stock to Buy in March with a $22.50 price target, noting that Hallador deliberately left its Fall 2028 capacity uncontracted — a gap analysts read as space reserved for a potential deal with a hyperscaler seeking reliable power for AI data centres.

The company enters this commitment from a clean balance sheet. It held no outstanding bank debt as of March 31, with a $120 million credit facility undrawn.

The turbine purchase does not trigger automatic construction. Hallador has set out a four-step path: secure equipment (done), enter MISO's ERAS interconnection study (imminent), market the power output under long-term purchase agreements (ongoing), and take a final investment decision only after the MISO study concludes — an outcome expected around September 2026. The company retains the option to build, sell the project with its new equipment, or sell the turbines independently.

If everything falls into place, commercial operations would begin between late 2028 and mid-2029. The September interconnection result is the next hard checkpoint — the moment when Hallador's three exit options stop being theoretical.
