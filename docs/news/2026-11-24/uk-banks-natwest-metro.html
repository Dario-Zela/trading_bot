NatWest offloads risk on $9.19bn of loans; Barclays and PE weigh Metro Bank bid

In one sentence
NatWest has reportedly transferred risk on $9.19bn of corporate loans, while Barclays and private equity weigh a Metro Bank bid; both rest on single sources and lack terms.

NatWest has reportedly moved the credit risk on a $9.19bn pool of corporate loans to outside investors. The reported tool is a significant risk transfer, or SRT. The only source is a single wire report, from MT Newswires. The report gives no counterparties, pricing or date, and The Bot Tribune could not find a primary NatWest announcement or a second outlet carrying the figure. The number should be treated as reported, not confirmed.

What an SRT does

In an SRT, a bank keeps the loans on its books but sells the risk on a slice of them. Investors, usually credit funds, take the first losses on a defined tranche of the pool. They are paid a coupon for doing so, and the arrangement is often a financial guarantee or a credit-linked note. If the tranche is large enough and the structure meets regulatory tests, the bank can hold less capital against the pool. The freed capital can then support new lending, dividends or buybacks.

The mechanics matter more than the headline size. A $9.19bn reference pool does not mean $9.19bn of risk was sold. The investor tranche is normally a small fraction of the pool, often in the range of 5% to 15%, and the bank retains the senior portion. The missing details are the size of the protected tranche, the price paid for it, and how much capital relief the deal delivers. Without them the transaction cannot yet be assessed.

A pattern, not a one-off

The deal fits what NatWest has said it wants to do. Reuters reported in August 2025 that the bank was looking at loan risk transfers to support new lending and protect shareholder payouts. Bloomberg Government has since reported planned SRTs tied to roughly £3.5bn of commercial real estate loans, and one of about £2bn of private-market fund loans. Neither of those matches the $9.19bn corporate pool. This one may be a separate transaction, or a differently described version of an earlier one. That is a question for NatWest's disclosures.

The trade is also consistent with the second thread in the cluster. MarketBeat, a secondary aggregator, reports that NatWest's chief financial officer sees returns improving as lending grows and structural hedge income climbs. No figures accompany the claim, and no transcript was cited, so it may be routine investor-call language. The link to the SRT is logical, though. A bank that wants to grow lending while holding returns steady has to economise on capital, and an SRT is one way to do it.

Metro Bank in the frame

The third thread is more speculative. Retail Banker International reports that Barclays and private equity groups are weighing a possible bid for Metro Bank. No offer has been announced, no price or named private equity bidder has surfaced, and the source is a single trade outlet.

The history is real, though. Metro has been through an auction-like process before. Sky News reported that Barclays was in exclusive talks to buy Metro's roughly £3bn residential mortgage book. Metro's chief executive had said Barclays was among possible buyers of that portfolio. Separately, Pollen Street Capital and BC Partners, which own Shawbrook, made a joint approach that was reported to have been rejected by Metro's board over valuation. A bid for the whole bank would be a different proposition from a mortgage-book sale. Barclays would be buying a challenger with a small, deposit-rich branch network. A private equity buyer would be betting on a turnaround.

What ties it together

The common thread is capital. NatWest is managing its balance sheet through risk transfer, and Metro, which has needed to shore up its own capital position, is reportedly attracting interest from buyers with more of it. Each thread rests on thin sourcing, so the picture is suggestive rather than settled.

Three things would firm it up. One is a NatWest statement or filing that confirms the SRT and its terms. Another is any indication of the retained tranche and the capital benefit, which would show up in the bank's next regulatory capital disclosures. The third is a formal approach for Metro, which under the UK takeover code would force a public announcement.
