Green groups condemn plan to abolish Environment Agency and Natural England

In one sentence
Green groups call plans to scrap the Environment Agency and Natural England 'scapegoating'; the Tories have pledged abolition and ministers have only said both bodies are under review.

Green groups are condemning a plan to abolish England's two main environmental regulators. The picture is murkier than the headline suggests, though. The Conservatives have formally pledged to abolish both the Environment Agency and Natural England. The Labour government has said only that both are under review, as part of a wider cull of arm's-length bodies. It has not confirmed that either will go.

The distinction matters. The 'scapegoating' charge is aimed at a political argument that has now been taken up on both sides of the Commons: that regulators, not ministers, are the reason homes and infrastructure do not get built.

Two plans, one target

The Conservative proposal came first. On 5 October, Victoria Atkins, the shadow Defra secretary, told an NFU breakfast at the party conference in Birmingham that a Conservative government would abolish Natural England and the Environment Agency and bring their working powers under direct ministerial control. She described it as the first stage of a year-long review of Defra's 34 quangos. No successor body was named. Powers that worked would return to Defra, she said, while those that 'get in your way' would be scrapped. Agency staff doing a good job would be invited to stay 'on team Defra', Farmers Weekly reported.

Seven weeks later the government moved onto similar ground. Louise Haigh, the First Secretary of State, told the CBI conference on 23 November that politicians had 'duck[ed] accountability' by passing responsibility to regulators. 'As part of rewiring the state, we will reform arm's length bodies to increase public control,' she said. Each body would be asked what should be 'devolved, dissolved, or delivered by departments', and the process had 'already begun with the Environment Agency, Natural England, Ofwat and the Drinking Water Inspectorate'.

Haigh did not say which bodies would be dissolved. The Times reported that Natural England is among those being considered, and the government has not confirmed it. Plans to merge Ofwat and the Drinking Water Inspectorate with the Environment Agency's water functions were announced last year. Many of the proposals may also draw on a report by Jon Cunliffe, which recommended replacing Ofwat with a new super-regulator.

Atkins said the government was copying part of the Conservative approach and should adopt all of it. 'Seven weeks ago, the Conservatives announced the first phase of our plan,' she said.

The 'scapegoat' charge

The word has come from Wildlife and Countryside Link, the coalition of environmental charities. Its chief executive, Dr Richard Benwell, said politicians had found 'a bigger scapegoat: Natural England itself', and that ministers should not make regulators 'scapegoats for political failures', according to the Forestry Journal. The coalition organised an open letter, signed by the leaders of 40 environmental organisations, warning that scrapping Natural England would 'shake the credibility of any government claiming to care about our natural world'.

Craig Bennett, chief executive of the Wildlife Trusts, went further against the Conservative plan in October, calling the abolition of the regulators 'gross negligence from any party that aspires to government'. He said it would not encourage growth and would expose the economy and food supplies to climate and nature risks. Tony Juniper, Natural England's own chair, said: 'It's never been the case that healthy nature is in opposition to economic development.'

The case for the plan rests on a real grievance. Critics accuse Natural England of blocking housing and infrastructure, and ministers have blamed it for derailing the Ebbsfleet garden city. Sam Richards of Britain Remade cautiously welcomed the possible scrapping of Natural England, but said it would achieve little alone. 'This should be a small part of a wider reform package, including to the Habitats Regulations,' he said. The Countryside Alliance backed the Conservative plan, arguing that rural decisions should be made by elected ministers rather than bureaucrats.

Richards's point is the strongest evidence for the 'scapegoating' reading. Natural England mostly applies law that Parliament wrote. Abolishing the body while leaving the Habitats Regulations in place would move the decision, not remove the constraint.

What is at stake

The Environment Agency handles pollution permits, flood risk and water regulation. Natural England handles protected habitats and conservation. Neither plan sets out where those duties would land, who would enforce them, or how an organisation sitting inside a ministry would keep its independence from the minister who might want a project approved.

That is the argument made by the campaigners: independent regulators exist partly to be unpopular. Haigh's counter is also coherent. She says ministers should accept responsibility, and that 'the buck stops with us'. Her argument is about who is accountable, and in principle it could be met without cutting protection.

The evidence to watch is the detail. A review of each body's functions is under way, but no timetable has been published. The questions are whether the Habitats Regulations are reopened alongside any abolition, whether statutory duties are kept, and whether enforcement staff and budgets move with the functions. Until then, the dispute is about a direction of travel, not a legal change.
