Britain's landlords are getting bigger, raising the prospect of a two-tier rental market

In one sentence
A BBC Business report suggests larger landlords are consolidating as smaller ones exit, but no figures were available, so a two-tier rental market remains a forecast.

A report from BBC Business has put a familiar worry about the private rented sector in a new form. Larger landlords are getting bigger, and smaller ones are leaving. If both continue, England's rental market could split in two. One tier would be professionally managed portfolios run by a few large operators. The other would be a shrinking group of individual owners with a handful of properties each.

The claim comes with limits. The material supplied for this piece contained no portfolio sizes, landlord counts or dates. The BBC article could not be checked, and the 'two-tier' label is a forecast, not a reported outcome. What follows is therefore an account of the mechanism and of what would confirm it. It does not establish that the shift is under way.

Why the squeeze would favour scale

The pressures on small landlords are well documented, even if their combined effect is not measured here. Since 2017, mortgage interest relief for individual landlords has been restricted to a basic-rate tax credit. The stamp duty surcharge on additional homes was raised to 5% in October 2024. The Renters' Rights Act, which abolishes fixed-term assurance shorter tenancies and no-fault evictions, adds compliance and possession-risk costs. Each of these falls on a landlord with a single buy-to-let flat. Each is a smaller share of the costs of a firm with hundreds of units, a legal team and a lender that knows its name.

Scale also helps on finance. Institutional and professional landlords can usually borrow on better terms than an individual on a buy-to-let mortgage. They can spread void periods, repairs and regulatory change across many tenancies. A landlord with two houses who decides to sell has a clear exit. A larger operator is more likely to be the buyer than the seller.

What a two-tier market would mean

The effects would not all run one way, which is why the trend matters to policymakers.

On the favourable side, professionally run portfolios tend to bring consistent standards, formal complaints processes and longer-term investment in stock. Tenants in the upper tier might get better-maintained homes and less arbitrary treatment.

On the other side, fewer landlords means less choice. A small landlord often rents at below-market rates to a tenant they know, or accepts people that automated referencing screens out, such as those with thin credit files, pets or housing benefit. If that supply shrinks and larger operators set the market rate, the people who relied on it would have fewer options. Concentration could also give the biggest operators more pricing power in the towns where they hold most of the stock. That is a hypothesis, not an observed result.

The supply effect is the most direct. Homes sold by exiting landlords do not disappear. They are bought by owner-occupiers, by other landlords or by institutions. The net effect on the number of homes to rent depends on who buys them, and no data on that was supplied.

What would settle it

The question can be answered with figures. The useful measures are the share of rented homes held by landlords with one to three properties, the number of landlords with 100 or more, and the flow of homes from small landlords into owner-occupation. Government English Housing Survey releases, landlord registration data and lender statistics on buy-to-let lending would all bear on it. Until a source supplies those numbers, 'consolidation' describes a direction of travel, not a measured shift.

The next test is the implementation of the Renters' Rights Act's tenancy reforms. Sales by small landlords, or a lack of them, over the coming quarters will show whether regulation is pushing owners out or only changing how they operate.
