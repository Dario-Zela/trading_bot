Gilts in focus as 10-year yield holds above 5% before the BoE stability report

In one sentence
The 10-year gilt yield is about 5.2%, down from late October, and Thursday's routine Financial Stability Report is the next test of whether the BoE sees strain from its November hike.

The yield on Britain's 10-year government bond is sitting a little above 5%, and the Bank of England publishes its Financial Stability Report on Thursday. The two are linked mainly by timing. The report is a fixture of the Bank's calendar, published twice a year. The more interesting question is whether a central bank that raised interest rates this month is adding strain to the financial system it is about to assess.

Where gilts stand

The 10-year yield was about 5.21% on 23 November, according to Trading Economics. That is roughly 0.14 to 0.15 percentage points lower than a month earlier, and about 0.66 points above its level a year ago. The yield has eased in five of the last six weeks. Late-October readings were higher: 5.44% on 20 October and 5.36% on 28 October, ahead of the Chancellor's first budget.

So the yield is above 5%, but it has been falling, not rising. Two recent drivers pushed it down:

  • An AI-driven equity selloff sent investors into government bonds on 20 November.
  • A flash PMI showed UK private-sector growth slowing, with services at a five-month low. Softer-than-expected inflation and a mixed labour report added to the pull towards bonds.

That sits awkwardly with the idea of a tightening Bank pushing long gilts higher. On the evidence so far, the Bank's move has not driven yields up since it was announced.

The hike

The Monetary Policy Committee raised Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4% at its meeting ending 4 November. The Bank's own Monetary Policy Report records an 8–1 vote, with one member preferring a 50bp rise to 4.25%. Some secondary reports give a 9–0 vote. The Bank's figure is the one to rely on.

Markets are pricing about two more hikes over the coming months, according to LSEG data cited by Trading Economics. The next MPC announcement is on 17 December.

What the report could say

The Financial Stability Report is due at 10.30am on Thursday 26 November, alongside the Financial Policy Committee's record. The previous edition, in July, flagged AI as a growing threat to cyber and operational resilience and kept the countercyclical capital buffer at 2%. On 13 November Governor Andrew Bailey said private credit and stablecoins could pose systemic risks before they reach systemic size.

The gilt market itself is a plausible subject. Pension funds, which hold long-dated gilts, and mortgage lenders, which price off swap rates tied to gilt yields, are both sensitive to the level of long yields. Government borrowing costs matter too, given the fiscal backdrop. Nothing published so far shows that stress has built up in any of these areas. A yield of 5.2% is high by recent standards, but it is lower than a month ago, and the evidence of strain would be in how fast yields move and how orderly trading is, not in the level alone.

What to watch

Thursday's report will show whether the FPC treats gilts as a live risk or leaves the focus on private credit, stablecoins and AI. The 17 December MPC decision then tests the market's pricing of further rises. A hike that lifts short rates while long yields keep drifting down would narrow the gap between the two, which is a story of its own.
