PIP review expected to call for a rebuilt disability assessment system

In one sentence
Sir Stephen Timms's review of PIP is expected to call for a rebuilt assessment system and a fast track for some claimants, as arguments over welfare cuts for young people continue.

A government review of Personal Independence Payment, the benefit that helps disabled people in Britain meet extra living costs, is expected to say that the way the state decides who qualifies needs rebuilding. It is also expected to propose quicker payment for some claimants. The final report from Sir Stephen Timms, the social security minister who has led the work, was due before MPs on Tuesday. Its contents had not been published when this article was written, so what follows rests on advance reporting and on the review's own earlier documents.

Advance coverage, including the BBC's, says the report will call for a "fundamental overhaul" of PIP assessments. That would be a notable admission from inside government. It follows an interim report in July that called the benefit highly valued by claimants but "not fit for purpose".

What the review has already said

PIP replaced Disability Living Allowance for working-age adults from 2013. It is paid on the basis of how a health condition or disability affects daily living and mobility, not on the condition itself. Claimants are scored in an assessment, usually by a health professional working for a DWP contractor, and the score determines whether they receive a standard or enhanced rate.

The interim report, published on 9 July, said Timms found that the assessment too often became "a needless fight". He said unnecessary reassessments eroded trust in decisions. The review drew more than 38,000 responses to its call for evidence, according to Sky News.

A September annex set out emerging ideas that point the same way:

  • Face-to-face assessments where evidence is thin, contradictory or would benefit from further exploration.
  • Different pathways for different groups, so that people with clear evidence of need are not put through full assessments.
  • A review of the 28-day hospitalisation rule, which affects payments to people admitted to hospital.

I could not confirm the status of the annex, so these should be read as emerging proposals, not settled recommendations.

The expected recommendations

Pre-publication accounts add detail on three points. First, a fast-track route for the most severely ill claimants. One account puts that group at about 10% of claimants, but the figure is unverified. The logic is that a lighter process for people whose need is obvious frees assessment capacity for harder cases. Second, an assessment that looks at the actual costs of a person's disability, with cash payment remaining the core of the benefit. Third, non-cash support, floated in draft in September, which is reportedly expected to be optional.

The discovery material gave no timescale, cost or claimant numbers for faster payment. Nor did I find DWP figures on appeal rates or backlogs that could be tested against the review's claims. The report itself will need to supply them. Without them it is hard to judge how far "quicker" payment would shorten waits that many claimants describe as lengthy.

The constraint of cost

The review's remit includes keeping PIP financially sustainable. Ministers agreed not to change the benefit until Timms had reported. That sets up a tension: a more generous, less adversarial process could raise costs, while the government has been looking for savings in the welfare bill. The question for the Treasury is whether fewer disputes and fewer reassessments pay for themselves, or whether a rebuilt system simply admits more people.

The government has not said whether it will adopt the recommendations. Reporting also notes that it remains unclear how the Timms review will fit with the separate Milburn review of youth unemployment.

The youth question

That uncertainty matters because of a parallel argument about young claimants. The Guardian reports research warning that planned disability benefit cuts affecting under-25s risk worsening youth destitution. I could not trace the specific study the Guardian cites, so its figures are not reproduced here.

Other evidence points the same way, though most of it comes from advocacy groups and think tanks and uses different years and methods. Scope's analysis, cited by charities, estimated that about half of households with an under-22 claimant of the Universal Credit health element are already in poverty. The Institute for Government has found that 79% of young people on Universal Credit are in financial precarity, against 48% of 19 to 21-year-olds overall. Around four in five under-25 claimants list a psychiatric disorder as their main condition, according to Irish News reporting.

It is also disputed what exactly is proposed. One report said the Milburn review was expected to recommend ending PIP and the Universal Credit health element for 16 to 24-year-olds. Contact, the disability charity, said that the report did not currently recommend removing the health element for disabled young people under 22. A Glasgow Centre for Public Policy analysis argued that savings could be outweighed by added pressure on the NHS and other services.

What to watch

The immediate test is the text of the report: whether it sets out numbers for fast-track eligibility, a timetable, and a costing. After that comes the government's response, and whether it treats the Timms recommendations as a package or picks from them. A state that concedes its assessment system is failing has to say what it will pay to fix it. The same Whitehall that asked for the review is also weighing cuts for the youngest claimants.
