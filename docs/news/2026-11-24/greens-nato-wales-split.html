Greens shift on Nato and nuclear weapons as Welsh members vote to split

In one sentence
Zack Polanski welcomed a thinktank report backing Nato and Dreadnought while party sources insist policy is unchanged, as Welsh Greens voted on splitting from the English party.

The Green Party has spent two days in which its two most basic questions, what it thinks about war and who it is, appeared to be up for revision. On Monday night its leader, Zack Polanski, welcomed a report that says the UK should stay in Nato and cannot, and perhaps should not, cancel the next generation of nuclear submarines. Days earlier, members of the Welsh Greens gathered in Bangor to vote on a motion to separate from the Green Party of England and Wales.

The two stories are separate, but they point at the same strain. A party that has grown quickly under Polanski is finding that its leader, its policy book and its devolved branches do not always agree.

One report, not a new policy

The document in question comes from Verdant, a thinktank described as independent but closely linked to the Greens. It is written by Sacha Deshmukh. According to coverage of the Guardian's original report, it argues that the UK should remain in Nato and push for reform of the alliance's structures with allies. It also says the government should rethink national security strategy around value for money, for example by buying weapons off the shelf rather than commissioning bespoke systems. It calls for more investment in climate adaptation.

The nuclear passage is the most striking. The report says Britain could not, and potentially should not, cancel the Dreadnought contract for the next generation of nuclear submarines. It adds that the UK should work more closely with France on the missiles those boats will carry. They are being developed for the US Trident missile Britain uses now.

Polanski's own endorsement was less specific than the report. "At its core, national security is about food on the table, warm homes and safe communities," he said, adding: "I welcome Verdant's call for a comprehensive security framework." His statement did not explicitly endorse the Nato or Dreadnought conclusions, though the headline framing across outlets did.

That distinction matters, because the party says nothing has changed. Party sources told the Guardian that its defence policies are unchanged since 2024. At that election the Greens called for the UK to sign the UN treaty banning nuclear weapons and to begin dismantling Trident immediately. The party's published policy is, in fact, more nuanced than its reputation. It commits to Nato membership while pursuing reform and a no-first-use nuclear policy. Anti-Nato sentiment has long run through the membership, but formal policy has not required withdrawal.

The awkwardness is Polanski's own record. He has said Britain should leave the alliance, arguing in an interview with the Guardian's Politics Weekly podcast: "Donald Trump has so much domination within Nato that I don't believe it's possible to reform Nato from within." In February he said he would honour Nato's Article 5 mutual defence commitment, a sign that his position had been shifting for months.

A party with a defence problem

The likeliest reading is electoral rather than ideological. Party sources describe the endorsement as part of an internal debate about reassuring a wider group of voters on security. A YouGov poll last year found only 17% of voters trusted the Greens on defence, the lowest of 18 issues tested.

The argument has already been aired inside the party. Some members have pushed it to drop its opposition to drones and anti-ballistic missiles, which caused a rift before this year's conference. The Verdant report is likely to prompt more of the same. Backing a thinktank's paper is a cheaper way to test the ground than rewriting a policy that only conference can change. It leaves the leader room to say he has not changed the rules, while signalling to wavering voters that he is willing to talk.

So the answer to the central question is, for now, that the Greens' formal stance has not moved. What has moved is the leader's willingness to be seen next to a document that says otherwise.

Wales weighs a break

The Welsh vote is a different kind of test. Welsh Green members were due to decide at the party's conference at Bangor University on 21 and 22 November on a motion that would commit them to seeking independence from the Green Party of England and Wales by 2034. According to Nation.Cymru, it needed two-thirds of members voting at conference to pass. The BBC has reported that members voted to split. The count, margin and the exact terms of any separation were not available in the sources checked for this article, and they should be confirmed before the details are treated as settled.

The motion, quoted by the New Statesman, says: "We must stop thinking of ourselves as the second party in this relationship… we are our own party."

The idea is not new. Welsh members voted in 2018 on whether to leave, and a turnout of about 20% rejected the split, with 65% of those voting against. The Scottish Greens separated from the English party in 1990. The Welsh leader, Anthony Slaughter, said in 2023 that an independent party was "not only inevitable but it's also desirable." Polanski himself was previously reported to have backed the idea of independence.

What has changed is the temperature. Phil Davies, the Welsh co-deputy leader, resigned in October after a "Zionism is Racism" motion passed at the English party's Brighton conference. "It is firmly my position that we must split from GPEW and stand on our own two feet as a Welsh party," he wrote. Slaughter, who missed that conference while recovering from cancer surgery, faces a possible leadership challenge from the Monmouthshire councillor Ian Chandler in internal elections next month.

What ties the threads together

Both stories are about who speaks for the Greens, and on what authority. Defence is reserved to Westminster, so a Welsh party would have little say over Trident or Nato. But the Welsh vote shows that the English party's conference decisions, and its leader's positions, now carry real costs for a devolved branch with its own politics and its own Senedd group.

The next markers are concrete. Welsh Green leadership elections take place next month, and the terms of any separation will determine whether 2034 is a firm date or an aspiration. On defence, the test is whether the Verdant report is taken up at a future conference, or whether the leadership quietly leaves the 2024 policy in place. Until a vote is held, the party's position and its leader's remain two different things.
