Victims' commissioner warns the state is failing to keep the public safe

In one sentence
Victims' Commissioner Claire Waxman says survey data from 6,000-plus victims shows a system failing to protect the public, citing court backlogs, under-reporting and strained probation.

England and Wales's Victims' Commissioner has told the government that the justice system is still failing the people it is meant to protect. The warning comes with a body of survey data behind it, which is what lifts it above the usual annual complaint.

Claire Waxman's latest review draws on a survey of more than 6,000 victims. It was reported on 23 November by the news site Brit Brief. The BBC's original coverage could not be retrieved, so the figures below come from that report and from the commissioner's own earlier publications. They should be checked against the full document.

What the survey found

The headline numbers describe a system that many victims never reach, and that others abandon. According to the reported findings:

  • 28% of victims did not report their crime to the police. The figure is 38% among Black victims and 45% among Asian victims.
  • 39% of those who experienced rape or sexual assault did not report it.
  • 47% of Crown Court cases were postponed. In 28% of cases the trial took place more than a year later than first scheduled.
  • A record 23,706 Crown Court cases had been outstanding for more than a year, almost 30% of the total.
  • Only 14% of victims knew their rights under the Victims' Code.
  • 46% were dissatisfied with police communication. Of those, 85% said they would not report a crime again.
  • Fewer than a quarter (24%) were referred to support services.

The last two figures matter most for the public-safety argument. A victim who will not report again is a crime that never enters the system, and an offender who is never supervised or stopped.

The safety argument

Waxman's warning rests on cases as well as statistics. The report cites Simon Levy, a serial sex offender who killed Carmenza Valencia-Trujillo and Sheryl Wilkins and raped a third woman. He was on bail and being monitored at the time. He received a whole-life order. In August Waxman called his case one of the most shocking examples of systemic failure in recent years. She said she was "gravely concerned that these failures and oversights will not be one-offs", and she asked the Lord Chancellor to commission an independent multi-agency review.

The report also revisits Zara Aleena, murdered in Ilford in June 2022 by Jordan McSweeney, nine days after his release from prison on licence.

Waxman's own words are blunt. Victims are "still being let down by a criminal justice system that too often treats them as an afterthought", she said, and "the cracks in the system are too deep to paper over". Katie Kempen, chief executive of Victim Support, called the findings "a damning indictment".

Pressure from early release

The report's forward-looking worry is capacity. Waxman has argued that early prisoner releases to ease overcrowding, combined with growing community supervision, will push more work onto police and probation services that are already stretched. She has said she is "unconvinced" those agencies have the capacity they need. The Chief Inspector of Probation, Martin Jones, has repeatedly raised concerns about workload, staffing shortages and risk management.

Government response

The victims minister, Alex Davies-Jones, said the government inherited a growing court backlog and prisons "almost full to the brim". Ministers say they have invested £550m in victims' services over three years, are acting on the Crown Court backlog, and will publish a new Victims' Code next year.

Waxman wants an "end-to-end" review of the victim's experience, from reporting to support after the legal outcome. She is due to meet Prime Minister Andy Burnham in the coming weeks, before he sets out a 10-year plan for government. She has argued that any credible plan must include a plan for justice, because court delays feed directly into prison pressures. Whether that plan commits to an independent review of cases like Levy's, or to funding for probation, is the next measurable test.
