Meta's Muse shopping agent reaches UK; San Francisco worries about an AI crash

In one sentence
Meta's Muse shopping agent reaches UK users before Christmas, and it raises unanswered liability questions as bubble worries grow around the AI boom.

Meta has begun rolling out Muse, an AI agent that can shop for people, to users in the UK, according to the Guardian. It arrives in the run-up to Christmas, the busiest gifting weeks of the retail year. Much about the rules for software that spends a customer's money is still unsettled.

The Guardian's report describes Muse as able to handle Christmas shopping. The rollout details are thin in what could be verified: no UK retailer list, pricing or date was confirmed in other coverage seen for this piece. Reports from early November, including one in the Times, said only that a launch was coming within weeks.

What Muse does

Meta announced Muse on 8 September in the US. The Next Web's account says the agent can read email, book travel, fill in forms, negotiate and pay with a user's card. It keeps working after the app is closed. Payments run through Stripe's Link, and the report says they carry purchase protections. Meta pairs Muse with a separate system, called Sentinel, that lets low-risk tasks through and asks for confirmation before consequential ones. Axios reported a free tier plus subscriptions at $20 and $100 a month. Meta says the product carries no ads.

That answers a question the UK launch raises: Muse is built to complete purchases, not just recommend products. Whether the UK version has the same powers is not confirmed.

The friction already visible

Some of the plumbing is not yet cooperating. Amazon blocked Muse from shopping on its site in September, citing unauthorised access and customer security. Resy has told customers not to point automated agents at its platform. The Telegraph reported, as relayed by the FourWeekMBA site, that five UK banks have moved to block AI agents from customer accounts. That second claim is secondhand and should be treated with care. If it holds, it matters: an agent that cannot pay is a recommender.

The liability question is the harder one. If an agent buys the wrong item, orders twice or acts without proper authority, it is unclear who bears the loss. The candidates are the shopper, the retailer, the card issuer and Meta. The UK's consumer-protection and payments rules were written for people clicking buttons, not software acting on loose instructions. The Christmas timing sharpens the point, since gift orders are deadline-driven and mistakes are costly to unwind.

A boom with nervous neighbours

Muse comes from a company at the centre of the AI investment surge, and that surge is drawing unease close to home. The BBC reports that bubble fears are now being voiced in San Francisco, where the boom is concentrated, and not only by investors. The BBC brief supplied no figures, and a full BBC text could not be retrieved, so the specifics are left to that report.

Wider warnings are easier to document. The Bank of England, the IMF and JP Morgan's Jamie Dimon have all cautioned about an AI bubble. Dimon was quoted saying a burst would be "really bad, and not just for people in AI" because it would drag down the rest of the economy. Others note a difference from the dotcom era: today's biggest spenders are large, profitable companies, not debt-funded telecoms.

What to watch

The two stories meet on risk. Consumers are being invited to hand spending authority to a product whose maker sits in a sector some think is overvalued. The first test is practical: whether UK banks and big retailers let Muse transact, and what Meta says about who pays when an order goes wrong. The second is regulatory, and no UK body has yet set out how agent-made purchases will be treated.
