Nestlé sees limits to reformulation; Unilever shifts ad spend from TV to influencers

In one sentence
Nestlé's CEO reportedly says reformulation cannot fix ultraprocessed food, raising the prospect of portfolio changes, while Unilever keeps moving ad money from TV to influencers.

Nestlé's chief executive is reported to have told investors that rewriting recipes can only do so much about ultraprocessed food. That is awkward for an industry that has made reformulation its main answer to regulators and health campaigners. If trimming sugar, salt and additives is not enough, the question moves from the recipe to the portfolio.

The original report is in the Wall Street Journal, and its text could not be checked for this article. The CEO's exact words, any figures and the specific regulatory measures it cites are therefore not reproduced here. What can be said is limited to the context around it.

The reformulation defence

Philipp Navratil took over as Nestlé's chief executive in September 2025, after Laurent Freixe left. By May 2026, trade press described him as pursuing a strategic overhaul built around fewer, clearer growth platforms, with an aim of restoring growth. FoodNavigator also reported that the group was dealing with what it called a reformulation crunch, as GLP-1 weight-loss drugs change what consumers buy.

Nestlé's reformulation work has mostly been about ingredients. Nestlé USA has reportedly removed certified artificial colours from its range ahead of schedule, and a strawberry Nesquik now takes its colour from beet juice powder. Nestlé has also published nutrition-profile data for its products for the first time, after pressure from the investor group ShareAction.

Those are changes to individual ingredients. Ultraprocessed-food rules, and the research behind them, target the degree of industrial processing, which a swap of colourant or a cut in sodium does not change. An academic critique published in Public Health Nutrition in 2017 made this argument. It said reformulation policies concentrate on a narrow set of nutrients and can lend legitimacy to continued consumption of ultraprocessed products. A chief executive conceding that limit would be saying publicly what critics have long argued.

What follows from it

If recipes cannot carry the defence, the options are structural: pruning categories that are hard to clean up, repositioning towards premium or health-oriented lines, or buying and selling businesses. Navratil's "fewer, clearer platforms" framing already points in that direction. Regulators are also still working out what an ultraprocessed-food definition would look like. A formal US definition was still awaited at last report, and critics warn it could disrupt large parts of the food system.

The specifics matter, and they are not yet confirmed. Which brands the CEO regards as beyond repair, and whether he attached any disposal plans or targets, would determine whether this is a shift in strategy or a change in tone.

Unilever moves the other lever

A separate Guardian UK report, also not verified against its text, says Unilever is shifting advertising money from television to influencers, with Channel 4 named as the broadcaster hit. No budget figure or time frame for the Channel 4 effect was available. The direction of travel is documented, though. In March 2025 Unilever's chief executive, Fernando Fernandez, said the share of its media spend going to social channels would rise from about 30% to 50%, and that the company would work with about 20 times as many influencers. The Drum reported in July 2025 that creator rates had risen as a result.

The two stories are not the same, but they have a common thread. Large consumer groups are reworking how they make and sell products at the same time. For broadcasters, the question is whether Unilever's decision is structural or particular to one advertiser. Unilever is also still spending an estimated $4bn or more on broad-reach channels, so television has not been dropped.

What to watch

The next test is whether Nestlé puts numbers behind the remark, through disposals, brand reviews or capital-allocation changes at its next results. Whether regulators adopt a processing-based definition of ultraprocessed food will also bear on how far reformulation can go as a defence.
