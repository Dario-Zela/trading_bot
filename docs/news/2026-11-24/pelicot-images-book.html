Gisèle Pelicot 'humiliated' after nude photos sent to media to promote a book

In one sentence
Gisèle Pelicot says a Paris publisher sent intimate photos of her to media to promote an essay, and she plans legal action; the publisher has apologised and halted the book's promotion.

Gisèle Pelicot, whose trial made her a symbol of the fight against sexual violence in France, says intimate photographs of her were sent to the press by a small Paris publisher to promote a book. She has said she will take legal action. The episode raises an old question in an awkward new form: who controls a survivor's image once a public trial has made her name known?

What happened

The book is Emprises, an essay by the philosopher and psychoanalyst Sabine Prokhoris, published by Tinbad. It includes a chapter on Ms Pelicot, and its back cover urges readers to "think freely" about coercive control. Prokhoris has been critical of the #MeToo movement.

French media reported that Tinbad sent media outlets intimate photographs taken by Ms Pelicot's former husband, Dominique Pelicot, as part of the promotion. Tinbad says no images of rape and no pornographic videos were circulated. I could not confirm from the sources I reached when the images went out or how the publisher obtained them. One report says Tinbad's head, Christelle Mercier, told Le Canard Enchaîné that she had not obtained Ms Pelicot's permission.

Ms Pelicot, 73, told BFM TV she was "deeply shocked" and felt "exposed and hurt once again". She added: "I refuse to let my privacy become an advertising tool." She said she would take legal action against both the author and the publisher.

Two complaints in one

Her lawyer, Christophe Bigot, separates the grievance into two parts. One is the sending of intimate images without her consent "as part of a purely commercial communication". The other is a book that, in his words, "calls into question her status as a victim". The first is a question of privacy and consent. The second is a dispute over argument, and the law treats the two very differently. Another lawyer, Stéphane Babonneau, is reported to have called the use of the images "despicable" in Libération. The reports I found do not explain how his role relates to Bigot's.

Tinbad has apologised to both women on its website. It called the incident an "unfortunate and inappropriate communication", has stopped promoting the book and will not reprint beyond the 280 copies already in circulation. Prokhoris has put "full responsibility" for the "inappropriate" campaign on her publisher.

A public name, a private body

The background explains why the case lands as it does. Dominique Pelicot drugged his wife and recruited dozens of men to rape her over about a decade. At the Avignon trial in autumn 2024, she waived her right to anonymity so the proceedings would be held in public. Her stated reasoning was that shame should change sides. Fifty-one men were tried. In December 2024 her former husband received the maximum 20 years.

That decision made her a public figure by choice, but it was a choice about the trial. It was not a licence for anyone to use the material. Her memoir, A Hymn to Life, came out in February, so she has already told her own story on her own terms. The Tinbad episode is the reverse: her image used by others, for someone else's sales.

Ms Pelicot has tested privacy law before. A separate case against Paris Match over photographs ended in a settlement. That case is a different matter from this one.

What to watch

Two things will show how far the damage is contained. The first is whether Ms Pelicot files against Tinbad and Prokhoris, and whether the courts treat the images as a privacy breach separate from the book's argument. The second is how editors and publishers handling trial-related or intimate images respond. Tinbad's quick retreat suggests the commercial case for such material is weak. It does not settle where the legal line sits.
