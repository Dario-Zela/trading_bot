US moves to blacklist Megaspeed as questions grow over Nvidia's political cover

The Megaspeed International logo at its office in Singapore's Ocean Financial Centre. — Taryn Ng / AFP via Al Jazeera
In one sentence
Commerce has put Megaspeed and six affiliates on the Entity List, extending US chip controls to firms further down the Nvidia supply chain while Trump's backing of Huang draws scrutiny.

Washington has put Megaspeed, the Singapore-based cloud firm at the centre of the Nvidia chip scandal, on its export blacklist. It has done so even though the company has not been found to have broken any export rule. The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) published the listing on Monday 23 November. The move shifts American enforcement away from the chipmaker and the first sale and towards the Asian operators further down the supply chain.

The listing

BIS added Megaspeed International and six linked firms to the Entity List. They are Swiftdata, which took over Alice Huang's ownership stake in late 2023, along with Megaspeed's Thai and Malaysian units, including Speedmatrix, and MSFusion. It also added Apex Enterprise Solutions, which Reuters describes as Alibaba's Singapore subsidiary. Al Jazeera counts six other firms and Reuters names seven in total, so the exact tally should be checked against the Federal Register notice. Commerce said the companies posed a "significant risk of these entities being or becoming involved in the diversion of advanced computing commodities". Al Jazeera quotes the filing as saying they were "acting contrary to the national security or foreign policy interests of the United States".

In practice, anyone shipping advanced US technology to these entities, Nvidia included, now needs an export licence. Megaspeed says it runs a "legitimate and lawful business". Alibaba calls the listing of Apex "erroneous" and says it will pursue legal options.

The company's history explains why it drew attention. Reuters reports that it was incorporated in 2015 as 7Road International and renamed in March 2023. It became an official Nvidia cloud partner in Asia-Pacific that November. US authorities opened an export-control investigation in 2024. Singapore began looking at possible false declarations on server purchases in February 2025. In June 2025 officials from both countries inspected its Singapore site, and Nvidia put Megaspeed on its internal "no sell" list. Alice Huang, the former chief executive, left in August 2025. Reuters says she is under Singaporean investigation only for possible offences connected to her role as chief executive. Reports alleging that she acted for Chinese intelligence have been denied by Beijing, which called them "groundless speculation".

The financial exposure is large. Reuters cites a prepayment of about $4.2bn by Apex to Megaspeed and other firms to rent computing capacity in Southeast Asia, and Speedmatrix assets of about 8.8bn ringgit ($2.14bn) at the end of 2024. The Financial Times has put Speedmatrix's purchases of Nvidia-based equipment at least $4.8bn. No source gives a shipment count of chips that reached China.

A listing that took more than a year

The listing came late. The Financial Times reported that the Pentagon and the State, Energy and Treasury departments had pushed for it. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said publicly that the decision rested with Commerce, which had not acted. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick's department had earlier declined to add Megaspeed and had gone more than a year without adding any company to the Entity List. Commerce rejected the charge that it had been passive on enforcement.

The Justice Department and Commerce have separately confirmed a criminal investigation into Megaspeed over suspected diversion of chips to China. Singapore's prime minister, Lawrence Wong, has said his country "does not want to be used and abused as a conduit" to circumvent US export controls.

Political cover

The timing has drawn attention. Jensen Huang returned as Nvidia's chief executive on the same day, hours before the listing. Al Jazeera reports that President Trump said he had personally pressed the board to reinstate him. Trump told CNBC he felt "very strongly" about Huang's role and played down national security concerns. Nvidia shares rose 31.5 percent on the news, according to Al Jazeera, and the S&P 500 closed up 4.7 percent.

The question for critics is whether presidential backing for one chipmaker sits easily beside an investigation touching that same company. Nvidia's board opened its own review on 8 November under the former federal prosecutor Joon Kim. Congressional committees have sought Huang's testimony, with a closed-door interview set for 1 December and a Senate Banking session proposed for 3 December. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, has spoken of "red flags". Reports on whether California has opened a formal inquiry conflict. I found no primary account of the US attorney general dodging questions on Nvidia. Todd Blanche is reported to have said only that the Justice Department had not concluded whether Alice Huang was a spy. That claim is best treated as unverified.

Nothing so far shows that Nvidia itself broke the rules. The company says it blocked Megaspeed in June 2025, well before the listing.

What the test is

Entity List designation is a blunt instrument. It cuts off supply and signals risk to other suppliers, banks and customers. It is not a finding of guilt. Its effect depends on whether suppliers, cloud partners and Asian regulators treat it as binding.

The immediate catalysts are Huang's testimony on 1 and 3 December, Lutnick's proposed appearance on 10 December, any court action by Alibaba over Apex, and the outcome of the Justice Department and Singapore investigations. How those play out will show whether US export controls can follow chips beyond the first sale. They will also show whether political favour for a champion company affects how hard that follow-through is.
