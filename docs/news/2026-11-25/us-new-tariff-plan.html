US prepares new tariff plan for trading partners, with no rates or timing yet

In one sentence
The BBC reports Washington is preparing a new tariff plan, but with no rates, countries or timing published, the July Section 301 duties of 10-12.5% remain the only firm figures.

Washington is reportedly preparing a new tariff plan aimed at its global trading partners. The reporting so far, a BBC Business item that this desk has not been able to read in full, gives no rates, no list of countries and no date. Until the plan is published, the story is mostly the uncertainty around it.

The BBC report is the only source for the new plan, and a search turned up no corroborating coverage from November. The details below are confirmed background from earlier in the year. They are not details of the new plan.

What is known, and what is not

What is established is the legal backdrop. According to coverage by Axios, PBS and others, the Supreme Court ruled in February that the administration's earlier sweeping global tariffs exceeded presidential authority. A stopgap 10% levy followed. In late July it was replaced by duties of 10% to 12.5% on 60 trading partners, imposed under Section 301 of the Trade Act. The stated justification was that those partners had failed to enforce bans on forced labour. Goods already subject to sector tariffs, such as steel and aluminium, were excluded. So were oil and gas, fertiliser and products that qualify for duty-free treatment under the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement.

The EU said at the time that the levies were in line with the commitments agreed in the EU-US Joint Statement. USTR Jamieson Greer framed the measure as a matter of enforcement parity: "The United States has had a forced labor import ban for nearly a century, and rigorously enforces it; it's well past time for our trading partners to do the same."

That is where the record stops. It does not say whether a new plan would amend the Section 301 duties, add to them or replace them. It does not say which authority it would rest on, and it does not say whether it would cover the same 60 partners or a different set. The BBC's wording, that the US is "preparing to unveil" a plan, implies an announcement is near. It does not say when.

Why the legal route matters

The February ruling is the main reason the mechanism matters as much as the rate. The July duties rest on a different statute from the one the court struck down, and commentators said at the time that this renewed the prospect of litigation. A new plan built on yet another authority, or one that stretches Section 301 further, would raise the same question. Exporters would have to price in the risk that duties are challenged and later unwound, and not just the rate.

The process is also slower than a presidential proclamation. Section 301 actions generally involve an investigation, a proposed list and a comment period. If the new plan follows that path, a published proposal would come well before any duty is collected. If it uses a faster tool, the gap between announcement and effect could be days.

Exposure for UK and EU exporters

For European exporters, the relevant benchmark is the 10% to 12.5% band already in place. The EU treated the July measures as consistent with its own deal with Washington. A plan that left that band unchanged would be a non-event for them. A plan that raised rates or removed the carve-outs would not. The UK's position is less clear from the sources available, because the reporting reviewed does not say how the UK was treated in July.

Equity markets face the same ambiguity. Earlier rounds of tariff news moved prices when rates and dates were specific, and not when they were rumoured. A plan with no numbers gives investors little to price. The sectors most likely to react first are those with heavy transatlantic export exposure: autos, luxury goods, machinery and pharmaceuticals. Reports on pharmaceuticals in July were themselves inconsistent, with one account of a 100% duty on generics from August 2028 and another of a cut to zero. That suggests the sector's treatment is unsettled.

What to watch

The first test is whether the BBC report is confirmed by the White House, the USTR or the Federal Register. A published notice would settle the rates, the list of countries and the legal basis in one go. If the plan arrives as a Section 301 proposal, the comment period will set the earliest date the duties could take effect. Until then, the only firm numbers are the July ones.
