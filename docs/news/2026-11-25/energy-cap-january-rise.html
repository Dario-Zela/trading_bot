Energy price cap confirmed for January, with a reported 26% rise in household bills

In one sentence
Ofgem's January cap is reported at £2,166 for a typical household, a 26% rise driven mainly by wholesale gas, testing the Bank of England's tolerance for energy-led inflation.

Britain's household energy price cap is reported to be rising by about 26% from 1 January, taking the annualised bill for a typical dual-fuel household from £1,723 to £2,166. The cap is set quarterly by Ofgem, and the January-to-March figure was due today. One caveat applies throughout. The Ofgem page itself could not be retrieved in time. The figure comes from secondary reports and from the consultancy forecast they match.

What is known, and what is not

The arithmetic is consistent across sources. Cornwall Insight, the consultancy whose forecasts the market follows, projected on 18 November a cap of £2,166, up £443 or 26% on the October-to-December level. That would be the highest since 2023. The calculation uses Ofgem's standard consumption values: 2,500kWh of electricity and 9,500kWh of gas a year, paid by direct debit.

Aggregator reports published today say Ofgem has confirmed £2,166, which works out at roughly £37 a month more. MoneySavingExpert's table puts the rise at 25.9%. Strictly, 26% is the change on the current quarter, £2,166 against £1,723, or 25.7%. The same reports also describe it as a year-on-year rise, a label that does not match the quarterly comparison.

Other forecasts bracketed the number. They ran from £1,999 (Cornwall Insight, 30 September) to £2,215 (British Gas, 9 November). One consultancy's earlier forecast of a 16% rise and a supplier-average estimate of 21% show how much the projection moved as autumn wholesale prices climbed. The official Ofgem figure should be checked before any number here is treated as final.

Wholesale gas, not levies

The cause matters more than the size, because it decides whether this is a one-off or a lasting inflation driver. Cornwall Insight attributes the rise largely to "high and volatile" wholesale prices, gas in particular, and links them to disruption from the Middle East conflict. Levies and policy costs are not named as the driver. The forecast also assumes the cut in VAT on domestic electricity, from 5% to 0%, stays in place. That cut has been extended to 30 June 2027.

The distinction has a practical side. The cap tracks wholesale costs with a lag, because Ofgem's calculation draws on prices over an observation window before each quarter. A gas-led increase can therefore reverse as quickly as it arrived if futures fall. A levy-led increase, like the 0.2% rise in January 2026 that Ofgem partly attributed to policy costs including the Sizewell C nuclear project, tends to stay in bills. The 4% rise in October and now a rise of about a quarter point to a market-driven pattern. The VAT extension to June 2027 also means a later withdrawal of support would push bills up again.

The inflation question

A 26% rise in a bill of this size is large for consumer prices. Household energy carries a weight in the CPI basket of a few percent, so a jump of this scale could add a noticeable fraction of a percentage point to headline inflation when it feeds through in January and February data. The exact effect depends on the Office for National Statistics weights and on the base from a year earlier, which this reporting did not check.

For the Bank of England the issue is less the first-round effect than the second-round one. Policymakers have long worried that repeated energy shocks shape what households and firms expect of inflation, and so what they ask for in pay settlements. A rise driven by wholesale gas is the kind the Monetary Policy Committee usually looks through, if it is judged temporary. That judgement is harder to make when the cause is geopolitical and the duration unknown.

The government faces a separate pressure. A £443 annual increase lands in the coldest quarter, when consumption is highest, and any support, whether targeted payments or further tax changes, would carry a fiscal cost on top of the VAT concession already running.

What to watch

The first marker is the Ofgem publication itself, which will settle the precise figure and the standing-charge split. The next is the January CPI release in February, which will show how much of the rise reaches the headline rate. After that come the forward gas curve, which will indicate whether the April-to-June cap, announced in late February, rises again or eases, and the Bank's next rate decision and its commentary on pay settlements. The 30 June expiry of the electricity VAT cut is a later date to watch.
