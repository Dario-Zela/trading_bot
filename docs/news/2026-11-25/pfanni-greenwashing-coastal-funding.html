CLIMATE · GREENWASHING AND COASTS · By S. Whitfield · 2026-11-25 · 3 min read

German court bans Unilever's 'sustainable' Pfanni claim; England gets £11m for coastal erosion

In one sentence
A German court reportedly found Unilever's 'sustainable' Pfanni claim misleading, while England gets a modest £11m for eroding coasts; details of both remain unverified.

A German court has ruled that Unilever misled consumers by describing its Pfanni potato products as "sustainable", according to a report in the trade title Just Food. The court, the date and the exact wording of the claim were not available to The Bot Tribune, and nor was the appeal status. Unilever has not been heard from in the material reviewed. Those details matter, and the account below rests on the report alone.

Pfanni is a long-established German brand of mass-market potato products, owned by Unilever. If the ruling is as reported, it shows that a generic green word on everyday packaged food can be struck down in court, rather than only drawing criticism from regulators or campaigners.

A pattern, not a one-off

The ruling fits a run of challenges to Unilever's food labelling. In the Netherlands, an advertising committee found in 2024 that a "sustainably grown peanuts" claim on Calvé peanut butter was insufficiently substantiated, as only about 95% of the peanuts met the standard. In 2025 the Dutch consumer group Consumentenbond said it had found a misleading sustainability claim on more than 247 of over 450 products from nine Unilever food brands. According to law firm Taylor Wessing, Unilever agreed with the Dutch regulator, the ACM, to drop most vague sustainability claims from packaging from 1 January 2026. Britain's Competition and Markets Authority has also examined Unilever's environmental claims. Unilever has denied that its claims are misleading.

The common thread is vagueness. A word such as "sustainable" invites the question of what is sustainable, compared with what, and on whose evidence. Courts and regulators have been asking companies to answer it on the label or not to make the claim.

The practical effect of one German ruling on one brand is limited. Its weight comes from adding to the others, and from the signal to consumer-goods firms that unsubstantiated wording carries legal risk.

England's coastal money

Separately, the Guardian reports that £11m of new government funding is going to coastal areas in England at risk of erosion. The report's recipient areas, allocation and timing could not be checked here. The figure is modest beside the scale of the problem. Earlier programmes give a sense of it: a £36m Coastal Transition Accelerator Programme was aimed at North Norfolk and East Riding of Yorkshire, which together hold 84% of the properties at risk from erosion in England over the next 20 years. In January 2026 the Environment Agency announced up to £30m for coastal adaptation pilots running from 2026 to 2029, with local areas expected to contribute 10%.

What ties them together

The two stories are only loosely linked, but both are about whether environmental language is matched by substance. A label has to be able to be defended, and a funding announcement has to change what happens to households on a crumbling cliff edge. The next tests are concrete. For Pfanni, they are the written judgment and whether Unilever appeals. For the coast, they are which communities receive the £11m and whether it pays for relocation and adaptation, rather than only for studies.
