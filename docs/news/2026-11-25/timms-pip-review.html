DISABILITY BENEFITS · By C. Tanaka · 2026-11-25 · 6 min read

Timms review proposes linking PIP to claimant costs, as IFS says welfare bill would rise

In one sentence
The Timms review wants PIP awards to follow genuine extra costs but sets no criteria; the IFS warns that changes within fixed spending would create losers as well as winners.

A year-long review of personal independence payment, the main benefit for disabled people in England and Wales, has recommended that awards follow claimants' genuine extra costs more closely. It has stopped short of saying who would gain or lose. The Timms review, published on 24 November, says the criteria will probably need a "rebalancing", and that some people would receive more and others less. Ministers now have to decide what that means, and the argument over whether it is a saving or a re-targeting of support has begun before any criteria exist.

Two points in the story as first reported do not hold up against the published material. The report contains no eligibility criteria and no claimant-loss figure. The Institute for Fiscal Studies has not, in the coverage reviewed, said the package would raise the welfare bill. Its position is subtler, and the gap between the headline and the evidence matters to the politics.

What the review actually proposes

The 138-page final report was written by Sir Stephen Timms, who is also the social security and disability minister, with co-chair Sharon Brennan. It calls PIP "a valued and important benefit" but says the system through which it is delivered "is no longer fit for purpose". The claims process, it says, is "complex, hard to navigate, burdensome".

The central design change is a new route through the system. A light-touch initial gateway would sit on top of two fast-track pathways, one for serious, well-evidenced conditions and one for end-of-life care. Everyone else would go through an impact assessment, followed by an optional conversation about local services. The review wants assessment to focus on participation rather than "what people cannot do", and awards to be "responsive to need".

The cost link sits inside that assessment. The report recommends an extra-costs element, using questions that do not require people to calculate costs or produce receipts. It also proposes a cash award reflecting both ongoing and occasional costs. This is the "link to claimant costs" of the headline. It would not turn PIP into a reimbursement scheme. Nor would it change the benefit's character: the review says PIP should stay non-means-tested, non-taxable and payable in and out of work, with cash first. It adds a voluntary "service exchange scheme", modelled on Motability, through which people could swap part of their cash award for services.

Other recommendations are administrative. They include fewer unnecessary reviews for people with lifelong or degenerative conditions, a simpler PIP2 form better suited to fluctuating conditions, a review of the 28-day hospitalisation rule and of mandatory reconsideration, and better assessor training. The report also says the Work Capability Assessment should be abolished only with robust safeguards against work-related requirements in Universal Credit.

Timms said eligibility criteria are not set out because those decisions belong to the elected government. The report asks that any new criteria get independent scrutiny, testing with disabled people and careful transitional protections.

The money

PIP is paid at between £30.30 and £194.60 a week. The latest official figures show a record 4.09 million people entitled to it in England and Wales, more than four times the number a decade ago. Spending is forecast at £32.1bn in 2026/27, rising to £41.5bn in 2030/31.

That trajectory is the backdrop to the review. It was launched after a Labour backbench rebellion over welfare cuts under Sir Keir Starmer, and the government of the time promised to delay changes to PIP criteria until the review reported. The terms of reference also require PIP spending to stay within the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecast. A review that cannot add to the forecast, and is meant to produce a more generous award for those with higher costs, leaves one arithmetic outcome: others must receive less.

The IFS has made essentially that point. It said linking PIP more closely to disability-related costs could tailor it better to need, but raised challenges in defining those costs and measuring them fairly. Eduin Latimer, a senior research economist there, said any reform that changes entitlements while holding spending fixed would create losers as well as winners. That is a statement about distribution, not about the size of the bill. The report's own text, as far as it could be read, carries no costing, and no IFS estimate of a rise in spending has been found.

Savings or re-targeting?

The Resolution Foundation supplied the number that makes the cost-cutting reading plausible. It found that about one in six working-age disability benefit recipients face no extra costs from their conditions. It estimated that £4.4bn could be saved if payments were stopped or reduced for them on the basis of reported limitations. Louise Murphy, a senior economist there, said the review's wording leaves the government flexibility to justify winners and losers in its White Paper.

The review's authors resist that reading. They rejected the Milburn review's suggestion that under-25s be barred from PIP in favour of a youth allowance, saying they did not want a two-tier system. Charities including Dimensions, Sense and Mencap welcomed fast-tracking and fewer reassessments but asked for assurances that no claimant would be left worse off.

The political camps are already visible. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said he "won't shy away from reforming the system" and reaffirmed his goal of reducing the welfare bill. Jon Trickett, a Labour rebel in 2025, said the review should not mean cuts or age-related reductions. Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, said it "tinkers around the edges of a failed system". Her complaint is that the report is too timid, which is the opposite of the claim that it is a hatchet job.

The distinction between the two framings is practical. If the extra-costs element is generous and the thresholds are set to protect today's recipients, spending rises, which the OBR constraint rules out. If it is set tightly, the same design saves money. The report's silence on thresholds leaves both possible.

What comes next

The government has accepted two recommendations: fewer reviews for people with lifelong or degenerative conditions, and fast-track payments without a full assessment for those with serious, well-evidenced conditions and those receiving end-of-life care. Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden will decide on the rest. A White Paper on welfare reform, which will also weigh the Milburn review, is expected early next year.

That document will be the first to contain actual criteria, thresholds and a costing. Only then can anyone count how many of the 4.09 million would gain or lose, and whether the IFS's warning about fixed budgets turns into a concrete figure. The figures for how many claimants could lose support, and how that splits between daily living and mobility, remain unpublished and should be treated as unverified until then.
