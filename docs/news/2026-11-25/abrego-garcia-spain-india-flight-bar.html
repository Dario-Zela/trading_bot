Ábrego García leaves US for Spain; Indian opposition figure barred from UK flight

In one sentence
Ábrego García has reportedly left the US for Spain, closing a case that tested executive power without a clear ruling; terms are unverified, as is an Indian figure's UK flight bar.

Kilmar Ábrego García, the Salvadoran man whose wrongful removal to a Salvadoran mega-prison became one of the defining immigration cases of Donald Trump's second term, has reportedly left the United States for Spain. If the departure holds, the case ends without the thing it was long thought to be heading towards: a clear judicial answer on how far the executive can go, and how far courts can make it undo what it has done.

The departure, its date and its terms could not be independently confirmed at the time of writing. The report comes from the BBC. Searches of other outlets turned up no corroboration, and the most recent coverage found described a different state of play: his release from immigration detention in December 2025 on a Maryland judge's order, and an administration that said it would appeal. Details of any agreement with Madrid, or with the US government, are therefore unknown.

How the case got here

Ábrego García, who had been living in Maryland, was deported to El Salvador in March 2025 and held at the CECOT prison. An immigration judge had earlier barred his removal there. The government conceded in court filings that the removal was an administrative error. On 10 April 2025 the Supreme Court unanimously ordered the administration to "facilitate" his return.

He came back in June 2025, but to face federal human-smuggling charges in Tennessee, not as a straightforward remedy. The government said that if convicted he would be sent back to El Salvador. Later it tried to send him to third countries, among them Uganda and Costa Rica. Costa Rica's own officials contradicted Washington about whether it would take him. Spain was floated only hypothetically; one advocate told NBC News that, without guarantees he could stay, a named destination would be "a way station on the way right back to El Salvador".

Why the exit matters

The legal question was never really about one man. It was whether the executive can remove someone it admits it should not have removed, then control the terms on which he returns. Courts ordered facilitation, and the government complied in a minimal, contested way. A voluntary departure to a third country, particularly one negotiated while charges and appeals were live, would let both sides claim an outcome without a ruling that sets a limit.

The significance therefore turns on terms still unseen. Did the criminal case end? Did the government concede anything on the original removal? Did he leave by choice, or under pressure? Each answer would point to a different reading, from a negotiated settlement to a removal by other means.

A second case of travel curbs

The BBC also reported that an Indian opposition figure, described as a "cockroach" leader, was barred from a UK-bound flight and said Delhi had "lost the plot". The name, date, airline and reason were not available. The "cockroach" label matches the Cockroach Janta Party, whose founder, Abhijeet Dipke, was detained at Delhi airport in October 2026 before a protest. The BBC report has not been matched to him, and nothing here should be read as saying it is him. Indian authorities have previously stopped travellers bound for London, including a UK-based NHS doctor halted at Mumbai in January over a police case about a Facebook post.

The two stories share one theme: the ability of states to decide who may cross a border, and how readily courts or foreign governments can contest it.

What to watch

The first test is documentary: a court filing in Maryland or Tennessee, or a statement from his lawyers or the Spanish government, would show whether the departure came with a settlement and what happened to the charges. For the Indian case, the airline's or the authorities' stated grounds for the bar will decide whether it is a routine legal restriction or a political one.
