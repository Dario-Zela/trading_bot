Ministers open consultation on the age of consent

In one sentence
The government has opened a consultation on the age of consent, which is 16 across the UK; the proposal's details are unconfirmed and no decision has been taken.

The government has opened a public consultation on the age of consent, asking for views on a legal threshold that has gone unchanged in Britain for more than two decades. The announcement was reported by the BBC. Only that headline and a one-line summary were available when this article was written. The consultation's exact question, scope, closing date and the department running it could not be confirmed, so this article does not guess at them. What is established is the legal background, and a consultation is not a decision.

What a consultation is, and is not

A UK government consultation invites the public, campaign groups, lawyers and professional bodies to respond to a set of questions, usually over several weeks or months. Ministers then publish a response, which may lead to legislation, to a narrower change, or to nothing. Opening one signals that the government wants evidence or political cover for looking at an issue. It does not commit ministers to a change, and the summary available gives no sign that any decision has been taken.

The age of consent is among the most settled provisions in the criminal law. A consultation that touches it is therefore likely to draw close attention, whatever direction the questions point. Which direction that is has not been verified.

The law as it stands

In England and Wales the age of consent is 16, set by the Sexual Offences Act 2003. That Act also contains offences that sit alongside the headline age. Sexual activity with a child under 13 is treated as a separate, more serious category in which consent is legally irrelevant. Separate rules cover adults in positions of trust, such as teachers, which apply to people under 18.

Scotland has its own statute. The Sexual Offences (Scotland) Act 2009 also sets the age of consent at 16. Northern Ireland legislates separately too, through the Sexual Offences (Northern Ireland) Order 2008. Its age of consent is also 16, not 17 as an early summary of this story suggested. That detail matters because the threshold is the same across the UK but is written into different laws in different legislatures.

The devolution point shapes any reform. Criminal law in Scotland and Northern Ireland is largely devolved, so a change announced at Westminster would not automatically apply across the whole UK. Whether this consultation covers one jurisdiction or several is one of the questions the available material does not answer.

What to watch

Three things will decide how significant this turns out to be. The first is the wording of the consultation itself: whether it asks open questions about the existing law or puts forward a specific proposal. The second is the closing date, which sets when a government response could appear. The third is how the devolved administrations in Edinburgh and Belfast respond, since any change affecting them would need their own legislative route or agreement.

Until the consultation document is published in full and read, the accurate summary is limited. The government has asked a question about a long-settled law, and the answer, if there is one, is still some way off.
