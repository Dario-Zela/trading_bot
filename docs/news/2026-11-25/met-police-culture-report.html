Report finds racism, misogyny and homophobia persist in the Met

In one sentence
A Casey follow-up led by Dr Gillian Fairfield finds the Met still institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic, and warns more scandals are "highly likely".

A review published on Wednesday has concluded that the Metropolitan Police is still institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic. The finding comes three years after Baroness Casey's audit said the same of the UK's largest force and prompted a programme of reform. The new assessment, led by Dr Gillian Fairfield, asks whether that programme has changed how the Met behaves or only how it records its intentions.

Its answer is mixed but unflattering. "Fundamental, systemic issues remain," the review says. Some things have improved. The Met's culture, in the review's reading, has not yet changed in a way that staff and the public can see.

What the review found

The review was published on 25 November 2026 as a progress check on the Casey report. The Met accepted all 10 of its recommendations, but the findings are harder to accept than the recommendations.

On racism, the review credits some progress. It also finds that the force still treats the problem "too often... as a reputational threat rather than an opportunity for learning". Senior leaders the team met avoided the word "racism" and spoke of "disproportionality" instead. Dr Fairfield's verdict was blunt: "If it cannot name it, it cannot face it."

On misogyny, specialist programmes and a push to recruit and promote more women have helped. Many women inside the force, and many members of the public, are still not convinced the change goes deep.

On homophobia, trust among LGBTQ+ communities remains low. Gay men reported some improvement. Lesbian and trans communities said they still routinely feel discriminated against.

The review also found:

  • Staff in public protection teams said they carry at least double the number of sexual and domestic abuse cases they should. The review judged that incompatible with proper service to victims.
  • Some officers and staff fear retaliation if they report misconduct.
  • The Met did not implement Casey's specific recommendations for the Parliamentary and Diplomatic Protection Command, where Wayne Couzens and David Carrick served. Its own changes there are not yet fully embedded.
  • Bereaved families gave critical accounts, and some said detectives gaslit them or lied about investigations.

The shadow of the closed unit

The warning with the most weight concerns units that operate out of public view. The review says further high-profile scandals are "highly likely" unless the Met deals with closed cultures in areas such as Royalty and Specialist Protection. "Until it does so," it adds, "we judge further scandals are highly likely."

That echoes the logic of the original Casey audit. It was commissioned by the Mayor of London after Sarah Everard's murder by a serving officer, Couzens, in 2021. It found that misconduct flourished where officers were isolated, poorly supervised and rarely challenged. The Fairfield review suggests those conditions have not been fully removed.

Reaction: the argument over a word

The response has turned in part on language. Sir Mark Rowley, the Met Commissioner, accepted that there are "systemic, deep issues" with racism, misogyny and homophobia. He stopped short of calling the force institutionally racist, though he has said he is not afraid to use the word racism. Some reports of the review say he pointed to about 1,800 officers and staff removed from the force. The review found that the reforms were "yet to be fully embedded and achieve the desired impact". He called the families' accounts "heartrending".

Sadiq Khan, the Mayor of London, took a harder line. He said he was "appalled" by the treatment of some victims. He argued that the Met cannot change its culture while institutional racism, misogyny and homophobia are denied, underplayed or tiptoed around. Shabana Mahmood, the Home Secretary, said further improvement was needed and welcomed the Commissioner's commitment, noting that the Met had accepted every recommendation.

The dispute over terminology has real stakes. A force that is willing to say "institutional" has accepted that the problem lies in its structures and incentives as well as in individual bad officers. The review's central criticism is that the Met's leadership still frames the problem the other way round.

What comes next

The Met has accepted the recommendations, so the question now is delivery. Two things will test it. One is whether the force changes the Parliamentary and Diplomatic Protection Command in the way Casey asked, and opens up the other closed units. The other is whether it cuts public protection caseloads to a level at which investigators can do the job.

Both need resources and sustained attention from the Home Office and the Mayor's office, which share oversight of the force. Neither the Home Secretary nor the Mayor has yet set out new levers or deadlines in response to the review. Until they do, the Met's progress will be judged on its own account of itself, which is what the Fairfield review says is the problem.
