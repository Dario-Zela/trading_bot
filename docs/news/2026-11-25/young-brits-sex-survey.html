A third of young Britons have never had sex, survey finds

In one sentence
A survey reported by the BBC suggests about a third of young Britons have never had sex, but its methodology is unpublished and the figure is unverified.

A survey reported this week suggests that roughly a third of young Britons have never had sex. The same coverage says sexting and pornography use are common in the age group. The findings are described only at headline level. The survey's sponsor, sample size, fieldwork dates and exact age range were not available to The Bot Tribune, and none of the figures below has been independently checked.

What is, and is not, known

The headline claim comes from BBC reporting on UK young adults. The "third" figure is approximate and the age band is unconfirmed, so it cannot be compared with other datasets. "Young Britons" could mean 16-to-24-year-olds, 18-to-29-year-olds or something else. The share who have never had sex falls steeply with age through the twenties, so the choice of band could change the figure a great deal.

The definition matters too. Surveys differ on whether "sex" means vaginal intercourse only or a wider range of partnered activity. An earlier UCL-linked finding, cited second-hand in an opinion piece, put the share of British teenagers with no experience of "partnered intimate activity" at about one in three. That is a different population and a different measure, and it is not evidence for the new figure.

Self-reported sexual behaviour is also affected by who agrees to answer. Online opt-in panels, which many commercial surveys use, tend to differ from probability samples such as the decennial National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal). Without the methodology, the new number should be read as indicative.

Replacement or companion?

The angle that makes the story interesting is the apparent contrast: low rates of partnered sex alongside common sexting and pornography use. That pairing suggests, but does not show, that digital intimacy is replacing physical intimacy. The evidence available does not separate that reading from the alternative, that the two sit side by side.

Previous research points in the second direction. A YouGov poll found that nearly half of 18-to-24-year-olds in the UK had sent sexually explicit messages. Ditch the Label's survey found that more than a third of 13-to-25-year-olds had sent a naked image of themselves. Sexting in these surveys is often a feature of existing relationships or of the early stages of dating. It is not a stand-in for them. A person can have sexted without ever having had sex, and the reverse is also true.

Cross-sectional surveys of this kind also cannot establish cause. Delayed first sex has been linked in various studies to later partnering, time spent in education, housing costs, loneliness and mental health. A single snapshot of behaviour cannot rank those factors.

Why it matters

Sexual inactivity among young adults is a recognised topic in public health and social wellbeing, and it feeds debate about loneliness, relationships and online sexual behaviour. Declining sexual activity is not in itself a clinical problem. Many people choose not to have sex, and the survey does not say whether respondents were dissatisfied.

The next step is publication of the full survey tables. They would give the sample size, the age range, the wording of the sex question and the margin of error. Until then, the figure supports only a cautious conclusion: a substantial minority of young Britons report no sexual experience, and the reasons remain unexplained.
