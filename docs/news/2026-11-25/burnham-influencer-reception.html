Burnham attacked over influencer reception at No 10

In one sentence
Burnham faces Tory attacks that he has made No 10 a 'joke' after an influencer's clip on its steps, while No 10 defends creator outreach but calls the video unacceptable.

Downing Street invited about 60 social media creators to a reception earlier this month. Now the Prime Minister is defending the event after one guest filmed a clip on the steps of No 10 that the government itself calls unacceptable. Conservatives say the episode shows Andy Burnham has turned the building into a "joke". The government says speaking to creators is a legitimate way to reach people who no longer read newspapers or watch the news.

The video

The row centres on Max Balegde, a creator with about 3.6 million TikTok followers. He filmed himself outside the door of No 10 miming to a viral meme audio whose lyrics are explicit and, in LBC's account, include a derogatory term for white people. He captioned it: "What an honour to be asked to introduce the Prime Minister." Balegde had also given a speech at the event. He has since deleted the clip from TikTok and Instagram.

A Downing Street spokesperson said the content was "clearly not acceptable" and that creators are responsible for what they post. Asked whether No 10 had requested its removal, the spokesperson would say only that it was right the creator took it down. Safeguarding Minister Satvir Kaur said she would not use such language and did not want to hear it anywhere. She still defended the wider approach: "I think it's right that the Prime Minister talks to lots of different people and lots of different media outlets."

A second piece of content has added to the criticism. Influencer Luke Hamnett posted a video of himself recreating Hugh Grant's dance from Love Actually inside No 10. It ends with him finding Burnham waiting to interview him, and in the interview he praised the Prime Minister's work. Burnham's spokesman said that content reflected how the public now receives information.

The attacks

Kemi Badenoch said it was "jarring to see a Labour PM turn it into a bit of a joke". She added that "being relatable doesn't mean treating the public as though they are children who need entertaining". Other reports quote her calling the video a "disgrace" and saying Burnham should apologise for using No 10 as "his personal studio". She argued that hostile states would watch the footage and judge Britain unserious.

The attacks came from several directions. Neil O'Brien described "cringey slop" and "taxpayer-funded" influencers. Ben Obese-Jecty wrote that the government had "become a circus". James Cleverly, the Conservative London mayoral candidate, mocked the dance and what he called a "softball interview". Nigel Farage called the use of the door "puerile" and "demeaning". He said he would not ban influencers from No 10 but would not let them behave as they did.

Beyond the punchline

The jokes matter less than three practical questions about the arrangements.

  • Filming. Attendees handed in personal devices and were given government phones and cameras, with footage sent to them afterwards. A government spokesperson called this "not abnormal" and declined to say whether the footage was reviewed for security.
  • Approval. A smaller group filmed with the Prime Minister, and one attendee said they had to run ideas past his team. That sits awkwardly with the government's line that creators alone answer for their posts.
  • Money. None of the accounts reviewed says whether anyone was paid or sponsored for the event. They link it to wider scrutiny of more than £2m of government spending on influencers since July 2024, a figure reported by the BBC that this article has not checked independently. Without clear disclosure, Conservatives can keep using the word "taxpayer-funded".

The strategy was not invented by Burnham. Keir Starmer set up a "new media unit", and the approach has intensified since July. Burnham opened his TikTok account only in May, but his videos have drawn millions of views, and the strategy has had real successes. Critics worry that creator-friendly formats reduce scrutiny of the Prime Minister. A softball interview in a hallway is a different thing from a select committee.

What to watch

Whether this travels beyond Westminster will depend on whether further clips emerge from the evening. It will also depend on whether No 10 publishes who was invited, on what terms, and at what cost. Any such disclosure could come through parliamentary questions or freedom of information requests. Until then the row remains a dispute over taste. A confirmed payment or approval arrangement would make it a dispute about rules.
