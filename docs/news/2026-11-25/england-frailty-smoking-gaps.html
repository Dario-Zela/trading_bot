HEALTH · INEQUALITIES · By Dr. P. Aaronson · 2026-11-25 · 3 min read

England has the widest frailty gap of 31 countries; middle-aged women least likely to quit smoking

In one sentence
Two reported studies suggest England's health gaps concentrate in midlife, but the underlying papers could not be verified; earlier research points the same way.

Two pieces of health research reported this week point at the same pattern. England's inequalities in health appear to open up in midlife and to fall hardest on particular groups. The headline findings are a widest-of-31-countries gap in frailty and a low smoking-quit rate among middle-aged women. Both come from a single outlet's report, the Guardian's. This desk has not been able to locate either underlying paper, so the claims below are attributed rather than confirmed.

What is claimed

The first study, as reported, compares frailty across 31 countries and finds that England has the largest gap between its more and less advantaged people. Frailty is a clinical term for reduced resilience: a person who is frail is more likely to fall, be hospitalised or lose independence after a minor illness. The measure used, the age band studied and the size of the gap have not been verified. A search for a 31-country comparison turned up no matching paper.

The second study, again as reported, finds that middle-aged women are the group least likely to stop smoking. The rates and age bands are unverified.

What earlier research shows

Published work on each theme is consistent with the reported direction. It does not confirm the new figures.

On frailty, a 2025 analysis of the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, summarised by the NIHR Health and Social Care Delivery Research unit, estimated that women in the wealthiest fifth of the population can expect around 11 more frailty-free years than women in the poorest fifth. For men at age 50 the gap was about 10 years. A separate 2022 study by Sinclair and colleagues put frailty prevalence among English adults aged 50 and over at 8.1%. It found a roughly fourfold variation between the most and least frail local areas, with higher rates in urban and coastal places.

On smoking, a 2025 paper in BMC Medicine by Jackson, Notley and Cox used the Smoking Toolkit Study, a monthly survey of a representative sample of adults in Great Britain. It found that smoking prevalence is high and quit success low among less advantaged women in midlife. Success rates were lower for women from less advantaged backgrounds, particularly between ages 45 and 60 (prevalence ratios of 0.70 to 0.73). The authors link the pattern to perimenopause and the menopausal transition. That study covers Great Britain, not England alone, and it describes disadvantaged women, not middle-aged women as a whole. It may not be the paper the Guardian reported.

Why the two may be linked

The common thread is circumstantial. Smoking is a well-established driver of frailty and of earlier disability, and the decades from 45 to 60 are when accumulated exposures begin to show. If disadvantaged women in midlife are both the least likely to quit and the most exposed to later frailty, the two findings would describe one process from two ends. The existing papers are suggestive on this point, but none of them tests the link directly. An association across separate datasets is not evidence of cause.

There are further reasons for caution. A "widest gap" ranking depends on how frailty is defined, which countries have comparable data and whether the gap is measured by income, education or wealth. Quit rates vary with the survey, the period and whether vaping is counted as quitting.

What to watch

The practical test is whether the papers themselves bear out the reported rankings. If they do, attention will turn to whether England's stop-smoking services and its planned tobacco restrictions reach women in their forties and fifties. Until the papers are identified and checked, the headline findings should be treated as reported, not established.
