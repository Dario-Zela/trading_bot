Tiny cars take off across Europe as cheap EVs force a price reckoning

In one sentence
Small, cheap EVs and microcars are gaining ground in Europe, forcing carmakers like Renault to show that sub-€20,000 electric cars can make money against Chinese rivals.

Europe's cheapest electric vehicles are no longer a curiosity at the edge of the market. Two kinds of small car now meet there: the quadricycle, which is legally not a car, and the sub-€20,000 city car, which is meant to be one. Both are testing whether volume manufacturers can make money at prices that Chinese entrants are already willing to meet.

Two routes to a cheap EV

The first route is regulatory. Citroën's Ami, on sale in Europe since 2020, is a two-seat electric quadricycle. That legal category sits below full cars, with lighter safety and performance requirements. It is why the Ami can be priced far below any conventional EV: it carries less car, and less compliance cost.

The second route is to build a proper small car at home for less. Renault's Twingo E-Tech Electric is aimed at a price below €20,000 and is built in Slovenia. Electra, a charging operator, lists a launch price from €19,500 in Evolution trim. The car has a 27.5 kWh LFP battery and a quoted range of up to 263 km, according to Slovenian press. The Slovenian importer expects to sell only 50 to 80 a month in that country. Renault began sales in Slovenia in April 2026, according to an Italian trade-agency report. Reported national incentives, such as a Slovenian grant that Electra says could cut the price to about €15,300 for eligible households, widen the gap further.

The Twingo matters beyond its own volumes. It tests whether a European carmaker can build a small EV in Europe at a price that leaves a margin. Small cars have long been low-margin products, and the cost of batteries and compliance does not scale down with the size of the car.

The Chinese factor

Chinese brands are expanding in the same space. BYD and Leapmotor are widening their small-EV ranges in Europe, and they compete on price directly with incumbents. One comparison puts the Twingo against BYD's Seagull, sold in some markets as the Dolphin Surf. Motor1 cites about €25,000 as the benchmark for the Renault 5, Fiat Grande Panda and Hyundai Inster, which puts the Twingo's sub-€20,000 target well below the current small-car cluster.

That is the squeeze. Chinese makers arrive with lower cost bases, and a small car is the segment where a price gap is easiest for buyers to see. European incumbents must either match them with cheaper platforms or cede the entry end of the market, where first-time EV buyers are won.

What to watch

The measure is margin, not volume. Investors in European auto stocks should look for how much Renault, Stellantis, which owns Citroën, and Volkswagen say about unit profitability on their cheapest EVs, and for registration data showing whether Chinese small cars gain share. The next test is Twingo registrations across larger markets than Slovenia. The follow-up is any Chinese response on pricing, including tariff-driven adjustments. If the cheap segment grows but earns little, it would add volume while thinning the margin of the carmakers that build it.
