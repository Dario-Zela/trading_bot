China-linked battery projects could win UK funding despite security warnings

In one sentence
Reports say UK-backed battery storage funding may flow to projects using Chinese kit despite security warnings, with Ofgem's final decisions due within weeks and no awards yet confirmed.

Britain is trying to build a domestic battery industry and to keep Chinese-made technology out of its power grid. A report published on 25 November suggests those two aims may be colliding. The Guardian reports that battery projects with Chinese links could receive UK public support despite security warnings. The story is at the 'could' stage. No award has been confirmed, and no sum has been published. This account relies on secondary summaries of that reporting and on the Guardian's own findings as relayed by them, so the details below should be read with that caveat.

What is on the table

The money in question appears to come through Ofgem, the energy regulator. Its programme backs long-duration energy storage: facilities that can soak up renewable power and return it to the grid for at least eight hours. A provisional shortlist was published in June. Of the 16 shortlisted projects, 11 use lithium-ion batteries. Final funding decisions are expected in the coming weeks.

Two developers account for seven of those lithium-ion proposals. Field Energy has five shortlisted projects and has mostly bought batteries from Envision Energy, a Chinese company that a group of US senators has linked to the Chinese Communist Party. Statera Energy has three, one pumped-hydro and two lithium-ion. It has previously bought systems from Envision and from Sungrow, another Chinese manufacturer.

Both developers say security is a priority and that their sites comply with national security guidance. Amit Gudka, Field's chief executive, said he hoped the company could help establish a UK battery manufacturing sector.

Where the security advice bites

The complaint from critics is about how the scheme is judged. Ofgem's assessment is heavily weighted towards the cost of each project. It reviews proposed IT and security arrangements only so far as they might threaten delivery dates in the early 2030s. Its criteria do not explicitly address the risk of cyberattack across a project's operating life. Developers must follow the government's general cybersecurity guidance, and that is the main security requirement.

Industry figures argue this gives too little weight to the security and economic case for UK-made storage over cheaper Chinese imports. Invinity Energy Systems, a British maker of vanadium flow batteries with one project on the list, may reconsider its plans to manufacture in the UK. The company has previously threatened to pull out after losing out to Chinese battery makers in a government support scheme.

The security concern is not abstract. A German cybersecurity firm said it gained access to hundreds of thousands of European solar installations during an 'ethical hack' aimed at Sungrow. Michael Collins, a former deputy head of national security strategy at the Cabinet Office, has warned that Europe is 'sleepwalking' into economic and national security risks from its reliance on Chinese clean-energy equipment.

The government has also admitted a gap in its own knowledge. Energy minister Michael Shanks has said ministers have no detailed assessment of how much of the UK's battery capacity is owned or financed by China.

Official response

An Ofgem spokesperson said proposals were assessed under the legal and policy framework set by government and Parliament, and that consultation responses were being reviewed before final decisions. A government spokesperson said protecting the energy system was a priority, that investment in the sector remained subject to 'rigorous national security scrutiny', and that ministers were working to build secure, resilient battery supply chains.

An old tension

The dilemma is not new. The government has approved Chinese-linked battery investments before. One was an 80-megawatt storage project backed by a joint venture that included KX Power, a UK-registered asset manager with Chinese investor ties, after a national security review. The AESC gigafactory in Sunderland, a roughly £1bn plant backed by National Wealth Fund and UK Export Finance guarantees, is run by a company partly owned by Envision since 2019. Each of these cases was cleared. That suggests security review in Britain works case by case rather than as a bar on Chinese capital or equipment.

What to watch

The decisive moment is Ofgem's final decision, due within weeks. Three things will show whether security guidance binds industrial policy. The first is whether the awards go to the Chinese-linked lithium-ion proposals. The second is whether Ofgem or ministers add security criteria before they do. The third is whether Invinity and other UK manufacturers follow through on any retreat. The sums, and the exact content of the security advice that the reporting says is being overridden, remain unconfirmed.
