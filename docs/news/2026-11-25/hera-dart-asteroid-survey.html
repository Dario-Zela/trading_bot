ESA's Hera arrives to survey the crater left by DART's asteroid strike

Artist's impression of ESA's Hera spacecraft in orbit around the Didymos system — ESA / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
ESA's Hera has reached the Didymos asteroid pair to measure what NASA's 2022 DART impact did to Dimorphos, which will show how far kinetic impactors can be trusted for planetary defence.

A European spacecraft has reached the pair of asteroids that NASA hit on purpose four years ago. It is there to find out what the hit did. ESA's Hera, which is about the size of a small car, is now flying around the Didymos system. It sits roughly 25 to 30 kilometres from the two rocks and about 196.5 million km from Earth. It is the first probe to return to a small asteroid after a deliberate high-speed impact.

The word "return" needs a qualification. Hera is making its first visit. NASA's DART spacecraft was destroyed when it struck the small asteroid Dimorphos, so nothing was left to come back.

The experiment

On 26 September 2022 the 580 kg DART craft hit Dimorphos at about 6.1 km/s. Dimorphos is a moonlet roughly 151 metres across. It orbits the larger Didymos, which is about 730 metres wide. Neither asteroid posed any threat to Earth. The aim was to test whether a spacecraft can shift an asteroid's path, which would matter if one ever did.

The result was clear. Ground-based telescopes measured Dimorphos's orbit around Didymos and found it had shortened by about 33 minutes. The first announcement put the figure at 32. The refined analysis gives 33.0 minutes, plus or minus 1.0. That equates to a change in orbital speed of roughly 2.7 millimetres per second.

The more interesting number is the momentum. The impact threw off a large amount of rock and dust, and the escaping debris acted like a rocket exhaust, pushing the asteroid further. Researchers express this as a "beta" factor, where 1 means only DART's own momentum was transferred. A 2023 Nature paper put beta at about 3.6, assuming both bodies have a density of 2,400 kg per cubic metre. The error bars matter: other analyses allow a range from about 2.2 to 4.9, depending on what Dimorphos is made of inside. Either way, the debris contributed more push than DART did.

That is where Hera comes in. Telescopes could see the orbit change, but not why it was so large. The answer depends on things only a nearby spacecraft can measure, such as Dimorphos's mass, its internal structure and the shape of the crater.

Slow flying

Hera launched on 7 October 2024. It made a gravity-assist pass of Mars on 12 March 2025, then spent most of 2026 on a long trajectory. Braking burns in October slowed it for the approach. On 18 October its camera first split the two asteroids into separate points of light. ESA says it arrived about a month ahead of the original schedule.

The flying is deliberately gentle. Didymos's gravity is about 40,000 times weaker than Earth's, and Dimorphos's about 200,000 times weaker. Hera moves in what ESA calls "rectangular orbits", firing its thrusters on a cycle of three days, then four, to turn its path by 90 degrees. Its speed relative to the pair is about 12 centimetres per second, which ESA likens to a quickly crawling insect.

The mission has five planned phases. The first is early characterisation, which is under way and lasts about six weeks. Next come the release of two CubeSats, then detailed characterisation, close observation and an experimental phase. According to ESA, the detailed phase starts next month. It uses bowtie-shaped arcs that begin about 20 km out.

"Our aim right now is to gather the big-picture findings of relevance to supporting the mission's planetary defence goals," said Michael Kueppers, the mission's scientist at ESA.

What the survey has to settle

Hera's instruments include two framing cameras, a hyperspectral imager, a laser altimeter accurate to about half a metre, and a thermal infrared camera supplied by Japan's space agency JAXA. Radio tracking will give the masses and gravity fields of both bodies.

Two 12 kg CubeSats will do the close work. Juventas carries a low-frequency radar and a gravimeter to probe Dimorphos's interior. Milani carries a spectrometer to read surface composition and detect dust.

The central question is whether Dimorphos is a loose pile of rubble or something more cohesive. That would explain why the deflection was larger than many pre-impact models predicted. ESA's team says those models "suggested many different ways the asteroid body might react". Hera will show which, if any, was right. It will also show whether DART left a clean crater or reshaped the whole body.

The answer limits how far the technique can be trusted. A kinetic impactor is simple and uses existing rockets, but its effect depends on the target. If a similar rubble pile gave a beta of 3.6, an engineer can plan around it. If the real figure turns out to be nearer the low end of the range, a future mission would need more warning time, a heavier craft or several impacts.

Hera's controllers in Darmstadt, Germany, talk to it for about eight hours a day through ESA's deep-space antennas. The next data point is the move into detailed characterisation in December. That is when close-up views of Dimorphos and its impact site should start to arrive. Hera will later attempt a landing, but the CubeSat deployment comes first.
