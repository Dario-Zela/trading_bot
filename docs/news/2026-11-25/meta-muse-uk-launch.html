Meta launches Muse personal AI agent in the UK with retail partners

In one sentence
Meta's Muse AI agent went live in the UK on 24 November with M&S and easyJet among its partners, as the ICO, CMA and FCA issued cautions.

Meta has put Muse, its personal AI agent, on sale in the UK. The agent books, buys and organises on a user's behalf. It arrived on 24 November, in time for Christmas shopping, and three UK regulators issued guidance to consumers and firms on the same day.

The warning is not a ban or an enforcement action. It is advice. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) and the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) have all said, in different ways, that software which acts on a person's behalf can go wrong, and that the person is left to manage the risk.

What launched

Muse is pitched as a general assistant for tasks such as arranging meetings and shopping. Reports of the launch name Marks & Spencer and easyJet among the new partners. The coverage describes them as part of a larger group, so the full list is unconfirmed. This article could not verify the retailer roles independently, and the details of what Muse does with each partner have not been published in the sources reviewed.

The product is not new elsewhere. Muse launched in the US and Canada in September and has since expanded to Japan, Singapore and Mexico. Retail Gazette reported earlier in November that it has more than 11 million users, with shopping its most popular function. US partners include Walmart, Best Buy, Gap and Sephora. Amazon blocked Muse from shopping on its site in September, citing customer security and unauthorised access.

The UK version is narrower than the American one. It cannot access a user's bank accounts or give personalised financial advice, according to reports of the launch.

What the regulators said

The ICO told users to be careful about what they share, and said it will monitor the rollout. Meta has agreed to a voluntary monitoring and reporting scheme with the regulator. The ICO and CMA advice is practical:

  • agents can make mistakes, and autonomy can amplify them;
  • users should check how much the agent can do without asking;
  • users should check what data it can reach and what it stores;
  • users should start with limited access and avoid sharing personal details about themselves or other people.

ICO chief executive Paul Arnold said that "privacy must be built into consumer-facing agentic products from the outset."

The FCA's message was aimed at firms, not shoppers. It said companies must work out whether they are offering regulated financial services, and that calling yourself a technology provider does not exempt you from the rules on financial promotions, advice or payment services. The FCA named no companies. The timing makes the target easy to guess, though the regulator did not draw the line itself.

Meta said: "We've engaged constructively with the appropriate regulatory authorities and will continue to do so."

What to watch

Two things will show whether this is a footnote or a turning point. The first is the ICO's monitoring scheme: whether it produces published findings, and what they say about errors and data handling. The second is the financial line. Muse can shop, and shopping ends in a payment. If the agent starts to touch payments in the UK, the FCA's warning becomes a live question. The ICO already has a call for evidence open on agentic AI, and its responses will show how far firms and the regulator disagree on who is responsible when an agent makes the wrong purchase.

The launch was reported from syndicated copies of The Independent's coverage and trade reports, so the partner list and scope should be checked against Meta's own announcement.
