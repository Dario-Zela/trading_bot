Russia's own figures imply almost 500,000 deaths in the Ukraine war

In one sentence
Leaked Rosstat records, unverified but widely judged authentic, imply at least 450,000 Russian war dead to August 2026, and perhaps 550,000 once late registrations are counted.

Russia has never published how many of its soldiers have died in Ukraine. The last official figure, 5,937 dead, came in September 2022 from the then defence minister, Sergei Shoigu. Independent counts since then have had to work around that silence. A leaked set of records from Russia's state statistics agency, Rosstat, now suggests the toll is close to half a million. That is the number behind the headline. It is not a published total, and it is not an official release.

What the figure is, and what it is not

The records emerged in early November. Two hacking groups, the Belarusian Cyber Partisans and Ukraine's Silent Crow, said on 1 November that they had breached Rosstat's network and passed the files to news organisations. Rosstat acknowledged cyberattacks between 29 October and 1 November. It said it had found no evidence that its databases were compromised or that protected data had leaked.

The data has not been independently verified, and the claim of an official source rests on that point. Analysts who have worked with it are confident, though. Janis Kluge, a Russia specialist, wrote that "there is no longer any doubt that the leaked Rosstat database is authentic". He called the idea that it was doctored to inflate the toll "highly unlikely".

The arithmetic, as reported by Al Jazeera and the Financial Times, runs as follows:

  • About 410,000 deaths are listed directly as war-related.
  • A further 42,000 are fighting-age men whose deaths were confirmed in court, though their bodies were never recovered.
  • The FT identified about 12,000 more likely military deaths among men who died at or near the front.

That gives at least 450,000 up to August 2026. The FT puts the figure nearer 550,000 once late registrations are counted. The "almost 500,000" in the headline sits between those two figures, and the BBC's wording was not available to this writer. The source therefore matters as much as the number. This is Russia's own bureaucracy recording its own dead. It is not Moscow choosing to disclose them.

Why registration lag matters

The figures are almost certainly incomplete. Deaths in Russia can take months or years to register, particularly when a body is missing and a court must first declare a soldier dead. Any count of this kind therefore rises for a long time after the period it covers. The Institute for the Study of War has said the figures probably understate Russia's real losses because of these reporting limits.

The leak also does not settle the question of definitions. It covers Russians, mostly soldiers. The FT counted 4,141 probable foreign soldiers with foreign citizenship. Bloomberg, using a wider test that includes people born outside Russia, counted 11,335. Ukrainian officials say about 14,000 North Korean troops were sent to Kursk. They do not appear in the data. Comparing the Rosstat total with other tolls is therefore not like-for-like, particularly with estimates that include wounded, missing or allied forces.

How it compares with the independent counts

The leak lands on top of years of painstaking outside work. Mediazona, Meduza and BBC Russian have built a list of named dead from obituaries, local news and graveyard imagery. They also use Russia's registry of inheritance cases, which records when property passes after a death. Their method converts those filings into estimated deaths using a coefficient calibrated on career officers, the most stable group on the list.

Their figures have climbed with each update:

  • About 47,000 in July 2023.
  • About 120,000 in July 2024.
  • About 220,000 in August 2025.
  • 352,000 through the end of 2025, in their May 2026 estimate.
  • A named list of more than 150,000 verified names by August 2026, a floor rather than a total.

Those figures are not directly comparable with the Rosstat total, which runs to August 2026 rather than December 2025. Even so, the leak is broadly in line with the independent estimates. The team's trajectory, extended by the pace of recent fighting, points to the same order of magnitude. Western governments have said similar things. The head of GCHQ said in May that nearly half a million Russian soldiers had been killed, and British defence intelligence has put the figure around 500,000 within a wider estimate of 1.5 million casualties. Those estimates come from intelligence assessments, not from Russian data, which makes the convergence more notable.

The leak also adds detail the outside counts could only approximate. The FT reported about 120,000 deaths with a recorded location in the Avdiivka and Pokrovsk area and 48,000 around Bakhmut. The average age of the dead rose from 34 in the war's first year to 40 in 2025. Men aged 35 to 44 were the largest group, at 158,000. Poorer and minority regions were hit hard: Zabaykalsky Krai, home to many Buryats, recorded about 12,300 deaths.

What it means

The numbers are enormous but not demographically ruinous. Kluge noted that the dead amount to about half a percent of Russia's working-age population. His point was that the effect is political and not arithmetic: "You cannot say Russia is winning this war if it is losing population at this rate." The ISW argues that Russian casualties have outpaced recruitment since July, which would mean the manpower strain is already visible.

Three things will show whether the figure holds. Rosstat or the Kremlin may confirm, deny or dispute the files. That has not happened so far, beyond the agency's statement on the cyberattacks. Mediazona and its partners are likely to reconcile their inheritance-based model against the leaked records, which would be the first direct test of that method. And late registrations will keep adding names. If the FT's estimate of about 550,000 is right, the toll up to August will eventually be recorded well above the figure now circulating.
