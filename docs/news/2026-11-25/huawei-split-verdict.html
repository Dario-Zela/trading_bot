Huawei found guilty of stealing trade secrets, acquitted of racketeering

In one sentence
A US jury convicted Huawei on trade-secret theft but cleared it of racketeering, according to the BBC, limiting the broadest charge while giving prosecutors an IP win.

A federal jury in Brooklyn has delivered a split verdict in the long-running US prosecution of Huawei. According to the BBC, it convicted the Chinese telecoms group of stealing trade secrets but acquitted it of racketeering. The outcome gives the Justice Department a conviction against one of China's best-known technology companies. It also withholds the broadest label prosecutors had sought, that of an organisation run as a criminal enterprise.

This article relies on the BBC's account of the verdict. Counts, the exact charges on which the jury ruled, and any sentencing date had not been independently confirmed from the court record at the time of writing.

A case nearly a decade in the making

The prosecution grew out of an indictment unsealed in 2020 in the Eastern District of New York. It accused Huawei and affiliates of racketeering conspiracy, conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud conspiracy and obstruction. The bank-fraud and obstruction allegations were tied to claims that the company misled financial institutions about its dealings in Iran through a affiliate, Skycom, in breach of US sanctions. The trial opened in September 2026. Reporting indicates that the jury began deliberating on 18 November, with 12 counts in play against Huawei and three affiliated companies.

The trade-secret strand centres on robotics-testing technology taken from T-Mobile US. That background comes from earlier civil and criminal matters, not from this verdict. Separately, a Texas jury found in 2019 that Huawei had misappropriated secrets from the startup CNEX, but it awarded no damages. That was a civil case, but it shows that courts in the US have already heard similar allegations about Huawei's conduct.

What the split tells us

Racketeering law, known as RICO, was written to reach organised crime. Applied to a corporation, it asserts that the company itself functioned as a vehicle for a pattern of crime, not that individual employees broke rules. That is a high bar. It requires jurors to accept a single connecting story across years of conduct and several continents.

A trade-secret conviction asks for less. Jurors can look at specific acts, such as who obtained particular information, how, and for what use, and decide whether those acts crossed the line. The verdict, as reported, suggests the jury was willing to find that on the narrower question and unwilling to endorse the larger theory. That is an inference from the pattern of outcomes, not from any juror statement, and the reasoning will not be public unless jurors choose to speak.

The defence had argued, among other things, that some of the material described as secret was publicly available. A jury can accept that argument for some counts and not others. A split result does not need a single explanation.

What follows

Several things now matter. The first is sentencing. A corporation cannot be jailed, so penalties would come in fines, probation conditions or restrictions, and the court's schedule will show how large a consequence this is. The second is appeal. Huawei has contested the case throughout and can be expected to challenge the conviction, and prosecutors will have to decide whether to challenge the acquittal's practical effect on the wider narrative. Acquittals themselves cannot normally be retried.

The third is the policy backdrop. Washington has used export controls, entity listings and criminal cases in parallel against Chinese technology firms. A conviction on IP theft supports the argument that Huawei's conduct warrants those restrictions, while the racketeering acquittal gives the company a line to use in Beijing and in third-country markets. Watch for the sentencing date and for any filing on post-trial motions, which will show how far each side intends to take the dispute.
