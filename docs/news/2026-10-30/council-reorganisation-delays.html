Council overhaul delayed in nine of 14 proposed English areas

In one sentence
Ministers have withdrawn decisions on nine of 14 proposed English council reorganisations, pushing back a flagship devolution reform and prolonging uncertainty for councils.

The government's plan to replace England's two-tier councils with single unitary authorities has slipped again. Ministers have withdrawn earlier decisions on nine of the 14 areas covered by the latest round of proposals and will revisit them. The effect is to push back one of the government's flagship devolution reforms and leave the local-government map unsettled for longer.

The figures below come from a syndicated report dated 29 October. I could not retrieve the BBC original, so exact wording and some details are unconfirmed.

What has changed

According to that report, five areas will proceed after a further three weeks of consultation: East Sussex, Gloucestershire, Oxfordshire, Warwickshire and Worcestershire.

The other nine are Derbyshire, Devon, Hertfordshire, Kent, Lancashire, Leicestershire, Lincolnshire, Nottinghamshire and Staffordshire. Ministers have withdrawn the decisions they took on these in July. The stated reason is that they are seeking "legal clarity". Four other areas, Essex, Hampshire, Norfolk and Suffolk, had their plans withdrawn in September and remain under review.

Local Government Minister Jim McMahon is reported to have told MPs that the review found the original decision-making "robust", even as the decisions were pulled back. That leaves a gap between the official line and the practical result, which is delay. Shadow local government secretary Katie Lam called the handling "a farce". The Local Government Association's chair, Eamonn O'Brien, asked for urgent clarity on costs and next steps and for a reset in relations between government and councils.

The slippage is broad, not confined to one awkward county. Counting the September withdrawals, the pause now covers a large part of the country.

A longer timetable

The reported new schedule is slower. Local elections in May 2027 are to go ahead on existing boundaries. Shadow council elections, previously planned for 2027, move to May 2028. Shadow authorities would then run for a year before taking over from the councils they replace.

Mr McMahon is reported to have promised an update on another set of areas by Christmas, with an outcome for every area by early 2027. Cambridgeshire and West Sussex are among those still awaiting decisions.

Reorganisation has been tangled up with the election calendar before. Earlier this year the government postponed elections in dozens of council areas, then abandoned the plan for 30 of them. Each change of course adds uncertainty for councils trying to plan budgets, staffing and service contracts around bodies that may or may not exist in two years.

Why it matters

Merging councils is a precondition for the devolved powers the government wants to hand to regional mayors. Longer timetables mean those powers arrive later. Meanwhile, councils must keep running social care, planning and waste services under an unresolved structure.

The story's reach is mostly domestic. For residents, the question is when their council will change and whether the savings promised for larger authorities materialise.

The next markers are the promised update by Christmas and the early-2027 outcomes for each area. The May 2027 elections will show how voters respond to boundaries that are meant to be temporary.
