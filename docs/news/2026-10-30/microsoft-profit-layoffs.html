Microsoft profits soar days after announcing thousands of layoffs

In one sentence
Microsoft reported a $36.6bn quarterly profit days after offering buyouts to about 60,000 staff, suggesting the cuts are about funding AI spending, not financial strain.

Microsoft said this week that its profit for the three months to the end of September rose to more than $36.6bn (£27.5bn). The result came two days after the company said it would offer buyouts to about 60,000 workers. The two announcements sit uncomfortably side by side. They also fit a pattern that is becoming familiar at the biggest technology companies: cutting people while spending heavily on artificial intelligence.

One point on terms first. The headline calls these layoffs, and the cuts are widely reported that way. Microsoft's own offer, as described in the BBC's account, is voluntary buyouts for eligible staff in Canada, India, the UK and the US. The company says the aim is to adapt to "changing needs". Departing workers would get financial support and career-planning help. How many people take the offer, and whether compulsory cuts follow, has not been disclosed.

The numbers

The scale of the offer is large. Microsoft employs more than 220,000 people worldwide, so 60,000 is more than a quarter of the workforce. That is the group eligible to be asked, not the number who will go.

The results are strong. Revenue for the quarter was $92.3bn, up 19% on a year earlier, led by cloud computing. Microsoft Cloud, the enterprise services unit, grew revenue by 30%. Shares rose almost 2.9% in after-hours trading in New York on Wednesday.

Chief executive Satya Nadella said AI has produced "real-world impact across every industry." The company also listed "substantial investments" in AI among the financial risks it faces.

Margin management, not distress

A company in trouble does not usually report a 19% rise in revenue. The timing points to a different reading: the buyouts are about the shape of Microsoft's costs, not its survival.

The logic runs through the cost of AI. Building data centres and buying chips is very expensive, and the money has to come from somewhere. Amazon, Meta and Alphabet are together expected to spend more than $700bn this year on AI chips and infrastructure. Payroll is one of the few large lines a company can shrink on purpose. Cutting it while revenue grows lets profit margins hold even as capital spending climbs.

This is an inference, not something Microsoft has said. The company's stated reason is the vaguer "changing needs". But the evidence points one way: strong results, a flagged risk from AI investment, and a voluntary offer sized to shift a large share of headcount.

What the market is asking

Investors are not rewarding AI spending automatically. The same BBC account notes that Alphabet's shares fell 9.75% after its quarterly net profit dropped 25% from a year earlier, with the company saying it would keep spending heavily on AI. Microsoft, by contrast, paired its spending with growth in cloud revenue and a move to cut costs elsewhere, and its shares rose.

That contrast is the useful signal. Markets appear to be asking whether the AI bills are being matched by revenue and discipline. Microsoft's cloud growth is the revenue half of the answer. The buyout offer is the discipline half.

For workers, the reading is less comfortable. If record profits and large job cuts keep arriving together, a good quarter offers no protection for a job. For regulators, a company reducing staff by choice while earning more raises different questions from one shedding jobs in a downturn, including how departures are handled and what is owed to those who leave.

What to watch

The first test is take-up: how many of the eligible 60,000 accept, and whether Microsoft says in later filings or statements that it will need further cuts. The second is Microsoft's next quarterly results, which will show whether margins hold as AI spending rises. Alphabet's slide shows how quickly investors can turn on a company whose costs outrun its returns.
