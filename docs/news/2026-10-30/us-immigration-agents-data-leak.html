Details of thousands of US immigration agents leaked in major hack

In one sentence
A group called the Department of Peace has published records on more than 41,000 DHS staff, about 6,600 of them at ICE, and DHS has only said it is aware of a potential breach.

A hacking group calling itself the Department of Peace has published records on more than 41,000 staff of the US Department of Homeland Security (DHS). Thousands of them work in immigration enforcement. The material appeared online on Wednesday 28 October, according to the BBC and outlets that followed its reporting. DHS has said only that it is "aware of a potential breach". The people exposed now face a question that is hard to answer after the fact: what happens when an agency's staff list is public and can't be recalled?

What was taken, and by whom

The data sits in a searchable database called "Icepick". It was published with the help of the cybersecurity researcher and hacker maia crimew and is hosted by Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets), a nonprofit that publishes leaked material. Crimew has said she was not involved in the hacking itself and compiled the data into a searchable form.

The breakdown reported by the BBC:

  • More than 21,000 people at US Customs and Border Protection (CBP).
  • Nearly 6,600 at Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).
  • Further records from Homeland Security Investigations, the Federal Protective Service and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency.

The fields reported are names, work addresses, phone numbers and a description of each person's role. Some outlets also report email addresses. Reports say the trove also names dozens of previously unknown operations, the agents who led and worked on them, and some of the technology used, including products from Cellebrite and Palantir. The material reportedly identifies people involved in undercover work.

The figures come from reporting, not from DHS, which has not confirmed a count. The reports describe work contact details rather than home addresses. That distinction matters, though work addresses and phone numbers are often the first step to finding a person's home and family.

How the hackers say they got in

The group says it reached the agent profiles through Okta, a single sign-on service. Organisations use such services so that staff log in once to reach many internal applications. Okta told the BBC: "Okta is secure, and we have no evidence our data or systems were compromised."

The two statements can both hold. An attacker can abuse a sign-in system through stolen credentials or a misconfigured account without any flaw in the vendor's own software. No one has publicly established which of these happened, and the hackers have not been independently identified. Until DHS or investigators say more, the route in is the group's claim.

The groups behind the release say it is not a full directory of DHS staff. They say the aim is to replace "anonymity with accountability". Emma Best, a co-founder of DDoSecrets, said the leak identifies officials overseeing covert operations, the hunt for leakers and key personnel in the deportation programme. A Department of Peace spokesperson cited a July statement by DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin that he was "turning up the heat" on enforcement.

That framing sits within a heated debate. ICE agents have shot at more than two dozen people this year and killed at least nine, according to the BBC, including Alex Pretti and Renee Good in Minnesota, neither of whom was an arrest target. Agents often wear masks and rarely identify themselves despite a legal requirement to do so. Supporters of the leak point to this as the reason for exposure. Critics see a list of ordinary employees, many of them clerical or technical, who now have no way to take their details back.

The safety problem

DHS has said it "is actively working to address the situation swiftly and effectively" and will update the public. It also issued an internal bulletin to officers on Wednesday, the Washington Post reported.

John Sandweg, a former ICE official in the Obama administration, told the Post it was a "nightmare scenario" that could endanger agents and jeopardise investigations. "The array of threats they face as a result of this is stunning," he said.

The exposure is hard to contain for a plain reason. Once a database is mirrored, it cannot be pulled back. Agents can change phone numbers, but names and roles stay attached to them. Anyone undercover has a separate problem: if a name is linked to an operation, the cover is damaged however quickly the site comes down. The Icepick site was reported unavailable on Thursday afternoon, but copies of this kind of data tend to spread.

This is also not the first such episode. In January, an ICE tracking site run from the Netherlands published details of about 4,500 Border Patrol and ICE employees, which it said came from a DHS whistleblower. The Department of Homeland Security said then that anyone responsible would be charged if identified. A month before this leak, the group ShinyHunters breached an FBI system and took personal information on thousands of current and former agents, their relatives and applicants. The Federal Election Commission was also knocked offline this week by a suspected hacking attempt.

What to watch

Three things will show how serious this is. The first is whether DHS confirms the scale and the route of entry, and whether it concedes that single sign-on credentials were the weak point. The second is whether prosecutors act against the publishers, who are spread across jurisdictions, as the January case showed. Hosting outside the US makes that hard. The third is whether the data is used to threaten agents or their families, which would turn a data-security embarrassment into a physical-safety case. The FBI has arrested at least three people over the ShinyHunters breach, so a similar response here would be a signal. For now, thousands of employees are waiting for their agency to tell them what was taken.
