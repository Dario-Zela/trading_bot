Michelin-star chef defends kitchen after food hygiene rating row

In one sentence
Gareth Ward, chef at two-Michelin-star Ynyshir in Wales, defended his kitchen after a one-star hygiene score, which the council has since raised to five.

A Michelin-starred restaurant in mid-Wales has spent much of the year explaining why a one-out-of-five food hygiene score does not mean what it looks like it means. The chef behind it, Gareth Ward of Ynyshir near Machynlleth, has said publicly that he is "not embarrassed" by the rating. His case is that the kitchen runs to the highest standard in the world, and that an inspection built for ordinary restaurants sits awkwardly on one that handles raw ingredients in an unusual way.

The rating

Ynyshir holds two Michelin stars. The Food Standards Agency scheme scores premises from 0 to 5, and a 1 means major improvement is necessary. Reports on the inspection said officials noted dead flies, a dirty knife, floor conditions and a risk of cross-contamination from raw meat. A spokesperson for the FSA side said the score meant the restaurant was operating below the minimum legal standard.

Ward answered in interviews, including one with the BBC, and also criticised journalists for how the inspection details were reported. He has described the inspectors' approach as out of step with a high-end tasting-menu kitchen.

The defence

The argument drew support from outside the kitchen. The food critic Giles Coren said health and safety rules "don't really apply" to restaurants of this kind. That is a rhetorical flourish rather than a legal position. The rules do apply, and the rating system exists so diners can see how a kitchen performed on the day. The row shows the gap between what the scheme measures (documented procedures, cleanliness, contamination controls) and what a Michelin guide rewards (the food).

Where it stands

The story has moved on since the original coverage, which dates from January and February 2026. The Independent reported that the council has since awarded the restaurant a hygiene rating of five, the top score. That outcome points to a routine process: a failing mark, remedial work, a re-inspection. It does not suggest any wider fault in how starred kitchens are inspected.

A caveat on sourcing: the BBC article that prompted this item could not be retrieved, and the details here come from reports citing it. The date, inspecting council and exact wording of the inspection report should be checked against the local authority's published record. If the row has reopened with a fresh inspection, that record is where it will show first.
