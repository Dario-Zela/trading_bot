Syrians struggle with soaring prices as crisis tests post-Assad recovery

In one sentence
Rising prices, from electricity to bread, are squeezing Syrian households and testing the post-Assad government's attempt to stabilise and rebuild the country.

Syria's new authorities are trying to rebuild a shattered country while the people they govern face prices that have outrun their wages. That is the tension at the centre of the post-Assad transition. Bashar al-Assad's government fell in early December 2024 and he fled to Moscow. Ahmed al-Sharaa, who led the group that toppled him, became interim president in January 2025. Almost two years on, whether households can afford food, bread and power may shape the transition's stability as much as the rebuilding of roads, homes and power stations.

The BBC report that prompted this brief was not available for checking, so the figures below come from other outlets and international bodies. They give a consistent picture, but they are not the BBC's own numbers.

A fragile starting point

The early signs were encouraging. One report put annual inflation at 6.4 per cent in January 2025, down from 118.9 per cent a year earlier, as the new leadership stabilised the currency and sought international support. That was a sharp fall from a very high base, and it came with a low starting level of activity, so it did not signal that living standards were recovering.

The World Bank estimated in October 2025 that reconstruction would cost $216 billion. A UN report has said about 90 per cent of Syrians live in poverty. A population in that condition has almost no cushion against price rises.

Subsidies withdrawn, bills multiplied

The sharpest shocks have come from policy as well as from markets. In late 2025 the Energy Ministry raised electricity prices by as much as 6,000 per cent, which prompted public anger among residents already worn down by sanctions and 14 years of war. Residents also point to bread, which is no longer subsidised. Removing subsidies is a standard step in rebuilding public finances, but it lands hardest where incomes are lowest.

Public patience seems to be wearing thin. An analysis by Israel's Institute for National Security Studies found that by April only 13 per cent of Syrians believed the government was doing enough about rising food and energy prices. That is one survey, reported second-hand, and should be read with caution. It is still a notable figure for a government whose legitimacy rests partly on delivering a better life than the one it replaced.

The outlook is not reassuring. Reporting on an IMF assessment in August 2026 said the economy was recovering, but that poverty remained widespread and inflation was expected to pick up this year on strong domestic demand and higher import prices before easing in 2027.

What to watch

The next test is whether outside money and sanctions relief arrive fast enough to ease the squeeze. Reconstruction funding, investment in power generation and the pace of any further subsidy cuts will decide whether prices stabilise. The IMF's expectation of easing in 2027 gives a rough horizon: the interim authorities need to hold together through a difficult winter before that.
