Bereaved families will not face tax bill over pension delays under new rules

In one sentence
A UK change to pension inheritance-tax rules would stop bereaved families being billed for tax caused by administrator delays, though key details remain unconfirmed.

The government has moved to stop bereaved families being charged for tax that arises only because a pension administrator was slow. The change is to the rules that bring unused pensions into inheritance tax. The detail matters here, and some of it could not be confirmed before filing.

The starting point is a rule that takes effect in April 2027. From 6 April 2027, most unused pension funds and certain death benefits will count as part of a dead person's estate for inheritance tax. Until now, a pension pot passed outside the estate. Death-in-service benefits and transfers to a spouse or civil partner stay outside the tax, though they may still need to be reported.

The problem the change addresses

The difficulty is timing. Inheritance tax is due within six months of a death, and interest on late payment starts to run from the seventh month. The rate has recently been about 7.75%, according to AJ Bell. The personal representative, usually a relative, has to work out the tax. They depend on pension schemes to supply valuations and details of the benefits.

That dependence is the weak point. Steve Webb of consultancy LCP warned that the new process would "undoubtedly" slow things down for bereaved families. The trade body PIMFA called the proposals "unworkable" and said they risked "serious problems for bereaved families". STEP, the body for trust and estate practitioners, argued that families should not be penalised for delays outside their control. It also pointed out that payment notices cannot be submitted until probate has been granted. Ordinary processing times could therefore push a family into paying interest.

The Treasury had resisted the obvious fix. It kept the six-month window, despite a House of Lords suggestion of up to a year. It offered other mitigations instead. Schemes can withhold up to half of a death benefit while the tax is settled. Personal representatives can also tell a scheme to pay the tax directly from the pension fund.

What the new rule does

The reported change goes further than those mitigations. Families would not be billed for tax, or the penalties that come with it, where the delay is down to the administrator. The BBC's political desk carried the story. This piece could not retrieve that report. It therefore cannot confirm the effective date, the exact wording, or whether the protection covers interest, penalties or both. No minister or official is named in the material available. Those details should be read from the government's published text rather than from this summary.

The market angle, such as it is

This is mainly a personal-finance and process story, and the market effect is small. The 2027 rule has pushed advisers to rethink the order in which retirees draw down assets. Whether pensions are spent before other wealth now matters more. A softer penalty regime does not undo that. It removes one source of risk for families, and it lets schemes take longer over paperwork without exposing the estate to interest. Any effect on fund flows or insurers is hard to see from what has been reported. The money still sits in the same pots.

What to watch

The test is the legislative text and HMRC's guidance ahead of April 2027. Those will show who decides that a delay was the administrator's fault, and what evidence families must produce. Earlier amendments floated in the Finance Bill debate in January 2026 included a review of delays and a dedicated HMRC helpline for pension inheritance-tax queries. Whether either was adopted is still open.
