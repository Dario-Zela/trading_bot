MPs say most of British Council's £197m debt should be written off

In one sentence
MPs on the Public Accounts Committee urge ministers to convert most of the British Council's £197m Foreign Office debt into a grant, warning its future is still precarious.

A committee of MPs has told the government to stop treating the British Council as a debtor to be pursued and start treating it as an institution to be kept alive. The Commons Public Accounts Committee says ministers should explore writing off as much as possible of the £197m the council owes the Foreign Office. The way to do that, it says, is to convert the loan into "a grant or similar".

The report, Financial Sustainability of the British Council, was published on 30 October. Its recommendation is advisory. The Treasury and the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO) are under no obligation to act on it, and neither had published a formal response when the report appeared.

How a £60m loan became £197m

The British Council is the UK's cultural-relations body. It operates in just under 100 countries and claims a reach of more than 700 million people a year. It is an arm's-length body of the FCDO, an established charity and a commercial organisation at once. Most of its income comes from selling English teaching and exams overseas. The FCDO's grant-in-aid supplies only about 15% of annual revenue.

That commercial exposure caused the trouble. When the pandemic closed its overseas classrooms and exam halls, Boris Johnson's government lent it £60m on commercial terms. The committee puts the debt owed to the FCDO at £197m and says the council has made net losses of £184m since July 2020. An initial 2020 business plan failed. The loan was renegotiated in 2024 and is now due to be repaid over 15 years.

The council has already cut deeply. It has shed more than a third of its staff, with more than 2,000 full-time-equivalent posts lost since 2021. It has sold historic assets, including a building in Madrid, and closed offices in nearly ten countries. Its turnaround plan calls for a further 25% reduction in headcount, more asset sales and closures in nine countries. Staff across Europe have struck and protested. The council does not expect an operating surplus before 2029–30.

What the committee wants

The report makes four main recommendations:

  • The FCDO should consider offsetting the loan against the council's soft-power value, which the committee puts at about £1bn a year, and convert as much of it as possible into a grant.
  • The FCDO and the council should agree what happens if the turnaround has not secured financial stability by 2029–30.
  • The council must fully consult staff on any further job cuts.
  • The council must resolve the tension between shrinking its operations and pursuing revenue growth.

The committee calls the council's future "still precarious" and warns that it is "overly reliant on the unlikely scenario" that economic conditions do not worsen. It describes a relationship marked by suspicion between the council and the FCDO. The department, it notes, says the council "must adapt to survive". The committee's answer is that adapting requires the department's "full and practical support".

The committee's chair, the Conservative Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, put it more bluntly. The council, he said, "needs the government's help to continue its vital work, not to be simply pursued as a debtor."

The precedent

The story is narrow, but the principle is not. Hybrid bodies such as the British Council sit between the state and the market. They are expected to earn their way, yet the state is their lender of last resort. A write-off, or a conversion into grant, would show that a Treasury loan to such a body is in practice a soft claim. Refusing would show the reverse.

The Treasury has resisted so far. Earlier this year, after a National Audit Office report on the council's finances, a minister said the Treasury expected the loan to be paid in cash and rejected an offer to hand over art in lieu. Write-off was reportedly also ruled out then, partly on subsidy-control grounds. The committee is asking ministers to revisit that position.

The market and international stakes are small. The consequences matter to universities, which rely on the council to promote UK study, deliver qualifications abroad and run scholarships. They matter, too, to those who count the council as part of Britain's soft power.

What to watch

Committee reports prompt a formal government reply, normally within two months. That reply will show whether the Treasury is prepared to move from collecting on the loan to absorbing it. The 2029–30 date is the other marker. If the council is still not in surplus by then, the question will return, probably at a higher price.
