Obesity plan 'doomed to fail', MPs warn

In one sentence
MPs on the Health and Social Care Committee say the government's obesity 'moonshot' is doomed to fail without a prime minister-led plan, clear targets and tougher action on the food industry.

A cross-party committee of MPs has concluded that the government's plan to "end the obesity epidemic" is "doomed to fail" unless ministers rethink it and the prime minister takes charge. The warning comes in the final report of the Commons Health and Social Care Committee, published on 30 October. It lands hardest on two groups: the food and drink companies that would face any new rules, and the NHS, which treats the consequences when prevention falls short.

What the committee found

The target is the "moonshot ambition on obesity" in the 10 Year Health Plan for England, launched in July 2025. The committee, chaired by the Liberal Democrat MP Layla Moran, says that more than a year on, basic questions have no answers. The government has not defined what ending the epidemic means. It has set no overall reduction target and no interim milestones. It has also not explained how progress will be measured or how a plan spanning several departments would work, according to reports of the document in the Guardian and The Grocer.

The report also says the strategy fails to separate two jobs: preventing obesity in future, and supporting and treating people who already have it. The MPs say that confusion has held back progress under successive governments.

The committee does not hedge its verdict. It says it has "no faith in the government's commitment to this aim or ability to achieve it", and warns of an "inevitable crash landing" (as quoted by The Grocer). Its central argument is about leadership. "The government will fail to tackle a challenge of this scale unless action is led from the top," the report says. Moran put it more bluntly: "if the prime minister isn't calling the shots on such a huge societal issue, the loose ends won't join up and the moonshot will crash land."

The scale of the problem is set out in official statistics cited in the coverage. About 30% of adults in England have obesity, and 66% are overweight or have obesity. Close to a third of children are overweight or have obesity. Rates have risen sharply since the 1990s and have levelled off, but show no sign of falling.

The burden on food and drink firms

Much of the report concerns measures that would fall on industry, and which it says are stalled or diluted. According to The Grocer's account:

  • Nutrient profiling model. First proposed in 2018, it would reclassify thousands more products as high in fat, salt or sugar (HFSS) for advertising and promotion rules. It remains stuck in a standoff between the Department of Health and Social Care, health campaigners and manufacturers. Industry argues it would be economic self-sabotage and would waste reformulation investment.
  • Junk food advertising restrictions. These took effect in January. Campaign groups say they were "gutted" after industry lobbying, and the MPs say children remain constantly exposed to promotion of unhealthy food.
  • Mandatory healthy-sales reporting and targets. These have been delayed in this Parliament. The Grocer reports that supermarkets and manufacturers support the idea in principle, but there is significant disagreement over how to police it and what targets to set.

Moran said an effective plan "will mean standing up to the food industry". Her list of measures is to regulate the constant presence of adverts and promotions for unhealthy food and drink, simplify product labelling, set targets for supermarkets to sell more healthy food, and restrict fast food outlets near schools. The report does not, on the coverage available, cost these measures. The practical expense to firms would depend on the form any rules take.

The NHS and the government's reply

For the NHS, the report's sharpest phrase concerns treatment. Access to weight-loss services, including the new drugs, varies so much by area that the committee calls it a "postcode lottery". The government describes obesity as one of the biggest challenges facing the health service. The reports reviewed give no figures on NHS cost or capacity, and none are inferred here.

The Department of Health and Social Care responded by pointing to action already taken:

  • TV advertising of less healthy food before 9pm, and paid online advertising, has been restricted.
  • Volume-price promotions on less healthy food and drink have been banned.
  • Councils have stronger powers to block new hot food takeaways near schools.
  • Weight management support is being expanded, including NHS weight-loss drugs for those with the greatest clinical need.

The dispute is therefore less about whether anything has been done than about whether it adds up. The committee's point is that individual measures exist without a framework to join them or a target to test them against.

What happens next

The committee wants a cabinet committee on obesity chaired by the prime minister, and a defined goal with interim markers so that MPs can hold ministers to account. It also wants a cross-government action plan that names responsible departments, actions and funding, and covers prevention and treatment for adults and children. Ministers should present that plan to Parliament within three months. That puts the test in late January 2027. Whether the government accepts the recommendation, and whether the stalled nutrient profiling model and healthy-sales reporting move in the meantime, will show how seriously it takes the warning. Select committee reports carry no binding force, and the government's formal response is still to come.

Katharine Jenner, executive director of the Obesity Health Alliance, backed the call for a prime ministerial "personal mission". She described "decades of promises" and "endless delays, industry pressure and a failure to deliver".

This account relies on the Guardian's report, syndicated by AOL, and on The Grocer. The full committee report was not reviewed directly, so figures and wording are as quoted in that coverage.
