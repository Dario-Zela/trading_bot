FCC adopts rules requiring device testing in US or reciprocal countries by 2028

In one sentence
The FCC is moving to accept certification testing only from US or reciprocal-agreement countries from December 2028, but the final order text has not been verified.

The US Federal Communications Commission has moved to end its recognition of electronics test labs in countries that have no reciprocal deal with Washington. Under the plan, testing that supports FCC certification would have to happen in the United States or in a country with a mutual recognition arrangement. The proposed cut-off is 1 December 2028. The aim, according to trade-press accounts, is to pull more certification work out of China, where the bulk of it is now done. The change would also land on test houses and manufacturers in other countries that lack such a deal.

One caveat first. Nearly every report found ahead of this piece was written before the commission's 29 October vote, and the final order text was not available to check. The details below are what the FCC and law-firm and test-house commentary describe. Whether the adopted version differs from the proposal is not confirmed.

What the FCC has set out

The work sits in ET Docket 24-136. In April 2026 the commission voted 3-0 (Chairman Carr and Commissioners Gomez and Trusty) on a Second Report and Order and a further proposal. The April release said it would bar recognition of labs and certification bodies in countries without a mutual recognition agreement (MRA) with the US, or a comparable reciprocal trade arrangement. Affected labs would be phased out over two years once final rules were in place.

The October item is described as a Third Report and Order and a further notice. Commentators put the end date for accepting results from non-reciprocal economies, China included, at 1 December 2028. Existing certifications are reported to stay valid, so products already approved would not need retesting. Pre-compliance and R&D testing that no certification application relies on would fall outside the rule.

The April order also created a fast-track review for devices tested in US or reciprocal-country labs. It also required labs to disclose where their FCC-recognised testing staff are located, and strengthened enforcement. The FCC says it has already withdrawn or denied recognition for 23 "bad labs" it considers national-security risks.

Why it matters beyond China

The FCC's own release notes that until 2015 the commission allowed testing only in the US or MRA countries. The current plan is a return to, and tightening of, that older model. Trade reports cite a figure of about 82 per cent of approved electronics being tested in China. The FCC release itself does not give that number, so it should be treated as a secondary estimate.

The Benzinga-sourced framing that UK and EU firms could be hit is plausible but unproven. Whether a given country's labs survive depends on the final list of qualifying economies. The US has MRAs covering telecom equipment with several partners, but the scope of each differs. A manufacturer whose products are tested in a country outside that list would need to move the work. Bureau Veritas and TÜV SÜD have both published guidance noting that the formal order, with the final country list and transition terms, defines the real exposure.

The practical effect would fall on three groups:

  • Test labs outside the US and MRA countries, which would lose the ability to issue results the FCC accepts.
  • Manufacturers with supply chains and engineering teams near those labs, who would face re-routing prototypes and paying for slots at approved labs.
  • US and allied labs, which could see demand rise, though capacity and queue times are untested.

The FCC has concluded that compliance costs would not outweigh the benefits it can quantify, according to summaries of the item. No independent estimate of the cost to industry was found.

What to watch

The published order and its Federal Register entry will settle the open questions: which countries qualify, what counts as a "comparable" arrangement, and how long the transition lasts. Comment filings in ET Docket 24-136 from trade bodies and test houses will show how large the affected market is, which no source has yet put a number on.
