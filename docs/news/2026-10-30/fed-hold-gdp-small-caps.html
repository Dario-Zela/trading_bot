Fed holds and Q3 GDP grows 3.4%, but megacap tech sags while small caps rally

In one sentence
The Fed's expected hold, with two dissents favouring a hike, barely moved markets; Thursday's tech-led sell-off followed a 3.4% GDP print, in-line core PCE and weak megacap earnings.

The Federal Reserve did what almost everyone expected on Wednesday: it left its policy rate unchanged. The target range stays at 3.75% to 4.00%, and 94 of 100 economists in a Reuters poll had called a hold. The briefing note for this piece described the decision as the trigger for a futures sell-off. The record does not support that. The sell-off came a day later, and the data and earnings that arrived on Thursday explain it better than the Fed does.

A hold with an edge

The decision itself was quiet. Reports of the meeting said stocks were little changed and Treasury yields were broadly steady, with the two-year around 4.76%. Bloomberg's account was that Chairman Kevin Warsh's press conference was enough to keep investors onside, in contrast with a July meeting at which markets tumbled when he failed to explain the committee's reasoning.

The committee was not unanimous. According to published accounts, two officials, Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, preferred a quarter-point rise. That is the useful detail in an otherwise unremarkable decision: the dissent was hawkish, which fits the pre-meeting debate over whether Warsh could deliver what one preview called a "hawkish hold". Warsh said officials had "made clear our readiness to act as circumstances require", but Bloomberg reported no clear signal on a December increase. Warsh also said that in 2027 one meeting will carry no rate vote and no post-meeting statement, leaving six scheduled chances to move rates.

That matters for the angle. Warsh has moved away from conventional forward guidance, and the market is left to infer the path from dissents, speeches and data. A leadership change that reduces explicit guidance raises the weight placed on each data release. On Wednesday that did not show up in prices. It did on Thursday.

The data day

The Bureau of Economic Analysis put third-quarter real GDP growth at an annualised 3.4% in its advance estimate, led by consumer spending. Consensus was a little lower: 3.2% in the LSEG poll cited by AOL, 3.1% in a Wall Street Journal survey cited elsewhere. The beat is real but modest.

The inflation figures are less comfortable than "growth without inflation" implies. September core PCE, the Fed's preferred gauge, rose 0.3% on the month and 3.1% on the year, in line with expectations. Headline PCE rose 0.5% on the month and 3.6% on the year, also as forecast. In-line numbers are not benign when the annual rate is more than a percentage point above the 2% target and the committee has two members arguing for a hike. Strong output and inflation matching forecasts give a hawkish committee no reason to ease. They also give it no new reason to tighten, which keeps the question of December open.

CME FedWatch put the probability of a quarter-point hike in December at about 79.6% on Thursday morning. If that figure is right, the market had already priced in a move before the Fed's hold. The hold was therefore a delay, not a change of direction. Traders still expect the rate to go up.

Where the selling landed

Shortly after Thursday's open the S&P 500 was down more than 0.4% and the Dow more than 0.3%. The Nasdaq Composite was down more than 1.4%, the steepest fall of the major indexes. That points to a sector, not the whole market.

The tech earnings are the likelier cause. Alphabet had its worst day since March 2020 on fears about AI's effect on Google search, which the Tribune covers separately. Microsoft's profit growth sat beside an announcement of thousands of layoffs days earlier. A Nasdaq fall that is three times the S&P's, on the day after earnings from the index's heaviest names, is the pattern company-specific news produces. A rate shock would hit the broader market more evenly.

The small-cap half of the story could not be confirmed. The triage notes said small caps rallied while megacap tech fell, and that would fit a rate-relief reading. But the Russell 2000 closing level and move for Thursday were not available from any source reviewed here, and the October 15, 20 and 22 sessions in one secondary recap showed a similar rotation on other days, which is not evidence for this one. The claim that the market is pricing rate relief rather than an AI-led growth story is plausible, but it is unproven. It also sits oddly with a futures market pricing a hike with near 80% odds.

On the question the briefing posed, the evidence points mostly one way. The Fed's guidance did not move markets on Wednesday, and the Thursday sell-off lines up with the earnings reports more than with Warsh.

What to watch

The next test is the committee's December meeting, where the market has a hike largely priced in. Between now and then come the October jobs report and the October inflation data. A core PCE print above 0.3% would strengthen the two dissenters' case. Another in-line reading would leave Warsh with the choice he avoided this week: to say plainly what the committee will do, or to let the data decide for him.
