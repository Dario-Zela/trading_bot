Police Scotland to target online gangs forcing children to self-harm

In one sentence
Police Scotland is reportedly launching an operation against online groups that coerce children into self-harm, but its scale and any charges are not yet known.

Police Scotland is preparing an operation against online groups that coerce children into harming themselves, according to the BBC. If the report is borne out, it would treat as a crime a harm that has mostly been handled as a matter of health and safeguarding. The force's own statement has not been checked, and no figures, dates, arrests or named groups had been published at the time of writing. The scale of the operation is therefore unknown.

What is known

The reported outline is short. Police Scotland is launching an operation aimed at networks that pressure children into self-harm, rather than at the children themselves. That distinction matters. A child who is hurting themselves has traditionally been met by schools, GPs and mental health services. A person or group that directs, incites or rewards that behaviour is a different kind of subject for police, and one with a more obvious route into the criminal courts.

The story rests on a single source, and the details that would show how serious the operation is have not been made public. These include the number of officers involved, whether specific suspects have been identified, and whether arrests have been made.

The wider picture

The tactic is not new to UK policing. The National Crime Agency has warned about so-called "Com" networks, loose online communities that it says target children and young people. According to the agency, victims have been coerced into self-harm or suicide attempts, including swallowing harmful substances and burning themselves. LBC has reported that at least 21 British victims have been caught up in group chats in which members earn points and notoriety by pushing vulnerable people, mostly teenage boys, towards harm.

There is also English precedent for prosecution. The Crown Prosecution Service has reported that a 16-year-old boy was sentenced at Southampton Youth Court after encouraging other internet users to self-harm, alongside separate offences involving indecent images of children. That case shows that existing law can reach this conduct in England. None of the material found confirms how Scottish prosecutors would approach comparable cases.

Police Scotland already runs a dedicated online child sexual abuse and exploitation unit. Its figures for April 2024 to July 2025 show 863 investigations, 604 arrests and protective steps for 815 children. Whether the new operation sits inside that structure or alongside it has not been reported.

What it could change

The significance lies in the framing. If police treat coercive self-harm networks as organised criminality, the expectations on others shift. Platforms would face more requests for data and faster takedowns. Parents and schools would get clearer guidance on when to call the police rather than only a health service. The effect is limited until the operation's scope is known, and a high-profile launch does not guarantee charges. Evidence is hard to gather when members are anonymous, often young themselves and spread across jurisdictions.

The next markers are a formal Police Scotland statement and any first arrests or charges. The Crown Office's decisions on which offences to bring will show whether this becomes a sustained prosecution effort or stays an awareness campaign.
