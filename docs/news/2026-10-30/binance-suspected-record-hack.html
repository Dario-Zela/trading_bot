Binance hit by suspected $6.3bn hack, which would be the largest in crypto history

In one sentence
Binance says about $6.3bn was stolen via a compromised engineer's laptop, a record crypto theft, with withdrawals suspended, about $3bn frozen and Bitcoin down about 5%.

Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange by assets, said on Friday that about $6.3bn was stolen in a security incident. If the figure holds, it is the largest theft in crypto history, roughly four times the $1.5bn taken from Bybit in February 2025. The exchange has suspended deposits and withdrawals, says about $3bn of the funds has been frozen, and says it will cover all losses.

The story has moved on from the single headline report that first flagged it. Binance has confirmed the incident, and the outside estimates of its size differ only modestly. Cause, recovery and the effect on customers are all still open.

What is known

Binance's early finding is that an attacker compromised an engineer's laptop and used that access to make unauthorised transactions. Forbes, whose article could not be retrieved in full, headlines its account in the same way: the breach "started with one engineer's laptop". Binance says its teams are working with law enforcement and has promised a detailed technical post-mortem once the investigation ends. It has cautioned that the value of affected, recovered and frozen assets may change. Founder Changpeng Zhao said earlier in the day that the exchange appeared to have been hacked.

The independent numbers are close but not identical. TRM Labs counts about $6.36bn. Chainalysis puts the theft at $5.9bn. The gap is the kind that usually narrows as assets are traced and prices move, so $6.3bn is best read as an estimate.

TRM Labs has attributed the attack "with high confidence" to TraderTraitor, a North Korean unit linked to the Lazarus Group. That is the same lineage US authorities and analysts tied to the Bybit theft. TRM's Ari Redbord called the incident "unprecedented" and said theft at this scale by North Korea, which uses stolen crypto to fund weapons programmes, makes it "a global security issue". Attribution by a private analytics firm is an assessment, not a government finding, and Binance itself has not named the attackers.

The assets involved have not been itemised in the reports reviewed. That matters. Stablecoins can be frozen by their issuers, while bitcoin and other tokens generally cannot, so the mix will largely determine how much of the roughly $3bn frozen stays recoverable.

The custody question

Binance held about $155bn in assets, the most of any exchange, according to CoinMarketCap. Against that, a $6.3bn loss is about 4% of holdings. That is survivable for a firm that has pledged to make customers whole, and the pledge is the central claim to test. A promise to cover losses is only as good as the balance sheet and the liquidity behind it, and Binance has not said how it would fund a gap if frozen assets prove unrecoverable.

The method is more troubling than the size. The attack apparently did not break cryptography or exploit a flaw in a blockchain. It reached a single person's machine. The Bybit theft ran along similar lines, with the attackers manipulating what operators saw and approved. The Binance finding suggests that the controls separating one engineer's credentials from the movement of billions of dollars were not enough, though the post-mortem will have to show where they failed.

Sean Foley of Macquarie University told Al Jazeera that a loss of confidence in a major exchange could shock retail investors and dampen prices, and that the episode highlights the risks of centralised exchanges and might push traders toward decentralised alternatives. TRM's 2026 crime report puts all hacking losses in 2025 at $2.87bn across nearly 150 incidents, so this single event is more than double that annual total.

Bitcoin fell about 5% on the news, to below $77,000, according to Al Jazeera. Whether that is a one-day reaction or the start of a larger withdrawal of capital depends on how long withdrawals stay frozen. Reports reviewed so far do not mention any regulator statements or actions, and none has been confirmed.

A separate pressure on Bitcoin

The hack lands while Bitcoin faces a different, more speculative argument. Analyst Ben Cowen has argued that Anthropic's expected IPO could draw attention and money away from the asset, pointing to Bitcoin's pullback around SpaceX's $75bn listing in June as precedent. That account comes from BeInCrypto and other secondary coverage; the Benzinga item cited in the original triage could not be located, so the attribution is Cowen's, not confirmed from that source. Reports on Anthropic's timing and valuation also conflict, with some pointing to a fall 2026 listing and valuations ranging from roughly $1tn to $2tn. None of that is confirmed by a filing the article could verify.

The argument is a forecast, not a measurement, and no fund-flow data seen here shows a rotation. Its relevance is limited to this: if the exchange freeze keeps buyers on the sidelines while large AI listings compete for capital, the two pressures would compound. The hack is a direct shock to confidence in crypto's plumbing, while the IPO thesis is a conjecture about where marginal money goes.

What to watch

Three things will settle how large this becomes. The first is whether and when Binance reopens withdrawals, and whether the first outflows after reopening are orderly. The second is the post-mortem and any revision to the $6.3bn figure, including the split between frozen, recovered and lost assets. The third is the regulatory response, which has not yet appeared: a loss of this size at the biggest exchange is likely to sharpen scrutiny of custody and operational controls at centralised platforms. Until Binance publishes its findings, the numbers should be treated as provisional.
