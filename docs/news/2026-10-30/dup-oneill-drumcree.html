DUP blocks Michelle O'Neill's engagements over Drumcree

In one sentence
The DUP has ended joint First and Deputy First Minister engagements and barred Michelle O'Neill from acting alone, over her attendance at a Drumcree protest.

The Democratic Unionist Party has stopped Michelle O'Neill, Northern Ireland's First Minister, from carrying out official engagements. It is punishing her for joining a protest over the Drumcree parade. The party's leader says the joint work of the First Minister's office has ceased. It is the sharpest test yet of whether Stormont's power-sharing can survive a parades dispute that has run since September.

What the DUP has done

Northern Ireland's two top offices are a joint post. The First Minister and the deputy First Minister hold equal powers, and neither can act without the other's agreement. On 29 October the DUP leader, Gavin Robinson, announced that joint activities of the Office of First and Deputy First Minister had ceased. He added that O'Neill would not be permitted to act alone either, according to RTÉ.

The deputy First Minister, Emma Little-Pengelly of the DUP, formally withdrew her agreement for O'Neill to attend a series of engagements in Glasgow on 30 October. These included a UK economic forum with Prime Minister Andy Burnham and the leaders of the devolved administrations. A planned joint meeting with the Irish president, Catherine Connolly, was also halted. Robinson said that was because of O'Neill's conduct, though Sinn Féin disputes how it was postponed. O'Neill also cannot attend the Belfast cenotaph remembrance event in her capacity as First Minister next month.

Some reports describe the block as covering all of her engagements and others as covering joint ones only. No end date has been given.

The Drumcree background

The dispute is over an Orange Order parade at Drumcree, near Portadown. For decades the route down the largely nationalist Garvaghy Road has been contested. This year the Parades Commission allowed the parade, but the Northern Secretary, Chris Bryant, banned it. A High Court judge upheld the ban, ending a standoff of about two weeks.

The protest that provoked the DUP was held on the Garvaghy Road in September. Sinn Féin politicians, O'Neill among them, attended. It was not notified to the Parades Commission, which makes it unlawful. The DUP calls it "reckless" and "lawbreaking". O'Neill has said she attended as Sinn Féin's deputy leader rather than as First Minister. Police have since invited those suspected of offences to interview, and O'Neill attended last week. The Sinn Féin TDs Pearse Doherty and Matt Carthy have also attended voluntarily.

Reaction and what comes next

O'Neill called the move "arrogant and supremacist" and a "reactive stunt" driven by the DUP's internal pressures. She said she had contacted Bryant, who called it "deeply frustrating" that the Executive would not be represented and urged everyone to engage. Naomi Long of Alliance said the block damaged Northern Ireland. The TUV leader, Jim Allister, called the DUP response "beyond pathetic".

The pressure on Robinson comes largely from within his own party. The MP Carla Lockhart has urged Little-Pengelly to leave the Executive. That would automatically remove O'Neill and effectively collapse devolved government. Robinson has resisted. Lockhart resigned as a party officer last week. The DUP MLAs Diane Forsythe and Maurice Bradley have also called for O'Neill's removal.

In that light the engagement block looks like a middle course. It signals anger and denies O'Neill a public platform without bringing down the institutions. The cost falls on Northern Ireland, which will have no Executive presence at the Glasgow forum.

The test is whether the block stays a gesture or hardens into a route to collapse. The next Assembly election is due in May, which gives both parties reason to avoid looking weak in the meantime.
