Climate academic Andreas Malm barred from entering UK

In one sentence
The Home Office cancelled Swedish climate scholar Andreas Malm's travel authorisation as 'not conducive to the public good', giving no detailed reason, which has prompted a free-speech row.

The Home Office has cancelled the travel authorisation of Andreas Malm, the Swedish climate scholar, days before he was due to speak at a London event. The decision has turned a routine-looking border action into a dispute over whether foreign academics can be kept out of Britain because of the company they keep.

Malm, who teaches human ecology at Lund University, is best known for Fossil Capital (2016), a history of how coal and steam power shaped the carbon economy, and for How to Blow Up a Pipeline (2021), which argues that climate movements should consider sabotaging fossil-fuel property.

What happened

According to an email seen by Al Jazeera, the Home Office cancelled Malm's Electronic Travel Authorisation on Wednesday. The stated ground was that his "presence in the UK is not considered to be conducive to the public good". He was told to apply for a visa, after which officials would "consider your circumstances in more detail".

The timing matters. Standard visitor visas take about three weeks to process, so he could not have obtained one for the panel, due about a week after the decision. It was organised by the British socialist magazine Tribune, and Malm was to appear alongside Huda Ammori, a co-founder of Palestine Action. The UK proscribed that group last year as a terrorist organisation. Ammori is challenging the ban, and the Supreme Court is due to hear her case on 4 November.

The Home Office had not answered questions from Al Jazeera by publication. The email gives a formula, not a reason in the ordinary sense. It does not say whether the concern was Malm's writing, his views on Palestine or the panel.

Malm's response

Malm believes the panel prompted the cancellation. He has called the decision "guilt by association" and says he has visited the UK regularly for decades. He says his "position on Palestine has always been public" and that he has said and written nothing about it since his last visit in May.

Gareth Dale, a reader in political economy at Brunel University of London, called the move "a monstrous attempt by the Burnham government to suppress free speech". Verso, Malm's publisher, has also protested, assuming Palestine was the cause because none was given.

Why it matters for climate debate

On the available evidence, the climate angle is indirect. Nothing in the reporting says officials cited How to Blow Up a Pipeline or any statement on fossil-fuel policy, and the book has been in print and on British shelves for years. The event Malm was due to join was about Palestine, not climate.

That distinction is the heart of the free-speech question. If the refusal rests on association with a proscribed group's co-founder at a public panel, it raises one set of issues about guilt by association and the reach of terrorism law. If it were ever shown to rest on Malm's published arguments about sabotage and climate protest, it would set a different and broader precedent for academic critics of fossil-fuel policy. The bare "public good" wording cannot tell the two apart, and the Home Office guidance says such exclusions must be made personally by the Home Secretary or a minister acting for them.

What to watch

The first test is whether the Home Office explains the decision or reverses it; its lack of comment so far leaves the stated ground unexplained. The second is the Supreme Court hearing on 4 November, which will shape how far association with Palestine Action can be treated as grounds for exclusion. Until a reason is given, this remains a single case, but one that academics and free-speech groups are likely to cite.
