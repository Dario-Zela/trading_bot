Alphabet suffers worst day since March 2020 as AI fears hit Google search, wiping about $500bn off its value

In one sentence
Alphabet fell roughly 12% — its worst day since March 2020, erasing about $490bn — after a double miss, negative free cash flow and heavy AI spending; the search-threat link is plausible but unproven.

Alphabet, the parent of Google, lost roughly an eighth of its stock market value in a single session after its third-quarter results. Reports put the fall at between about 10% and 13%, and the sum erased at about $490bn, close to the $500bn in the headline. The move is described as the company's worst day since March 2020, when pandemic panic took about 12% off the shares on 16 March. Part of the story is still unsettled: what exactly frightened investors. The sources reviewed point to several causes at once, and the AI threat to search is one reading of the move, not an established finding.

What happened

Alphabet reported after the close on Wednesday 28 October. Shares were already down about 10% in after-hours trading, according to CNBC's report that evening. By the end of Thursday's regular session the loss had widened. GuruFocus put the decline at 12.5%, and another outlet cited a fall from about $321.89 to $283.29, a drop of more than 12%. Bloomberg's opinion desk described the move as nearly 11%. The spread reflects different measurement points — after-hours, intraday and close — not a dispute about direction.

The Nasdaq fell more than 400 points on the day, Benzinga reported, and the S&P 500 snapped a three-day winning run as Alphabet and Caterpillar both sold off, per CNBC's live coverage. Alphabet's fall was large enough to move the index, not just the stock.

What the numbers showed

The quarter was not weak on growth. Revenue rose about 25% from a year earlier, according to one summary. But it came in near $127.6bn against analyst estimates of about $128.2bn, and CNBC described the result as Alphabet's first miss on both revenue and earnings in nearly four years.

The cost side drew as much attention as the top line:

  • Capital spending was about $51.4bn in the quarter, mostly for AI data centres and computing capacity.
  • Free cash flow turned negative, at about minus $9.07bn in one account.
  • Operating margin fell to 31.8%.
  • Earnings per share were reduced by about $0.55 by an $8.7bn loss on equity stakes.
  • Guidance: Alphabet held its 2026 capital spending plan unchanged and said it expects a significant increase in 2027, according to CNBC's headline.

On cloud, the 10-Q for the period to 30 September shows $567.7bn of contracted but unrecognised revenue, of which $560.8bn relates to Google Cloud. That is a very large order book, though the company redefined the measure in the first quarter of 2026 to include contracts of one year or less, so comparisons with earlier periods need care. Earlier-quarter figures in circulation, such as 17% growth in Search and other and 82% in Cloud, could not be tied to the third quarter in the sources available, and are not relied on here.

The search question

The premise that investors sold Alphabet because they now see generative AI as a direct threat to its search advertising is plausible but not demonstrated by the material reviewed. Search remains the company's main profit pool, and the market has punished the stock on search fears before: in May 2025 it fell more than 7% on reports that Apple was exploring AI search for Safari. The reasoning for such a link is simple. If answers are given by chatbots rather than a page of ads, the click that funds the business becomes less certain.

But the reports of this episode emphasise a different mix: a first double miss in almost four years, negative free cash flow and a spending bill with no end in sight. Bloomberg's opinion column argued that the quarter was "wobbly" but "no sign of a crisis". Segment-level Search figures for the quarter were not available to this desk, so a claim that search revenue slowed cannot be confirmed. What can be said is that a strong order book in cloud did not prevent the sell-off, which suggests investors were weighing the whole equation — returns on AI spending, margins and search durability — not one line.

The stock's earlier path supports that reading. Semafor reported in August that Alphabet had lost about $700bn from its May peak as AI bills climbed, with shares down more than 15%. Thursday's fall came on top of an existing doubt about whether the investment would pay.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The first is the detailed Search and YouTube figures in the earnings materials and call, which will show whether growth in the core ad business actually slowed. The second is whether the 2027 capital spending figure, still unquantified beyond "significant", arrives with evidence that cloud backlog is converting into recognised revenue fast enough to cover it. The third is how the other large buyers of AI infrastructure fare: the Fed-day tech weakness and CoreWeave's slide in the same week show the market is already testing the AI spending cycle across the sector. Alphabet's next quarterly report, due around late January, will be the next formal data point.
