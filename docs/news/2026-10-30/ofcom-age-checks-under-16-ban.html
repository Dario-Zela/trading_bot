Ofcom outlines age-check methods ahead of under-16 social media ban

In one sentence
Ofcom has started setting out which age-checking methods platforms could use, which would turn a possible under-16 social media ban into a technical compliance task.

Ofcom has begun setting out which age-checking methods it would accept from online platforms. That matters because a proposed under-16 social media ban would be enforced by technology, not by a line in a statute. Every service with UK users would need a system that can tell a 15-year-old from a 16-year-old and prove it did so.

This piece rests on the BBC Technology report that flagged the story and on Ofcom's published approach to age assurance. The full text of the latest Ofcom document was not checked. The exact list of methods, the deadlines and the legal status of the ban are therefore not stated here as settled fact.

What a checking regime involves

Ofcom already has a template. Under the Online Safety Act 2023, its age-assurance duties for pornography services took effect in July 2025. The regulator's guidance there required "highly effective" age assurance and ruled out self-declaration, where a user simply ticks a box saying they are an adult.

That earlier guidance named the kinds of method that could qualify:

  • facial age estimation, where software guesses age from a selfie;
  • matching a photo ID against a live image;
  • open banking checks;
  • credit card checks;
  • mobile network operator checks;
  • digital identity wallets.

It is reasonable to expect any social media regime to draw on the same toolbox. But which methods Ofcom now accepts for social media, and at what accuracy, is the detail that decides the cost. That detail should be read from Ofcom's own publication.

Why the engineering is the story

A ban is only as strong as its checks. Facial estimation is fast and needs no ID document, but it is least reliable near the cut-off, and a 15-year-old and a 16-year-old can look much alike. ID matching is more accurate but means collecting passports or driving licences, and it excludes people who have neither. Bank and mobile-operator checks lean on data held by third parties.

Each route raises privacy questions. Platforms must show they can verify age without building databases of children's faces and documents. Regulators also expect these systems to be auditable, so a platform can show Ofcom how often checks fail and how often they are bypassed. Teenagers have a record of getting round such barriers with borrowed accounts, VPNs and spoofed images.

For platforms, this is a product and compliance cost, whether they build the checks themselves or buy them from specialist verification vendors. Smaller services without a large trust-and-safety team are likely to feel it most.

The open question: is it law?

The status of the ban is the missing piece. The brief that prompted this article does not say whether an under-16 ban is enacted, proposed or still in consultation. That decides whether platforms must build now or can wait.

The comparison most often drawn is with Australia, whose minimum-age law for social media took effect in December 2025. Australia put the duty on platforms and left them to choose how to check. Its experience of how well that works is the closest evidence available to UK policymakers, though it has not been verified for this piece.

What to watch

Three things will settle the weight of this story. One is whether the government confirms a legal minimum age and a start date. Another is whether Ofcom's accepted methods come with numerical accuracy thresholds. The third is how the largest platforms respond, since their choice of vendor and method will set the practical standard for everyone else.
