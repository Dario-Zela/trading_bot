Food waste could be a source of dietary fibre, experts say

In one sentence
UK food-science researchers say peels, rinds and stalks could add dietary fibre to foods, but the evidence is early, small-scale and not yet shown to work commercially.

Food scraps that normally go in the bin, such as peels, rinds, stalks and seeds, may be a cheap source of dietary fibre. That is the argument from food-science researchers in the UK. The idea is appealing, but the evidence is early and has not been shown to work at commercial scale.

A note on sourcing: the BBC report that prompted this piece was not retrievable during verification, and the study named in the brief could not be confirmed. The details below come from published UK work on the same idea. They may not be the study the BBC covered.

The problem being addressed

Most UK adults eat less fibre than the recommended 30g a day. The British Dietetic Association puts the share meeting that target at roughly one in ten. Separately, the UK throws away about 9.5 million tonnes of food a year, and fruit and vegetables make up 35% of the edible portion, according to King's College London.

The researchers' proposal joins the two problems. Many of the parts of plants that people discard are fibrous, so using them might raise fibre intake and cut waste together.

What the evidence shows so far

The most detailed public example is King's College London's "Root-to-Tip" project, led by Dr Rachel Gibson of the Department of Nutritional Sciences. It was funded in August 2024. Researchers measured fibre in the rinds, stalks and peels of twelve fruits and vegetables. They report that swapping the usual flesh for these parts, such as butternut squash peel, can raise a meal's fibre content by up to 15%. The university describes these as initial results. Its page does not say whether they have been peer reviewed or published.

King's chefs have turned the approach into 29 recipes on campus menus. The team plans to track sales, customer acceptance and plate waste against standard dishes. Graeme Collie, King's Food's executive chef, said some discarded parts, such as the seeds and string of butternut squash, contain more fibre than the parts usually eaten.

An earlier industry study points the same way. Reports on work by the research body Campden BRI describe replacing 20% of the wheat flour in tortillas with butternut squash peel powder. Fibre reportedly rose 97%, from 3.3g to 6.5g per 100g, enough to qualify for a "high fibre" claim. That figure comes from trade reports, not a peer-reviewed paper that could be checked here.

What remains unproven

Several questions are open, and none of the sources answers them.

  • Scale and cost. A canteen can cook peels into a dish. A factory needs a steady, safe supply of waste streams that are processed and dried. No cost figures were found.
  • Food safety. Waste used as an ingredient has to be kept apart from true refuse and handled under food-hygiene rules. The sources do not address how.
  • Taste and acceptance. Fibre-rich peels can change texture and flavour. King's says it will test whether customers buy the dishes.
  • Health effect. A higher fibre figure on a label is not evidence that people will eat more fibre over time or that their health improves. That would need longer trials.

What to watch

The King's team says it will assess sales, acceptability and plate waste, and seek external funding to test the approach at other UK universities and in home and retail settings. Peer-reviewed publication of the fibre measurements, and any cost figures from food manufacturers, would show whether this goes beyond a promising early idea.
