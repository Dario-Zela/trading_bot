Record purge count as Xi stresses 'party discipline'

In one sentence
Xi pressed for party discipline at a Beijing plenum as the party's own figures show record punishments, including the September expulsion of two former top generals.

Xi Jinping has used a four-day Communist Party gathering in Beijing to press officials for more loyalty and discipline. It comes as the party's own figures show the largest wave of punishments in his time in power.

The meeting, a plenum that opened on Monday 26 October, was billed in advance as an exercise in tightening the party's ranks, according to NPR's preview. Xi's emphasis on "party discipline" is not new. It has been the organising idea of his anti-corruption campaign since 2012. What has changed is the scale, and the rank of the people being caught.

The numbers

The figures come from different reporting periods and do not line up neatly. One commentary citing the party's anti-graft body puts the total punished across the party and government systems in 2025 at 983,000, a record. The same commentary says the number of minister-level officials disciplined rose to 115, from 73 in 2024. These are secondhand, and the watchdog's own tally for 2026 will not be published until the year ends. The direction is clear all the same, and the punishments are no longer confined to the lower ranks. Officials have also been disciplined in growing numbers for "formalism" and "bureaucratism", offences that amount to going through the motions rather than taking bribes.

The military has been hit hardest at the top. In September the party expelled two former senior generals, Zhang Youxia and Liu Zhenli, Pakistan's Express Tribune reported. They were accused of disloyalty, abandoning party principles, and failing to enforce discipline or supervise their staff and families. Zhang had been Xi's second-in-command on the Central Military Commission and was long seen as one of his closest allies in uniform.

What the purges are for

A record count can be read two ways. One reading is that corruption is getting worse. The other, which analysts tend to favour, is that Xi keeps raising the standard the party applies to its own people, so more conduct counts as a violation. The discipline campaign serves several purposes at once. It removes rivals and the doubtful, it tells everyone who remains where the limits are, and it keeps the machinery of the party and the army answerable to one man.

The cost is on the other side of the ledger. Officials who fear being the next case have reason to avoid decisions, delay approvals, or wait for explicit instructions from above. That makes policy more centralised, and perhaps more predictable in its direction, but it can also make local government slower and more cautious. Any effect on markets is speculative for now. No change in economic policy or key personnel has been confirmed in connection with the plenum.

What to watch

The plenum's closing communiqué, due when the meeting ends later this week, should show whether the party names new disciplinary targets or announces changes to senior appointments. After that, the anti-graft body's year-end statistics will show whether 2026 passes last year's record.
