WHO says up to 45% of dementia cases could be delayed or prevented

A healthy brain (left) compared with one affected by Alzheimer's disease, which accounts for 60-70% of dementia cases worldwide. — Garrondo / NIH Alzheimer's Disease Education and Referral Center, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
WHO says up to 45% of dementia cases are linked to modifiable habits, not drugs — guidance that could redirect health spending away from costly amyloid antibodies.

The World Health Organization has told its 194 member states that nearly half of dementia cases worldwide could be delayed or avoided altogether — not through a drug, but through the accumulation of ordinary health habits over a lifetime. The updated guidance, published July 15, is WHO's first revision of its dementia risk-reduction advice since 2019, and it lands at a moment when the pharmaceutical industry has just begun to see real revenue from the opposite approach: expensive antibody infusions designed to clear amyloid plaque after the disease has already taken hold.

The headline number — up to 45% of dementia risk attributable to modifiable factors — is drawn from population-level analysis of tobacco use, alcohol consumption, social isolation, physical inactivity, air pollution, and poorly managed conditions such as hypertension and diabetes.[^1] WHO's list of recommended interventions is notably unglamorous: cognitive training and social engagement for people with mild cognitive impairment, exercise, smoking cessation, a healthier diet, hearing aids where needed, and tighter control of blood pressure and blood sugar. The guidance explicitly advises against routine use of vitamin B, vitamin E, omega-3 supplements or multivitamins in people without a diagnosed deficiency, citing insufficient evidence.[^1] "Countries now have clear, evidence-based recommendations they can put into practice immediately to protect people's cognitive health," said Director-General Tedros Adhanom Ghebreyesus.[^1]

A prevention trial with real numbers behind it

WHO's timing is not incidental. Last year's U.S. POINTER trial, published in JAMA, gave the prevention case its first large randomized-controlled backing: a two-year, five-site study of 2,111 older adults found that a structured lifestyle program — combining exercise, the MIND diet, cognitive training and monthly blood-pressure checks — produced measurably better global cognition scores than a self-guided version of the same advice.[^2] That is a different kind of evidence than the clinical trials underpinning today's approved drugs, and considerably cheaper to deliver at scale.

The comparison matters for capital allocation. An estimated 57 million people live with dementia globally, with close to 10 million new diagnoses a year, at an annual economic cost of roughly $1.3 trillion, about half of it unpaid family care.[^1] Against that backdrop sits a fast-growing but still narrow drug market: Biogen and Eisai's anti-amyloid antibody Leqembi posted global sales of $168 million in the first quarter of 2026, up 74% year-on-year, with Eisai projecting blockbuster status — ¥250–280 billion, roughly $1.6–1.8 billion — by fiscal 2027, competing against Eli Lilly's donanemab (Kisunla).[^3] Analysts have pegged the broader Alzheimer's drug market at $13.7 billion by 2029.[^4]

None of this makes the antibody franchises worthless — WHO's guidance targets prevention in people without diagnosed disease, not treatment once dementia is established, and the two markets are not strict substitutes. But a global health authority formally prioritizing cheap, non-pharmacological intervention does shift the burden of proof onto disease-modifying drugs to justify their cost per patient against a prevention baseline that public payers may now feel obliged to fund first. National health systems built around WHO recommendations tend to reallocate budgets accordingly, and insurers watching both bills will be the ones deciding which model gets paid for first. The next test comes as more countries publish national dementia strategies referencing the updated guideline, and as Eisai's fiscal 2027 sales targets for Leqembi come due.
