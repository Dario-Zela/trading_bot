Fed's inflation hawks gain ground ahead of July 29 decision

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Markets price a 25-30% chance of a Fed hike on July 29 after Governor Waller, once the Fed's leading dove, joined the case for tightening as CPI holds at 4.2%.

The Federal Reserve has spent much of the past year telling markets a simple story: rate cuts were coming once the labour market cracked. That story is being rewritten in real time, and the man doing much of the rewriting is a policymaker who, only months ago, was making the loudest case for cuts.

Governor Christopher Waller told an audience in New York on July 13 that "if we get another hot reading on core inflation this week, then the FOMC will need to consider tightening monetary policy in the near term." Coming from Waller — previously the most consistent dove on the committee — the remark did more to move rate expectations than a dozen speeches from the hawks would have. Options and futures markets now price something close to a one-in-four to one-in-three chance that the Fed raises rates at its July 28-29 meeting, rather than holding, an outcome that was barely on the table a month ago.

A dove recants

Waller's discomfort is instructive because of where he started. Through 2025 he pushed harder than almost anyone on the Board for the Fed to begin cutting, arguing that a cooling labour market justified it even with inflation still above target. That calculus has flipped. "The desire to avoid past mistakes is often the author of new ones," he said, warning that the Fed must not repeat 2021 and 2022, when it waited too long to respond to rising prices. He was careful to hedge — the Fed "shouldn't fight the last war" by hiking reflexively — but the fact that a former dove is now openly discussing tightening tells its own story about where the committee's centre of gravity has moved.

He is not alone. At the Fed's June meeting, nine of 18 policymakers pencilled in at least one rate increase before year-end, and 17 of 18 judged inflation risks tilted to the upside — a marked hawkish shift from the projections issued just three months earlier. New chair Kevin Warsh, who took over from Jerome Powell in May after a Senate confirmation fight, has set the tone from the top, saying the Fed has "no tolerance" for persistently elevated inflation and vowing to make it "a thing of the past."

The numbers behind the pivot

The data giving Waller and Warsh cover is not subtle. Headline CPI ran at 4.2% year-on-year in the most recent reading, more than double the Fed's 2% target and the hottest print since April 2023. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, sits lower at 2.9% but has been drifting the wrong way for months. Waller has pointed to causes beyond the obvious energy shock — including AI-related investment and supply pressures — as evidence the problem is broader than a single commodity spike.

That energy shock is real, too: crude has traded above $100 a barrel since Houthi strikes on Saudi Aramco infrastructure escalated alongside the wider Iran conflict, feeding directly into headline inflation even where core price pressures are more contained. Bond markets have already recalibrated. The two-month Treasury yield, most sensitive to the immediate policy decision, has jumped 13 basis points to around 3.95%. Further out, the 10-year yield has climbed to roughly 4.71%, with the 30-year near 5.17% — levels not far off multi-year highs, and a signal that investors are pricing not just a possible July move but a policy rate that stays higher for longer.

What a hold would still mean

Even with hike odds elevated, a hold remains the base case — CME futures pricing has swung between roughly 35% and 46% probability of a hike over the past two weeks, more hawkish than the 25-30% seen in options markets, but a hold is still the more likely single outcome on July 29. The more consequential shift may be qualitative rather than quantitative: a Fed that has openly repositioned around inflation risk is one that will need sustained evidence of disinflation, not just a single soft print, before it entertains cuts again. Waller's own bar illustrates it well — he says he would need "several months of lower readings" on core inflation before feeling confident enough to hold rates rather than raise them, let alone start cutting.

For rate-sensitive markets, that recalibration matters more than the coin-flip odds on any single meeting. Long-duration Treasuries, growth equities and REITs priced for an imminent easing cycle are the most exposed if the inflation-first framing proves durable rather than a reaction to one hot summer of energy prices. Financials, by contrast, stand to benefit from a higher-for-longer rate path through improved net interest margins.

The Fed announces its decision at 2:00pm Eastern on July 29, with Warsh's press conference thirty minutes later. Unusually, this meeting carries no updated dot plot, which puts extra weight on the statement's language and on whether Waller — or any other member — dissents in favour of a hike. The next full test of the committee's new framework arrives with the August CPI report, the first inflation print policymakers will see after this meeting, and the one likely to determine whether July marks a pause in the hiking debate or its opening move.
