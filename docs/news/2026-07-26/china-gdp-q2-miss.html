China's economy grows 4.3% in Q2, weakest since 2022, as exports surge

The Yangshan deep-water port near Shanghai, part of the export infrastructure now carrying more of China's growth than domestic demand. — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's Q2 growth missed target at 4.3% as exports hit a record $412bn and car shipments topped 1 million a month for the first time — domestic demand did not follow.

China's economy grew 4.3% in the second quarter, the slowest pace in three and a half years and short of the 4.5–5% growth Beijing had targeted for 2026. The headline number would ordinarily read as a modest miss — a rounding error against the 5% expansion recorded in the first quarter. But the composition beneath it tells a sharper story: exports jumped 27% in June to a record $412 billion, and Chinese car exports topped one million units in a single month for the first time on record. Growth arrived, in other words, but it arrived from abroad.

Where the growth isn't

The domestic side of the ledger is where the target slipped away. Retail sales rose 1% year-on-year in June, a recovery of sorts after May's 0.6% decline — the first monthly drop in Chinese consumer spending since December 2022. Property investment fell 18% in the first half of the year, a deeper contraction than the 16.2% decline recorded through the first five months, underscoring that the real-estate downturn now in its fifth year has not found a floor. Factory-gate prices turned positive last month for the first time in more than three years, but for the wrong reason: the rise reflects commodity costs, particularly oil, pushing input prices up rather than any pickup in industrial pricing power.

Those oil costs trace back to the same conflict rattling markets everywhere. Crude touched $114 a barrel in May as the Iran war disrupted shipping near the Strait of Hormuz — the same dynamic behind this week's oil surge past $100 following the Aramco strike. China has so far absorbed the shock better than most importers, helped by strategic reserves and a diversified energy mix, but higher fuel and input costs squeeze exactly the households and factories struggling to spend and invest.

Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for Asia-Pacific at Natixis, put the imbalance bluntly: "No domestic demand, all about exports — it's really quite unsustainable, to be frank."

Chips, cars and a record export month

What is sustaining growth is a narrower slice of the economy that is, for now, thriving. Integrated-circuit exports surged 96.1% year-on-year in the first half, reaching $177.3 billion, as global demand for AI infrastructure pulls hard on Chinese semiconductor and computer-parts manufacturing. Overall exports were up 17.6% for the first half and accelerated sharply into June's 27% jump — a pace that outran even bullish analyst forecasts.

The automotive numbers are the most visually striking piece of the divergence. China shipped 1.037 million vehicles in June, up 11.6% from May and 75.1% from a year earlier, with new-energy vehicles accounting for more than half the total. It is the first month vehicle exports have cleared the million mark, and it caps a first half in which total car exports reached 5.1 million units, up 65.3% year-on-year. BYD alone shipped 471,091 vehicles overseas in the second quarter, up 82.5% on the year, and is targeting 1.5 million in overseas sales for 2026 — a target that would mark another jump from the 1.05 million it shipped in 2025.

An economy leaning on someone else's demand

The split has a name among China-watchers now circulating well beyond research notes: the "two-track economy," in which advanced manufacturing — chips, batteries, EVs — races ahead on foreign orders while the domestic side, tied to property and household confidence, stagnates. It is not a new critique of Chinese growth, but the scale of the June export and car-shipment numbers makes the imbalance harder to wave away as a one-quarter blip.

Beijing's own response suggests officials see the risk. The government has released its first five-year consumption plan, aiming to lift annual retail sales toward roughly $9 trillion by 2030, and fiscal spending is expected to pick back up after losing momentum in the second quarter. But analysts broadly expect calibrated, targeted measures rather than the kind of large-scale stimulus that might jolt households back into spending — a signal that Beijing is not yet treating the domestic slowdown as an emergency.

For traders, the immediate read-through is less about China's growth number and more about what it implies elsewhere: a manufacturing base still capable of flooding global markets with cheap EVs and chips even as its own consumers stay on the sidelines, and a commodity-demand picture increasingly hostage to export orders rather than construction or retail. The next test comes with July trade data in mid-August and any follow-through on Beijing's consumption plan — alongside a Federal Reserve decision on July 29 that will shape the dollar and, with it, the currency backdrop against which Chinese exporters are competing.
