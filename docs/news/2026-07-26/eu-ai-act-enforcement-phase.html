EU AI Act enters enforcement phase ahead of August transparency deadline

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which finalised the AI Act's Digital Omnibus package in July. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
EU AI Act transparency rules bind from August 2, but it's still unclear how much applies to trading algorithms versus the chatbots and research tools around them.

Eight days from now, a new line in EU law starts to bite. From August 2, any company operating in the European Union that puts an AI chatbot, voice assistant or content-generating tool in front of a customer has to say so — clearly, and in a way an ordinary person would notice. That includes, potentially, a chunk of the algorithmic trading and fintech industry, and a fair few firms in London, Frankfurt and New York are still working out whether it includes them.

The rule is Article 50 of the EU's AI Act, and it became binding law on July 10, when the bloc's long-fought "Digital Omnibus on AI" package finally cleared its last hurdle — signed on July 8 and in force three days later. That sounds like Brussels tightening the screws. In one respect it did the opposite.

A deadline that moved, and one that didn't

The Digital Omnibus's headline achievement was a delay, not a crackdown. The toughest part of the AI Act — obligations on "high-risk" AI systems under Annex III, things like credit-scoring tools and insurance-pricing models — was due to bind from August 2, 2026. The Omnibus pushed that back sixteen months, to December 2, 2027, and further still, to August 2028, for AI embedded in products already covered by separate EU safety law. European Parliament and Council both signed off in June after industry lobbying that the original timetable was unworkable before technical standards even existed.

What didn't move is Article 50. Chatbot disclosure, deepfake labelling and machine-readable marking of AI-generated text, audio, image or video all stay on the original August 2 clock, with only a narrow four-month grace period for content-marking obligations on systems already live before that date. The European Commission published its final guidance on the transparency rules on July 20 — less than two weeks before enforcement starts. Fines run up to €15 million or 3 percent of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, and the EU's AI Office now has the power to bring them.

Where trading platforms actually sit

Here is the part causing the most head-scratching on trading floors: pure execution algorithms — the code that decides when and how to buy or sell — are not on the Annex III high-risk list at all. Credit scoring and insurance underwriting are named explicitly; algorithmic trading is not, and legal commentary describes it as more likely to land in the Act's lighter "limited-risk" tier, if it is captured at all. Trading firms already answer to a separate, older regime — MiFID II's algorithmic trading controls, which mandate kill switches, testing records and reporting to national regulators — and that framework, not the AI Act, remains the primary rulebook for the trading logic itself.

The uncertainty sits one layer up, in everything wrapped around the algorithm: robo-advice chatbots that recommend products to retail clients, AI-generated market commentary and research notes sent to subscribers, automated voice systems fielding client calls. Those are exactly the functions Article 50 was written for — anything that talks to a natural person or generates synthetic content has to disclose itself — and plenty of trading and wealth platforms bundle at least one of them into their client-facing stack. Law firms covering the sector, including Stephenson Harwood's ongoing AI briefings for financial services clients, have been urging firms since the spring to inventory every customer-facing AI feature separately from the trading engine itself, precisely because the two are judged under different rules with different clocks.

Who enforces it, and how hard

Supervision is split by sector rather than centralised. National financial regulators — the Central Bank of Ireland has said explicitly it will police AI use in the firms it already supervises — sit alongside the EU AI Office, creating the kind of layered oversight that gave firms pause about the original 2026 deadline in the first place. FINRA's 2026 oversight report, covering the US side of the same firms' operations, flags generative AI and "agentic" trading tools as a standing supervisory priority, with unchecked autonomy and thin audit trails singled out as the main risks. None of that maps neatly onto Article 50's narrower ask, which is disclosure, not a ban or a licensing regime — but neither regulators nor firms have much precedent to lean on for how strictly the EU intends to police the boundary.

The next test comes fast. August 2 is the compliance date; whatever enforcement action follows in the weeks after will be the first real signal of how literally Brussels reads its own transparency rule, and how much latitude firms get while the high-risk regime they were bracing for sits parked until December 2027.
