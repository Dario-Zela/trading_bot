ECB shortlists ten designs for redesigned euro banknotes

In one sentence
The ECB unveiled ten finalist designs — birds and rivers versus Callas, Beethoven and da Vinci — for the first full euro banknote redesign since 2002.

Christine Lagarde held up a stack of paper roughly the size and shape of money, except that it depicted a kingfisher on one note and Leonardo da Vinci on another, and told reporters that this was, in effect, Europe arguing with itself about what it wants to look like.

The European Central Bank has spent the past four years working toward this moment: on 23 July it unveiled ten shortlisted designs for the next generation of euro banknotes, the first full redesign since the currency entered circulation in January 2002. The shortlist splits cleanly into two rival visions. One, called "European Culture," puts historical figures on the notes — Maria Callas on the €5, Ludwig van Beethoven on the €10, Marie Curie on the €20, Miguel de Cervantes on the €50, Leonardo da Vinci on the €100, Bertha von Suttner on the €200. The other, "Rivers and Birds," traces water from an alpine spring to the open sea, pairing a bird with a European Union institution on each denomination — a wallcreeper and the European Parliament on the €5, working up through a kingfisher and the Commission, bee-eaters and the ECB itself, a white stork and the Court of Justice, an avocet and the Council, and finally a northern gannet over the Court of Auditors.

"Euro banknotes are not merely a means of payment," Lagarde said in presenting the finalists. "They are one of the most tangible expressions of Europe. By combining beauty and meaning, they will strengthen our common identity." It is a large claim for a piece of polymer that mostly gets folded into wallets and cash machines, but it is also the ECB's actual argument: that what's printed on a currency is one of the few visual symbols shared by 350 million people across twenty different countries, and that after twenty-four years the symbol is due an update.

How the shortlist got made

The process behind the ten finalists was, by central-bank standards, unusually open. More than 1,200 designers across the EU applied to a public competition; 25 were commissioned to develop full banknote series around one or both of the two themes, producing 39 complete submissions in total. An independent jury of 21 experts — drawn from graphic design, communications, history and, notably, neuroscience — judged the submissions anonymously before narrowing them to ten. The neuroscience seat on the jury is a small tell: banknote design has to work at a glance, be hard to forge and easy to authenticate by touch for visually impaired users, which is as much a cognitive-science problem as an aesthetic one.

That process now hands the decision to the public, sort of. The ECB has opened an online survey covering all ten designs that runs until 21 September, alongside a separate poll of a representative sample of euro-area citizens. Neither vote is binding — the Governing Council makes the final call, expected around the end of the year — but both are being read as a temperature check on how continental Europe wants to present itself, after a currency union that has spent much of the past six years absorbing energy shocks, a pandemic and a war on its eastern border.

Culture, nature, or both

Early public reaction, per social media chatter tracked by outlets including Smithsonian, has tilted toward the birds. Commenters have called the riverine designs "beautiful" and "simple"; the cultural-icon set has drawn a subtler complaint, that six historical figures can't represent the breadth of nationalities within a currency union this size, no matter how carefully chosen. The ECB has described the bird imagery as meant to evoke "dialogue and interdependence between our societies and nature," which reads like a deliberate hedge against the culture theme's inevitable arguments about whose composers and scientists made the cut.

None of this touches monetary policy. The new notes, whichever theme wins, will carry the same denominations and the same legal-tender status as the ones currently in wallets; old notes keep circulating and keep their value, with no forced changeover date yet set. This is a branding exercise, not a currency operation — closer in spirit to a national mint's coin redesign than to anything that shows up in a rate-setting statement.

What happens next is straightforward: the public surveys close on 21 September, the Governing Council reviews the results alongside the jury's own recommendation, and a final design — or possibly a hybrid drawing on both themes — is expected to be picked before the year is out. Actual printing and circulation, on past ECB form, would follow years later.
