PlayStation Network suffers sixth outage in four months

A PlayStation 5 console and DualSense controller — PSN authentication and store access were both knocked out in the July 24 outage. — Wfrmsf / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
PSN's sixth outage in four months knocked out logins, the Store and multiplayer for over six hours on July 24, with Sony still silent on the cause.

PlayStation Network went down worldwide on the morning of July 24, knocking out account sign-ins, the PlayStation Store and online multiplayer for more than six hours. It was the sixth time this has happened since March — roughly once every three weeks — and Sony still hasn't said why.

The outage began around 8:30am Eastern time and hit almost every core PSN function at once: account management, the Store, cloud saves, Trophy syncing, party chat and licence checks needed to launch digital games. At the Esports World Cup, organisers had to delay the EA Sports FC 26 championship because players couldn't get online. Sony's status page eventually listed every major service as impaired, then quietly switched back to "all services are up and running" by mid-afternoon without an explanation in between.

The likely trigger, though Sony has not confirmed it, lines up with a known networking problem at Amazon Web Services' US-WEST-2 region that morning — the same slice of cloud infrastructure PSN leans on for authentication and matchmaking. If that's right, it wouldn't be the first time an AWS hiccup has taken down a platform that outsources its backbone rather than running it in-house; it would just be the latest reminder of how much of the internet's "resilience" is actually one company's uptime.

A pattern, not an incident

What makes July 24 notable isn't the outage itself — networks fail — but the company it keeps. PSN has now gone down at least six times since March 21, with further disruptions logged on April 15-16, April 23, May 21 and June 21 before July's episode. Independent monitoring by outage-tracker IsDown found that Sony never formally acknowledged several of the earlier incidents on its own status page, even while player reports piled up on Downdetector and social media in real time.

Sony has not offered compensation for any of the 2026 outages, a contrast with 2025, when a nearly day-long failure prompted the company to credit affected accounts. That restraint is more conspicuous now: Sony raised PlayStation Plus subscription prices in May, meaning subscribers are paying more for a service that has been less dependable than at any point since the catastrophic 2011 breach that took PSN offline for weeks.

None of this shows up yet in Sony's numbers — the games division just posted record operating profit of ¥463.3 billion ($2.9 billion) for the fiscal year through March, and PlayStation's monthly active users sit at 125 million, down from a seasonal December peak but not obviously outage-driven. That's the tension for anyone pricing Sony as a subscription and services business rather than a hardware maker: recurring revenue depends on recurring trust, and six unexplained outages in four months is not the record of a company treating its network as critical infrastructure.

Sony has not published a post-incident report for any of the 2026 outages, nor said whether it plans to. The next test comes whenever the seventh one lands — and whether Sony explains it before players find out for themselves.
