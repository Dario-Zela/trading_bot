Pogačar seals fourth Tour de France title in wildfire-shortened finale

Tadej Pogačar in the yellow jersey on the Champs-Élysées after sealing his Tour de France title. — Eric Salard / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Pogačar sealed his fourth Tour title as wildfires forced organisers to cut the final stage to 89km; Van Aert won a rain-soaked Champs-Élysées finish.

Tadej Pogačar rolled onto the Champs-Élysées in the yellow jersey he had worn for most of three weeks, but the parade waiting for him there was smaller than the one Paris had planned. Sunday's final stage of the Tour de France was cut almost in half — from 133km to 89km — after French authorities pulled security officers off the route to fight wildfires that have scorched more than 36,000 hectares of forest in the country's southwest. Pogačar still won the race. He just won it in a Tour that had to bend around a national emergency to finish at all.

The Slovenian's overall victory, his fourth, was barely in doubt. The drama on the final day belonged to Wout van Aert, who attacked alone up the rain-slicked cobbles of Montmartre and held off the bunch to take the stage — a result that, for once, had nothing to do with the wildfires and everything to do with a rider descending a wet Parisian hill faster than anyone thought wise.

A lead too large to threaten

By the time the race reached Paris, the general classification had long stopped being a contest. Pogačar arrived in the capital with a buffer of six minutes and 26 seconds over Remco Evenepoel, built stage by stage through the Alps and Pyrenees and defended, rather than extended, on the brutal Alpe d'Huez finish two days earlier — a stage won by Richard Carapaz, who used it to cement his grip on the King of the Mountains jersey while Pogačar shipped a token 39 seconds he could easily afford. It was a Tour in which Pogačar's main opponent was rarely another rider; Friday's Alpe d'Huez stage was instead marred by crowd trouble, including a crash caused by an unruly spectator that hospitalised climber Einer Rubio with facial injuries requiring 20 stitches. Against that backdrop, a shortened final stage was, in a strange way, the least disruptive story of the week.

A parade route rewritten by fire

The change to Stage 21 was announced with uncharacteristic bluntness for an event built on tradition and ceremony. Fires around the Arcachon basin near Bordeaux and near Biscarrosse in the Landes had already forced roughly 200,000 people from their homes, part of a wider French wildfire season that has burned an estimated 100,000 hectares nationwide this summer and pushed the government to activate the European Union's Civil Protection Mechanism, requesting international firefighting support. With the interior ministry redeploying officers earmarked for crowd control in Paris to reinforce the fire lines instead, race organiser ASO said it was adapting the route from Thoiry to Paris "in the spirit of national solidarity with the affected regions and populations." The stage kept its scheduled centrepiece — three ascents of the Butte de Montmartre, introduced to the race only last year — but dropped two of its outer loops, starting and finishing on the Champs-Élysées instead of threading further out into the western suburbs.

It is a small adjustment set against a large one: the Tour has survived two world wars, doping scandals and a pandemic-forced reschedule, but rarely has it had to hand over its own security detail to a wildfire response. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has pointed to climate change as a driver of the unusually early and intense fire season, a diagnosis that sits uneasily next to a sporting calendar still built for the stable Julys of an earlier decade.

Van Aert claims the wet finish

The shortened course did nothing to dull the finale itself. Van Aert launched his move roughly 400 metres from the summit of the last Montmartre climb, riding away on wet cobbles that had turned the descent into a genuine hazard, and held his gap to the line by 19 seconds over Davide Ballerini. It was his tenth career Tour stage win. "The rain made things a bit difficult, but I managed to stay upright and had the full support of my teammates," the Belgian said afterwards, adding that winning again on the Champs-Élysées felt "really special," particularly with Montmartre now a fixture of the finale rather than a one-off novelty.

What the truncation signals

None of this changes the arithmetic at the top of the general classification. Pogačar's fourth title is secure regardless of how many kilometres the final stage covered. What lingers is the precedent: a 122-year-old race, whose entire romance rests on a fixed route through a stable French summer, was rewritten days before its finish because the state's emergency services had somewhere more urgent to be. Organisers have faced extreme heat before and rerouted individual mountain stages for storms, but conceding security resources to a wildfire crisis is a different order of disruption — one that fits a pattern rather than standing as an isolated inconvenience. The next test comes quickly: France's fire season typically runs into September, and ASO will have to decide, well before next July's route announcement, how much resilience to build into a race designed around the assumption that the weather, at least, could be counted on.
