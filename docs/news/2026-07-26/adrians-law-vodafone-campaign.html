Family of drowned Vodafone manager campaign for 'Adrian's law'

In one sentence
The family of a Vodafone manager found drowned after a franchise deal turned sour is pushing for 'Adrian's law' to protect future franchisees.

The lake at Bosworth Water Park, in Leicestershire, is the kind of place families go for pedalos and picnics. In February 2018, Adrian Howe walked into it and did not come home. He was 51, a Vodafone store manager of more than two decades' standing, and by his family's account a man who had spent the preceding weeks convinced that a franchise deal he'd been pushed into was about to ruin him.

"We need to know that he didn't die in vain."

That is what his widow, Tracey, has said of the campaign she has waged since — one that has now reached the door of Downing Street and put a phrase into circulation that campaigners are calling Adrian's law: a proposed statutory shake-up of the power imbalance between big franchisors and the small operators who run their shopfronts.

A thriving store swapped for a struggling one

Howe had been offered a franchise in 2017 on the strength of a strong-performing outlet. By the family's account, Vodafone changed the terms before the deal closed, handing him a weaker store instead — one he had to open within days, having secured it, his family say, against the family home. Relatives told the Yorkshire Post he had become fixated on the belief the arrangement would leave them financially ruined. He drowned before the store's doors opened.

His case became public only after the Guardian revealed in December 2024 that Howe was one of a much larger group: 62 former Vodafone franchisees, close to 40% of its UK network, later brought a High Court claim alleging the company had cut sales commissions, levied heavy fines for minor errors and pushed operators into taking on debt — losses the claimants put as high as £85m. Some described suicidal thoughts brought on by the financial strain; the dispute has drawn repeated comparisons in Parliament to the Post Office Horizon scandal, another case of a corporate franchise model colliding badly with the people running its counters.

From constituency case to the Commons

Tracey Howe is not a claimant in that suit — the family's fight has always been separate, aimed less at compensation than at changing the rules for whoever comes next. Through her MP, Johanna Baxter, she put the question to the prime minister directly during business questions: would the government review franchising legislation so that no one else felt "undue pressure" signing away their savings for a shop? Keir Starmer said he would look closely at the case. Ministers have since floated a statutory code of practice or a national arbitration system for franchise disputes — the kind of formal referee that currently doesn't exist in UK franchising law, which still runs largely on ordinary contract terms and voluntary codes.

The backdrop shifted again this month. On 17 July, Vodafone reached a confidential settlement with the 62 franchisees, ending the High Court claim before it reached trial — no admission of liability, terms undisclosed, on top of £4.9m the company had already paid out in goodwill and refunded fines. For the Howe family, watching from outside that settlement, it closes one chapter without answering the question they've been asking for two years: not what Vodafone owes its former operators, but what protection the law owes the next Adrian Howe before he signs.

What's next

The Department for Business and Trade has yet to say whether a formal code or arbitration body will follow the ministerial noises made in the Commons — that decision, still pending, is the one Tracey Howe's campaign is aimed squarely at.
