Rayner rules out rent controls, admits 1.5m homes target is 'challenging'

Angela Rayner, reappointed Housing Secretary in Andy Burnham's first cabinet, has ruled out rent controls for England. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Rayner ruled out rent controls despite Burnham's earlier openness to them, while admitting Labour's 1.5m homes pledge has grown 'more challenging' as construction costs rise and completions lag.

Angela Rayner has ruled out rent controls in England, telling BBC Breakfast that the government would not follow the path taken north of the border — and, in the same breath, conceded that Labour's marquee promise to build 1.5 million homes this parliament has become harder to keep.

"We won't be looking at rent controls if that's what you're getting at at the moment," Rayner said. "That is not something that we're looking to do because we've seen what's happened in areas like Scotland. It hasn't necessarily brought rents down."

The remarks matter beyond the housing brief because of who was floating the opposite idea a week earlier: Andy Burnham, the former Greater Manchester mayor who became prime minister after Sir Keir Starmer's departure, and who called for a rent freeze as recently as three years ago. As mayor, Burnham had kept the door open to capping rents as part of a wider package to ease living costs. His own housing secretary has now shut it.

A demotion for rent caps, a promotion for tribunals

Rayner's preferred instrument is the Renters' Rights Act, which took effect earlier this year and lets tenants challenge rent increases at the First-tier Tribunal rather than have a government-set ceiling imposed on landlords. Under the Act, a landlord can only raise rent to prevailing market rates, tenants can contest an increase for a £47 fee, and the tribunal can confirm or lower a proposed rent but never raise it. "I don't think that rent freezes, rent controls, is a way forward at the moment," Rayner said, arguing the Act was "already having a significant impact on the market."

The Scotland comparison is doing real work in that argument. Rent controls introduced there during the cost-of-living crisis were followed by landlords exiting the market and supply tightening in cities including Edinburgh — precisely the outcome critics warn England risks if it goes further than tribunal-based challenges. Landlord groups, including the National Residential Landlords Association, have welcomed Rayner's position, while tenant campaigners who backed Burnham's earlier comments will see it as a retreat.

The harder admission

More consequential for the government's economic credibility is what Rayner said about supply. "It certainly got more challenging, and we've seen things like construction costs go up, which has had a real negative impact on that," she said of the 1.5 million target — first set when she held the same job in 2024. "It was when I made the target, but I'm going to keep the target."

The numbers explain the caution. England built roughly 204,500 homes in the twelve months to March 2026, barely up on the previous quarter and well below both the government's own assessment of housing need — about 367,000 a year — and the pace required to hit 1.5 million by the next election. Housing starts fell 4% in the first quarter of the year and NHBC data point to completions staying under 220,000 in 2026-27. Rayner is not abandoning the pledge, but she is no longer pretending it is on track.

What it signals

Taken together, the two positions describe a government leaning on supply-side fixes — planning reform, tribunal-mediated rents — rather than price controls, even as its own delivery numbers undercut confidence in that strategy. For housebuilders and landlords, ruling out rent caps removes one source of policy risk; the admission on the 1.5 million target adds another, since it invites the same scrutiny already building around Burnham's fiscal pledges and the gilt market's unease with them.

The next test comes with the government's autumn housing statistics and any planning-reform detail Rayner sets out to try to accelerate starts. Until completions move decisively above 220,000 a year, the gap between the target and the trend line will keep widening — and keep this story in the headlines.
