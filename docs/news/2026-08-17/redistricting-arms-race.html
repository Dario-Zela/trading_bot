Redistricting arms race escalates ahead of US midterms

The U.S. Capitol, where control of the House majority is increasingly being pre-decided by state-level mapmakers. — U.S. House of Representatives / Office of Rep. Robert Garcia, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nine states have redrawn House maps since last year, handing Republicans a net edge of roughly ten seats before a single midterm vote is cast.

In a normal decade, congressional maps get redrawn once — after the census, then left alone for ten years. Not this cycle. In the eighteen months before the November midterms, nine states have torn up their district lines and started again, not because populations shifted but because whoever held the pen in a given statehouse decided the fight was worth having. The result is a House majority that may turn less on how people vote than on who drew the map they voted on.

The scramble began in Texas last summer, when President Trump leaned on state Republicans to add up to five more GOP-friendly seats mid-decade. Missouri followed with a special session that carved Kansas City into surrounding rural districts, signed into law on September 28. Ohio's redistricting commission approved a map on October 31 that turns a 10-5 Republican edge into 12-3. North Carolina redrew its 1st District — previously anchored by the state's Black-majority counties — to lean conservative, worth another seat to the GOP. California voters answered in November, passing Proposition 50 to suspend the state's independent redistricting commission for this cycle only and add roughly five Democratic-leaning seats. Utah went the other way by court order: a state judge threw out a Republican map in November and installed one with a Democratic-leaning Salt Lake City seat.

Then came a second wave once the Supreme Court, ruling 6-3 in Louisiana v. Callais in late April, weakened the Voting Rights Act's protection against dismantling majority-minority districts. Florida's legislature moved within days, shifting four Democratic-held districts onto more Republican-favorable lines; Governor Ron DeSantis signed it May 4. Tennessee broke up its sole Democratic seat, centred on majority-Black Memphis, the same week. Louisiana postponed its own House primary to redraw around the ruling, expecting to hand Republicans one or two more seats. Alabama teed up contingency maps in case courts lift injunctions blocking an earlier GOP redraw.

The tally

Add it up and Republicans are positioned to gain as many as sixteen seats from the new maps, against roughly six for Democrats — a net swing of about ten seats before a single midterm vote is cast, in a chamber Republicans currently hold by single digits. Virginia Democrats tried to claw some of that back with a ballot measure that would have flipped four Republican seats; voters approved it in April, but the Virginia Supreme Court struck the amendment down on May 8, leaving the state's old lines in place.

Running out the clock

The timing is not incidental. Maps finalised in the fall of 2025 or the spring of 2026 leave plaintiffs only months to litigate before ballots are printed — Wisconsin's pending congressional challenge, for instance, is not expected to be resolved until after November regardless of its merits. Texas's own map survived only because the Supreme Court intervened in April, overturning a lower court that had found it diluted Black and Latino voting strength, and left it in force through 2030.

What happens next is mostly procedural rather than dramatic: primaries run through the new lines this autumn, remaining lawsuits in Louisiana, Alabama and Wisconsin grind on in the background, and the actual verdict — whether the mapmakers' math holds up against real turnout — arrives with the midterm results themselves on November 3.
