Record rains trigger deadly landslide in South Korea's Geoje

An LNG carrier docked at Samsung Heavy Industries' shipyard in Geoje, one of two major shipbuilders idled by Monday's flooding. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nearly 800mm of rain in under 48 hours triggered a fatal landslide in Geoje, South Korea's shipbuilding hub, exposing gaps in climate risk pricing.

Geoje, the island city on South Korea's southern coast that builds much of the world's LNG carriers and drillships, took on more rain in 36 hours this weekend than it typically sees in two months. By 8 a.m. Monday, the city had recorded 805.7mm of rainfall since Saturday — with a peak intensity of 124.5mm in a single hour, the heaviest hourly rate measured there since records began in 1972, according to the Korea Meteorological Administration.

The rain triggered a landslide that struck an apartment block in Okpo-dong just before 5 a.m. Monday, burying a first-floor unit. Rescuers pulled two residents from the debris; a third was found in cardiac arrest and later died in hospital. Two others suffered minor injuries. Rivers overflowed across the city, roads went under water, and public transport was suspended in several districts. Authorities issued an emergency evacuation alert to all residents shortly after 2 a.m., and hundreds moved to shelters as more than 20 emergency calls came in from people trapped in flooded homes and vehicles. Neighbouring Tongyeong recorded 671.9mm over the same period.

"All residents ordered to evacuate" — Gyeongsangnam-do provincial emergency alert, Monday, August 17

A blind spot in risk pricing

Geoje is not an ordinary coastal town. It hosts the two largest shipyards of Hanwha Ocean and Samsung Heavy Industries, which between them build a large share of the world's LNG carriers, drillships and naval vessels — output that feeds directly into global energy and defence supply chains. Both companies told staff to stay home Monday as flooding cut transport links, an operational disruption that barely registers as a line item against annual output but illustrates a broader mispricing problem.

South Korea's own meteorological data show why. The KMA's 113-year climate analysis, released this year, found national precipitation has risen roughly 17.8mm per decade, with the clearest trend not in total rainfall but in its concentration: more days with hourly bursts above 50mm, arriving in shorter, sharper windows that overwhelm drainage and slope stability built for a gentler climate. Sixteen locations recorded hourly rainfall above 100mm in 2024; fifteen did in 2025. Geoje's Monday reading beat both.

Equity investors have historically treated single-day flooding as a rounding error for manufacturers with global order books, and reinsurers price Korean flood exposure on decades-old return periods that this kind of event is quietly revising upward. Research from the Stockholm Environment Institute this year flagged the same gap in global supply-chain reinsurance more broadly: physical climate risk is accumulating faster than the models used to price it are being updated. A shipyard idled for a day is a footnote. A monsoon season that keeps delivering 1-in-50-year rainfall in consecutive years is a different kind of liability, and one that current cost-of-capital assumptions for South Korea's industrial coast do not yet reflect.

Recovery crews were still clearing mud and debris from Okpo-dong on Monday afternoon, with more rain forecast through the week as the monsoon trough lingers over the southern coast. The next test of that trough — and of how quickly Geoje's shipyards return to full output — will be a clearer signal of how much slack really exists in the region's climate risk buffer.
