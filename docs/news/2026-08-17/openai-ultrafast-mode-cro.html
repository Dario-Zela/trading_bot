OpenAI unveils 'Ultrafast' GPT-5.6 mode, names new chief revenue officer

In one sentence
OpenAI unveiled a 14x-faster 'Ultrafast' mode for GPT-5.6 Sol and hired its second chief revenue officer in eight months, part of a broader industry pivot to selling speed and deployment over raw model capability.

OpenAI spent this week making its case that speed, not raw intelligence, is now the thing standing between AI and everyday business use. On 13 August it began previewing "Ultrafast" mode for GPT-5.6 Sol, a version of its flagship model that runs up to 14 times faster than the standard tier — and, on the same day, replaced its chief revenue officer for the second time in under a year.

Neither move expands what the model can do. Both are about getting it into more hands, faster, and selling it harder once it's there.

What 14x actually means

Ultrafast pushes GPT-5.6 Sol to roughly 750 output tokens per second, according to Cerebras, the chipmaker supplying the hardware behind it. The gain doesn't come from a better model — it's the same Sol, run on Cerebras's wafer-scale chips, which keep the model's weights on-chip in 44GB of on-die memory rather than shuttling them back and forth from separate memory banks the way conventional GPUs do. That's the bottleneck Cerebras is selling itself as having solved.

On OpenAI's own GDP-Val benchmark — a test built around tasks like drafting legal briefs, financial models and engineering reports — the company reported a 5.6x end-to-end speedup with no measurable drop in output quality. That's the number that matters more than the headline 14x: it suggests the trade is closer to a free lunch than a speed-for-accuracy swap, at least on OpenAI's own test.

The catch is availability. Ultrafast is a limited preview, offered first through OpenAI's API to a hand-picked set of customers, with no public pricing and no confirmed general-release date. Early access has reportedly gone to coding tools, financial research desks and customer-support platforms — plus OpenAI's own incident-response teams, who are using it to read logs and synthesise traces faster during outages. The deal sits inside a much larger arrangement: OpenAI committed roughly $10bn to Cerebras compute earlier this year as part of a multi-year plan to deploy 750 megawatts of Cerebras hardware.

A second revenue chief in eight months

The same day, OpenAI named Dali Rajic — most recently president and chief operating officer at cybersecurity firm Wiz, before that in senior roles at Zscaler and AppDynamics — as its new chief revenue officer. He replaces Denise Dresser, the former Slack chief executive who took the job just eight months ago, in December 2025. OpenAI says Dresser will stay on through a transition before moving on.

Two CROs in under a year is a fast churn rate for a role that exists specifically to build durable relationships with enterprise buyers. It reads less as a verdict on Dresser personally and more as a sign OpenAI is still working out what kind of sales operation it needs as it moves from selling a chatbot subscription to selling infrastructure commitments measured in gigawatts and billions of dollars.

The industry-wide shift

OpenAI isn't alone in swapping research bragging rights for deployment plumbing this month. Microsoft has rolled out a "Frontier Company" initiative backed by $2.5bn to embed thousands of engineers directly inside enterprise customers' operations. Amazon Web Services announced roughly $1bn for a similar forward-deployed engineering push. Meta has been weighing a reported multibillion-dollar offer to sell Anthropic — a direct competitor to its own Llama models — spare compute capacity, which would be a strange arrangement if the race were still purely about who builds the smartest model.

Taken together, the pattern is a labs sector that has quietly concluded the constraint on AI adoption isn't capability anymore — it's latency, cost, and the unglamorous work of getting a model wired into a company's actual workflows. A model that can reason brilliantly but takes ten seconds to answer is a worse product, in commercial terms, than one that's merely competent and answers instantly.

What to watch next is whether Ultrafast graduates from limited preview to a priced, generally available tier — and at what premium over standard GPT-5.6 Sol. That number, once it exists, will say a lot about whether frontier labs think speed is a feature customers will pay extra for, or one they'll expect for free.
