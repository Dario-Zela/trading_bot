Arron Banks admits hiring investigators to probe Tory chair

Kevin Hollinrake, the Conservative Party chairman, said investigators were sent to dig into him after he raised questions about Reform UK's finances. — Chris McAndrew / UK Parliament (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Arron Banks confirmed hiring private investigators to probe Tory chair Kevin Hollinrake and a Sunday Times reporter after they scrutinised Farage's £5m gift.

Kevin Hollinrake had been asking a straightforward question: where did the £5m come from? He got an answer of sorts — not from Nigel Farage, but from a private intelligence firm that started digging into his own affairs.

Arron Banks, the insurance millionaire who bankrolled the Leave.EU campaign and remains one of Reform UK's most prominent backers, has confirmed commissioning investigators to look into the Conservative Party chairman. The admission, first reported by the Mail on Sunday and amplified across Westminster this week, came as Banks was also revealed to have ordered a separate probe into a Sunday Times journalist covering the same story.

What Hollinrake was asking

As party chairman, Hollinrake has led the Conservatives' push for scrutiny of Reform UK's finances — specifically a £5m gift Farage received from Christopher Harborne, a Thailand-based crypto and aviation billionaire, shortly before Farage stood in last year's general election. Farage did not declare the gift at the time. The Electoral Commission and the Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards both opened inquiries into the payment in May, and the Conservatives referred Farage to the standards commissioner under rules requiring MPs to declare any "personal benefit" received in the twelve months before taking office.

It was in this capacity — asking the kind of questions party chairs are supposed to ask about a rival's donors — that Hollinrake found himself the subject of a private investigation.

The investigators

The firm used, according to reporting confirmed by Banks himself, was Precision Risk and Intelligence — a company he previously owned. Banks has not fully explained what the investigators were tasked with finding, but he did not deny commissioning the work. Pressed on whether Reform UK knew of the operation, he was cagey, telling reporters: "I row my own boat." In an earlier exchange about a separate matter he remarked, "I've always come across people in this world who like to give it but can't receive it."

The pattern extends beyond Hollinrake. The New Statesman reported that Banks also directed investigators toward Gabriel Pogrund, the Sunday Times' investigations editor, whose reporting had disclosed that Farage was secretly funded for years by George Cottrell — a convicted fraudster known as "Posh George" — a story Banks has disputed. Asked about that probe, Banks said: "We believe the Sunday Times used hacked information and that was an area of interest," adding, cryptically, "When you live by the sword, you die by the sword." The paper called it "reprehensible" that Banks's response to unwelcome reporting was to place a journalist under surveillance, and said its story was based on "responsible and professional journalism."

The response

Hollinrake made the episode public himself, writing on social media: "When you can't answer the questions, investigate the people asking them. After we called for scrutiny of Reform UK's finances, a close associate of Nigel Farage reportedly hired private investigators to dig into me — and journalists covering the same story."

Neither Farage nor Reform UK has disclosed what, if anything, they knew about the investigations before they became public.

Why it matters

The substance of the underlying dispute — whether Farage properly declared a £5m gift — is still working its way through two official inquiries, and neither has reported findings. What the Banks admission adds is a separate, uglier data point: that a wealthy backer of a party under scrutiny responded not by contesting the facts through normal channels, but by turning private investigators on the officeholder asking the questions and the journalist reporting them. It is a reminder that oversight of political money in Britain rests heavily on parties, regulators and reporters having the room to ask questions without personal cost — and that private wealth can be used to raise that cost directly.

The Electoral Commission and the standards commissioner are both still examining the Harborne gift, and their conclusions — expected in the coming months — will determine whether Farage broke any rules. Whether Banks's use of investigators draws scrutiny of its own, from the Information Commissioner's Office or elsewhere, is the next thing to watch.
