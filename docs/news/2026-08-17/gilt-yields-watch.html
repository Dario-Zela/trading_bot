Gilt yields hold near multi-decade highs as BoE hawks stay vocal

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the MPC voted 6-3 to hold rates in July. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Ten-year gilt yields near 5% as markets shrug off a growing hawkish minority on the BoE's rate committee and price in an extended hold.

UK government bond yields are behaving like a market that has made peace with a longer wait. Ten-year gilts are trading close to 5%, a level last seen with any regularity before the financial crisis, and the options market's pricing of the Bank of England's next moves shows traders have largely abandoned bets on an imminent rate cut — even as three of the nine people who set policy are quietly agitating for a hike.

That tension sat at the centre of the Bank's July decision. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6–3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, with chief economist Huw Pill joined by external members Catherine Mann and Megan Greene in voting for an immediate quarter-point increase. It was the third straight meeting in which the hawkish minority grew — from one dissenter in May to two in June to three in July — yet the committee's centre of gravity has not budged.

What the dissenters are worried about

Pill's argument, laid out in the minutes, was about signalling as much as inflation math: he wants Bank Rate raised to send an "unequivocal message" that the MPC will act against price pressure flowing from the energy market and the wider disruption in the Gulf. Greene made a similar pre-emptive case, arguing that moving early would cut the odds of second-round effects — wages and prices chasing each other upward — taking hold. Mann has been the committee's most persistent inflation hawk for two years running.

The backdrop gives them something to point to. UK inflation eased to 2.6% in the most recent reading, but the Bank's own forecast has it climbing back to roughly 3.2% by autumn as higher energy costs work through household bills — a reminder that the fall in headline inflation has not settled the argument about where prices go next.

Markets aren't buying the urgency

Despite that, pricing in the swaps market points to a Bank Rate that barely moves before year-end. Overnight index swap curves ahead of the 17 September meeting imply only a handful of basis points of net movement, consistent with a market assigning a hold well above 90%. A Reuters poll of more than 60 economists conducted after the July decision found most expect rates to stay at 3.75% through December — though tellingly, nearly 40% of respondents now see a hike as the more likely next move than a cut, versus a small minority still looking for one quarter-point reduction.

That split explains the gilt curve better than any single data point. Longer-dated debt has borne the brunt: 30-year gilt yields hit 5.78% in May, the highest since 1998, as investors demanded more compensation for holding UK government debt over a horizon where fiscal and inflation risks both loom. The 10-year, more directly tethered to the path of Bank Rate itself, has held in a tight band near 5% since touching that mark for the first time since 2008 back in March.

Why the standoff matters

For anyone pricing gilts or sterling systematically, the signal is less about the next meeting than about the ceiling. A minority pushing for tightening hasn't dislodged the majority's preference to hold, and the market has responded by pricing out easing rather than pricing in hikes — leaving Bank Rate effectively anchored at 3.75% with yields elevated to compensate for the risk that it stays there, or rises, longer than earlier forecasts assumed. Sterling firmed modestly on the July decision itself, a small confirmation that the vote, if anything, nudged expectations hawkish rather than dovish.

The next test lands on 17 September, when the MPC meets again against an inflation print due in the interim and a fresh round of energy-price data. A fourth dissenting vote for a hike would mark a genuine escalation; a reversion to two would suggest July's reading was the high-water mark for hawkishness this cycle. Either way, the gilt market looks set to keep pricing a Bank of England in no hurry to move — as long as inflation cooperates with that view.
