Seoul presses on with US drills despite Trump's call to scale back over Iran

US Army logistics troops inspect vehicles ahead of Ulchi Freedom Shield, the annual joint exercise Trump ordered scaled back. — U.S. Army / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump ordered US-Korea drills cut over Iran and North Korea ties, but Seoul's military started them on schedule anyway.

The soldiers moved out on schedule. That, in the end, is the story.

Hours before South Korea's 18,000 troops were due to begin this year's Ulchi Freedom Shield exercise with the United States, President Trump announced on Truth Social that he had ordered Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to "substantially reduce" the joint drills — citing his rapport with Kim Jong Un and Seoul's refusal to back Washington's Iran policy. South Korea's military did not wait for clarification. It started the exercise anyway, on Monday, as planned.

The 'No thanks' that started it

Trump's Sunday post tied two grievances together. The first was cost: joint exercises with South Korea, he wrote, have "long ago" been agreed to and represent an expense he now questions. The second was Iran. "I recently asked the President of South Korea if they would like to join us in the Denuclearization of the Islamic Republic of Iran," Trump wrote, "and they said, 'No thanks!'" He framed the drawdown partly as a consequence of that refusal.

The North Korea justification came wrapped around it. "Based on my very good relationship with Kim Jong Un, of North Korea, I am not happy with the fact that the United States has, long ago, agreed to participate in Joint Military Exercises with South Korea," Trump wrote, adding that the drills send a signal "totally inappropriate and hostile" to a North Korea he described as having been "unthreatening and respectful" during his presidency.

It is a notable formulation for a country that has tested eleven ballistic missiles so far in 2026, two of them in the fortnight before this year's exercise began. Pyongyang fired a short-range missile from the Wonsan area on August 6 and a longer-range one — travelling more than 700 kilometres before splashing into the sea, and flagged by analysts as possibly hypersonic — on August 12, days after Seoul and Washington confirmed the drill dates. North Korea has for years timed weapons tests to the exercise calendar, treating Ulchi Freedom Shield and its predecessors as rehearsals for invasion. Whether that pattern constitutes "unthreatening" is a judgment Trump's own military has not obviously endorsed.

Seoul doesn't blink

South Korea's Ministry of National Defence responded within hours, saying the exercise would "proceed as previously notified and scheduled." The Joint Chiefs of Staff confirmed to reporters that operations had begun Monday on the original eleven-day timeline, August 17 to 27, and declined to comment further on the president's remarks — a diplomatic silence that reads, in context, as its own kind of answer.

It is worth separating what actually changed in this year's exercise from what Trump claims to have changed. Twenty of the drill's forty field training events were already being pushed into September before Trump's post — a decision made on August 10, driven by summer heat and a desire to spread combined-defence training more evenly across the year, according to the allies' own announcement. That adjustment has nothing to do with Trump's Iran grievance or his North Korea diplomacy; it was logistics, settled before the political row began. The exercise proceeding this week is, by scale, comparable to prior years, built around new scenarios for drone warfare, GPS jamming and cyberattacks that reflect lessons North Korean troops brought home from fighting alongside Russia in Ukraine.

South Korean President Lee Jae Myung, who took office promising a steadier hand on the alliance than his predecessor, has so far let the defence ministry's terse statement stand as Seoul's position. That restraint — proceeding without argument rather than either capitulating or publicly rebuking a sitting US president — is itself a signal about how Seoul intends to manage a relationship that has grown more unpredictable.

What a presidential order actually reaches

The gap here is instructive. Trump can order the Pentagon to plan smaller, less frequent exercises going forward — and Hegseth, by all indications, will act on that instruction for future iterations. What a Sunday social media post cannot do is unwind an exercise that a treaty ally's own Joint Chiefs of Staff have already set in motion, backed by a defence ministry that answers to Seoul, not Washington. South Korea's compliance with the drawdown demand, if it comes, will show up in next year's exercise design, not this week's convoy inspections.

That distinction matters beyond the Korean peninsula. It is a live test of how far a US president's stated preferences translate into altered military posture when an ally has its own threat assessment, its own domestic political incentives, and its own bureaucracy executing plans on a timeline the White House does not fully control. South Korea faces an active, missile-testing neighbour it does not currently believe is "unthreatening," whatever the framing out of Washington. The next test of that gap comes when this year's postponed field-training events resume in September — and when Washington and Seoul must decide together what a genuinely "substantially reduced" Ulchi Freedom Shield looks like in 2027.
