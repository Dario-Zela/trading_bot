EU begins enforcing AI transparency rules, with toughest curbs still years off

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which is now enforcing the EU AI Act's transparency rules. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
EU chatbot and deepfake labelling rules are now enforceable with fines up to €15m or 3% of turnover, but rules for higher-risk AI systems don't bite until 2027-28.

Since 2 August, anyone building a chatbot for the European market has had to make one thing plain: this is a machine, not a person. Anyone generating a deepfake — a synthetic voice, a manipulated video, an AI image of a real place or event — has to label it as such. And anyone caught skipping that step faces fines of up to €15 million, or 3% of their global annual turnover, whichever is larger.

That is the headline outcome of the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act moving from law-on-paper to law-in-practice. The Commission's AI Office, working with national regulators across the bloc, began enforcing the transparency provisions this month — the first real teeth the Act has shown since it entered into force two years ago. But the rules taking effect now are the easy ones. The provisions meant to govern genuinely high-risk AI — systems used in hiring, credit scoring, migration screening, policing — have been pushed back to December 2027 and August 2028. For roughly a year and a half, Brussels will be regulating how AI introduces itself, but not yet what it's allowed to do.

What actually changed on 2 August

The new obligations sit in Article 50 of the AI Act, and they are narrower than they might sound. Interactive systems — customer-service bots, AI agents, virtual avatars — must disclose that a user isn't talking to a human, unless that's already obvious from context. Content that resembles a real person, place or event but was generated or altered by AI has to carry a visible label and, where practical, a machine-readable mark so platforms and detection tools can identify it automatically. The Commission has published a small set of standard icons for this purpose — a basic AI marker, one for fully AI-generated content, another for partial AI modification — free to use and, crucially, tested for whether ordinary users actually notice and understand them.

There's a grace period built in: tools already on the market before 2 August have until December 2026 to retrofit the machine-readable marking, even though the visible-label duty for chatbots and deepfakes applies immediately. Emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems now also have to tell people when they're being scanned, though law enforcement gets carve-outs.

Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, security and democracy, framed the move in the Commission's own language as making AI something "people and businesses can understand and trust" — while acknowledging, in the same breath, that the most capable models "create risks on an entirely new scale." That second half of the sentence is the tell: the rules landing now don't really touch that scale of risk. They touch disclosure.

Industry has, largely, gone along with it. Around 190 organisations — including Google, Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic and Mistral — had signed the Commission's voluntary Code of Practice on AI-generated content by the end of July, ahead of the marking obligations becoming mandatory. About half of the signatories are small or recent companies, which the Commission reads as evidence the code is usable by firms without a compliance department, not just the giants who can afford one.

The gap Brussels chose to leave open

The more consequential part of the AI Act — the rules for "high-risk" systems, covering things like biometric identification, employment screening, access to essential services, and migration and border management — was originally due to apply from 2 August 2026 as well. It won't now. Under a Digital Omnibus deal struck between the Council and Parliament earlier this year, those obligations were pushed to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk systems and 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI embedded in regulated products.

The official reason is more mundane than political horse-trading: the European standardisation bodies CEN and CENELEC missed their April 2025 deadline to finish the harmonised technical standards that let companies actually demonstrate compliance, and are now not expecting to have them ready before late 2026 at the earliest. Without the standards, businesses would have had a legal duty to comply with rules nobody could yet show them how to meet. The Commission opted to move the deadline rather than enforce a standard that doesn't exist.

Digital rights groups have been less forgiving of the practical effect. Campaigners point out that the delay leaves people subject to predictive policing tools, migration-risk scoring and biometric surveillance systems without the Act's promised safeguards — risk assessments, human oversight, documentation requirements — for an additional 16 months beyond the original date. Their objection isn't really about the delay's cause; it's that regulation of the systems with the most direct power over individual lives is precisely the part that's been allowed to slip.

An asymmetric compliance calendar

What's left is a patchwork that will hold for the better part of two years. A company running a customer-facing chatbot has clear, binding, immediately enforceable rules and a real fine schedule behind them. A company running an AI system that screens job applicants or flags asylum claims for extra scrutiny does not — not yet, and not until standards exist to tell it how. That asymmetry is not an accident of timing so much as a reflection of which obligations were easiest to specify first: telling a user "this is a bot" doesn't require a technical standard. Auditing a hiring algorithm for bias does.

Whether that sequencing becomes a template — disclosure rules first, everywhere, with substantive safeguards trailing by years — will be watched closely outside the EU too, given the bloc has historically set the pace other regulators eventually follow on digital rules. The next marker is the December 2026 deadline for machine-readable marking on tools already in the market, followed by the first real test of enforcement: whether the AI Office actually levies a fine, against whom, and how large. Until then, the 3%-of-turnover number is a number on a page rather than a demonstrated deterrent.
