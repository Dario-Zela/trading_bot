Domestic abuse safeguards under fire over early prison releases and alert-system flaws

HMP Wandsworth. Prisoners including domestic abusers become eligible for earlier release under the Sentencing Act 2026 from 1 October. — N Chadwick / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A murdered woman's parents and domestic abuse charities say two separate systems — early prison release and the national emergency alert — both fail to protect survivors from being found.

The government warned this month of a "very high risk of wildfires nationally." The alert that landed on millions of phones at 7pm on 14 August was, by the Home Office's own account, a proportionate response to 43 active wildfires and 11 major incidents serious enough to call in the army. It was also, according to domestic abuse charities, a moment of real danger for an entirely different reason: the loud, unavoidable buzz it triggers on every handset in range, including the second, hidden phones that abuse survivors use to call for help without their abuser knowing.

That episode landed in the same fortnight that the parents of a murdered woman went public with a warning about a much larger structural problem — one due to take effect on 1 October, when thousands of prisoners, including domestic abusers, become eligible for release earlier than their sentences originally allowed. Different mechanisms, same fault line: systems built for the general public, or for prison capacity math, that were not designed around the specific danger of someone being found.

A death two weeks after release

Michaela Hall was 49, a charity worker in Cornwall, when she was murdered in 2021 by Lee Kendall — two weeks after he was released from prison, where he had served time for assaulting her. The Probation Service had classed him as medium risk despite 34 documented incidents of abuse, including strangulation and threats made with weapons. "It was a catalogue of mistakes, laziness and victim blaming," her mother, Anne Hall, said. Her father, Peter, put it more starkly: "There's going to be more violence and there's going to be more death. It's very simple."

The Halls are speaking now because the same category of miscalculation is about to be built into policy at scale. The Sentencing Act 2026 moves the automatic release point for most standard determinate sentences from roughly the halfway mark to as early as one third, freeing prison capacity across England and Wales — an estimated 5,000 offenders under the associated scheme launching 1 October. Murderers, those serving life terms, and — after direct intervention from the Prime Minister — rapists and serious child sex offenders were carved out of the earlier-release provisions. Offences closely tied to domestic abuse, including stalking, coercive control and non-fatal strangulation, were originally slated for similar protection. That protection did not survive the bill's passage through Parliament. Roughly 3,000 domestic abuse offenders a year are expected to be released early as a result.

Farah Nazeer, chief executive of Women's Aid, does not hedge on the consequence: "Women will die as a result of this. There is no doubt in my mind." Dame Nicole Jacobs, the Domestic Abuse Commissioner, and the Victims' Commissioner wrote to ministers on 6 July asking for a delay to allow time to notify victims and put safety measures in place; the government has not paused the timetable. The Ministry of Justice points to £700m in new probation funding, £550m for victim support and an expanded electronic tagging programme as evidence the risk is being managed, alongside £10m specifically earmarked for the release scheme.

The notification gap

The money answers a resourcing problem. It does not fully answer a structural one: Britain has no comprehensive system for telling domestic abuse victims when their abuser is coming out. The Victim Contact Scheme — the mechanism that would ordinarily alert a survivor to a release date — is opt-in, and only guarantees information to those whose abuser received a custodial sentence of 12 months or more. Given how frequently domestic abuse convictions carry sentences shorter than that threshold, a meaningful share of survivors sit outside the scheme's reach entirely, dependent on informal channels or discovering a release only when the person shows up. Jacobs has called for the government to publish the true scale of releases so resourcing can be judged against the actual number, rather than an estimate: "If these figures are anywhere near the level of abusers set to be let out, then it is critical the Government is transparent about the scale of releases, so we can be confident the right level of resource is in place to keep victims safe."

When the whole country's phone buzzes at once

The wildfire alert exposed a smaller-scale version of the same blind spot, this time in emergency broadcast design rather than sentencing policy. The Emergency Alerts service is built to reach everyone in a coverage area simultaneously and audibly — which is precisely the feature that makes it useful for flood or fire warnings, and precisely what makes it a liability for someone concealing a second phone from a partner who monitors their movements.

"Survivors of domestic abuse frequently turn to second phones to help them access lifesaving support or flee from abuse," said Veronica Oakeshott, Women's Aid's head of external affairs, adding that it is vital such devices stay hidden. Maggie Evans of the charity Hourglass went further: "Domestic abuse victims should never be put at risk by the very systems meant to keep us all safe." Both charities say they received no advance warning that the alert was coming and no chance to circulate safety guidance to survivors before phones across the country lit up. A government spokesperson defended the alert as a direct request from fire and rescue services responding to a genuinely dangerous night, and said it was working with domestic abuse charities ahead of future system tests. Refuge has since published a guide showing survivors how to disable alerts on a given device — a workaround, not a fix, since it depends on the victim already knowing the alert is coming.

What ties it together

Neither the sentencing reform nor the alert system was built with malice toward victims; both were built to solve a different, legitimate problem — prison overcrowding in one case, mass public warning in the other — without anyone specifically modelling what happens to a person whose safety depends on not being found. The pattern across both stories is the same: notification infrastructure that assumes the person receiving information is not also the one who needs to stay hidden from someone else who might see it.

The next concrete test is 1 October, when the early-release scheme takes effect and the first wave of domestic abuse offenders begin walking free ahead of schedule. Whether the government publishes the release figures Jacobs is asking for, and whether any victim-notification fix accompanies the tagging and funding announcements already made, will show whether Wednesday's warnings were heeded or simply logged.
