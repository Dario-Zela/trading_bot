Fed's rate path splinters as soft retail data meets hawkish dissent

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A surprise 0.6% drop in July retail sales bolsters the case for a Fed rate cut, even as three officials just dissented for a hike.

The Federal Reserve heads into its September 16 meeting more openly divided than at any point in Kevin Warsh's short tenure as chair, and the economic data arriving in between is doing nothing to settle the argument. A retail sales report that missed consensus by the widest margin in over a year has strengthened the case for a rate cut just as three of the Fed's own policymakers are on record wanting to raise rates further.

The result is a September decision that, as of this week, genuinely could go either way — cut, hold, or even hike — a level of uncertainty that has been rare for a Fed under pressure to look decisive.

A retail print that broke the streak

Commerce Department data released August 14 showed US retail sales fell 0.6% in July, to $763.6 billion, against a consensus forecast for a 0.1% gain. It was the first monthly decline since October 2025 and the steepest since May 2025. Excluding autos, sales fell 0.3% versus an expected 0.2% rise; the closely watched "control group" measure — which strips out volatile categories and feeds directly into GDP calculations — dropped 0.4%, its worst showing since January 2025.

The weakness was broad rather than confined to one distressed corner of the consumer economy. Nonstore retailers, essentially online sellers, fell 2.2%, likely flattered in June and punished in July after Amazon moved its Prime Day promotion forward a month. Auto and parts dealers dropped 1.8%, and gasoline stations fell 0.9% on lower pump prices. Year-over-year, spending is still up 5.0%, so this is not a collapse — but the month-on-month direction, arriving alongside a University of Michigan consumer sentiment index that slid to 51.0 in August from 55.2 in July, is the kind of signal the Fed's doves have been waiting for.

It followed a July jobs report that was arguably worse: nonfarm payrolls fell by 23,000 against expectations for an 83,000 gain, with the two prior months revised down by a combined 103,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.1%, but for the wrong reason — people leaving the labor force rather than finding work — and annual wage growth slowed to 3.2%, the softest since May 2021.

Three no votes, one warning

Set against that backdrop, the Fed's July 29 decision to hold its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% for a fifth straight meeting looked, on paper, like caution. The vote behind it was anything but. Three regional bank presidents — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas — dissented in favour of a quarter-point hike, the most dissents on an FOMC vote since September 2016.

All three pointed to the same underlying grievance: inflation has now run above the Fed's 2% target for five consecutive years. "In my view, now is the time for the FOMC to act to speed the return of PCE inflation to our 2 percent objective and deliver on our commitment to price stability," Hammack said, adding that the longer above-target inflation persists, the more costly it becomes to reverse. Logan struck a similar note: "Without any policy restraint, inflation will likely continue to trend above target until there's an unanticipated shock," she said, arguing that "modest action in the near term would reduce the likelihood of needing to take sharper action later."

Warsh, asked about the split at his post-meeting press conference, did not try to paper over it. "I asked for a good family fight, and I got one," he said. "That's the designed feature." It was a notably relaxed answer for a chair barely two months into the job, and one that signalled he intends to let the committee's disagreements play out in public rather than manufacture consensus statements that mask them.

The dissenters are not the whole committee, however. Governor Stephen Miran, who has pushed for larger, half-point cuts at nearly every meeting since joining the board, remains on the opposite end of the spectrum, and Governor Christopher Waller has previously signalled openness to easing once labour-market softening is confirmed. The July vote was 9-3 for a hold — the hawks lost, but three dissents is enough to establish that the median voter's patience is not unlimited in either direction.

The math for September

That is the tension the retail sales miss now walks into. Data that would ordinarily tilt a committee toward cutting is landing on a Fed where a meaningful bloc explicitly wants to tighten, and futures markets have spent the past two weeks swinging between those poles rather than settling on one. The path of least resistance — a hold, buying time for clearer data — has support from both camps for different reasons, but it satisfies neither the officials who see inflation entrenching nor those who see the labour market cracking.

Equities have so far shrugged: the S&P 500 slipped only marginally on the retail sales release and remained on track for a third straight weekly gain, suggesting traders are not yet pricing in a policy mistake in either direction. That calm is itself notable given how wide the range of plausible September outcomes has become.

What happens next hinges on two more data points due before the Fed meets: the August jobs report, the first week of September, and the August CPI reading shortly after. Another weak payrolls number would likely tip the committee toward a cut and isolate the hawkish dissenters further; a hot inflation print would do the opposite, and could turn September from a coin-flip on the size of a possible cut into a real debate about whether the Fed moves at all. Either way, the disagreement that produced three dissents in July is not going away — it is waiting on the next number.
