Kushner holds direct Hamas talks as Netanyahu rejects troop withdrawal

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, who has rejected the Board of Peace's Gaza roadmap pending 'genuine' Hamas disarmament. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Kushner held rare direct talks with Hamas on disarmament, but Netanyahu's public refusal to withdraw troops until Hamas is 'genuinely' disarmed leaves the two sides demanding opposite first moves.

Jared Kushner spent ninety minutes on Sunday in a seaside conference room in El-Alamein, Egypt, doing something no senior American official has done openly in this war: sitting across from Hamas's top leadership and pressing them, face to face, on how they intend to give up their weapons. Twenty-four hours later he was due in Jerusalem, where the man he needs to sell the results to, Benjamin Netanyahu, has already said the plan those talks are meant to advance is unacceptable.

That sequence — a rare direct negotiation followed almost immediately by a public rebuff from the Israeli prime minister — is the story of the Gaza roadmap in August 2026. Ten months after a ceasefire ended the war's major combat operations, the diplomatic architecture built to lock that ceasefire into something permanent is straining against a basic disagreement over what happens first: Hamas laying down arms, or Israeli troops pulling back.

In one sentence: Trump's envoy Jared Kushner held rare direct talks with Hamas leadership on disarmament, but Netanyahu has publicly rejected withdrawing troops until Hamas is "genuinely" disarmed — exposing a widening gap between what negotiators are agreeing and what Israel's government will accept.

A rare seat at the table

Kushner met Hamas chief Khalil al-Hayya in the Egyptian resort town alongside former British prime minister Tony Blair and Nickolay Mladenov, the Bulgarian diplomat serving as the Board of Peace's High Representative for Gaza. Officials from Egypt, Qatar and Turkey sat in as mediators. It was al-Hayya's first trip abroad since Hamas named him overall leader in July, succeeding a chain of commanders killed by Israel since October 2023.

The substance was narrow but consequential: verifiable steps toward the disarmament Hamas agreed to in principle on 30 July, when it endorsed handing its weapons to a new transitional body, the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza. A source with knowledge of the talks described the session as "very productive," and Hamas representatives reaffirmed the commitment to disarm. But they attached a condition that goes to the heart of the current impasse: they said they would only begin handing over weapons once Netanyahu "publicly commits" to the roadmap he has just as publicly rejected.

"Genuine disarmament, not fictitious disarmament"

The roadmap Kushner is trying to rescue is the Board of Peace's 15-point plan, released as a follow-on to Trump's original ceasefire framework. It envisions Hamas disarmament, an Israeli withdrawal and the transfer of Gaza's administration to the NCAG proceeding as a phased, roughly parallel process, with an international stabilisation force filling the security vacuum as Israeli troops pull back.

Netanyahu rejected that sequencing outright on 9 August. "The IDF will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed," he told his cabinet, adding that he meant "the heavy weapons, the less heavy weapons — all the weapons. We are talking about real disarmament, not fictitious disarmament." Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich went further, framing the war's objective as Hamas's total elimination "militarily, civilly, and as a governing authority," and calling the roadmap "180 degrees contrary to that objective."

What Netanyahu has not done is define what "genuine" disarmament looks like in verifiable terms — how it would be confirmed, by whom, and on what timeline relative to a withdrawal he insists must come last. Board of Peace officials say privately that Netanyahu has shown "more flexibility" behind closed doors than his public statements suggest. If true, that gap between rhetoric and negotiating position is itself a problem: Hamas, Qatar and Egypt have no way to plan around a standard that shifts depending on the room.

The gap that could break the roadmap

This is the disconnect Kushner's shuttle diplomacy is trying to close, and it is not a small one. Hamas wants a public Israeli commitment before it moves. Netanyahu wants proof of disarmament before he moves. Each side's precondition is the other side's promised outcome, and mediators are being asked to sequence two things that, as currently defined, cannot both come first.

The practical stakes are concrete: Kushner is also pressing Netanyahu to honour ceasefire-adjacent commitments — 600 aid trucks a day into Gaza, infrastructure repairs, authorisation for the international stabilisation force to deploy, and a humanitarian zone in Rafah — none of which depend on resolving the disarmament-sequencing dispute but all of which have been held hostage to it. Officials involved in planning the weapons handover now say the first tranche is unlikely before the end of the year, partly because the stabilisation force itself is still being trained, and diplomats expect further drift once Israel's October elections and the coalition-formation period that follows absorb Netanyahu's political attention.

What to watch

Monday's Kushner–Netanyahu meeting in Jerusalem is the immediate test: whether the envoy can extract language specific enough to satisfy Hamas's demand for a public commitment without Netanyahu abandoning the sequencing he has staked his coalition's support on. Beyond that, the first actual weapons handover — if it happens at all before year-end — will be the only concrete evidence that either side has moved off its stated position. Until then, the roadmap exists mostly on paper, sustained by shuttle diplomacy rather than by any agreement on what comes first.
