Calls grow for public inquiry into Jason Arday 'witch hunt' after his death

Jason Arday, pictured in 2024, became Cambridge's youngest-ever Black professor in 2023. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Homerton College's principal and 31,000-plus petitioners are demanding a public inquiry into press coverage they say hounded Jason Arday to his death.

Jason Arday was, by his own account, non-verbal until he was eleven and unable to read or write until eighteen. By 2023, at 37, he was Cambridge's youngest-ever Black professor, a sociologist of education whose life story — dyslexia, autism, a childhood diagnosis of global developmental delay — was told and retold as a parable of academia opening its doors. On 14 August, nine days after resigning his post, he was found dead at a address in Battersea, south London. The Metropolitan Police say his death is being treated as unexpected but not suspicious.

What has followed is not the usual academic post-mortem. More than 31,000 people, several MPs and Homerton College's own principal are now demanding a public inquiry — not into Arday's conduct, but into the press that pursued him.

From plagiarism claim to resignation

The allegations began in July, when Nathan Cofnas — a self-described "race realist" philosopher sacked from a Cambridge fellowship in 2024 over his writing on race and intelligence — ran Arday's 2015 doctoral thesis through plagiarism-detection software and published his findings on Substack. He identified roughly 180 passages he said were identical or near-identical to a 2009 thesis from Brunel University. The Telegraph picked up the story, and further reporting scrutinised inconsistencies in Arday's account of his own life and honorary appointments.

Arday denied plagiarising, though he acknowledged "mistakes" in the doctorate. Liverpool John Moores University, which awarded the PhD, investigated and cleared him. Cambridge and Jesus College nonetheless opened their own inquiries into his qualifications and honorary titles, and Arday resigned his professorship on 5 August, quitting his Jesus College fellowship the same week.

"Hounded to death, his words"

Lord Woolley of Woodford, Homerton's principal and a friend and mentor of Arday's, has become the most prominent voice demanding scrutiny of that coverage. He told BBC Radio 4 the campaign against Arday was "the most horrendous, toxic, vicious witch hunt" — "a blood sport" — and recounted a conversation days before Arday's death: "He said he felt hounded to death, his words, that he couldn't see a way out."

The campaign group Good Law Project, which had been supporting Arday since the investigations opened, published an open letter calling for "an immediate public inquiry into how to ensure a responsible press," saying signatories had "warned multiple media outlets privately that their relentless pursuit of Dr Arday risked his suicide." Signatories include Green Party leader Zack Polanski, Labour MPs Bell Ribeiro-Addy, Clive Lewis and Nadia Whittome, broadcaster Carol Vorderman and NEU general secretary Daniel Kebede.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham stopped short of backing an inquiry, calling the death "a tragedy on so many levels" and urging "a moment for reflection" rather than judgment. Cambridge vice-chancellor Deborah Prentice offered condolences but has not commented on the calls for scrutiny of the coverage her university's own investigation helped set in motion.

The unresolved tension is one Woolley himself named: rigorous checks on academic credentials remain necessary, he said, but Arday's treatment differed markedly in scale and tenor from other plagiarism cases. Whether that argument survives contact with a formal inquiry — and whether any inquiry could compel newsrooms to answer for coverage of allegations that, however aggressively pursued, were substantially true — will shape how the press handles the next academic misconduct story, and how much liability, if any, editors are asked to carry for what happens after publication. No government response to the petition has yet been scheduled.
