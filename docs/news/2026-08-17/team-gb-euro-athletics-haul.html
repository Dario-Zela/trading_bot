Team GB claim dominant medal haul at European Athletics in Birmingham

In one sentence
Amy Hunt became the first athlete to win four golds at one European Championships and Dina Asher-Smith the event's most decorated ever, as GB topped the sprints but finished second overall to Italy.

Four gold medals. One athlete. Ninety-two years of European Athletics Championships, and nobody had ever done it before Amy Hunt did it on a Sunday evening in Perry Barr.

The 24-year-old sprinter swept the women's 100m, the 200m, the 4x100m relay and the mixed 4x100m relay at Alexander Stadium, finishing her week with a championship record of 39.97 seconds alongside Jeremiah Azu, Dina Asher-Smith and Zharnel Hughes. It capped a Birmingham 2026 that also turned Asher-Smith, at 30, into the most decorated athlete — male or female — in the championships' history, and pushed Hughes past Sir Mo Farah as Britain's most successful male performer on the European stage. For a home crowd that had spent much of the week watching seats fill only slowly, the closing weekend delivered exactly the fireworks organisers had hoped for.

A week that built to something unprecedented

Hunt opened the championships by winning the 100m in 11.00 seconds on the first night of competition — an event held on British soil for the first time — beating a field that included the defending champion Asher-Smith, who finished fifth. "I am so proud of myself," Hunt said afterwards. "It's not about the execution and how far back I was at 30m to go, I am not going to remember that in a few years' time, just that I won a fabulous gold."

Three days later she added the 200m in 22.19 seconds, becoming the first woman since Asher-Smith herself in 2018 to complete the sprint double at a European Championships. A third gold followed in the women's 4x100m relay on the Saturday, and the fourth came in Sunday's mixed relay, the last event of the meet, timed perfectly for a finale.

Hunt was unambiguous about who deserved equal billing. "I am stood next to the real life GOAT," she said of her relay teammate. "She is the most decorated European athlete in history. I am stood next to some amazing women. That was stunning to come together. I had the time of my life."

Asher-Smith's record, and what it says about longevity

Asher-Smith's own tally — 11 medals, eight of them gold, across a career stretching back to her breakthrough in 2014 — moved her past Poland's Irena Szewińska, whose ten medals had stood as the championship record since 1978. Szewińska won hers across five editions between 1966 and 1978; Asher-Smith has been collecting hers since she was a teenager training in southeast London, through five separate European Championships cycles that have survived injuries, a global pandemic and the ordinary attrition of a sprint career.

"To be in this position is great, and very humbling too when you think about all the incredible athletes that I look up to and am fortunate enough to run with," she said. Her week in Birmingham included 200m bronze and two relay golds — not her most eye-catching individual showing, but enough, added to a decade of accumulated hardware, to settle the record beyond argument.

Hughes, meanwhile, quietly built his own piece of history: two golds this week took his European Championships career total to six, edging him past Farah, Roger Black and Matthew Hudson-Smith as Britain's most successful male athlete at the event.

Dominant, but second on the table — and a question worth asking

Here is the wrinkle in an otherwise clean story of home dominance: Great Britain did not top the overall medal table. That went to Italy, who beat GB into second by the narrowest of margins — 0.03 seconds — in Sunday's men's 4x400m relay, securing back-to-back victories in the standings. GB finished with nine golds, eight silvers and two bronzes to Italy's tally, a strong showing but not the outright statement the sprint headlines might suggest.

That gap between the story and the scoreboard is worth sitting with. European Championships sit a rung below the World Championships and the Olympics in the athletics hierarchy — shallower fields, fewer of the world's genuine elite prioritising it in a crowded year. Records set here carry real weight for the athletes who set them, and Hunt's and Asher-Smith's achievements are unprecedented by any measure of the event's own history. But an unprecedented performance at a second-tier championship is a different kind of story than one set against the world's best, and ticket sales reflected some of that ambivalence: early-week sessions at Alexander Stadium ran barely half full even as Friday, Saturday and Sunday evenings sold out entirely, organisers adding extra seating to meet weekend demand.

The real test of what Birmingham produced comes next year. Hunt, still only 24, and Hughes both have the 2027 World Championships and the horizon of Los Angeles 2028 ahead of them; whether four European golds translates into a global final, let alone a global medal, is the question British sprinting now has eighteen months to answer.
