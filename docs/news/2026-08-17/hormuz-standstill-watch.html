Hormuz shipping standstill drags on as Iran ties reopening to sanctions relief

Satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which about a fifth of the world's oil and gas normally passes. — NASA MODIS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Hormuz transits sit near 7% of pre-war levels after a US-Iran deadline expired unresolved, with Brent up 24% since the war began.

A 60-day deadline for the United States and Iran to negotiate an end to their war lapsed on August 16 with neither side giving ground, and the Strait of Hormuz — the channel that normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil and gas — stayed effectively shut to commercial traffic. The window had been set in a 14-point memorandum signed in Islamabad on June 17; Tehran now says it never considered that framework to have taken effect at all, which means there was, in its telling, nothing to extend.

The numbers show how far the strait remains from normal. Roughly ten vessels crossed on August 10, against a pre-war daily average of 130 to 140 — about 7% of typical traffic, according to Al Jazeera's tally. Lloyd's List Intelligence counted 78 transits in the week of August 3-9, down from 95 the week before. War-risk insurers have booked $1.5bn-$2bn in claims from roughly 70 shipping casualties since the conflict began in late February, and Gulf container volumes are down by an estimated 1.8m-2m TEU for the first half of the year, with regional imports off 21% and exports off 31%.

Six conditions, no discount

Iran's terms for reopening the strait, laid out by senior official Mohammad Baqer Zolqadr, run to six points: an end to threats and insults, a permanent halt to military action against Iran and its regional allies, withdrawal of US naval and air forces from around Iranian waters, full war reparations "without reductions or discounts," removal of sanctions, and unconditional release of frozen Iranian assets. Washington has not published a dollar figure for what it would accept, and President Trump has floated the opposite — reparations from Iran covering "a 50-year period" of American and civilian losses in the region, alongside a claim that the US now has effective control of the waterway. "Pretty soon I'll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States," he said on August 14. Iran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, replied that the strait "has been Iranian, is Iranian, and will remain Iranian." Talks between Tehran and Oman over a technical shipping-lane arrangement are described by Iranian officials as separate from, and running ahead of, any decision to actually reopen the passage — a distinction Tehran has been careful to preserve even as it claims those talks are progressing.

A floor, not a spike

Brent crude closed at $88.29 a barrel on August 17, roughly 24% above the $71-72 level it traded at just before the war began on February 28 — and the US Energy Information Administration is now forecasting a 2026 average of $87, not the return to pre-war pricing that a short-lived crisis would imply. That forecast is the tell: six months in, with transit volumes still in single digits and reparations attached as a precondition rather than a talking point, the market is pricing Hormuz less as a temporary chokepoint and more as a standing discount on global spare capacity.

The US Treasury is reportedly preparing a fresh sanctions package against Iran's economy for the week ahead, which will test whether pressure can move Tehran or simply hardens its bargaining position further. The next marker is whether the Oman-brokered shipping-lane mechanism produces an actual rise in transit counts — so far it hasn't — or whether, like the Islamabad deadline, it lapses as one more unmet interim step.
