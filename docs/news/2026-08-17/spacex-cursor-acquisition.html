SpaceX closes $60bn acquisition of AI coding startup Cursor

Elon Musk, whose SpaceX has absorbed AI coding startup Cursor in a $60bn all-stock deal that closed on August 14th. — JD Lasica / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
SpaceX has closed a $60bn all-stock deal for AI coding startup Cursor — a price close to Northrop Grumman's entire market value.

SpaceX makes rockets. As of this week, it also owns one of the most popular tools for writing computer code. On August 14th, the company completed a $60 billion all-stock acquisition of Cursor, the AI coding assistant built by a four-year-old startup called Anysphere — one of the largest software acquisitions ever recorded, and a deal that has as much to do with the AI arms race as it does with anything that flies.

From option to close

The deal did not happen all at once. In April, SpaceX struck an unusual arrangement with Anysphere: pay $10 billion up front for exclusive partnership rights, with an option to buy the whole company outright later for $60 billion in stock. That option pre-empted a funding round Cursor had all but finished — a $2 billion raise, co-led by Andreessen Horowitz and Thrive Capital with Nvidia as a strategic backer, that would have valued the company at around $50 billion. SpaceX's offer beat it.

SpaceX exercised the option on June 16th, days after its own Nasdaq debut, formally agreeing to buy Cursor for $60 billion in stock. The transaction closed on August 14th, according to a regulatory filing, folding Cursor into SpaceXAI, the artificial-intelligence arm SpaceX built around Musk's xAI after merging with it in February. Michael Truell, Cursor's 25-year-old co-founder and chief executive, framed the deal as a technical marriage rather than an exit: "We are excited to share that SpaceX has exercised their option to acquire Cursor in an all-stock transaction with the goal of building the world's most useful AI models," he said in a statement, adding that Cursor would keep serving its existing customers.

Truell and three MIT classmates — Sualeh Asif, Arvid Lunnemark and Aman Sanger — founded Anysphere in 2022. Cursor, an AI-powered code editor that can autocomplete, review and independently execute programming tasks, went from an $8 million seed round in 2023 to a reported $3 billion-plus in annual recurring revenue by this May, a growth curve investors have called the fastest of any business-software company on record. That trajectory is what pushed its valuation from $9.9 billion in June 2025 to $29.3 billion five months later to $60 billion now — roughly doubling twice within a year.

Why a rocket company wants a code editor

The official rationale is compute and code. SpaceX's absorption of xAI in February valued the combined entity at $1.25 trillion and set the company on a course toward what its executives describe as orbital data centres — moving AI workloads into space to draw on Starlink's satellite network, constant solar power and free radiative cooling. Cursor, in a statement of its own, said the deal gives it "access to the largest fleet of GPUs in the world," a resource that matters more than office space or headcount in a market where training and running large models is the binding constraint. For SpaceX, Cursor is a fast, working product with paying customers and real usage data — a shortcut into the AI-coding race that OpenAI and Anthropic already lead, without having to build a coding assistant from nothing.

That race is crowded and consolidating fast. GitHub Copilot still commands roughly 42% of the market with 4.7 million paying subscribers, and Windsurf — a rival that briefly overtook Cursor in developer rankings — was itself absorbed this year after Google paid $2.4 billion to license its technology and hire its founding team, with the remaining company sold on to Cognition for $250 million. Buying Cursor outright, rather than licensing its technology or poaching its engineers, is SpaceX's way of skipping that slower, messier route straight to a market leader.

What $60bn actually buys

The number is the story, and it invites an uncomfortable comparison. Sixty billion dollars for a coding-assistant startup is roughly three-quarters of the entire market capitalisation of Northrop Grumman — the company that builds the B-21 stealth bomber — and not far off Lockheed Martin's. A four-year-old software company, with a product that costs a few hundred dollars a year per developer, is now worth close to what investors assign to firms that build fighter jets and missile systems. That is either proof that AI tooling commands a valuation premium aerospace hardware cannot match, or a sign that the AI sector's pricing has come unmoored from anything resembling revenue multiples.

The market's own verdict has been shaky. SpaceX shares priced at $135 on their June 12th Nasdaq debut, spiked to $225.64 on June 16th — the day the Cursor deal was announced — then slid as low as $104.83 by August 3rd, well below the IPO price, before a stronger-than-expected second-quarter earnings report ($7.81 billion in revenue against $6.93 billion forecast) pulled the stock back above $135 this week. In other words, investors cheered the Cursor deal on day one, spent seven weeks second-guessing it, and have only just been talked back into neutral. That volatility is the real signal here: not whether SpaceX can afford $60 billion in its own stock, but whether the market believes AI coding tools are worth what the deal implies, or whether this is the kind of pricing that only makes sense until it doesn't.

The next test lands with SpaceX's next earnings report, when analysts will look for the first real numbers on what Cursor contributes to SpaceXAI's revenue — and with GitHub and Google's next moves, which will show whether this deal reshuffles the coding-assistant market for good or simply set a high-water mark for what one AI acquisition can cost.
