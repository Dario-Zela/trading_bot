China's tech boom can't offset deepening investment slump

In one sentence
China's Q2 growth cooled to 4.3% as fixed investment fell at its steepest pace since 2020, with AI-chip exports the only pillar holding up the headline number.

China's economy is splitting in two. On one side, factories are humming with orders for AI chips, servers and robotics components, and exports of high-tech goods are up nearly 40% this year. On the other, the building sites, shopping malls and household balance sheets that used to power growth are going quiet — investment is contracting at its steepest pace since the pandemic, and consumers are saving rather than spending. Both halves are real. Neither cancels the other out.

The headline number from Beijing's National Bureau of Statistics landed on 15 July: gross domestic product grew 4.3% year-on-year in the second quarter, the weakest reading since late 2022 and below the 4.5% economists had pencilled in. It was also a step down from the 5.0% clocked in the first quarter, leaving growth over the first half at 4.7% — trending in the wrong direction relative to Beijing's own 4.5–5.0% full-year target.

An investment slump with no modern precedent

The drag came almost entirely from capital spending. Fixed-asset investment fell 5.7% in the first half and 5.9% over the first seven months, worse than the roughly 5% decline economists had forecast and the weakest reading since May 2020, when much of the country was still under Covid lockdowns. Private-sector investment — the part of the economy least propped up by state direction — dropped 8.5% year-on-year over the same stretch, a contraction that points to businesses simply declining to commit capital.

Property remains the anchor dragging the number down. Real-estate development investment fell roughly 16–18% in the first half, continuing a slide that has now run for more than four years and cut cumulative property investment nearly in half in nominal terms since its 2021 peak. Analysts at Rhodium Group estimate the sector alone has been subtracting close to two percentage points from annual GDP growth since 2024. Unlike the sharp, headline-grabbing defaults of 2021 and 2022, this is now a slow bleed — developers still short of cash, unsold inventory still overhanging regional markets, and little sign of the sector finding a floor.

Deflation compounds the problem. Chinese producer and consumer prices have now fallen, on and off, for roughly ten consecutive quarters, and real interest rates remain high even as nominal rates sit near historic lows. That combination discourages exactly the kind of private investment Beijing needs: why borrow to expand capacity when the price you'll sell at keeps falling and the real cost of the loan keeps rising?

Consumers are retreating too

Retail sales tell a similar story. Growth has hovered barely above 1% for much of the year, and sales fell outright in May for the first time in more than three years before a modest recovery — up 1.8% in July — that still leaves consumption well below what a 4%-plus GDP print would normally imply. Household caution tracks the property slump directly: real estate is where most Chinese wealth is stored, and falling home values have made families more inclined to save than spend, even as Beijing has rolled out trade-in subsidies and other consumption incentives over the past year.

Where the strength actually is

Set against that, industrial output and exports look almost like a different economy. Industrial production rose 5.3% in June before easing to a still-solid 4.8% in July, and high-tech manufacturing's purchasing managers' index has been running well above the headline reading, in expansion territory. The clearest driver is semiconductors: China's chip exports jumped 117% year-on-year in July, extending a surge that saw the country ship roughly $177bn of integrated circuits in the first half — up more than 96% on the year, partly on higher memory prices, partly on genuine volume growth tied to the global AI buildout. High-tech exports overall are up around 40% for the year to date.

The catch is that this strength is externally driven. As Macquarie analysts put it, external demand has been the bright spot of China's economy in 2026, and how much support Beijing needs to provide domestically depends largely on how long that holds. Trivium China analysts have made a related point: much of the recent lift in prices and activity reflects an external shock — the scramble for AI hardware — rather than an organic revival in consumer confidence. That is a fragile foundation. A slowdown in global AI capital spending, a fresh round of US tariff action, or simply the fading of front-loaded orders ahead of prior tariff deadlines could remove the one pillar currently holding the headline number up.

What it means for markets

For now, Beijing has resisted the kind of large stimulus package markets have periodically priced in. UOB's Woei Chen Ho reckons Beijing will lean toward selective, targeted measures — support for specific consumption categories, incremental easing for developers — rather than a broad-based push, and the IMF's advice that Beijing pursue sustained fiscal expansion until deflation clears durably has so far gone largely unheeded. That restraint matters for markets: it implies commodity demand tied to construction (steel, cement, industrial metals) stays structurally weak even as demand for chip-adjacent inputs stays strong, and it leaves emerging-market currencies and Asian equities exposed to swings in Chinese sentiment that owe more to Washington's tariff calendar than to Beijing's own data calendar.

The next test comes with August activity data, due in mid-September, which will show whether July's chip-export acceleration was a peak or a staging post, and whether retail sales can build on July's tentative recovery. A Communist Party plenum widely expected before year-end is also likely to address the investment shortfall directly — the question markets are asking is whether Beijing treats it as a cyclical dip to be managed or a structural rebalancing to be accelerated.
