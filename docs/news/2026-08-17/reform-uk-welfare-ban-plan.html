Reform UK unveils £50bn plan to strip benefits from foreign nationals

Nigel Farage's Reform UK unveiled plans to ban almost all welfare payments to foreign nationals. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK's £50bn welfare ban on foreign nationals exempts 4.5m EU settled-status holders, but risks reciprocal benefit losses for British expats abroad.

It arrived with the subtlety Reform UK has made its trademark: a press conference, a very large number, and a promise to end something the party's Treasury spokesman called "plain immoral." Nigel Farage's party wants to strip almost all welfare payments — universal credit, housing benefit, pension credit, jobseeker's allowance, child benefit, free childcare, disability benefits — from anyone who isn't a British citizen. The claimed prize is more than £50 billion a year off the welfare bill. The catch, buried a few paragraphs into Reform's own briefing, is that the policy runs straight into a legally binding treaty and a population of more than four million people who came to Britain, in many cases, at Britain's own invitation.

The number and the hole in it

Robert Jenrick, the former Conservative shadow justice secretary who defected to Reform in January and now speaks for the party on the Treasury brief, put it plainly: "Forcing British workers to pay for the benefits of foreigners is not just economically illiterate but plain immoral." Reform's policy chief, Zia Yusuf, framed it as the defining choice in British politics — foreign nationals should "bear the brunt" of fiscal tightening, he argued, before any British citizen is asked to sacrifice anything. "UK welfare should be for UK citizens," he said.

The headline figure — £234 billion in savings over several decades, £50 billion a year once fully phased in, £21 billion annually from the foreign-national ban alone by year five — is the kind of number built to travel well in a soundbite and badly in a spreadsheet. Labour's chairwoman, Anna Turley, called it "unfunded, unworkable, and falling apart in real time," pointing to a structural problem in Reform's own arithmetic: the plan exempts EU citizens with settled status, who account for close to a tenth of universal credit claimants. Strip them out and the pool of people actually affected shrinks to roughly 431,000 non-EU migrants with indefinite leave to remain — under 3% of claimants. The gap between the exempted population and the savings target is not small.

Why the EU carve-out doesn't hold

That carve-out is not generosity — it's a legal necessity Reform can't yet escape. More than 4.5 million people hold settled status under the EU Settlement Scheme, protection guaranteed by the Withdrawal Agreement, an international treaty that preserves benefit access for EU citizens who were living in the UK before the end of 2020. Farage's answer is to renegotiate that treaty. European diplomats have been blunt about the odds: Brussels has no incentive to reopen a deal that already favours EU citizens' settled position, and unpicking Part Two of the Withdrawal Agreement would mean years of exactly the negotiation Britain spent the back half of the last decade trying to finish. Labour's line — that the plan means "ripping up the UK's withdrawal agreement with the EU, plunging the UK back into years of Brexit renegotiations" — is not much of an exaggeration.

It also isn't just a legal problem. Turning benefit eligibility into a bargaining chip cuts both ways, and the other end of that chip is sitting in Spain, France and Portugal.

The expat mirror

Reform has already conceded the point. Buried in its own costings is a £500 million provision for British expatriates who could lose benefit entitlements abroad if EU states retaliate in kind, and who might then return to the UK and draw on the very system Reform is trying to shrink. Roughly 1.2 million UK-born people live across the EU, many drawing state pensions, healthcare access or unemployment support under reciprocal arrangements that mirror what Reform now wants to end for EU citizens in Britain. Strip EU nationals of UK benefits and there is little diplomatic reason for Madrid or Paris to keep extending the same courtesy to pensioners in Alicante or Dordogne.

Business reaction has been quieter than the political fireworks, but the underlying worry is straightforward: sectors already short-staffed — social care, hospitality, parts of the NHS workforce — lean heavily on EU labour that came to Britain partly because settled status offered parity with British workers. Making that status conditional, or threatening to unwind it, doesn't change who holds a UK passport; it changes who wants to stay.

What's next

The policy lands as Reform sits at the top of the polls — narrowly ahead of Labour in some trackers, narrowly behind in others, but consistently the story of 2026's local elections, where the party posted its best-ever results. That momentum is exactly why the maths matters: a £50 billion pledge that can't survive contact with the Withdrawal Agreement is a manifesto liability once voters start asking who actually pays it. Watch for Brussels' formal response, due once the European Commission's press service confirms whether it even considers renegotiation on the table, and for how Reform revises the costing once the EU exemption — clearly unsustainable at current savings claims — gets tested in the Commons.
