Ferrari's first electric car fetches record $40m at charity auction

Ferrari Luce Chassis 0 on display at RM Sotheby's Monterey auction, August 2026 — Lcaa9 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Ferrari's first EV sold for $40m at a charity auction — 36 times its estimate — but the number reflects donor generosity, not retail demand.

The most expensive new car ever sold at auction is not, strictly speaking, a very good measure of what the car is worth. On Saturday night in Monterey, California, a bidder paid $40 million for the first production unit of the Ferrari Luce — the marque's first fully electric model — against a pre-sale estimate of $1.1 million. That is a 36-fold overshoot, achieved in a room built for exactly that outcome: a charity gala, not a showroom.

The car, known as Chassis 0, went under the hammer at RM Sotheby's annual Monterey sale, with every dollar earmarked for the Ferrari Foundation, the marque's 501(c)(3) that funds educational programmes. RM Sotheby's waived its buyer's premium too, so the full sum flows through untouched. It is a one-off "Tailor Made" build finished in a custom Madreperla paint that shifts from green to violet depending on the light, and it will not be delivered until the first quarter of 2027 — assuming the buyer, whose identity has not been disclosed, actually wants to drive it rather than store it.

A record built for breaking

Ferrari and RM Sotheby's have run this play before. Last August, a one-off Daytona SP3 raised $26 million at the same charity sale for the same foundation — then a record for a new Ferrari at auction. This year's Luce beat it by more than half, and now sits within range of the $48.4 million paid privately for a 1962 Ferrari 250 GTO in 2018, long the benchmark for what any Ferrari, of any era, can fetch. The comparison flatters the Luce more than it should: the GTO's price reflects decades of scarcity and racing pedigree distilled into 36 surviving cars. The Luce's chassis-0 status is manufactured scarcity — real, but only a few months old.

The production Luce lists at roughly $640,000, which makes Saturday's sale about 62 times the sticker price of the car ordinary buyers are queuing for. And they are queuing: Ferrari's initial allocation of fewer than 500 Luces for 2026 sold out within two months of the May unveiling, well before deliveries begin.

The other number that matters

That May unveiling was not universally adored. Ferrari's stock fell more than 8% the day the Luce was revealed, as commentators mocked its five-seat, glasshouse-roofed styling — the work of Jony Ive and Marc Newson's studio LoveFrom, in its first project with the Italian marque — comparing it variously to a Honda Accord and an oversized Apple Store fixture. Former Ferrari chairman Luca Cordero di Montezemolo went further, suggesting the prancing horse badge be stripped from the hood. Analysts were split on what the sell-off actually meant: RBC Capital Markets called it overdone, while Morgan Stanley's Edouard Aubin upgraded the stock to Overweight days later, arguing the punishment exceeded the offence.

Set against that backdrop, a $40 million charity bid says less about retail appetite for the Luce than it does about what a handful of ultra-wealthy donors will pay for a marquee lot, a tax-deductible headline and the marque's goodwill in one purchase. The waitlist for the standard car — sold out at a fraction of the price — is the number that will actually tell whether Ferrari's electric bet has legs. Deliveries begin in the fourth quarter of 2026, and the first resale prices on the secondary market, whenever they surface, will be the honest test the auction floor couldn't provide.
