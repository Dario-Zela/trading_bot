Buyers circle BP's £2.5bn North Sea exit as supermajors retreat

A North Sea oil platform — BP is exiting the basin after six decades of production. — Gary Bembridge / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
BP has put its five North Sea hubs up for sale for £2.5bn, with Neo Next+, Shell-Equinor JV Adura and Ithaca Energy the leading bidders as the last UK supermajor exits after 60 years.

BP has begun the formal sale of the five North Sea production hubs that have anchored its business in UK waters for sixty years, issuing confidential sale documents to prospective buyers this month in a deal expected to fetch around £2.5 billion. The move, confirmed by people close to the process, marks the moment Britain's last homegrown oil major follows Shell, ExxonMobil, Equinor, Chevron and TotalEnergies out of a basin they all once considered core.

The assets on the block are not marginal ones. Two hubs sit in the central North Sea; three lie west of Shetland, including the Clair and Schiehallion fields, whose roughly eight billion barrels of oil in place make them the largest known remaining resource in UK waters. Combined, the five hubs produce around 117,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day — a sliver of BP's 2.3 million barrels a day globally, but a substantial regional prize for whoever buys it, and a livelihood for the roughly 1,100 staff at BP's Aberdeen operation.

Who wants it

BP is seeking a single cash buyer for the whole portfolio rather than parcelling it out field by field, and according to analysts at Rystad Energy, three names dominate the shortlist. Neo Next+ — the enlarged operator formed from HitecVision-backed NEO Energy's tie-up with Repsol UK — is seen as the frontrunner, having just bought out BP's stake in Culzean, the North Sea's largest gas field, giving it a recent track record of absorbing BP acreage. Adura, the joint venture Shell and Equinor formed to consolidate their own UK positions, is also circling; it already holds assets west of Shetland that would sit naturally alongside Clair, offering the kind of infrastructure overlap that tends to sweeten a bid. Ithaca Energy, the London-listed independent majority-owned by Israel's Delek Group, rounds out the trio — notable because BP was in advanced talks to sell roughly the same package to Ithaca alone for close to £2.7 billion as recently as June, before those negotiations collapsed. Smaller independents including Serica Energy and Enquest are said to be circling too, though they are more likely bidding for a break-up than the whole portfolio, lacking the balance sheet to write a single cheque of that size.

That none of the front-runners is a name familiar to most retail investors is itself the story. A decade ago, a portfolio this size would have drawn interest from BP's supermajor peers. Now the buyers are private-equity-backed consolidators and joint ventures purpose-built to hoover up mature assets that no longer fit an oil major's return targets.

Why BP is walking away

The arithmetic behind the exit is not subtle. The UK's Energy Profits Levy — the windfall tax introduced after Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent gas prices spiralling — currently pushes the effective tax rate on North Sea production to as high as 78%, with the levy itself fixed at 38% through March 2030 after Westminster scrapped the investment allowances that had softened its bite. BP's most recent annual report put a number on the pain: the Labour government's decision to raise the levy by three percentage points and extend it to 2030 cost the company an extra £539 million in a single year. Against that backdrop, five hubs generating under 120,000 barrels a day simply do not clear the hurdle rate BP applies to capital it could deploy in the Gulf of Mexico, Iraq or its LNG portfolio instead.

The sale also fits a wider pattern under Meg O'Neill, who took over as chief executive in April after Murray Auchincloss's abrupt departure. O'Neill has pushed BP back toward a simplified upstream-downstream structure and set a target of $20 billion in disposals by the end of 2027, aimed squarely at cutting debt and restoring capital discipline after a bruising few years for the shares. The North Sea sale follows the £10.1 billion disposal of the Castrol lubricants business to Stonepeak, the sale of the Gelsenkirchen refinery in Germany, and a $1.5 billion deal covering US Permian and Eagle Ford midstream stakes. BP has already banked $5.3 billion in divestment proceeds last year and is guiding toward $8-9 billion this year alone; the North Sea package, whatever it ultimately fetches, is a meaningful chunk of that. The company has also paused its $750 million quarterly share buyback, underlining how much the balance sheet — not growth — is driving decisions right now. "These assets will be better positioned as part of another company," O'Neill said when the sale process was launched, adding that the UK would "continue to play an important role" in BP's future — a line that reads as reassurance to Westminster more than a statement of intent about upstream production.

A basin changing hands

What BP's exit completes is a rotation that has been under way for the better part of a decade: the supermajors trading mature, tax-heavy, high-cost North Sea barrels for independents and consolidators built to run leaner. Rystad's Ranjan Saxena has described the pattern bluntly — majors exiting give "regional players and independents a chance to buy into key fields and help keep them profitable through production-life extensions," precisely because those buyers accept lower returns and longer horizons than a supermajor's shareholders would tolerate. Ithaca, Neo Next+ and Adura are all products of that logic: assembled specifically to hold ageing North Sea infrastructure that oil majors no longer want on their books.

For energy traders and the wider market, the identity of the eventual buyer matters beyond the headline price. A Neo Next+ win would concentrate an even larger share of UK production in a single HitecVision-backed consolidator, reinforcing a trend toward fewer, larger independent operators setting the pace on North Sea output decisions. An Adura win would hand Shell and Equinor — nominally exited players — indirect control of assets they once owned outright, blurring the line between "supermajor retreat" and simple internal reshuffling. Either way, decisions on how hard to produce Clair and Schiehallion, and for how long, will increasingly sit with balance sheets built for the North Sea's twilight years rather than its boom.

What comes next

Bidders are working through BP's data room now, with binding offers expected within weeks given BP's preference for a rapid, single-buyer sale. The Ithaca precedent is a caution against assuming a quick close — its own advanced talks fell apart earlier this year over price, and a £2.5 billion valuation still leaves room for disagreement on how much of that eight-billion-barrel resource is realistically recoverable at current tax rates. Watch for whether BP holds firm on a single-portfolio sale or splits it once bids come in; a break-up would tell its own story about how much appetite even the specialists have for taking on the whole of BP's remaining North Sea footprint at once.
