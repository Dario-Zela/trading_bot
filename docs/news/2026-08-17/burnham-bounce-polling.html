'Burnham bounce' gives Labour a narrow lead a month into new PM's tenure

Andy Burnham's official portrait as prime minister, taken at 10 Downing Street on 22 July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
A month into his premiership, Andy Burnham has pulled Labour narrowly ahead of Reform in the polls, but voters still don't trust how he'll pay for his spending pledges.

A month ago Andy Burnham was mayor of Greater Manchester, a job with no seat in the Commons and no keys to Downing Street. Today he is prime minister, and the polls that once looked like a death sentence for Labour have, for now, turned in his favour.

According to Ipsos polling for LBC, conducted 30 July–4 August among 1,033 adults, Labour has moved to 28%, ahead of Reform UK on 25%, with the Conservatives trailing on 18%. That is a reversal from June, when Reform led Labour by two points — a swing large enough that a pollster has given it a name: the "Burnham bounce." Lord Ashcroft's tracker, using a different methodology that asks how likely people are to vote for each party, finds a similar four-point Labour lead as of 8 August.

The man himself is doing better than the party. Ipsos has Burnham as the public's preferred choice for "most capable candidate to run the country" on 36%, well clear of Nigel Farage on 15% and Kemi Badenoch on 13%. Personal favourability data compiled by the New Statesman shows his ratings climbed 12 points in July alone, leaving him at 42% favourable against 30% unfavourable — the most positively viewed figure in British politics at the moment, if not by a wide margin.

A modest bounce, honestly measured

The scale of the shift should not be overstated. Burnham's own net satisfaction rating sits at 29% — LBC's polling puts him eight points behind where Keir Starmer stood at the equivalent point in his premiership, a comparison that undercuts any reading of the bounce as a triumphant relaunch. Seventy percent of respondents remain dissatisfied with how the country is being governed, and only 18% are satisfied with Labour's performance specifically. Regionally the picture is lopsided: Burnham polls dramatically better than Starmer did in the north of England — as much as 20 points better on likability in some breakdowns — while London shows barely any change between the two men. This looks less like a national reset than a leader whose appeal is concentrated in the places where Labour was haemorrhaging support to Reform.

Starmer resigned on 20 July, handing over to Burnham after a period of intensifying pressure inside the parliamentary party over the government's direction. Burnham took office already carrying the reputation, built over a decade as Greater Manchester mayor, of a politician willing to argue publicly for higher public spending and against further privatisation of services. That reputation is now the central fact of his premiership — and, per the polling, its central vulnerability.

Where's the money coming from

Lord Ashcroft's polling organisation tested nineteen of Burnham's policy pledges individually and found only six commanded majority support as both welcome and affordable in voters' eyes. The gap between the two is the story: people like what Burnham is offering more than they trust how it will be paid for.

"Where is the money coming from?"

That question, in some phrasing, recurred across Ashcroft's focus groups. One participant captured the underlying suspicion of the spending pledges specifically: "He's giving away lots of little snippets at the moment. We'll see how he deals with the bigger issues." Another, more pointed, suggested Burnham's elevation says more about Labour's internal politics than a public mandate: "He's been picked by the party because he's the one that will do their bidding without arguing."

Most voters expect that if Burnham's plans require money the government doesn't have, it will come from higher taxes rather than cuts elsewhere — a expectation the New Statesman's polling review notes is itself a marker of how far trust in Labour's fiscal restraint has eroded since 2024. Burnham has framed his agenda around reversing privatisation, arguing it has made public services more expensive rather than cheaper, but that argument has not yet translated into public confidence about the bill.

What the bounce actually measures

The honest reading is that Burnham has succeeded at something real but narrower than a governing mandate: he has made Labour likeable again to voters who had drifted toward Reform, particularly outside London, without yet persuading anyone that his spending agenda adds up. Both things can be true simultaneously, and polling analysts on both the Ashcroft and New Statesman sides are explicit that the bounce could evaporate quickly if it isn't followed by clarity on funding.

The next test is fiscal, not electoral. Burnham has so far offered pledges rather than a budget; the moment he has to reconcile the two — likely at the next fiscal event — will show whether voters' current goodwill survives contact with tax rises or spending cuts. Until then, the polls describe a prime minister more popular than his party, leading a party more popular than it was in June, with neither number yet answering the question voters keep asking him directly.
