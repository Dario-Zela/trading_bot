Ofwat weighs drought surge pricing as England's water crisis deepens

Abberton Reservoir in Essex during a previous drought — English reservoirs have again fallen to historically low levels this summer. — Glyn Baker / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Ofwat is weighing drought-linked "surge pricing" for water bills as three-quarters of England remains in drought under hosepipe bans.

Ofwat, the water regulator for England and Wales, is weighing whether to let suppliers charge households more for water precisely when they need it most — during droughts. The idea, still at consultation stage, would let bills rise in summer and fall in winter, or spike once a household's usage crosses a set threshold. It arrives as roughly three-quarters of England sits in official drought, the driest July in 190 years has left reservoirs at some of their lowest levels on record, and around 27 to 30 million people are living under hosepipe bans imposed by nine suppliers.

In one sentence: Ofwat is exploring drought-linked "surge pricing" that would raise water bills in scarce periods, testing political tolerance for scarcity pricing on an essential service mid cost-of-living squeeze.

A textbook idea meets a hard political moment

The economics are unremarkable. Charge more when a resource is scarce, demand falls, supply lasts longer — it's the logic behind peak-hour electricity tariffs and congestion charging, applied here to tap water. Fifteen water companies are already running tariff trials on customers with smart meters, and Ofwat has floated two main models: seasonal pricing, where summer rates run higher than winter ones, and rising block tariffs, where the price per cubic metre climbs once a household passes a usage threshold.

Thames Water's version would charge double the standard rate above 685 litres a day — roughly eight baths or thirteen washing-machine loads, against an average household use of about 438 litres, according to Southern Water's own figures. Anglian Water's trial in Lincoln and Norwich, cited by Ofwat as an early proof of concept, charged £4.39 per cubic metre in peak months (May to August) against £1.78 in the off-season and a £2.69 standard rate — and reported that roughly half of participating customers cut consumption in response. The government wants average daily use down from 136.5 litres per person to 122 by 2038; regulators clearly see price as the lever most likely to get there.

Five suppliers, one regulator, a lot of scrutiny

The surge-pricing debate landed alongside a separate but related Ofwat announcement on 13 August: a draft determination provisionally approving £3.4bn in additional infrastructure spending across 13 companies, with five of them — Thames Water, Southern Water, Severn Trent, Wessex Water and South East Water — provisionally cleared to recover some of that cost through higher bills before 2030. That sits on top of bill increases of roughly 36% already sanctioned for 2025-2030 under Ofwat's last full price review. Consultation on the draft determination runs until 24 September, with a final decision due in December; the standalone surge-pricing tariffs, where approved, could take effect from April 2027.

Ofwat's own case for the reforms rests on scarcity and efficiency: an official statement framed the changes as designed to "support greater consideration of water scarcity and efficiency," while Helen Campbell, Ofwat's executive director for delivery, pointed to a wider £104bn investment programme the regulator says its 2024 price review decisions are already funding. The regulator's chief executive, David Black, is stepping down at the end of August, leaving the surge-pricing decision to land during a leadership transition.

The politics water can't dodge

The resistance has been immediate and cuts across the political spectrum that matters here — cost-of-living politics. Prime Minister Andy Burnham, a month into his tenure, said companies had asked customers to "pay more for years" while pollution incidents stayed at record levels and leakage went largely unaddressed, warning that "customers cannot be treated as a blank cheque" and should not be "a bottomless source of funding for other people's failures."

Water campaigner Feargal Sharkey put the underlying grievance more bluntly: suppliers have had statutory duties to help customers cut demand "for 40 years... and they have failed at that comprehensively," he said, arguing the new tariffs would "penalise consumers for that failure." Greenpeace's analysis puts daily leakage losses at 2.87 billion litres — equivalent to 1,150 Olympic swimming pools — a figure that undercuts the case for asking households to conserve while pipes still bleed supply. A parliamentary petition calling for renationalising the water industry has gathered more than 172,000 signatures. The Consumer Council for Water has taken the most measured position of the lot, saying suppliers are right to explore new tariff structures but that any scheme must be demonstrably fair and protect customers already struggling with bills.

What the test actually measures

Strip away the noise and Ofwat is running an experiment other regulators will watch closely: whether a government can ask the public to accept market-style pricing for a resource everyone needs, at the exact moment household budgets are tightest, without a political reversal. Energy regulators have flirted with dynamic pricing for years and mostly retreated from it at the retail level; water, with its smart-meter rollout still patchy and no genuine substitute for the product being rationed, may prove even less forgiving of a pricing model that looks, to an ordinary bill-payer, like being charged more for turning on the tap during the hottest week of the year.

The next marker is 24 September, when Ofwat's consultation on the wider £3.4bn cost-recovery package closes, with a final determination due in December — the same window in which the standalone surge-pricing trials will either gain traction toward an April 2027 rollout or quietly stall under political pressure. Reservoir levels between now and then, and whether autumn rain arrives on schedule, will do as much to decide the outcome as anything said in Whitehall.
