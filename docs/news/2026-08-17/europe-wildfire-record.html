CLIMATE · WILDFIRES · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-17 · 6 min read

Europe's wildfire summer sets a modern record for burned land

A wildfire burns near a cemetery in Piedralaves, in Spain's Ávila province, during the July 2026 blaze that became the largest in the country's recorded history. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Wildfires have burned 1.23m acres across Spain, France and Italy this summer, cutting grain and olive harvests and pushing reinsurers toward repricing wildfire risk.

The numbers, by mid-August, have stopped shocking anyone who has been watching them accumulate. More than 1.23 million acres — roughly the area of Cyprus — has burned across Spain, France and Italy since the start of summer, according to European Union figures released in early August. France's own tally, upward of 160,000 hectares, is now a modern national record, more than double the previous mark of about 72,000 hectares set in 2022. Heat itself has killed over 25,000 people across the continent since April, with Germany's Robert Koch Institute attributing some 11,900 excess deaths to the swelter.

What began as a familiar Mediterranean summer story — tinder-dry hillsides, holidaymakers evacuated, dramatic aerial footage of water-bombers — has widened into something that touches grain futures, olive oil shelves and the balance sheets of European reinsurers. Traders parsing this year's harvest data and insurance renewal terms are treating it less as a weather event than as a data point in a longer repricing of climate risk across two very different markets.

A summer that broke its own records twice

The season's arithmetic is unusual even by the standards of a decade that keeps setting wildfire records. Western Europe's June-July average temperature reached 21.62°C, about 2.79 degrees above the long-term norm and warmer than the previous record set in 2022, according to Copernicus data cited by CNN. Four separate heatwaves rolled through the continent between June and August, each pushing dry vegetation closer to ignition and pulling firefighting resources thinner.

Spain's worst single blaze, in Ávila province, burned more than 50,000 hectares — the largest wildfire in the country's recorded history — and forced over 63,000 evacuations across Madrid, Toledo and Ávila. In France, a fire complex in the Gironde, near Bordeaux, triggered what officials called the country's largest peacetime evacuation since the Second World War, with more than 220,000 people moved out at its peak. Across France and Spain together, evacuations topped 330,000 by early August.

Carbon Brief's running tally shows the picture is not quite the worst on record continent-wide — 2025 still holds that title by a narrow margin — but 2026 remains well above the 20-year average, and France, Spain and Italy have each individually broken their own historical ceilings. The European Forest Fire Information System put the bloc-wide burned area at 542,926 hectares as of 10 August, second only to 2025 for the same calendar date.

Fields, groves and vineyards feel the heat

The agricultural toll is where the story starts to matter to people who have never set foot near a fire line. COCERAL, the European grain trade body, has cut its 2026 EU-plus-UK grain forecast to 286.6 million tonnes, down 23.4 million tonnes on the year, with wheat production revised down to 140.8 million tonnes from an earlier estimate of 143.7 million. Spain's own agriculture ministry now expects an 18.5 million-tonne grain harvest, versus 24 million tonnes last season — a drop driven as much by a late-May heatwave as by the fires themselves. The European Commission has separately trimmed its sunflower forecast by 7% and grain corn by 6%.

Olive growers face a similar squeeze. Spain supplies roughly 40% of the world's olive oil, and prolonged drought stress is prompting trees to shed fruit early as a survival mechanism — a natural response that nonetheless shows up as lost tonnage. Spain's olive producers' association warned in May that 880,000 tonnes, nearly 70% of the entire 2025-26 season's output, had already been sold, with early field data pointing to a 37% decline in flowering fertility for the coming harvest. Producers expect olive oil prices, which eased this year after 2024's peak of $11.19 per kilogram, to resume climbing this autumn.

Bordeaux's winemakers had their own scare when the Gironde fire complex burned within reach of vineyards during a critical ripening window; growers diverted equipment and staff to protect the vines and are cautiously optimistic about avoiding smoke taint, but the region's wine trade body has still projected roughly 30% less output than 2025 — piling onto an industry already contending with falling domestic consumption and export tariffs.

Reinsurers recalculate a "secondary" peril

For the insurance industry, the more consequential shift may be conceptual rather than financial. Swiss Re Institute estimates that wildfires in France, Spain and Greece caused roughly €19.1 billion ($22 billion) in total economic damage and losses this summer, though — as with most European catastrophes — only a fraction is insured; the institute puts the historical share of insured natural-catastrophe losses in Europe at around 25%. Balz Grollimund, Swiss Re's head of catastrophe perils, noted that "extensive wildland-urban interface zones mean that a severe fire near densely populated areas could still generate substantial economic losses," even where this year's fires largely spared major towns.

Moody's has flagged the season as a signal event for how the market treats wildfire risk. Losses are stacking across property, business interruption — more than 13,000 evacuated Gironde businesses filed interruption claims despite suffering no physical damage — agriculture and forestry simultaneously, while fire behavior itself is outrunning historical models: responders reported pyrocumulonimbus storm clouds forming above blazes and fires merging into single, erratically spreading complexes. Wildfire has traditionally been filed alongside floods and hail as a "secondary peril" in reinsurance models — shorthand for frequent but individually modest losses next to primary perils like hurricanes. Rising loss trends, running at an estimated 8-11% annual real-terms increase since 1970, are pushing some in the market to argue that classification understates the risk.

Morningstar DBRS, for its part, judges the fires "an earnings event" rather than a solvency threat for Europe's large diversified insurers, given global reinsurance capacity of roughly $790 billion and recent property-catastrophe renewals that saw double-digit price cuts. But the agency expects that comfort to narrow at January 2027 renewals, with loss-affected programmes facing higher retentions and tighter aggregate cover. Regional pricing has already moved: home insurance premiums in France's Nouvelle-Aquitaine, the region around Bordeaux, have risen roughly 20% this year. France's total economic losses from the season could reach €10-15 billion, which would make it the country's costliest wildfire year on record even if insured losses stay a fraction of that figure.

What comes next

The immediate catalysts sit in two calendars. Grain and oilseed traders will get a clearer read on the damage when the European Commission publishes its next MARS crop bulletin and COCERAL issues its autumn revision, both due in the coming weeks as harvest data firms up. On the insurance side, the real test lands at the January 2027 reinsurance renewals, when treaty pricing and retention levels for loss-affected European programmes will show whether this summer moves wildfire decisively out of the "secondary peril" column — or whether, as in past bad years, the market absorbs it and waits for the next one.
