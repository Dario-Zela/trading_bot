BHP, Rio Tinto and Caterpillar test battery-electric haul trucks in Australia's Pilbara

A Caterpillar 793 haul truck — the same model as the battery-electric 793 XE Early Learner now operating at BHP's Jimblebar iron ore mine in Western Australia — Dietmar Rabich / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
BHP and Rio Tinto have launched a two-truck battery-electric haul trial in the Pilbara, but their own stated scepticism about the technology suggests regulatory insurance, not a decarbonisation plan.

Two battery-electric mining trucks — machines capable of hauling 256 tonnes of iron ore per trip — began formal operations at BHP's Jimblebar mine in Western Australia's Pilbara last week. The occasion drew Premier Roger Cook, two state ministers, and the combined communications machinery of BHP, Rio Tinto, and Caterpillar. The trucks had been on-site since December.

That six-month gap is instructive. The Cat 793 XE "Early Learner" machines arrived quietly at Jimblebar, logged more than 100 operating hours and 200 test laps on the red-dirt haulage roads, and only attracted a formal launch — complete with state government dignitaries — once the companies were confident they would not break down mid-demonstration. Mining decarbonisation, it turns out, is photogenic only when the machines cooperate.

What "Early Learner" Actually Means

Caterpillar's choice of label repays attention. These are not prototypes in the engineering sense — the company ran those through its Tucson, Arizona proving ground before shipping any unit to a customer site. Nor are they a commercial product. They occupy a deliberate middle stage: sufficiently developed to operate under genuine field conditions, but instrumentally designed to generate the performance data needed to validate engineering assumptions before Caterpillar commits to full production.

There are seven of them in the world. Two are at Jimblebar.

The truck matches the diesel 793's payload and produces zero exhaust emissions at point of operation. What remains untested at commercial scale is whether charging infrastructure can keep pace with continuous production mining — and that is precisely what the current phase is designed to study. The next significant test involves Dynamic Energy Transfer: overhead catenary wires strung along haulage ramps, and a cab-mounted pantograph that rises to make contact while the truck is in motion, feeding high-voltage DC power directly to the drivetrain and battery simultaneously. Regenerative braking contributes additional charge on the downhill legs.

It is an elegant engineering concept. It also requires rewiring the physical infrastructure of every ramp on every mine where it might one day be deployed.

The Hedge in Plain Sight

Here is what makes the June 23 event worth examining beyond the press release: BHP's own 2025 annual report described battery-electric haul technology as "not yet ready to be deployed." The company has cited "low technology readiness" in investor communications and cautioned that large battery haul trucks are "not advanced enough to scale."

Then it launched a field trial.

Geraldine Slattery, BHP's President Australia, captured the official position at the launch: the company is "on track to meet our target of at least a 30% reduction to our operational greenhouse gas emissions by FY2030 from FY2020 levels," while simultaneously noting it remains "premature to put a timeline" on when the electric fleet transition will actually happen. Tim Day, BHP's Western Australia iron ore asset president, described the exercise as "reimagining how we operate" — without specifying when that reimagining concludes.

This is a recognisable corporate posture. It hedges against two futures at once: if emissions regulation tightens or institutional shareholders impose harder ESG conditions, BHP can point to active field trials and established supplier relationships. If neither materialises on the expected timeline, the company has not committed to a costly infrastructure overhaul.

Rio Tinto, running a parallel trial of two Komatsu 930 battery-electric trucks at separate Pilbara sites, offers nearly identical framing. Iron Ore CEO Matthew Holcz described decarbonising haulage as "a complex challenge requiring industry collaboration" — a form of words that acknowledges the direction while signalling no urgency about the speed. Rio Tinto operates 18 Pilbara mines. So does BHP. Neither company has published efficiency metrics from the first three months of their respective trials.

The contrast with Fortescue — the Pilbara's third major iron ore producer — is sharp. Andrew Forrest's company has ordered up to 400 zero-emissions haul trucks from XCMG and Liebherr, with phased deliveries from 2028 to 2030, and claims the Liebherr T264 already working at its mines has matched diesel on total cost of ownership. Fortescue estimates fuel savings of roughly $400 million per year once the transition is complete. That is a materially different reading of the economics, and it helps explain why the company is ordering hundreds of trucks while its two largest rivals are studying seven.

What to Watch

The Dynamic Energy Transfer phase is the first real test of commercial viability, because catenary infrastructure is the load-bearing constraint on whether battery-electric trucks can sustain production mining rather than merely demonstrate it. If the pantograph charging system proves reliable at Jimblebar's grade and tonnage — iron ore haulage is among the most demanding duty cycles in mining — it resolves the most consequential outstanding question, and removes one of BHP's stated reasons for caution.

The second catalyst is the comparative data set that will eventually emerge when BHP's Caterpillar results and Rio Tinto's Komatsu results are set side by side. Two different technology approaches, tested in the same Pilbara environment, sharing learnings under the terms of their collaboration agreement — that is more useful industrial intelligence than either trial produces alone.

Both companies have tied broader fleet deployment decisions to trial outcomes, without naming a decision date. The clearest signal of whether this remains a regulatory hedge or the start of something larger will be whether either company chooses to publish meaningful performance metrics when the Dynamic Energy Transfer testing concludes — and whether those numbers look anything like Fortescue's.
