M&S fair value edges up to £4.31 as analysts remain split on the retailer's recovery

Marks & Spencer's Briggate store in Leeds — one of the UK branches in the retailer's ongoing store rationalisation programme — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Simply Wall St's £0.04 lift to M&S's fair value to £4.31 is a rounding-error revision set against analyst targets stretching from £3.90 to £4.40 — a spread that signals genuine valuation uncertainty.

A four-penny revision to Marks & Spencer's fair value estimate is not, in isolation, the kind of thing that moves institutional money. But the context behind this week's update from Simply Wall St — the model lifted its estimate from £4.27 to £4.31 — is quietly telling, because the assumptions driving it are almost unchanged.

The revision involved fractional tweaks: revenue growth moved from 6.32% to 6.31%, net profit margin from 3.64% to 3.62%. The slightly higher headline figure was reached mainly because the model's forward P/E multiple was nudged from 15.08x to 15.28x, and the discount rate shaved from 7.91% to 7.89%. These are the kinds of adjustments that emerge from incremental data rather than a fresh read on the business.

A wide field, no consensus

What gives the revision its modest interest is how it lands against the broader analyst landscape. Price targets for M&S currently span from £3.90 — Citi's cautious stance, cut from £4.10 on concerns about execution and recovery pace — up to £4.39 and £4.40, where Morgan Stanley and Bernstein have recently initiated coverage with Overweight and Outperform ratings respectively. With the stock trading around 378p as of Friday, every major analyst is nominally bullish; the disagreement is about how bullish, and why.

Morgan Stanley's case rests on M&S being positioned to benefit from UK food retail consolidation rather than being squeezed by it. Bernstein, placing the company within its European food retail coverage, appears to share that view. Citi, by contrast, appears less persuaded that the timeline for recovery is tight enough to justify a higher multiple.

The divide is not trivial. At one end of the range, the upside from current levels is modest. At the other, it implies a meaningful re-rating — but one contingent on M&S executing a programme that, so far, has demonstrated early signs and significant setbacks in roughly equal measure.

The largest of those setbacks was the ransomware attack that struck around Easter 2025, attributed to the Scattered Spider group. The incident ultimately cost the company £131.3m in direct response and recovery costs, contributed to a profit fall from £881m to £671m in the 2025/26 financial year, and knocked online sales offline for nearly seven weeks. The disruption lingered through year-end.

M&S is now pressing ahead with a structural overhaul — a £500m-plus cost reduction programme targeting 2027/28, a plan to rationalise full-line stores from 232 to 180, and a simultaneous expansion of Foodhalls from 316 to 420 locations. Whether that adds up to a recovery story or a slow-motion repositioning is precisely what the spread in price targets reflects.

The next concrete checkpoint is M&S's upcoming trading update, where progress on cost savings and any read-through from the post-cyberattack trading environment will be closely watched by the analysts who cannot agree on what the shares are worth.
