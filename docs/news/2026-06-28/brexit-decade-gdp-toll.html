A decade on, Brexit has left the UK economy 4% smaller — with little sign of closing the gap

The City of London financial district, viewed from London City Hall — the square mile has weathered the Brexit decade, but UK equities' share of global benchmarks has halved — Colin and Kim Hansen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Deutsche Bank's 10-year audit puts the UK economy 4% below its non-Brexit path, with 685,000 jobs missing and no structural rebound on the horizon.

Ten years ago on 23 June 2016, 51.9% of British voters chose to leave the European Union. The result was treated, in the hours after polls closed, as a financial shock — sterling fell 8% in a single night — and an economic puzzle second. The conventional wisdom was that the shock would be absorbed. It has not been.

The counterfactual grows wider

Deutsche Bank's anniversary report, drawing on a synthetic control model that constructs a "shadow UK" from economies with comparable growth trajectories, puts the current gap at roughly 4%. The UK economy is, in its estimation, 4% smaller today than it would have been had the 2016 vote gone the other way. Employment is around 2% lower — approximately 685,000 jobs short of the counterfactual — and consumer prices are about 0.7% higher.

The timing matters. Most of the divergence did not appear immediately after the vote. Central bank stimulus, a weaker pound that cheapened UK exports, elevated immigration, and corporate stockpiling all masked the underlying shift in the early years. The real separation emerged after 2021, when the UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement took full effect and pandemic distortions faded, exposing the structural trade barriers the deal introduced. Business investment has been the weakest individual contributor — corporations facing persistent uncertainty about market access pulled back sharply. UK goods exports have underperformed G7 peers by roughly 15% on a trend basis.

The 4% figure sits at the more conservative end of the academic range. The UK in a Changing Europe research consortium and the Office for Budget Responsibility both cite a long-run productivity hit of around 4%, while more recent working papers estimate 6% to 8% when cumulative compounding is accounted for. Deutsche Bank's number is lower but methodologically defensible: the synthetic control approach is the standard tool for precisely this type of causal question.

"The effect has not been a sudden collapse," said Jonathan Portes, professor of economics at King's College London, "but a gradual and cumulative drag on trade, investment and productivity."

What it means for portfolios

For traders, Deutsche Bank's report functions less as an academic exercise than as a valuation memo. Sterling — still down roughly 10% against the dollar and 12% against the euro from its pre-referendum level — has not recovered. The pound has effectively priced the risk premium of UK exposure into the exchange rate and left it there, with the average depreciation over the decade running at 7.5%.

The equity market tells the same story in a different register. The FTSE 100 has gained 62% since the referendum, a compounded annual return of just under 5%. In the same period, the S&P 500 returned 253% and the German DAX 151%. UK equity funds have recorded six consecutive years of net outflows, shedding roughly $160 billion in cumulative redemptions. The UK's weight in the MSCI All Country World Index has halved over the decade, from roughly 10% to around 4%.

The valuation discount is now structural by most readings. UK equities trade at a 30–35% price-to-earnings discount to their US peers. That discount has attracted M&A bidders and corporate buybacks — corporate insiders and overseas acquirers evidently see value where public investors remain wary — but it has not attracted the sustained inflows that would close it. Deutsche Bank's finding, by framing the cost as persistent rather than cyclical, provides a further argument against expecting a spontaneous re-rating of UK assets.

Economists project average annual growth of just 1.3% between 2026 and 2030, a number that reflects both the ongoing drag of trade barriers and the structural reality of fewer exporting firms, weaker competitive pressure, and reduced integration into European supply chains.

The review, and what it might recover

There is a live negotiation that could, at the margin, shift the picture. The 2026 review of the UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement is now underway, and targeted improvements — a veterinary or sanitary and phytosanitary agreement, linked emissions trading schemes, and some form of professional qualification recognition — are the most discussed options. Deutsche Bank estimates that meaningful improvements to the TCA framework could add 0.4% to 0.8% to UK GDP. Not trivial, but not a structural reset either: those numbers imply closing perhaps a fifth of the existing gap under optimistic assumptions.

Political context constrains even that. The Labour government under Keir Starmer has pursued a "reset" of EU relations while ruling out rejoining the single market or customs union. Roughly 52% of British adults say they would like to rejoin the EU, according to Ipsos polling, yet that shift in public opinion has not translated into a policy agenda that would move the economic needle materially.

The TCA review's formal conclusions, expected in the second half of 2026, are the next concrete catalyst to watch. Whether targeted sector-by-sector gains can be secured — and ratified before political momentum dissipates — will determine whether the 4% gap begins, however slowly, to narrow. A decade of data suggests it will not close on its own.
