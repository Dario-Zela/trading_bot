NatWest mandates AI ethics accreditation for all 60,000 staff

NatWest Group's registered head office at St Andrew Square, Edinburgh — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NatWest is putting all 60,000 staff through mandatory AI ethics training built with the University of Edinburgh, targeting completion by October 2026.

NatWest Group announced on June 22 that it is putting all 60,000 of its employees through a mandatory accreditation in AI and Data Ethics — a programme built with the University of Edinburgh and expected to run from now until October.

The training consists of eight e-learning modules pegged to the bank's own AI Ethics Principles, a collaborative half-day session on real-world application, and practical guidance for identifying and managing ethical risks. Employees have two to three months to complete it. On the other side they receive an internal NatWest accreditation and are committed to ongoing learning as the technology evolves — an open-ended obligation that gives the bank some room to keep updating the syllabus without launching a new programme each time.

The content was originally developed with Edinburgh's Edinburgh Futures Institute and piloted on about 90 colleagues across different parts of the bank. That around 50 per cent of pilot participants apparently went on to complete additional modules voluntarily is the kind of figure that sounds impressive in a press release but tells you little about what happened when it became compulsory.

The named voice behind the initiative is Dr Paul Dongha, NatWest's head of responsible AI and AI strategy. His framing — "equip colleagues with the skills and confidence to use it responsibly" — is the standard language of every AI governance push in financial services right now. Whether the eight modules actually shift behaviour or become the sort of annual compliance click-through that staff race through before a deadline is the test that nobody will publish results from.

Why a bank is doing this now

The regulatory backdrop matters here, even if NatWest does not name it explicitly. The Financial Conduct Authority has been clear it does not intend to write new AI-specific rules; instead it expects firms to manage AI risk through existing frameworks — consumer duty, the Senior Managers and Certification Regime, operational resilience rules. In January it launched a long-term review of how AI could reshape retail finance. What this regulatory posture means in practice is that banks are expected to demonstrate governance without waiting for a rulebook. Mandatory staff training is one of the more legible things you can point to in a supervisory conversation.

NatWest is also active in the FCA's AI Live Testing programme and has deployed Cora+, an agentic AI assistant that can now handle fraud reporting in real time through natural language. Putting ethics training in front of the entire workforce at roughly the moment the customer-facing AI is becoming more autonomous is not a coincidence.

None of that makes this a market event. NatWest has not disclosed what the programme costs, and there is no stated regulatory deadline driving the timeline. The full rollout completes in October; whether Lloyds, Barclays, or other major retail banks follow with similar programmes in the next twelve months will be a more meaningful indicator of whether this represents industry standard-setting or just one bank's compliance team working ahead of the curve.
