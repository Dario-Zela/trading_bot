Oil at $70: two stocks still worth holding as the Hormuz reopening weighs on prices

The Strait of Hormuz from orbit. About 20% of global seaborne oil trade passes through the 33-mile-wide chokepoint, which Iran closed in late February. — NASA/MODIS — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Crude at $70 reflects demand destruction, not just supply relief — ConocoPhillips and BP offer insulation via low breakevens and SPR-restocking upside.

When the Strait of Hormuz reopened in mid-June — following the June 17 peace memorandum between Washington and Tehran — the expected price bounce failed to materialise. West Texas Intermediate crude, which had traded as high as $112.95 a barrel in April as the US-Iran war strangled the world's most critical oil chokepoint, touched $69.63 on June 24, its first dip below $70 since the conflict began. Brent settled at $73.74 that same day. The 38% decline from peak tells a straightforward story of supply normalising — but the market's muted response to that normalisation tells a more troubling one.

The original spike was supply-side: roughly 20% of global seaborne oil trade passes through the Strait of Hormuz, and when Iran effectively closed it on February 28, the market repriced accordingly. What has changed is that high prices did their usual work. The International Energy Agency now forecasts global oil demand to fall by 1.1 million barrels per day across 2026 compared to 2025 — a downgrade of 700,000 bpd from its May estimate — with second-quarter deliveries down five million bpd year-on-year. Petrochemicals and aviation have taken the sharpest hits; broader industrial demand has followed. Crude's problem is no longer a closed strait; it is the demand hole that four months of elevated prices carved out.

Two names with room to run

Against that backdrop, ConocoPhillips (COP) and BP (BP) look relatively well-positioned among the sector's casualties. ConocoPhillips trades around $105.96 at a forward price-to-earnings ratio below 11 times, with a 3.1% dividend yield. Its production base in the Permian and Bakken gives it breakeven costs below $40 a barrel — meaning it generates free cash flow even if crude drifts lower. The company commits to returning 45% of cash from operations to shareholders through dividends and buybacks. BP, at $37.13 and a forward multiple below 8 times, offers a 5.3% yield and is targeting $6.5–7.5 billion in structural cost reductions through 2027.

The long-run argument for both rests not on the current spot price but on restocking demand. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve sits at its lowest level since 1983; commercial inventories drained during the Hormuz disruption are similarly depleted. Governments and refiners will need to rebuild. Both ConocoPhillips and BP concentrate the bulk of their production in North America and Europe — insulating them from a renewed Middle East flare-up better than Gulf-exposed peers, while still standing to benefit when restocking purchases hit the market.

The near-term watch is whether the IEA's June demand forecast proves too pessimistic or prescient. The agency expects a rebound of 2.5 million barrels per day in 2027 — but that recovery hinges on macro conditions stabilising. For now, oil's price signal is unambiguous: the market has stopped worrying about supply and started worrying about growth.
