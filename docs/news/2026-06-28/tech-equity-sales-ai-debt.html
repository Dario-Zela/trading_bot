Tech giants turn to equity sales to fund their AI surge as bond investors demand more

Inside a Google data center — Alphabet's $180-190 billion capex guidance for 2026 is overwhelmingly directed at AI infrastructure and compute. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Alphabet's record $85B stock sale and widening tech bond spreads signal a split: equity markets are absorbing AI dilution that debt investors are pricing more dearly.

The largest equity offering in history was not from an oil major or a bank reshuffling its capital. It came from a search company — Alphabet, Google's parent — which in early June priced $85 billion of stock, breaking the record set by Petrobras in 2010. The purpose was equally direct: servers, networking gear, cooling systems, and the AI compute capacity that runs on all of it.

That sale, which drew a $10 billion private placement from Berkshire Hathaway, is the most visible point in a pattern building across the technology sector for the past 18 months. Tech companies are raising equity at a pace not seen since the dot-com era, while bond markets — the traditional vehicle for large corporate investment — are simultaneously signaling that the price of tech credit is climbing. The divergence between the two is now wide enough to be a story in its own right.

Equity first

Alphabet's offering came in two tranches — an initial $45 billion, upsized from the planned $40 billion, with a further $40 billion slated for the third quarter. CEO Sundar Pichai described it as "part of our multi-year investment strategy to meet the AI opportunity ahead." The numbers behind that framing are stark: Alphabet has guided to capex of $180 to $190 billion in 2026, double last year's figure and six times its 2022 level.

The company's position illustrates a constraint now affecting the whole hyperscaler cohort. The four largest spenders — Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon — are channeling roughly 90% of their combined operating cash flow into capital expenditure in 2026, up from about 65% last year. Cash generation alone no longer covers the AI build.

Alphabet was not the only company choosing equity. SpaceX raised $75 billion in its IPO in mid-June. Meta has been evaluating a stock raise of its own. Anthropic recently went public. OpenAI is reported to be considering an IPO as early as next year. The template — growth company, AI rationale, equity issuance — is repeating with notable consistency.

The debt that follows

Equity, in this context, is not a substitute for debt. It is a precursor.

Five hyperscalers — Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, and Oracle — issued $121 billion in US investment-grade bonds in 2025, more than four times their average annual issuance from 2020 to 2024. Projections for 2026 point to $130 to $150 billion in hyperscaler net supply. Across the investment-grade market broadly, Wall Street expects around $300 billion in AI-related bond issuance this year. AI-related debt already accounts for 14% of the investment-grade market. The collective weighting of Meta, Alphabet, Amazon, and Oracle in the Bloomberg US Corporate IG Index nearly doubled in the year to April 2026, from 2.2% to 4.1%.

SpaceX illustrated the dynamic precisely. Less than two weeks after its IPO, the company raised $25 billion in bonds across five tranches, including 20- and 30-year maturities. The 2036 tranche landed at 1.4 percentage points over Treasuries — notably wide for a newly minted investment-grade issuer. The bonds weakened further in secondary trading, producing paper losses of around $360 million relative to Treasuries within days of launch.

SpaceX's case was unusual in one respect: rating agencies assigned investment-grade status despite projections of significant negative cash flow through at least 2027. But the broader tension it illustrated is general. New-issue concessions across hyperscaler bond deals have averaged around 12 basis points — against roughly 2.5 basis points for the broader investment-grade universe. Tech IG risk premiums rose to 0.79 percentage points in May, up from 0.74 the prior month.

Mike Talaga, global head of credit research at Janus Henderson, put the situation plainly: "The AI arms race is not just a tech equity story — it is a credit story."

What bondholders are watching

The co-existence of record equity issuance and rising bond spreads admits two readings, and market participants are not fully aligned on which holds.

The constructive view is that equity sales represent deliberate balance-sheet management. By raising capital before burning it, companies signal confidence and offer bondholders a larger equity cushion beneath their claims. Anthony Woodside of L&G Asset Management acknowledged as much, noting that equity raises mean "slower balance-sheet deterioration" — before adding that they also signal "there's a lot more debt coming too."

Tom Murphy of Columbia Threadneedle framed the dual dynamic more directly: the scale of equity issuance is "telling us that the amount of capital expenditure is probably going to go up." For bondholders, that is not a neutral message. Over the six months to February, median spreads on medium-term bonds from investment-grade hyperscalers widened by 10 basis points while median equity prices fell 12% — a period during which equity markets were digesting AI spending expectations that credit markets were pricing more cautiously.

JPMorgan estimates that AI and data-center investment will reach $5.5 trillion through 2030, of which $2.1 trillion will require debt-market financing — up from a prior $1.5 trillion projection. Absorbing that supply, at progressively wider new-issue concessions, will test the bond market's appetite in ways the equity market's current enthusiasm does not capture. Whether the premium investors are already demanding represents a sensible re-pricing of duration risk or an early signal of credit stress in the sector is a question the next round of earnings will begin to answer.

Alphabet's second $40 billion equity tranche is due this quarter. When it prices, and when the accompanying bond issuance follows, both markets will have another data point to disagree about.
