GSK wins FDA approval for lung cancer drug Nelkarmi

In one sentence
No FDA approval for 'Nelkarmi' could be found; GSK's lung cancer approval is Jideytro, from July, and the £3.4bn CFO claim matches only a ~£3.6bn consensus gap.

A headline circulating on aggregator sites says the US Food and Drug Administration has approved a GSK lung cancer drug called Nelkarmi. This newspaper could not confirm that. Searches of regulatory and trade coverage found no product of that name. They did find a different GSK lung cancer approval, which is the one that appears to sit behind the story.

What can be verified

The approval reported by trade outlets including Fierce Pharma, BioWorld and Dow Jones is for Jideytro (zidesamtinib), not Nelkarmi. Those reports date the FDA decision to July 2026, not this week. They say it came ahead of the agency's 18 September target date.

Jideytro came to GSK through its $10.6bn acquisition of Nuvalent. It is indicated for adults with locally advanced or metastatic ROS1-positive non-small cell lung cancer who have already received a ROS1 kinase inhibitor.

The approval rested on a single-arm trial, in which 44% of 117 patients saw tumours shrink or disappear. A single-arm trial has no comparison group, so that response rate cannot be read as a survival benefit. The reports do not say how durable the responses were.

A second Nuvalent drug, neladalkib, is reported to face an FDA decision on 27 November 2026. That date comes from trade press and has not been checked against GSK's own statements.

The £3.4bn question

The other half of the story is a claim that GSK's chief financial officer pointed to cancer drugs to bridge a £3.4bn gap in sales forecasts. No source found here attributes a £3.4bn figure to the CFO.

The nearest match is a gap of about £3.6bn between broker-consensus forecasts and GSK's own target of £40bn in annual sales by 2031. Coverage ahead of the company's 28 July strategy update described it that way. Those figures come from third-party consensus rather than company guidance. One of the articles itself cautions that the figure is not a forecast.

The same coverage puts consensus oncology sales rising from about £2.5bn to about £6bn, a gain of roughly £3.5bn. It also puts the expected decline in dolutegravir-based HIV treatments at about £4.7bn as patents expire. On those numbers, oncology alone does not fully offset the HIV loss. The numbers are secondary and may not match, so they point to the shape of the argument and are not a settled finding.

The new chief executive is quoted as saying: "The number 40 is doable, and I stand behind it." The CFO's exact words and the forecast period behind the £3.4bn figure remain unverified.

What to watch

The claim that a specific drug filled a specific gap should wait for primary documents: the FDA label, GSK's press release and the transcript of the CFO's remarks. The first test of the oncology offset is the neladalkib decision, reported for 27 November, which would show whether GSK's lung cancer franchise has more than one product. GSK's next quarterly results, when sales are reported against the 2031 target, will show how much of the gap the cancer portfolio is closing.

This brief will be updated if the Nelkarmi name is confirmed by the FDA or GSK. It may instead be an error in the aggregator reports.
