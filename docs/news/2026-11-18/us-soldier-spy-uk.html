US soldier held in UK over alleged Russia spying moved into US military custody

In one sentence
A US soldier arrested near Gloucester on suspicion of spying for Russia has reportedly been moved to US military custody, leaving open which country will prosecute him.

A US Army soldier arrested in England on suspicion of spying for Russia has been moved from British police custody into the custody of the US military, according to the reports that prompted this piece. The handover, if the details hold, turns a counter-espionage arrest on British soil into a test of how two close allies divide a case between them. The transfer itself rests on BBC and Guardian reporting that could not be independently confirmed here. The arrest is documented more widely.

The arrest

The soldier, aged 41 according to press accounts, was detained near Gloucester on Tuesday 10 November. He was held under sections 1 and 3 of the National Security Act 2023, which cover obtaining or disclosing protected information and assisting a foreign intelligence service. The Metropolitan Police said the arrest followed an investigation by Counter Terrorism Policing working with US authorities, including the US military and the FBI. The US Army said MI5 and UK Defence Counter Intelligence were also involved.

Reports citing a US military official say he was based at Imjin Barracks, which houses the headquarters of NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps in Gloucestershire. Gloucester is also home to GCHQ, though nothing published links the case to the agency. Accounts differ on his rank: some describe a senior soldier, others only a soldier. He had not been named in the coverage reviewed, and no charges had been announced.

Officials told reporters that no sensitive information had been passed to Russia. That is a statement from briefed officials, not a formal finding, and the investigation has not publicly established what, if anything, reached Moscow.

Why custody matters

Under the National Security Act, British police can hold a suspect for a limited period before they must charge or release them. A transfer to US military custody would remove the man from that clock. It would not necessarily end British interest in the case. A US official told The Washington Post that the soldier could also face military charges in the United States once British authorities had dealt with the case, which implies a sequence rather than a hand-off.

The legal basis for any transfer is the open question. Visiting US forces in Britain are covered by long-standing status-of-forces arrangements that give the US military a claim to jurisdiction over its personnel in some circumstances. Offences against British national security are a different matter, and Britain would normally want to keep the option of prosecuting them. Neither the Home Office nor the Ministry of Defence had set out, in the coverage reviewed, whether the move meant British proceedings were being dropped, paused or kept in reserve.

What to watch

The first marker is whether the Crown Prosecution Service authorises charges under the 2023 Act, and whether the man is named when it does. The second is whether the US Army or the Justice Department announces military charges, such as under the Uniform Code of Military Justice, and on what timetable. Both would show whether the case is heading for one courtroom or two. Until then, the picture is of an allied investigation that worked well enough to produce an arrest, and has not yet said who will try the suspect.
