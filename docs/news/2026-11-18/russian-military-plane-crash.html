Russian military plane crashes, killing all 19 on board

In one sentence
An Antonov An-12 military transport crashed in Siberia's Kemerovo region, killing all 19 aboard; officials point to equipment failure while a commission investigates.

A Russian military transport aircraft has crashed in western Siberia, killing all 19 people on board, the Defence Ministry said. The aircraft, a Soviet-designed Antonov An-12, came down on Tuesday in an unpopulated area near the village of Yagunovo, in the Kemerovo region, roughly 3,000km (1,900 miles) east of Moscow. No one on the ground was reported hurt.

What is known

The ministry said a commission from the Russian Aerospace Forces is working at the site "to establish all the circumstances of the incident." Regional governor Ilya Seredyuk said on Telegram that emergency crews had put out a fire at the wreckage and were assisting investigators. The Investigative Committee, Russia's main criminal investigative body, has opened a case into possible violations of flight-safety rules, a routine step after a fatal crash.

Preliminary findings point to equipment failure, according to Al Jazeera's account of the official line. That is an early assessment, not a finding, and it has not been independently confirmed. Some early reports of a fire in the air have not been verified either. Initial reports put the dead at 17 before the ministry gave the figure of 19.

The An-12 typically flies with a crew of about six, so most of those aboard were presumably passengers or additional personnel. The reports consulted do not say who they were or what the flight was carrying, and the ministry has not said where the aircraft was heading.

An old aircraft

The four-engine turboprop entered service in the late 1950s, and more than 1,000 were built. Some are still flown in Russia and elsewhere, and Russia's armed forces rely on such transports to move troops and cargo across the country's vast distances.

That age is the context in which a technical fault will be read. The crash comes weeks after a Tu-95 strategic bomber crashed in the Amur region in September, killing six crew and injuring a seventh. Earlier this year an An-26 crashed in Crimea, with 29 people reported killed. Two incidents involving ageing Soviet-era types do not establish a pattern on their own, and Russian aviation has always had a high accident rate. But a heavily used fleet, a war that has raised demand on it, and sanctions that complicate access to foreign parts and components are the usual explanations offered for strain.

Whether this crash adds to that case depends on what the commission finds. A maintenance or component failure would sit comfortably in that argument; a handling error, a weather factor or a training-related cause would not. Russian military investigations seldom publish detailed conclusions quickly, if at all, so the public record may stay thin.

What to watch

The next markers are the identification of the dead and any statement on the purpose of the flight, then whatever the Aerospace Forces commission releases. A Defence Ministry statement naming a specific fault would be the first sign of whether the technical-failure explanation survives a fuller inquiry.
