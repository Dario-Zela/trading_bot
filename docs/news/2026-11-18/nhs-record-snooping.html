NHS England logs more than 3,000 incidents of staff snooping on patient records

In one sentence
NHS data reported by the Guardian shows more than 3,000 incidents of staff improperly accessing patient records in England, raising doubts about how well access controls are enforced.

NHS staff improperly looked at patients' records more than 3,000 times in England, according to NHS data reported by the Guardian. The figure counts incidents of improper access. It does not count confirmed cases in which information was leaked or misused. The data's reporting describes the finding as "deeply concerning". That wording comes from the initial report, and this article has not been able to check it against the original text or the underlying dataset.

The distinction matters. Snooping, in the language of information governance, means an employee with legitimate system access opening a record without a clinical or administrative reason. The record might belong to a neighbour, an ex-partner, a celebrity or a colleague. No hacking is needed, because the member of staff is already logged in.

What the number does and does not show

A count of more than 3,000 incidents can be read in two ways, and the available evidence does not settle which is right.

On the first reading, the figure shows the system working. Audit tools flag odd access, and trusts record and investigate what they find. Organisations that detect more may simply be looking harder.

On the second reading, the figure shows controls that rely too heavily on detection after the fact. Staff in many roles can open large numbers of records, and an audit trail only helps if someone reads it. A total this size, spread across a workforce of well over a million, suggests the problem is not confined to a few individuals.

The second reading is the more plausible, but it is an inference. The triage material gives no breakdown by trust, role or outcome, and no information on how many incidents ended in disciplinary action or a referral to the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO). Those details would show whether the 3,000 is mostly curiosity and carelessness or something more serious.

A pattern that predates the figure

The new number sits alongside a run of earlier cases. The Guardian previously reported that between 2017 and 2022 there were 194 incidents of doctors allegedly accessing medical records without clinical justification that prompted a complaint to the regulator. That reporting also noted 24 doctors censured over record breaches in the period.

In 2026 the issue became more prominent. Reports said staff at Liverpool University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust had inappropriately accessed care records of victims of the 2024 Southport attack. HSJ reported that nearly 50 staff there were disciplined. Another trust, York and Scarborough Teaching Hospitals, reported 18 staff wrongfully accessing records since 2021, with eight cases referred to the ICO.

NHS England's chief executive, Sir Jim Mackey, has since told trusts to suspend immediately any staff suspected of accessing records without a legitimate reason, and has warned that staff could face dismissal or prosecution. These accounts come from secondary reports. The NHS England page announcing the instruction could not be loaded for this article, so the exact wording and requirements should be checked against it.

Why access controls are the question

Unauthorised access to personal data can be a criminal offence under data protection law, and professional regulators can act against clinicians. Deterrents exist. The weakness, on the evidence of the figure, is that they come into play after the access has happened.

Three questions will show whether the controls are tightening. First, whether trusts publish consistent figures, so that patients can compare organisations. Second, whether suspension on suspicion, as now directed, leads to more investigations that end in sanctions rather than quiet closure. Third, whether systems move towards restricting access by default, with a recorded justification required to open records outside a person's own caseload.

Patients' willingness to share sensitive information depends on trust that it will stay confidential. The next ICO enforcement update and any NHS England publication of trust-level audit data will show whether the 3,000 figure is a high-water mark or the start of a longer count.
