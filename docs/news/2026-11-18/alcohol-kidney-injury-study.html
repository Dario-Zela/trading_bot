Beer and spirits linked to higher risk of acute kidney injury, study suggests

In one sentence
A UK Biobank study of 250,791 drinkers links spirits (HR 1.40) and beer (HR 1.24), but not wine, to higher acute kidney injury risk, though it shows association only.

A large British study has found that people who mainly drink beer or spirits were more likely to develop acute kidney injury (AKI) over about 14 years than other drinkers. Wine drinkers showed no statistically significant increase. The result is an association in observational data, and it does not show that any beverage damages the kidneys.

The work, published on 20 October in Scientific Reports and led by Anglia Ruskin University, drew on the UK Biobank. It followed 250,791 current drinkers, recruited at 22 assessment centres across the UK, for a median of 14 years.[^1] Intake by beverage type was self-reported at the start. Abstainers were not included. AKI is a sudden fall in kidney function, and the study counted new cases as they arose.

The numbers

After adjustment for confounders, including liver disease, the study reported these hazard ratios, where 1.0 means no difference in risk:[^1]

  • Spirits: 1.40 (95% confidence interval 1.30–1.50)
  • Beer: 1.24 (1.17–1.32)
  • Red wine: 0.96, interval spanning 1.0
  • White wine: 1.06, interval spanning 1.0

In relative terms that is about 40% higher hazard for spirits and 24% for beer. Those figures do not give the absolute risk. AKI was uncommon in the cohort, and the percentages here come from secondary reporting that did not state the baseline rate. The paper itself should be checked for it.

The researchers also found that higher C-reactive protein (HR 1.41) and a higher neutrophil count (HR 2.60) were independently linked to AKI. Spirits and beer were associated with higher levels of these inflammatory markers, while wine was associated with lower levels.[^1] That fits an inflammation pathway, but the data do not establish it as the cause.

What the study cannot say

The design limits what can be concluded. An observational cohort can show that two things travel together, not that one causes the other. Drinking was recorded once, by the participants themselves, so it may be misreported and will not capture changes over 14 years. People who prefer wine also tend to differ from beer and spirits drinkers in income, diet and drinking pattern, and adjustment can only partly correct for that.

The absence of a significant wine association is not evidence that wine protects the kidneys, or that any alcohol is safe. A non-significant result means the study could not distinguish wine from no effect. It does not mean there was none.

The account here relies on a secondary report of the paper, and the author list was not available to this desk. Guardian UK is the original outlet behind the headline. The wording differs between "kidney damage" in headlines and "kidney injury" in the study. This article uses "acute kidney injury", the clinical term.

What to watch

The next step is replication in other cohorts, ideally ones that record drinking patterns such as binge episodes alongside beverage type. Until then the finding is a hypothesis worth testing, not a basis for advice on which drink to choose.

[^1]: Study coverage reported by Vinetur, citing Scientific Reports, 20 October 2026; UK Biobank cohort, 250,791 current drinkers, median follow-up 14 years. Figures not yet checked against the original paper.
