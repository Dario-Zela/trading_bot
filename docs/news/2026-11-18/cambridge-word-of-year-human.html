'Human' is Cambridge Dictionary's word of the year

In one sentence
Cambridge Dictionary has named 'human' its word of the year; whether the editors tied it to AI and authenticity is not yet verified.

Cambridge Dictionary has chosen "human" as its word of the year, according to the Guardian. It is an unusually plain pick. The word has no slang edge and no new sense to explain, and it is about as old as English gets.

The detail that would make this more than a culture note is the editors' own reasoning. That could not be checked for this piece, and nothing below assumes it. Whether Cambridge framed the choice around artificial intelligence, authenticity, or something else entirely is the open question. Readers should consult the dictionary's own announcement for the stated rationale.

A recent pattern

Cambridge's recent choices show why a reader might reach for the AI explanation. In 2023 the word was "hallucinate", picked because of a new sense: when an AI tool "hallucinates", it produces false information. In 2024 it was "manifest", and in 2025 "parasocial", the one-sided bond a fan forms with a public figure or, increasingly, a chatbot. Dictionary editors tend to favour words whose meaning is being stretched or pressured by current events. That suggests a reason to read "human" in the same light. But it is an inference from the pattern, not something the available reporting confirms.

Why an old word can still be news

"Human" is a word that gets defined by contrast. For most of its history the contrast was with animals, or with gods. Increasingly the foil is a machine that writes, draws and chats in passable imitation of the real thing. Anyone who has been asked to tick a box confirming they are not a robot has met the question in miniature.

A dictionary pick can capture that shift without any new coinage, because the argument is about what the word excludes rather than what it means. If Cambridge's editors leaned that way, the choice would be a small, tidy marker of how ordinary language absorbs anxiety about technology. If they did not, it may simply reflect a spike in lookups for a word people were reaching for in other contexts.

What to watch

The thing to check is the editors' published explanation, including any lookup data Cambridge cites and whether its own example sentences touch on AI. That will settle whether this is a one-line culture note or a modest story about language tracking the machines. Other dictionaries tend to announce their own picks around this time of year. A shared theme across them would say more about the mood than any single choice.
