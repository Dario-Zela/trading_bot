Nvidia says it will not reinstate Jensen Huang as it reports soaring sales

In one sentence
Nvidia's chairman said the board will not reinstate Jensen Huang, as the company reported revenue of over $115bn, roughly double a year earlier, in its first results since his exit.

Nvidia's board has said it will not bring back Jensen Huang, the co-founder who resigned as chief executive last week, even as the company reported a quarter in which revenue roughly doubled. The two facts sit awkwardly together: the business Huang built has just posted its strongest numbers yet, and the board has shut the door on the man most associated with them.

The numbers

For its fiscal third quarter, Nvidia reported revenue of more than $115bn, about twice the level of a year earlier, and net income of $65.3bn, according to AFP reporting carried by Yahoo Finance. Other outlets put revenue at about $115.8bn, with data-centre revenue near $107.6bn and a gross margin of 74.8%. Earnings per share were reported at $2.77 against analyst expectations of $2.48 in one account; other outlets give slightly different per-share figures, so that number should be treated with care.

Guidance was also raised. Nvidia pointed to roughly $130bn of revenue for the fourth quarter, according to one analyst summary, and Yahoo reports that the full-year growth forecast was lifted to 75% from 70%. Shares rose a little over 2% in after-hours trading. That recovered only a fraction of the damage: the stock has lost more than a quarter of its value since the allegations about Huang emerged in late October.

The board's position

Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director with immediate effect. An 8-K filing dated 12 November gave no reason. Press reports link the departure to allegations of extramarital affairs, one with an alleged Chinese spy; Huang's side of that account has not been tested in public. Chief financial officer Colette Kress became interim CEO while remaining CFO, and director Harvey Jones became the company's first chair, per a third-party analysis of the filing.

On the results call, Jones was blunt.

"The Nvidia board unanimously accepted his resignation and does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO."

He declined to discuss the circumstances. He said the board wants a new chief executive quickly but will not rush the choice. Huang had said before the results that he would return if the board offered him the role. Reuters, citing three unnamed people, had earlier reported that the board was not considering it.

Kress described the company as "business as usual": "Our momentum is real. Our demand is accelerating, our supply is growing."

The pressure outside the boardroom

The board's statement lands against an unusual public campaign. Elon Musk is among those urging Nvidia to restore Huang. A "Stand With Jensen" petition had about 177,000 signatories as of 16 November, calling for his return "while an independent investigation establishes the facts". The Financial Times reported that Huang is considering a comeback and that a group of Silicon Valley figures has lobbied the White House. President Trump said on Monday that "a lot of people are asking Jensen, they think Jensen should go back." Al Jazeera notes that Trump's disclosures suggest he held between $13m and $62m of Nvidia stock at the end of August, and that the White House is reportedly planning to name Nvidia among its AI "national champions".

The board has, in effect, chosen to be judged on its process rather than the share price. Analysts quoted by Al Jazeera estimate Nvidia could lose 25 to 30 percent of its value without Huang; the stock's fall since October is already of that order.

What to watch

The numbers are the argument for continuity: demand, by Kress's account, is accelerating, and a quarter at this scale suggests the order book does not depend on one person. The harder test is the next one. Nvidia has to name a permanent chief executive, with no timetable given, and the results of any investigation into the allegations will shape whether the board's refusal holds. Shares and the fourth-quarter report, due early next year, will show whether customers and investors accept the answer.
