Milburn's Neets report: hiring tax breaks, a welfare overhaul and a Labour party tension

In one sentence
Milburn's Neets review proposes hiring tax breaks and welfare reform; unions have raised concerns, and Burnham has pledged speed without promising to accept it all.

Britain's latest attempt to deal with the young people who are neither studying nor working has arrived as a package with an awkward shape. A review led by Alan Milburn into young people not in education, employment or training, the group known as Neets, proposes tax relief for businesses that hire them. It also backs an overhaul of the welfare system that surrounds them. The government has said it wants to avoid a 'lost generation'. The harder question is whether it can deliver that without the support of the unions and the Labour MPs it needs.

The detail is thin so far. This article draws on the headline findings and on press coverage of the report and the government's response. The size of the proposed tax breaks, who would qualify and what they would cost have not been independently checked against the report. Nor have the specifics of the welfare changes or their timetable.

What the review proposes

The report's central commercial idea is that employers should pay less tax when they take on young people. The logic is familiar. Hiring someone with no work history is a risk, and a tax incentive lowers the price of taking it. Whether such incentives change behaviour, or simply subsidise hiring that would have happened anyway, has long been argued over by economists. The report's answer to that argument, and the figures behind it, will need to be read in full.

The second strand is welfare. The government has backed a shake-up of the system, framed as a way to stop a generation drifting away from work. The shape of the changes is not confirmed in the material available. The political framing is, though. Ministers are presenting the overhaul as prevention rather than retrenchment.

A promise with a hedge

On 13 November, ITV News reported that Burnham had pledged to move quickly on the review but declined to give a blanket assurance that every recommendation would be accepted in full. The gap between the two statements matters. 'Quickly' signals political intent. 'Not in full' leaves room to drop the parts that prove costly or contentious.

The tax cuts are the most obvious candidate for that treatment. Unions have raised concerns about them, according to the discovery summary for this story. The named unions and their precise objections are not confirmed. Objections of this kind usually turn on a few points: whether firms might replace older workers with subsidised younger ones, whether the jobs created are secure, and whether the money would be better spent on training. The government needs union goodwill for any reform that touches pay, conditions or benefits, so the concern carries weight beyond the policy itself.

The tension inside Labour

The other pressure point is internal. In a BBC analysis, Chris Mason argued that Milburn may have given Labour MPs a moral case for welfare reform. Many backbenchers are wary of changes that look like cuts. Reform presented as the fiscal case for trimming spending is a hard sell to them. Reform presented as help for a young person with no route into work is an easier one.

Mason's piece is analysis rather than the announcement of a decision, and it does not suggest any policy has been settled. Its value is in describing the argument the party is likely to have. Milburn's report is a Labour-aligned review, which makes it harder for sceptics on the left to dismiss. It also puts them in the position of having to say why they would oppose it.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the 'move quickly' pledge has substance. The first is the government's formal response, and which recommendations it accepts, defers or quietly leaves out. The second is the costing of the hiring tax relief, which has to be published before the proposal can be judged. The third is the reaction of the unions once there is legislative text to oppose or support. The package pairs an incentive for employers with changes to the rules for claimants. A government that wants both will have to hold together Treasury caution, union concern and a restless parliamentary party, and the 'not in full' hedge suggests it is already planning for some of those to pull apart.
