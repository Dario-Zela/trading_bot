Siemens sets dividend and 2027 guidance as US chiefs ask for policy stability

In one sentence
Siemens has set out its dividend and 2027 guidance while its US chief and Mercedes' leadership ask for policy stability; the '12% undervalued' tag is a third-party model estimate.

Siemens has set out its dividend and its guidance for 2027. In the same news cycle, its US chief and Mercedes-Benz leadership have said they want policy stability for their American workforces, which together exceed 60,000 people. The two items are separate in origin but share a theme: a large industrial can plan its payouts and its earnings range, but it cannot plan the trade and regulatory environment in which it earns them.

What Siemens has said

The dividend and outlook announcement is the routine part of the story. Large German industrials publish a proposed payout and a forward earnings range with their annual results, and the market reads the two together: the dividend signals how confident management is in cash generation, and the guidance sets the bar for the following year.

The summary material available to this desk gives no figures for either. The payout per share and the 2027 ranges are therefore not quoted here. They will be in the company's results release and accompanying presentation, and should be read there rather than inferred. What can be said is structural: a company that commits to a dividend and a multi-year outlook is telling investors it sees its order book and margins as predictable enough to underwrite both.

The valuation claim

One piece of commentary has attached itself to the announcement: Simply Wall St. estimates Siemens shares are roughly 12% undervalued. That figure comes from a discounted-cash-flow style model run by a retail-investor analytics site. It is not a company disclosure, and it is not a finding.

Such estimates are only as good as their inputs. They rest on assumed growth rates, margins and a discount rate, and a change of a point or two in any of them can move the answer by more than 12%. A model output of this kind is a statement about the model's assumptions. It says little about where the shares will trade.

The US payroll question

The more substantive thread is the one reported by Fortune: Siemens' US head and Mercedes-Benz leadership say they want policy stability for the more than 60,000 people they employ in the United States. The exact wording and the speakers' names could not be independently confirmed, so the sentiment is reported here, not a direct quote.

The ask itself is unsurprising. Both groups run American factories, and both depend on cross-border supply chains in which a tariff change or a shift in regulation can alter costs between one quarter and the next. Neither is asking for a particular policy. They are asking for a predictable one, which is a request about planning horizons more than about politics.

That is the gap worth noting. A dividend and a 2027 outlook describe a company's own plans. They do not control the tariff schedules, subsidy rules or permitting regimes that those plans assume. Guidance of this kind usually carries conditions about the trading environment, and the more policy shifts, the more those conditions matter.

What to watch

The first check is the results release itself, for the dividend per share and the 2027 ranges. The second is the wording around trade and tariffs in the outlook: whether Siemens treats US policy as a stated risk to its range or as an assumption already built in. Any further public remarks from the US executives on the specific measures they have in mind would show how much of the stability request concerns trade and how much concerns regulation.
