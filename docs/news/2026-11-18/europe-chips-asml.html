Europe sets out plans for the next chip wave; Bernstein stays Outperform on ASML

In one sentence
Europe's latest chip-ambition plans have thin funding behind them so far, and Bernstein's unchanged Outperform on ASML rests on AI demand, not on Brussels.

Europe wants a place in the next generation of chips, and the policy talk is again running ahead of the money. The latest plans, reported by the BBC, matter for investors only if they can narrow the gap with the firms that already control the bottlenecks. In Europe that mainly means ASML, which makes the lithography machines that print advanced chips, and Siemens, whose industrial and design-software businesses sit deep in chip supply chains. The BBC report as summarised to us carries no funding figures, dates or named policy documents, so this piece tests the announcement against what Brussels has actually committed.

What is actually funded

The most concrete recent item is small. On 5 November the European Commission announced €525 million for the European Chip Design Platform, aimed at early-stage chip design start-ups. It offers design tools, access to intellectual property and manufacturing, and grants of up to €16 million. The reasoning is sound: European firms earn less than 1% of global chip-design revenue, and new factories need local designers to create demand for them. Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, said Europe "is ready to act on its technological sovereignty goals."

The scale is the problem. €525 million is real money for start-ups, but it is a rounding error next to the capital cost of a leading-edge fab. It also sits under the existing EU Chips Act, which in 2022 set a goal of lifting Europe's share of global chip production from roughly 9–10% to 20% by 2030, backed by about €43 billion of mostly public and private investment pledged. A 2025 European Court of Auditors report concluded that target is very unlikely to be met. Separate reporting from May this year describes a draft successor Act and a €700 million grant for Imec's 2-nanometre research; we could not confirm the status of either from the sources reviewed.

Where ASML and Siemens fit

Europe's difficulty is that it already owns one of the most valuable chokepoints in the industry and still lacks the rest. The most advanced chips are made almost entirely in Taiwan and South Korea, with TSMC producing about 70% of chips made for other firms. ASML sells the machines to all of them. European policy can help ASML only indirectly: more local fabs and research centres mean more local customers, but ASML's order book is driven by global AI-related spending, not Brussels. ASML's chief executive has himself warned that European chip output might largely be exported, which raises questions about the demand the plans assume.

Siemens' exposure is different and more incremental, tied to industrial automation and chip-design tooling. Neither company's outlook turns on a design-start-up scheme. Siemens' own latest guidance is covered separately in our Siemens report.

The Bernstein note

The second thread is a routine one. Bernstein reiterated its Outperform rating on ASML, according to fiscal.ai. We could not confirm a price target, new estimates or a stated reason, and found no November note beyond that listing. The context is clear enough: Bernstein upgraded ASML to Outperform in January, naming it its top European semiconductor pick for 2026, and in July raised its US-listed price target to $2,623 from $1,971, citing AI-driven lithography demand. A reiteration adds little to that. It shows that the bullish case on ASML rests on AI demand, not on European industrial policy.

What to watch

The test of European intent is specific: whether a successor Chips Act arrives with a funded budget and named projects, and whether any leading-edge fab commitment follows. Until then, the plans are a directional signal. They are not a catalyst for ASML or Siemens shares.
