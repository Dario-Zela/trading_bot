Russia and Ukraine trade heavy strikes as Kherson drone attacks target civilians

Destruction at a Kyiv shopping centre following an earlier Russian missile strike on the capital in 2026 — spoilt.exile / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Russia fired 24 unintercepted ballistic missiles at Kyiv while a Kherson drone hunted a civilian vendor — both exposing the same air-defense supply gap.

Kyiv woke before dawn on Wednesday to the sound of ballistic missiles it could not stop. Russia fired 24 of them, along with four hypersonic Kinzhals and 115 Shahed-type attack drones, at the Ukrainian capital and its surrounding oblast overnight. None of the missiles were intercepted. By the time the fires were out, at least 15 people were dead — 14 of them in Kyiv Oblast, one in the city itself — and 51 injured, four critically. A 20-storey residential block in the Obolonskyi district burned through the night; warehouses collapsed in Desnianskyi with people still inside, two of whom rescue crews pulled from the rubble. "Kyiv and the region are under ballistic attack. More missiles at the city," Mayor Vitali Klitschko warned as the barrage was still under way.

It was the second mass strike on the capital in five days — an August 1 attack had already killed nine — and part of a wider weekend of reciprocal, long-range violence that has left dozens dead on both sides of the front line, from a Ukrainian drone strike on a Moscow-region warehouse to Russian strikes on Sumy and Mykolaiv. Two children were among the dead in Sumy. In Kherson, meanwhile, a different kind of attack was making headlines: video of a Russian first-person-view drone chasing an unarmed vegetable vendor through a market stall until it caught him. Different in form, the same weekend's violence points at a single, harder question — whether either side's capacity to strike is now outpacing the other's ability to defend, and what that imbalance means for how much longer, and how brutally, this war can be fought at range.

A capital with no shield left

The scale of Wednesday's barrage was not an aberration. Ukrainian military intelligence estimates Russia is now producing 60 to 70 Iskander-M ballistic missiles and 10 to 15 Kinzhal hypersonics every month — up from roughly 40 Iskanders and four or five Kinzhals as recently as spring last year, a rise of more than 66 percent in a year. Stockpiles have grown to nearly 600 Iskander-Ms and over 100 Kinzhals, alongside a further 60 or so North Korean KN-23 missiles Pyongyang has supplied. Moscow has also reportedly upgraded the newest Iskanders with countermeasures designed specifically to defeat Patriot radar locks.

That production curve has run ahead of the West's ability to arm Ukraine's air defenses. The Patriot's PAC-3 MSE interceptor — the only munition reliably capable of downing a ballistic missile in flight — is built by Lockheed Martin at a rate the company hopes to lift to 650 a year by 2027. Russia's monthly ballistic output alone already exceeds that annual figure on an annualized basis. Ukraine's own interceptor stocks, strained further by the diversion of Patriot batteries and munitions toward the Middle East during this year's Iran conflict, have been reported by Ukrainian officials as critically low. Wednesday's result was mechanical rather than surprising: zero of 24 ballistic missiles intercepted. Zelensky has separately accused Moscow of expanding missile production using components from companies the West has yet to sanction, arguing the supply-chain gap is a policy failure as much as an industrial one.

The Kherson 'safari'

While Kyiv absorbed missiles from beyond the horizon, Kherson faced a more intimate form of violence. On Tuesday, police released footage of an FPV drone pursuing a 52-year-old market trader named Yuriy around his vegetable truck before detonating beside him. He survived with shrapnel wounds, blast trauma and a concussion. "A Russian drone deliberately hunted down a man selling vegetables and detonated right next to him," Zelensky said, adding that Russian forces "showed zero regret" and had even acknowledged the strike. "Every single day, ordinary people in Kherson face these Russian 'safaris'," he said. "The world must see this." Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha called it a "barbaric war crime."

It was not an isolated case. Ukrainian police say Kherson, sitting across the Dnipro River from Russian-held territory, is targeted by such drone runs almost daily; on a single day this week, 18 civilians were wounded and two killed in the region. The city has strung anti-drone netting over its main approach roads in response. The United Nations' Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine concluded as far back as September last year that this pattern — cheap, first-person-view drones deliberately tracking unarmed civilians — meets the legal threshold for a war crime. Kherson's exposure comes down to geography and munitions economics: FPV drones cost a few hundred dollars each and require no air-defense-grade interceptor to counter, only physical barriers, making the city both cheap to menace and hard to protect.

What the weekend adds up to

Taken separately, the two threads look like different wars: one industrial, fought with missiles whose production lines and interceptor economics can be tracked in monthly figures; the other intimate, fought with a $500 drone and a market stall. Taken together, they describe the same strategic fact — that Russia currently has more ways to hurt Ukrainian civilians than Ukraine and its allies have ways to stop it, whether the threat arrives from 300 kilometres up or from a rooftop two streets away. Ukraine's own long-range strikes on Russian warehouses and the Syzran oil refinery this weekend show Kyiv is not without reach of its own, but the casualty arithmetic — 15 dead in Kyiv overnight versus five in the Moscow region strike blamed on Ukraine — still favors the side with the deeper missile stockpile.

For now, both governments are pressing rather than pausing. Zelensky has said he wants to increase pressure on Moscow before winter, while voicing skepticism that pressure alone changes Putin's calculus. The immediate catalysts to watch are the pace of Lockheed's PAC-3 MSE deliveries against Russia's monthly missile output, and whether Western capitals move to sanction the component suppliers Zelensky has named — the two levers most likely to narrow the gap that let 24 ballistic missiles reach Kyiv unopposed this week.
