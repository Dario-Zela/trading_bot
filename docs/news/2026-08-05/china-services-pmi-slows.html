China services growth slows to near two-year low

A street market near Yuyuan Gardens in Shanghai — the consumer-facing services sector is where China's demand slowdown is now showing up most clearly. — Daniel Case / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's services PMI slumped to 50.4 in July, a near two-year low, as domestic demand cooled even as exports held firm.

China's services sector, long the steadiest part of an otherwise wobbly economy, lost its footing in July. The RatingDog China General Services Business Activity Index — the survey still widely known by its old name, the Caixin services PMI — fell to 50.4 from 54.1 in June, the weakest reading since September 2024. Economists polled ahead of the release had expected something closer to 53.7.

A reading above 50 still means the sector is growing, but the size of the drop is what matters here: more than three-and-a-half points in a single month, the sharpest deceleration the survey has flagged in this cycle. Services have historically been China's cushion — the place consumers kept spending even as property and manufacturing sagged. That cushion just got a lot thinner.

A cushion wearing thin

The composite output index, which blends services with manufacturing, slid to 50.8 from 53.6, its slowest pace of private-sector growth in a year. That composite weakness lines up with what the official data showed a few days earlier: the National Bureau of Statistics' manufacturing PMI fell to 49.2 in July, dropping into outright contraction and ending a four-month run of expansion, even as niche pockets like equipment manufacturing (51.4) and high-tech manufacturing (53.3) held up better.

Put the two surveys together and the picture is of an economy where factories and shops are cooling in the same month, rather than one offsetting the other. New business at services firms kept expanding in July, per the survey, but at the weakest pace since March. Business confidence — how firms feel about the year ahead, not just the month just gone — dropped to its lowest level since February 2020, the depths of the pandemic shock.

Not every sub-index moved the wrong way. New export orders held at 52.0, a second straight month in expansion, suggesting services firms tied to trade and tourism are faring better than those selling purely to domestic households. Employment rose for a third consecutive month, the longest hiring streak since the second half of 2024, and input-cost inflation eased to its softest since January. Yao Yu, founder of RatingDog, framed the report as one of a slowdown alongside relative bright spots — resilient exports, sustained hiring, cooling costs — rather than an unambiguous stall.

Reading the demand gap

The gap between exports and everything else is the tell. If it were a broad-based slump, new export orders would be falling too. Instead, it looks like a domestic-demand problem specifically: households and small service businesses pulling back on spending and credit even as external orders hold up. That fits a pattern flagged by economists through 2026 — household borrowing growth, which includes lending to small service-sector firms, had already slowed to record-low levels earlier in the year, evidence that the effects of prior stimulus rounds were fading before this survey landed.

For markets, the immediate read is on the renminbi and on rate expectations. A domestic-demand miss this sharp, layered onto a manufacturing PMI already in contraction, raises the odds that the People's Bank of China leans back toward easing after a year spent mostly on the sidelines, and adds to the case for further yuan softness against a dollar that has had its own turbulent year. It also feeds commodity-demand assumptions tied to Chinese consumption, from copper to crude, at a moment when oil prices are already sliding on unrelated Middle East diplomacy.

What Beijing does next

The timing puts pressure on policymakers just as they were signalling caution rather than urgency. The Politburo, meeting on July 30 — the day before the manufacturing PMI dropped and five days before this services reading — pledged "targeted" stimulus and stepped-up counter-cyclical support for the second half of the year, but stopped short of a large-scale package. Its language leaned on expanding "services consumption" and building households' underlying capacity to spend, rather than another round of one-off subsidies.

That framing will be tested by August data. If the services PMI keeps sliding toward the 50 line next month, the debate in Beijing is likely to shift from targeted nudges to something closer to the demand-side stimulus investors have been asking for since the property downturn began. The next RatingDog services release, due in early September, will show whether July was a one-month air pocket or the start of a trend — and the Fifth Plenum, set for October, is where any bigger policy shift would most plausibly be unveiled.
