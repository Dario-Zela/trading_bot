Apple launches fresh legal challenge against UK encrypted-data order

Apple Park, the company's Cupertino headquarters, where the encryption standoff with UK regulators is being fought from. — Daniel L. Lu / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Apple filed a new IPT complaint against a narrowed UK order demanding access to British users' encrypted iCloud data, months after a wider version was dropped under US pressure.

Apple has gone back to court in London, filing a fresh legal complaint with the UK's Investigatory Powers Tribunal over a government order demanding access to the encrypted iCloud data of British users. It is the second time in little over a year that Apple has fought the same fight — and this time, unlike the last, the company intends to see it through a public hearing rather than simply withdrawing a product and hoping the demand goes away.

The complaint, filed in July and confirmed by the tribunal in early August, challenges a Technical Capability Notice issued under Britain's Investigatory Powers Act. These notices are classified by default — companies that receive one are legally barred from confirming its existence, let alone its contents. That secrecy is itself part of what Apple is contesting.

A demand that keeps coming back

The dispute has a history. In early 2025, the Home Office served Apple with a notice demanding a backdoor into Advanced Data Protection, the opt-in encryption tier that leaves Apple itself without the keys to a user's iCloud backups, photos and messages. That original order sought access not just to UK customers' data but to anyone's, anywhere — including Americans'.

Apple's response was blunt: rather than build the access, it pulled Advanced Data Protection from the UK market entirely in February 2025, leaving British users without the option to fully encrypt their backups while the rest of the world kept it. The company has long maintained a standing line on this, repeated again during the row: "We have never built a backdoor or master key to any of our products or services and we never will."

The worldwide version of the demand did not survive contact with Washington. Diplomatic pressure from the US — reportedly including direct objections from the Trump administration, which framed a UK backdoor as a threat to Americans' data — led the Home Office to abandon it by August 2025. It was widely reported as a rare climbdown by UK security officials.

It did not last. By October 2025, the government had returned with a narrower notice, this time limited to the iCloud data of UK-based users only. That is the order Apple is now formally contesting.

Inside the tribunal

The Investigatory Powers Tribunal is an unusual venue — it exists specifically to hear complaints about surveillance and intelligence-gathering powers, and much of its work happens away from public view for the same reason the notices themselves are secret. But the tribunal has confirmed it will hear Apple's case in public sessions, using what lawyers call "assumed facts" — arguing the legal principles at stake without either side confirming or denying classified specifics.

Apple isn't alone in this fight. The privacy groups Liberty and Privacy International, along with two individuals, brought their own complaint against the same style of notice well before Apple's new filing, and that case was already scheduled for a December hearing. The tribunal has now notified Privacy International of Apple's parallel challenge, and the cases are expected to be heard alongside one another.

The Home Office's public justification for technical capability notices has stayed consistent throughout: it says the powers are necessary for investigating terrorism and child sexual abuse, where encrypted platforms can leave law enforcement locked out of evidence even with a warrant. Privacy campaigners counter that a capability built to reach one government's targets is, by construction, a capability that can reach anyone's — and that weakening encryption for one country's users weakens the underlying protocol for all of them, since iCloud's encryption architecture isn't sliced up by geography.

Why the outcome travels

Britain is not the only government leaning on encrypted platforms this year. The EU's ongoing "chat control" proposals, Australia's assistance and access laws, and India's traceability rules for messaging apps all sit on the same fault line: how far a state can compel a private company to weaken protections it built for everyone. A UK tribunal ruling that upholds — or strikes down — this kind of notice will be read closely in each of those capitals, less for its legal force outside Britain than for the precedent it sets on whether court challenges can actually check this kind of order at all.

For Apple, the commercial stakes are real but secondary to the principle it has staked its brand on. Advanced Data Protection remains unavailable to new UK sign-ups regardless of how the tribunal rules on this specific notice; restoring it would require the Home Office to drop the order outright, not just lose in court. A tribunal loss for the government would be a rare public rebuke of a secret surveillance power; a loss for Apple would hand regulators elsewhere a template for narrower, country-specific backdoor demands that are harder for tech companies to resist by simply exiting a market.

The December hearing, combining the Liberty/Privacy International complaint with Apple's, is the next fixed point. Given the tribunal's history with contested notices, a ruling is unlikely before well into 2027 — but the hearing itself, conducted partly in public, should surface more about how these notices are supposed to work than either side has been permitted to say so far.
