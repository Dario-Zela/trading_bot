Palantir shares soar 27% on blowout earnings, guidance raised again

Palantir CEO Alex Karp, whose commentary on the earnings call called the quarter "otherworldly." — Ben Dance / No 10 Downing Street, UK Government (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Palantir's Q2 revenue rose 93% and U.S. commercial sales surged 149%, prompting a second guidance raise this year and a 27% stock jump.

Palantir Technologies has spent six years telling investors that its government-contractor roots would eventually give way to a broader commercial software business. On Monday, the numbers finally caught up to the pitch.

Shares in the Denver-founded, now Aventura, Florida-headquartered company jumped 27% after it reported second-quarter revenue of $1.94 billion, up 93% from a year earlier and well ahead of the $1.8 billion Wall Street had penciled in. The standout line wasn't the topline figure — it was U.S. commercial revenue, which surged 149% year-on-year to $764 million. Palantir also raised full-year guidance for the second time this year, now projecting $8.15 billion in 2026 revenue, an 80% growth rate, up from a forecast of roughly $7.65 billion just three months ago.

"This quarter was otherworldly," chief executive Alex Karp said on the earnings call, a characteristically unrestrained turn of phrase from an executive not known for dry investor-relations language. Adjusted earnings came in at $0.41 a share against a $0.34 estimate, and the company posted a 63% adjusted free-cash-flow margin — a level that would be unusual for any software company, let alone one still expanding revenue at triple-digit rates in its largest new segment.

In one sentence: Palantir posted 93% revenue growth and 149% growth in U.S. commercial sales, then raised its full-year guidance again — sending shares up 27%.

The pivot investors waited years for

Palantir's founding business was never in doubt: government and defense contracts, built on data-integration software for intelligence agencies and the military, generated the bulk of revenue for most of the company's history. What was in doubt, for years, was whether the commercial side — pitched hard since the company's 2020 direct listing — would ever become more than a rounding error.

That argument is getting harder to make. U.S. commercial revenue has now compounded roughly 380% since 2024, according to the company's own reckoning, and management raised its full-year U.S. commercial guidance to above $3.42 billion, implying growth of at least 134% for the year. Total U.S. revenue — government and commercial combined — rose 115% year-on-year to $1.57 billion, up 23% from the prior quarter alone. U.S. government revenue itself grew 90%, so this isn't a story of the public-sector business slowing to let commercial catch up; both lines are accelerating at once, with commercial simply running faster.

The company's own scorecard for this — a blend of revenue growth and profit margin known as the "Rule of 40," where anything above 40% is considered strong for a software firm — hit 155% in the quarter. Combined with adjusted operating income guidance raised to $4.89–4.90 billion and free cash flow guidance of $4.5–4.7 billion for the year, the picture is of a company generating cash at a rate that has, until now, been rare among firms still growing this quickly.

Why the market moved so hard

A 27% single-day move in a company already carrying a market capitalization north of $300 billion is a large repricing by any standard, and it reflects more than a single beat-and-raise quarter. Government contractors trade at a structural discount to commercial software peers, largely because contract renewals, budget cycles and political risk make revenue less predictable than subscription-style enterprise sales. Democratic lawmakers have separately raised questions this year about the scope of Palantir's federal data contracts, adding a layer of political scrutiny that a diversified revenue base helps to insulate against.

The bet embedded in Tuesday's rally is that Palantir is converting from a contractor with a narrow customer base into a platform company with a broadening one — and that the market should therefore apply something closer to a software multiple than a defense-contractor multiple. Karp has framed the commercial surge as driven by "AI sovereignty" demand: enterprises wanting to deploy artificial intelligence models on their own data without ceding that data to train someone else's system, a pitch that dovetails with Palantir's original data-security-first architecture built for intelligence agencies.

Government and commercial revenue have sat at roughly a 55:45 split over the past four quarters. If commercial keeps growing at anything close to its current pace while government holds its own 90% growth rate, that ratio will keep shifting — reducing Palantir's reliance on any single agency or contract vehicle, even as the $10 billion enterprise framework the U.S. Army signed with the company last year shows the government relationship is hardly retreating.

What's next

The test now is durability rather than magnitude. A single quarter of 149% commercial growth is easier to produce off a smaller base than to sustain as that base compounds — Palantir's own guidance implies deceleration from 149% to "at least" 134% growth in commercial revenue over the balance of the year, which is still extraordinary but is a deceleration nonetheless. The next data point is the third-quarter print, expected in early November, where analysts will be watching whether U.S. commercial bookings — which rose 153% year-on-year to $2.13 billion in total contract value this quarter — convert into recognized revenue at a similar clip, and whether the 63% free-cash-flow margin holds as the commercial sales motion scales into smaller, less bespoke enterprise deals.
