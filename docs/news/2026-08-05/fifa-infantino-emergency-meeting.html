Infantino calls emergency FIFA meeting as Wales withdraws support

FIFA president Gianni Infantino, whose plan to sell a stake in a new commercial entity collapsed under confederation opposition. — Ricardo Stuckert / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
FIFA's collapsed $4.2bn stake sale has triggered open revolt among member federations, with Wales, Serbia and Sweden pulling support and Infantino's re-election suddenly contested.

Gianni Infantino spent the past decade consolidating more personal authority over FIFA than any president since João Havelange. This week he found out how quickly that can drain away. On Wednesday the FIFA president convened an emergency meeting of the organisation's leadership in Rabat, Morocco, days after abandoning a plan to sell roughly a fifth of a new commercial entity to outside investors — and hours after the Football Association of Wales became the first national federation to publicly withdraw its backing for his re-election.

"In one sentence: FIFA's collapsed $4.2bn stake sale has triggered open revolt among member federations and put Gianni Infantino's grip on the presidency in genuine doubt."

A $20bn idea nobody else wanted

The proposal, known internally as FIFA Forward Enterprise, would have hived off the commercial and operational machinery behind FIFA's tournaments — broadcast rights, sponsorship, ticketing, licensing, everything that turns the World Cup into money — into a new subsidiary valued at close to $20bn. FIFA would then have sold about 20% of it to outside investors, led by Thrive Capital, the fund run by Joshua Kushner, aiming to raise up to $4.2bn in cash.

On paper it solved a real problem. FIFA's income is lumpy: it gorges every four years on World Cup revenue and goes comparatively hungry in between, and Infantino has repeatedly talked about smoothing that cycle while funding an expanding calendar of competitions, including the enlarged 32-team Club World Cup. Selling a slice of the future income stream for cash today is a conventional piece of financial engineering — private equity does it constantly.

What blindsided member federations was the process, not just the idea. UEFA called its own emergency meeting on 30 July and its 55 members voted unanimously, 55–0, to boycott every FIFA competition if the sale went ahead. That is not a threat a governing body issues lightly — a boycott vote against the body that runs the World Cup is closer to a declaration of civil war than a policy disagreement. CONCACAF's leadership registered similar alarm. Inside FIFA's own Zurich headquarters, chief operating officer Kevin Lamour reportedly told staff they had been "deceived," calling the plan "a project of one person." Carlos Cordeiro, a senior adviser to Infantino, resigned in protest. By Friday, with Thrive Capital said to be pulling back from negotiations, Infantino issued a statement conceding the "proposal will not proceed," acknowledging it had created divisions "no longer in the interest of the objective set out in the first place."

Wales draws first blood

A scrapped deal is one thing; a public no-confidence vote is another. On Sunday, the Football Association of Wales told Sky News it "hereby confirms its withdrawal of support for the candidature of Mr Gianni Infantino, for re-election as FIFA President for the 2027–2031 term." The FAW's statement did not stop at the stake sale — it cited "recent failures in good governance, processes, leadership, values, stakeholder management, communications and sound judgement," adding: "Failing to put the best interests of football first is a failure we cannot accept."

Wales is a federation with limited voting weight on its own — one member among 211 in FIFA's congress — but its statement functioned as a permission slip. England's FA signalled it was preparing to follow, and Serbia and Sweden announced their own withdrawals within days. None of that unravels Infantino's coalition by itself: he has long drawn his deepest support from Africa and parts of Asia and the Americas, confederations with far more votes between them than Wales, England, Serbia and Sweden combined. But defections that start in Europe rarely stay contained there, and the FAW's language — governance, judgement, deception — is now on the record for every federation weighing whether to say the same thing.

The succession math

The timing matters because FIFA's presidential election is not abstract — it falls on 17–18 March 2027, in Morocco, the same country hosting this week's emergency meeting. Infantino, seeking what would be his fourth term and, under FIFA's own statutes, his final permitted one, had appeared unassailable as recently as this spring. That calculus has shifted. Victor Montagliani, the CONCACAF president, is reportedly weighing a challenge with informal encouragement from UEFA, which has never fully trusted Infantino since he left its ranks to run FIFA in 2016. A contested election, rather than the customary coronation, is now a live scenario for the first time in Infantino's presidency.

What makes this more than a personality dispute is the money underneath it. FIFA sits on broadcast and sponsorship contracts worth billions tied to the 2026 World Cup — co-hosted by the US, Canada and Mexico — and the commercial architecture for tournaments beyond it. A stake sale was Infantino's attempt to diversify that revenue base beyond the quadrennial World Cup cycle; its collapse leaves FIFA reliant on the same lumpy financing model it has used for decades, just as the organisation's calendar of competitions, and its costs, keep expanding. Investors watching sports-rights markets — and regulators who scrutinise sports betting and media-rights concentration — will read the episode as evidence that FIFA's governance, not just its finances, needs fixing before anyone hands it billions again.

The emergency meeting in Rabat was Infantino's attempt to control the narrative before it spread further. What comes next is a count: how many more federations put their names on statements like Wales's before March 2027, and whether Montagliani or another challenger turns informal grumbling into an actual ballot line.
