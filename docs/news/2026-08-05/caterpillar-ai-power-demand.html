Caterpillar lifts 2026 outlook on AI data-centre power demand

Cooling towers and backup generators atop a data centre — the kind of on-site power infrastructure driving Caterpillar's Power & Energy segment. — Rsparks3 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Caterpillar's Q2 profit beat and raised 2026 outlook show AI data-centre power demand is now a measurable earnings driver for heavy industry, not just chipmakers.

Caterpillar Inc. posted the best quarter in its 101-year history on Tuesday, and the reason was not bulldozers or mining trucks — it was the humming rows of engines and turbines that keep AI data centres running when the grid can't.

The Irving, Texas-based manufacturer reported second-quarter revenue of $20.5 billion, up 24% from a year earlier and the first time the company has cleared $20 billion in a single quarter. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $8.17, blowing past Wall Street's $6.19 estimate by nearly $2 a share and up 73% from the $4.72 posted a year ago. The company raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth forecast to a mid-to-high-teens percentage range. Shares jumped as much as 11% intraday, adding tens of billions of dollars to Caterpillar's market value in a single session.

Where the growth is actually coming from

Caterpillar has three main divisions — construction, resource industries and power & energy — and the story of the quarter sits almost entirely in the last one. Power & Energy segment revenue rose 17% to $8.2 billion, but the number underneath that headline is the one that matters for the AI-capex narrative: power generation product sales, the large reciprocating engines and Solar Turbines gas turbines that utilities and data-centre developers buy to backstop grid power, climbed 72% year over year. Segment margins expanded 250 basis points to 24.6%.

The order backlog — a proxy for demand not yet booked as revenue — hit a record $72.1 billion, up roughly 92% from a year earlier and $9 billion higher than the prior quarter alone. Fifty-nine percent of that backlog is scheduled to convert to revenue within twelve months, meaning the current growth rate has real visibility behind it rather than resting on hope. Caterpillar booked $9.4 billion in new orders in the quarter.

"There is a lot of discussion around AI demand," chief executive Joe Creed told analysts on the earnings call. "We have constant discussions with our customers, and all I can tell you is what our discussions with them is — no one is slowing down at the moment. If we can get more units out, they're asking us to give them more units." Creed said customers are now placing orders that stretch into 2028, and the company plans to nearly triple its large reciprocating engine manufacturing capacity from 2024 levels by the end of the decade to keep pace — investment concentrated in 2027 through 2029, alongside continued growth for Solar Turbines' gas-turbine line.

Beyond the chipmakers

Until now, the clearest earnings evidence of the AI infrastructure boom has come from chipmakers and hyperscalers — Nvidia's data-centre revenue, AMD's record quarters, cloud providers' capital-spending guidance. Caterpillar's results extend that evidence into a different part of the supply chain entirely: the physical plant needed to power the servers once they're built.

That matters because data centres cannot simply plug into the grid fast enough to match the pace of AI buildout. Utility interconnection queues in many U.S. markets now run years long, and developers increasingly turn to on-site generation — gas turbines, reciprocating engines, sometimes temporary power — to bridge the gap or provide backup and peaking capacity. Caterpillar's Solar Turbines unit, and its large-engine business more broadly, sit squarely in that gap. A joint venture with Cat dealers called Major Projects delivered its first units this quarter to support multibillion-dollar North American developments, including data-centre construction, according to the company.

The read-through is that AI capital expenditure is no longer a story confined to semiconductor demand curves. It is showing up as measurable, recurring revenue for an industrial company whose core business — excavators, mining trucks, engines — has existed for a century. For traders parsing which sectors have genuine, earnings-backed exposure to the AI buildout versus which are riding sentiment, Caterpillar's quarter is a data point that argues for a wider net.

The caveats

The rally is not without qualifiers. Caterpillar said tariffs and higher raw-material costs will add roughly $2.4 billion to expenses in 2026, with steel a particular pressure point; the company is not yet certain the disputed cost fully offset by pricing. And the stock's re-rating has left it trading at a premium — around 26 times forward EBITDA and roughly 35 times forward earnings by some analyst estimates — that assumes Power & Energy keeps growing at an above-cycle pace rather than reverting once the current wave of data-centre construction matures. Several banks raised price targets after the print — Oppenheimer, UBS and Evercore ISI among them — but the valuation now leaves little room for a demand pause.

The next checkpoint is Caterpillar's third-quarter report, expected in late October, when investors will look for whether the 92% backlog growth and 72% power-generation sales gain were a peak or simply the new run rate. Longer term, the capacity Creed described — nearly tripling large-engine output by decade's end — is itself a wager that AI-driven power demand keeps compounding through 2029, a bet now shared by a company whose fortunes were, until recently, tied mostly to commodities and construction cycles.
