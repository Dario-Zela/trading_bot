STANDING WATCH · HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-08-05 · 3 min read

Iran-Hormuz deal hopes keep oil sliding, stocks near records

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and LNG normally passes. — Richard Weil / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Bessent's hint of an imminent US-Iran Hormuz deal sent Brent down 5.3% and drove the Dow past 54,000 and the S&P to a record, even as Trump attacks oil majors' war profits.

Brent crude dropped more than 5% on Tuesday, the Dow closed above 54,000 for the first time and the S&P 500 hit a fresh record — all inside the same trading session, all traceable to eleven words from Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent.

"There is a chance we may have a deal today or tomorrow to open the Strait," Bessent told CNBC's Squawk Box, describing talks with Iran over the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth to a quarter of the world's seaborne oil and LNG normally passes. Markets did not wait for confirmation. Brent settled down 5.3% at $79.36 a barrel, a three-week low; West Texas Intermediate fell 5.7% to $75.77.

The blockade premium unwinds

The Strait has been the fulcrum of the oil market since the US and Israel struck Iran on February 28, and Tehran responded by choking traffic through the waterway — the largest disruption to global oil flows on record. Gasoline has borne the cost directly: AAA data put US pump prices roughly 36% higher than before the war began, with other trackers citing figures from 30% to 50% depending on the state and week measured.

Tuesday's move was pure repricing of that blockade premium, not evidence the oil is actually flowing yet. Qatar's foreign ministry said draft terms were "in very progressive stages"; Secretary of State Marco Rubio was more guarded, citing "progress made ... but not finality." The market has been burned on this before — a US-Iran agreement to reopen the Strait struck on June 17 collapsed within days. Traders are pricing a real chance of a deal, not a certainty.

Records, earnings, and a political wildcard

The oil move fed a broader risk-on rotation. The Dow gained more than 900 points, or 1.71%, to close above 54,000; the S&P 500 rose 1.79% to 7,737, its first record in two months; the Nasdaq jumped 2.59%. Earnings did the rest of the work: Palantir's stock surged nearly 30%, its largest single-day gain since 2024, after second-quarter revenue rose 93% year-over-year to $1.94 billion and management lifted full-year guidance to $8.15–8.16 billion from $7.65–7.66 billion. Caterpillar rose more than 10% after adjusted earnings of $8.17 a share beat consensus by nearly $2, revenue climbed 24% to $20.5 billion on data-centre power demand, and the company raised its 2026 sales-growth outlook to mid-to-high teens.

One day earlier, President Trump had aimed the opposite message at the sector still pricing in war-level premiums. He said Exxon Mobil and Chevron had made "too much money" from the conflict and that he "didn't like it," demanding the companies "give some of that back to the public" through lower retail prices. The numbers back his complaint even if the remedy is undefined: Chevron's second-quarter profit rose to $12 billion, up roughly 400% year-over-year, while Exxon's $14.5 billion more than doubled. Neither company has responded with a pricing commitment, and a sitting president publicly pressuring two of the market's largest energy names adds a layer of policy risk that a Hormuz deal alone would not remove.

The throughline across all three threads is the same repricing exercise: a war premium that inflated oil, gasoline and energy-company profits together is now being unwound in the other direction, and every asset that premium touched — crude futures, pump prices, energy equities, and the inflation and rate-cut math central banks run off them — is moving with it. A confirmed Hormuz agreement, rather than a Bessent hint of one, is the next data point that would make Tuesday's move durable rather than another head-fake like June's. Traders will also be watching whether Washington's rhetoric toward Exxon and Chevron hardens into policy, which could complicate the read for energy stocks even if crude keeps falling.
