Fed's Warsh holds rates, faces three dissents for a hike

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as the Fed's 17th chairman in May 2026, faced his first major policy split at the July FOMC meeting. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (official portrait)
In one sentence
New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh held rates at 3.50%-3.75% but faced three dissents for a hike — the first unified hawkish split since 2016 — and traders now price better-than-even odds of a September increase.

Kevin Warsh's first major test as Federal Reserve chairman ended in a split decision. The central bank held its benchmark interest rate at 3.50%-3.75% on July 29th, the sixth straight meeting without a change — but for the first time since September 2016, three policymakers dissented in the same direction, all pushing to raise rates instead. The vote was 9-3.

The dissenters were Dallas Fed president Lorie Logan, Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack and Minneapolis Fed president Neel Kashkari, each of whom wanted a quarter-point hike. Warsh, sworn in as the Fed's 17th chair on May 22nd after a bruising 54-45 Senate confirmation — the closest vote for the post in the modern era — did not try to paper over the split. "I asked for a good family fight, and I got one," he told reporters afterward. "That's the purpose, that's the design feature." He added that there was "large majority support for the decision that we made in the room," and that officials have "no tolerance for persistently elevated inflation."

The case for hiking

The dissenters' argument rests on a simple, uncomfortable fact: inflation has not returned to the Fed's 2% target in more than five years. "Inflation has remained stubbornly above 2% for more than five years, and I am not confident it will return to our objective on its own," Hammack said. Kashkari went further, arguing the Fed should have used Wednesday's meeting to begin a series of increases rather than wait. Logan had said earlier in the month that she believed rates should be "modestly" higher.

They were not alone in leaning hawkish. Fed governor Christopher Waller, who ultimately voted with the majority to hold, spent the weeks before the meeting warning that further hikes could be necessary if inflation didn't improve — though he also cautioned against "fighting the last war" by tightening prematurely. Core PCE, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, rose at its fastest pace in nearly three years in May, before a brief easing in June tied to a temporary Iran-U.S. de-escalation that pushed oil prices down. The committee's own statement called activity "expanding at a solid pace," with job growth still keeping up with the labor force — hardly a picture that argues for urgency in either direction.

A market caught leaning the wrong way

Going into the meeting, traders were unusually split: futures markets had priced in roughly a 35% chance of a hike, against 65% for a hold. The hold won, but Warsh's tone afterward did more to move markets than the vote itself. Fed funds futures now show better than 57% odds of a quarter-point increase at the September meeting, according to CME's FedWatch tool — up sharply from where odds sat before Warsh's press conference. Equities wobbled and then recovered on the day, with the S&P 500 and Nasdaq trimming early losses, while gold added 1.2%.

Part of the market's difficulty in reading Warsh is by design. He has been openly critical of the Fed's traditional forward guidance and its quarterly "dot plot" of rate projections, tools previous chairs used to telegraph intentions well ahead of decisions. A more opaque Fed under a chair willing to let dissents run in public is, almost by construction, a harder Fed to price.

What a divided committee means going forward

The practical stakes are straightforward. A 9-3 vote with all three dissents pointing the same way is a stronger signal of internal pressure than any single dissent has offered in nearly a decade — Bank of America's economists are now penciling in three quarter-point hikes before year-end, which would take the funds rate to a 4.25%-4.50% range. That is a materially different world for duration trades and rate-sensitive equities than the one priced as recently as late July.

Warsh inherited a committee already primed for disagreement — Waller, Hammack and Kashkari had all signaled hawkish leanings before the meeting even started — and his first major decision was to let that disagreement play out on the record rather than manage it away. The next data points worth watching are the August jobs report and the July core PCE reading due in the coming weeks, both of which will shape whether Wednesday's three dissenters pick up company — or whether Warsh's "large majority" holds through the Fed's September meeting.
