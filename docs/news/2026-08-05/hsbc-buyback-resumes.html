HSBC resumes $1bn buyback as profit rises 13%, exits Egypt retail

8 Canada Square, HSBC's global headquarters in London's Canary Wharf. — Tom Page / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
HSBC's Q2 profit beat forecasts by 13%, triggering a $1bn buyback after a three-quarter pause and confirming the bank's exit from Egyptian retail banking.

HSBC is buying back its own shares again, and the reason it stopped tells you almost as much as the reason it started.

The London-headquartered bank reported second-quarter profit before tax of $10.3 billion, up 13% year-on-year and comfortably ahead of analyst estimates, and used the moment to reinstate a $1 billion share buyback that had been on ice for three quarters. In the same results announcement, HSBC confirmed it has agreed to sell its retail banking business in Egypt to Emirates NBD — one more entry in a list of market exits that now runs to fifteen since the start of 2025.

Taken together, the two announcements describe a bank that has finished a specific piece of internal repair work and is now free to return capital to shareholders while continuing to narrow what it actually does, and where.

Why the buybacks stopped in the first place

The pause traces back to HSBC's move last October to take full ownership of Hang Seng Bank, the Hong Kong lender it already majority-controlled. The privatisation, valued at roughly $13.6 billion, bought out the 36.5% of Hang Seng shares HSBC did not already own and carried a same-day capital hit of about 125 basis points to HSBC's core capital ratio. At the time, management said explicitly it would suspend further buybacks for three quarters while it rebuilt that cushion through retained earnings.

That rebuild is now done. HSBC's core capital ratio stood at 14.1% at quarter-end, inside the bank's 14%–14.5% target range, having generated 190 basis points of capital in the first half alone. Return on tangible equity for the quarter came in at 19.1%, well above the bank's 17% target, and first-half revenue rose 6% to $38.2 billion. HSBC also raised its full-year guidance for banking net interest income to at least $46 billion and lifted its cost-savings target from $1.5 billion to $2 billion. Group chief executive Georges Elhedery framed the results as evidence the post-Hang Seng restructuring is on track: "HSBC is becoming the stronger bank we set out to build. We are executing our strategic priorities with pace, precision and discipline."

The $1 billion authorised is a fraction of the roughly $3 billion HSBC was returning to shareholders per quarter before the pause, and management has been careful about sequencing — the bank's stated capital hierarchy puts the dividend first, then organic growth and acquisitions, with buybacks last. A shareholder second interim dividend of $0.10 per share was also declared alongside the buyback.

The Egypt exit fits a pattern, not a one-off

Under the terms of the agreement, Emirates NBD's Egyptian subsidiary will take on HSBC Egypt's entire retail banking business — loans, deposits, accounts and the staff who run them — while HSBC keeps its corporate and institutional banking operations in the country to continue serving trade and investment clients. The deal is expected to close in the second half of 2027, pending regulatory approval, and will book HSBC a pre-tax gain of about $300 million. The capital impact on HSBC's group ratio is described as immaterial, which is itself a signal: this is not a distressed disposal, it is portfolio pruning.

Egypt is the latest in a run of similar moves. HSBC has this year completed the sale of its UK life insurance business, exited retail banking in South Africa and Sri Lanka, agreed to sell its Indonesian retail arm and Singapore life and health insurance unit, and reclassified its Malta business as held for sale. Management has attached a number to the strategy — fifteen business or market exits announced since early 2025 — and the stated logic is consistent across each: concentrate capital in businesses where HSBC already leads, rather than defend a presence everywhere it happens to operate. Trade finance, payments and wealth management in Asia and the Middle East are the areas the bank keeps naming as its comparative advantage; consumer retail banking in markets where HSBC is a mid-table player is what keeps getting sold.

What the market is reading into it

For a bank as globally systemic as HSBC, resuming discretionary capital returns is a statement about risk tolerance as much as a capital-allocation decision. Regulators do not let systemically important banks buy back stock while nursing doubts about the credit cycle ahead, and HSBC's own board would not authorise it while bracing for a deterioration in loan losses. The bank's full-year cost-of-risk guidance was left unchanged at roughly 45 basis points, even after absorbing a $400 million fraud-related loss tied to a UK financial sponsor and a $200 million hit from Hong Kong commercial property in the first half — evidence that management sees those as discrete, absorbed items rather than the leading edge of a broader credit problem.

HSBC's Hong Kong-listed shares have been trading near record highs through the summer, and the stock's reaction to Sunday's announcements extended that run. For traders using bank capital-return decisions as a proxy for macro confidence, the signal here is incremental rather than dramatic — a $1 billion buyback is modest against HSBC's balance sheet — but it arrives alongside similar moves from Shell, Airbus and BP this reporting season, part of a broader pattern of large-cap capital returns resuming after a cautious first half.

The next test of that read comes with third-quarter results, when HSBC expects to complete the current buyback tranche and signal whether a fourth-quarter follow-on is warranted. Investors will also be watching whether the Egypt sale price becomes public once the deal clears Egyptian and UAE regulators — a detail HSBC has so far declined to disclose.
