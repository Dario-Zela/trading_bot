EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which enforces the AI Act through its AI Office. — EmDee / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
EU AI Act transparency rules took effect 2 August, requiring chatbots and deepfakes to disclose AI involvement — but most member states still lack a regulator to enforce it.

A chatbot that doesn't tell you it's a chatbot. A video of a politician saying something they never said, with no label attached. An AI system quietly reading your face for signs of frustration while you're on hold with customer service. As of this week, all three are illegal to deploy in the European Union without disclosure — and the fines for getting caught start at €15 million.

The provision is Article 50 of the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act, and it became enforceable on 2 August, three days before this article was written. It doesn't ban any of the technologies above. It just insists that people be told, plainly, when they're dealing with a machine, a synthetic image, or a system reading their emotions. That sounds modest. In practice it means rewriting product interfaces, adding watermarking pipelines, and — for any company that serves EU users, wherever it happens to be headquartered — accepting Brussels as a de facto regulator.

What actually has to change

Article 50 covers four situations. Providers of chatbots and voice assistants must make it obvious — through a message, a label, anything short of an actual footnote — that the user is talking to software, unless that's already self-evident. Anyone generating synthetic audio, video, images or text has to embed machine-readable markers so the content can be identified as artificial, with an exception for ordinary photo editing. Deployers of emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems must tell the people being scanned. And AI-generated text published on matters of public interest — news, essentially — needs a disclosure unless a human editor has taken responsibility for it.

The penalty structure mirrors the EU's other big digital laws: up to €15 million or 3% of a company's worldwide annual turnover, whichever is larger, though smaller firms get the lower of the two figures rather than the higher. The European Commission's AI Office, working with regulators in each member state, is the enforcement body, and it has opened a complaints portal and a whistleblower channel to go with it. More than 180 companies — including most of the major model developers — have already signed onto a voluntary Code of Practice that gives them a presumption of compliance if they follow its technical recipe for watermarking and disclosure, a softer landing than waiting to be tested against the letter of the law.

A rule with no address to send the mail to

The catch is that "enforcement began" and "enforcement is possible" aren't quite the same thing yet. Article 50 applies from Brussels outward to any company whose AI touches an EU user, regardless of where its servers or executives sit — the same extraterritorial logic that made GDPR a global compliance benchmark rather than a European curiosity. But the national authorities meant to actually investigate complaints and levy fines are badly behind schedule. As of this spring, only a handful of the EU's 27 member states had formally designated the regulator responsible for policing the Act on their soil, despite a deadline that had already passed a year earlier. Hungary, Bulgaria and Malta had made no designation at all. The technical standards bodies that were supposed to translate the law into checkable engineering specifications are also running roughly a year late.

The EU has also just shown it will bend the calendar when the underlying scaffolding isn't ready. A separate, more demanding tranche of the Act — full conformity assessment for "high-risk" AI systems, the kind used in hiring, credit scoring or medical devices — was due to bind from this same August date. Under a Digital Omnibus deal reached in May, that deadline has been pushed back to December 2027. Article 50's transparency rules were not touched by that reprieve; they stand as written. But the fact that Brussels was willing to delay one pillar of its flagship AI law while holding firm on another suggests the Commission is triaging which fights it can actually referee right now, not declaring the whole apparatus battle-ready.

The test is whether anyone gets fined

Henna Virkkunen, the commission's executive vice-president overseeing digital policy, has framed the transparency push as reducing "deception and manipulation" while giving companies a workable path to compliance rather than a legal minefield. That's the intended trade: clearer rules against a credible threat of penalty. Whether it holds depends on a case nobody has filed yet. A law with a €15 million number attached but no functioning regulator behind it in most of the bloc is, for now, closer to a strong suggestion than a binding one.

The next marker to watch is 2 December, when a grace period for retrofitting existing generative AI systems with content-marking tools runs out, tightening the obligation for models already on the market. Beyond that, the real test is procedural rather than technical: the first complaint filed through the AI Office's new portal that actually produces a fine. Until one does, global AI providers are complying largely on the strength of reputational risk and the Code of Practice's promise of lighter-touch treatment — not because they've watched Brussels catch anyone.
