Justice Secretary apologises to PC Harper's family over release scheme gap

Justice Secretary Alex Norris, who apologised to PC Andrew Harper's family over the early-release scheme gap. — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Justice Secretary Alex Norris apologised after confirming PC Andrew Harper's killers aren't covered by exemptions to the early prisoner-release scheme.

The government has apologised to the family of PC Andrew Harper after admitting that two of the three men convicted of killing him will remain eligible for early release from prison — a gap that has exposed the limits of a scheme built, in part, on the promise of protecting cases exactly like his.

Justice Secretary Alex Norris told broadcasters this week that new exemptions to the early-release programme, designed to ease overcrowded jails in England and Wales, would cover rape, grooming and serious child sexual offences, but not manslaughter. PC Harper's killers, Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers, were convicted of manslaughter — not murder — in 2020, meaning both fall outside the carve-out despite the nature of their crime.

PC Harper, 28, died in August 2019 after being dragged for a mile behind a car in Sulhamstead, Berkshire, while responding to a report of a stolen quad bike. Cole and Bowers were each sentenced to 13 years and, under standard release rules, become eligible at the halfway point of their terms — a point they are now approaching. Henry Long, who drove the car, received 16 years and remains ineligible.

"That's created harm"

Norris did not dispute the outcome. "I know that's created harm, and it's not Lissie Harper's fault, and I'm sorry that she's been put into this situation as a result — I genuinely am," he said, referring to PC Harper's widow. He argued that widening exemptions further would not resolve the underlying problem: prisons in England and Wales are running out of capacity, and ministers have warned the system could stop functioning by October or November without releases. Around 5,000 offenders are now expected to be freed early, down from an original estimate of 6,000 after rapists and child sex offenders were excluded; the release window has already been pushed back from September to October, with those freed subject to electronic monitoring, random drug testing and, in some cases, driving bans.

Lissie Harper called the decision "disgusting," saying she was "beyond appalled and bristling with anger" that her husband's killers would be "free to roam the streets," and described the scheme as "an affront to justice" and "an utter betrayal." PC Harper's mother, Debbie Adlam, said the family was "bitterly disappointed," adding: "Andrew deserves better than this, and so do we."

The episode has become a political flashpoint for Prime Minister Andy Burnham's government, barely a fortnight into office. Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch said "you cannot put a price on keeping citizens safe and secure," while Reform UK and the Liberal Democrats both pressed ministers to intervene directly to keep Cole and Bowers inside. The irony is not lost on campaigners: PC Harper's death led directly to "Harper's Law," which mandates life sentences for killing emergency workers — a protection that does not reach back to cover his own killers' case.

The releases are due to begin in October, and ministers have pointed to a pledge of 14,000 new prison places by 2031 — of which roughly 3,000 have been delivered so far — as the longer-term fix. Whether that timeline holds, and whether pressure from the Harper family forces a further carve-out before then, will be the next test of a scheme already strained by the gap between its stated safeguards and what the law actually permits.
