HEALTH · MISINFORMATION · By Dr. P. Aaronson · 2026-08-05 · 6 min read

BBC investigation finds dewormer, scabies drugs sold as cancer cures

Ivermectin tablets seized by Austrian customs authorities — the same drug BBC reporters found marketed online as an unproven cancer treatment. — Bundesministerium für Finanzen (Austria) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
A BBC undercover probe found dog dewormer and scabies drugs sold as cancer cures — including on Amazon — prompting a Macmillan warning and MHRA seizures of over 150,000 doses since 2024.

A stairlift salesman in south Wales, visiting a customer's home to talk about mobility aids, changed the subject. The customer's father, Bob Holder, had a brain tumour and lung cancer. The salesman, Jason Sarver, began telling the family about two drugs he could supply — not from a pharmacy, but from his own stock — that he said could shrink tumours. The price was £100 for what he described as two to three months' supply.

The drugs were fenbendazole, an anti-parasitic sold to treat worms in dogs, and ivermectin, used against scabies and other parasites in humans and animals. Neither has any established evidence of anti-cancer effect in humans. An undercover BBC investigation has found both being marketed and sold as cancer treatments across the UK — including, until the broadcaster intervened, on Amazon.^1

"We're seeing an increasing number of people asking about these two products."

That's Professor Richard Simcock, chief medical officer at Macmillan Cancer Support, describing a trend the charity says has accelerated sharply. Macmillan says there is "zero real-world clinical evidence" that either drug treats cancer in humans, and its concern is not abstract: it warns that patients pursuing the drugs risk delaying or abandoning treatments that do work.

A marketplace with a gap

The claims trace back to a familiar pattern: laboratory studies showing the drugs can kill cancer cells in a petri dish, amplified on social media into promises they can cure cancer in a person. The BBC found videos and posts repeating those claims racking up thousands, in some cases millions, of views. Actor Mel Gibson gave the theory a mainstream airing in 2025 when he told a US podcast host that fenbendazole and ivermectin had helped three friends with cancer — a claim with no supporting clinical evidence.[^2]

What the investigation adds is a supply chain. Reporters found the drugs for sale on Amazon's UK marketplace, where third-party sellers can list products with comparatively little upfront vetting. Amazon told the BBC it removed the listings once contacted and said it had "proactive measures in place" to stop prohibited products being listed. That statement — action only after a journalist flagged it — is the crux of the story: the platform's own screening did not catch products that are illegal to sell for human use in the UK before a reporter did.

It is not the first time Amazon has had to pull health products from its UK site after scrutiny from regulators or press, and the recurrence points to a structural problem rather than an isolated lapse. Third-party marketplaces process millions of new listings, and automated checks are tuned to catch counterfeit branding and obvious contraband more reliably than they catch a legitimate veterinary drug being sold with cancer claims attached in the listing text or in linked reviews.

The regulatory and human cost

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) says it has seized 27,500 doses of fenbendazole and 124,000 doses of ivermectin intended for human use since 2024, working with Border Force — a scale that suggests enforcement is running well behind demand.^1 The MHRA said fenbendazole is not licensed for use in humans in the UK at all, and where a veterinary medicine is being supplied to people, or a prescription-only drug sold by someone who isn't a qualified healthcare professional, it can pursue criminal investigation and prosecution. Mr Sarver declined to comment when the BBC put its findings to him.

The risk Macmillan describes is not hypothetical. Lee Redpath, 45, died at Addenbrooke's Hospital in Cambridge on 29 April after taking fenbendazole he had bought online from a supplier in Ukraine, having seen social media claims about its "supposed anti-cancer properties." An inquest heard he had taken the drug for about a month before admission. Dr Gwilym Webb, a liver specialist who treated him, told the coroner fenbendazole has "no proven benefit for preventing or treating cancer in humans" and that Mr Redpath's liver failure followed doses far higher, and for far longer, than would be considered safe even in animals. The coroner recorded liver and renal failure as the cause of death, with fenbendazole-induced liver injury cited as a contributing factor.[^3]

In the United States, the BBC spoke to Jared Wright, whose mother Kelly died in January 2026 after declining oncologist-led treatment in favour of ivermectin and fenbendazole. Dr Martin Rolles, a consultant oncologist of 25 years' standing at the South West Wales Cancer Centre, told the BBC the drugs can also interact directly with cancer medication, compounding the danger for patients who take both. Dr Liz O'Riordan, a three-time cancer survivor and former breast surgeon, has taken to YouTube to counter the claims directly, arguing that the gap between a cell-culture result and a validated human treatment is precisely where this kind of misinformation thrives.

What it means for the platforms

For Amazon, the episode lands at an uncomfortable moment. E-commerce platforms operating in the UK and EU face tightening obligations under product-safety and online-marketplace rules that increasingly hold platforms responsible for what third-party sellers list, not just for their own inventory. A pattern of "remove after press contact" responses is the kind of evidence regulators and claimant lawyers look for when arguing that a platform's safeguards are reactive rather than preventive. Macmillan's public statement — naming the specific harm to cancer patients — gives the story a constituency that pure regulatory violations often lack, and it raises the odds that MPs or the MHRA press Amazon for a fuller account of how the listings passed screening in the first place.

The more durable question is whether marketplace moderation can plausibly keep pace with a misinformation ecosystem that manufactures demand faster than any single company can review supply. Removing the fenbendazole and ivermectin listings that the BBC found addresses this incident; it does nothing about the next unlicensed treatment claim to go viral. Macmillan says it wants to see the drugs' promotion tackled at the source — the video content driving the demand — rather than the retail point where it finally becomes transactable. Whether the MHRA opens a formal case against named sellers, and whether Amazon publishes any detail on how the products cleared its listing checks, are the next signals worth watching.

[^2]: Mel Gibson's claims were made on a US podcast in 2025 and have been reviewed by fact-checking outlets including Science Feedback, which found no clinical evidence to support them.
[^3]: Inquest proceedings reported by local UK press (Cambridgeshire coroner's court, Lawrence Court, Huntingdon).
