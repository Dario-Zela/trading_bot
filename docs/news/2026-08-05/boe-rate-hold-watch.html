Bank of England still split 6-3 on holding rates at 3.75%

The Bank of England building on Threadneedle Street, City of London. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The BoE held rates at 3.75% on a 6-3 vote — one dissent worse than expected — as three MPC members pushed for a hike on fears energy prices entrench inflation.

The Bank of England held its Base Rate at 3.75% on 30 July for a fifth consecutive meeting, but the vote that got there was tighter than markets expected — and tighter than it looks at first glance.

Six of the nine-member Monetary Policy Committee backed holding steady. Three — chief economist Huw Pill, external member Catherine Mann and Megan Greene — voted to raise Bank Rate a quarter point to 4%, arguing that energy costs stemming from the conflict in the Middle East risk becoming embedded in the UK's wage and price setting rather than passing through as a one-off shock. A 6-3 split is one dissenting vote worse, from the doves' perspective, than the 7-2 outcome economists had pencilled in, and one worse than June's vote too — the hawkish camp is growing, not shrinking.

The case for holding, and the case against

The backdrop is a familiar one by now. Brent crude spiked above $100 a barrel on 23 July, up from under $71 three weeks earlier, as the US-Iran conflict flared again, before easing back to around $92 by the time the MPC met. UK inflation eased to 2.6% in the year to June — its 21st straight month above the Bank's 2% target — and the Bank's own forecasters expect it to climb again, to roughly 3.2% in the autumn, as higher energy costs work through household bills and business costs.

That is the crux of the disagreement. The majority view, articulated by Governor Andrew Bailey at the post-decision press conference, is that there is "little evidence" of the kind of second-round effects — energy costs feeding into wage demands and then into prices more broadly — that would justify tightening pre-emptively. Bailey was explicit that the Committee is not edging toward a hike, restating the Bank's position from June that it would rather tolerate a slower return to target than tighten policy in response to what it still regards as an external shock.

The dissenters read the same data differently. Pill put it plainly in the minutes: he remains concerned about "more insidious second-round effects driven by catch-up dynamics in wage and price setting." Greene's argument was pre-emptive rather than reactive — that raising rates now would lower the odds of those dynamics ever taking hold, rather than waiting to confirm they have. One of the three dissents, reporting suggests, was triggered specifically by the collapse of a prior peace framework for the region, underlining how tightly the MPC's rate calculus is now tied to Middle East diplomacy rather than domestic demand.

Markets read the vote, not the statement

Sterling rallied on the outcome, with cable pushing above 1.3450 — though the move looked more dollar-driven than a genuine repricing of UK rate expectations, given the pound added only 0.2% against the euro. Gilts moved more emphatically: the 10-year yield pushed above 5%, and the 30-year — already at its highest since 1998 ahead of the Autumn Budget — traded near 5.75%, among the highest in the G7. That looks less like investors betting on a near-term hike and more like a risk premium being priced into UK debt as fiscal and inflation uncertainty compound each other.

What it signals

The practical takeaway is that rate cuts are off the table for now, and a hike — unthinkable a few months ago — is a live scenario if energy prices stay elevated into the autumn. Bailey's insistence that the Bank isn't shifting stance is itself notable: a governor doesn't need to talk down hike expectations unless the vote has created some. The next decision lands on 17 September, by which point the Bank will have a clearer read on whether September's inflation print confirms the second-round effects the hawks fear, or whether Bailey's caution about drawing too much comfort from calm wage data turns out to have been the right call.
