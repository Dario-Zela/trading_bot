Airbus jumps on new €5bn buyback and 2029 profit target

An Airbus A320, the workhorse of the narrowbody family whose ramp-up underpins Airbus's 2029 profit ambitions. — Pedro Aragão / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Airbus shares rose about 6% after it unveiled a €5bn three-year buyback and a 2029 profit target nearly double last year's, betting the aerospace recovery holds.

Airbus told investors it is done playing defence. At a business update in London on 21 July, the world's largest planemaker paired a new €5 billion share buyback with a 2029 profit target that implies core earnings will very nearly double from last year's level. The market's answer was immediate: the stock jumped as much as 7% on the day, before settling to a gain of roughly 6% — one of its sharpest single-day moves in years, and enough to push Airbus back toward record territory on the Paris exchange.

The headline number is the target itself. Airbus said it now expects adjusted EBIT — its preferred measure of core operating profit — of €12 billion to €13 billion in 2029, against €7.13 billion in 2025 and a 2026 goal of €7.5 billion. The commercial aircraft division, which builds the A320 and A350 families and accounts for the bulk of group revenue, is expected to contribute around €10 billion of that on its own. The target assumes a euro-dollar exchange rate of 1.22, a detail that matters for a company that prices jets in dollars but incurs much of its cost base in euros.

The buyback investors didn't see coming

The profit target gave analysts a number to model. The buyback gave them a reason to believe it. Deutsche Bank's Christophe Menard called it plainly: "The positive surprise, however, came from the €5 billion three-year share buy-back." Morgan Stanley, which lifted its price target on the stock from €160 to €200, went further, estimating the programme equates to roughly 1% of Airbus's market capitalisation per year, or about a quarter of the consensus cumulative free cash flow the company is expected to generate between 2026 and 2028. Combined with ordinary dividends, Morgan Stanley reckons Airbus is now committing to return 60% of that three-year free cash flow to shareholders.

That matters because Airbus has historically been stingy on this front, plunging cash instead into production ramp-up and, in leaner years, into supplier support during the pandemic-era slump. Vertical Research Partners analyst Rob Stallard framed the shift as an answer to a long-standing complaint: "Airbus' lack of longer-term guidance has long been a grievance with investors, and we think providing this forecast will help a lot in providing more assurance." A buyback is also a statement about confidence in cash generation specifically — a company doesn't commit €5 billion to purchasing its own stock if it expects to need that capital for a production crisis.

What has to go right

Chief executive Guillaume Faury was candid about why the company felt able to set numbers this far out: "We've put behind us a lot of challenges and we've cleared the skies to move forward, and we think we're in a good place to give an outlook." But some of that turbulence hasn't fully cleared. Roughly 40% of the A320neo fleet flies on Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan engines, and manufacturing flaws at the US supplier have forced repeated inspections and left aircraft grounded, pushing Airbus's own narrowbody ramp-up back by at least a year. The company now targets a monthly A320-family production rate of 70 to 75 by the end of 2027, a goal it once expected to hit earlier, and is pursuing damages from Pratt & Whitney over the delays. Airbus still held its 2026 delivery target of 870 aircraft after a stronger second quarter — deliveries of the A320 family alone rose to 271 units in the first half, up 15% year-on-year — but the engine dispute is a live reminder that the supply chain underpinning a 2029 target is not fully within Airbus's control.

The bet embedded in the target

The 2029 goal also assumes more than 1,100 aircraft delivered annually by then, alongside a multi-year up-cycle in global air travel that has, so far, shown no sign of cracking. That is the trade the market just endorsed with a 6% share price move: a bet that supply-chain normalisation continues uninterrupted, that engine makers catch up rather than fall further behind, and that airline demand for new, more fuel-efficient jets holds through a period when interest rates and macro conditions remain unsettled. None of those are new risks for Airbus. What is new is that the company has now put a number on what happens if they don't materialise — and started paying shareholders as though it expects they will.

The next checkpoint comes with third-quarter results, due in the autumn, when investors will look for confirmation that the delivery pace held and that the Pratt & Whitney dispute hasn't widened. Until then, the 2029 target functions as a promise the market has priced in early.
