MI6 ranked Europe's top foreign intelligence service in peer survey

The SIS Building at Vauxhall Cross, headquarters of Britain's Secret Intelligence Service, MI6. — Richard Cooke / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A peer survey of 60 European intelligence officers rated MI6 the continent's top spy service for recruiting sources in Russia, China, Iran and Turkey, ahead of France's DGSE.

There is no leaderboard for spies, no annual results call, no earnings guidance. So when 60 serving and former intelligence officers from 25 countries were asked, in effect, to hand out a Ballon d'Or for espionage, the result was always going to travel. The French magazine L'Express spent three months coaxing around 100 practitioners — CIA and Mossad officers among them — into ranking their European counterparts. The consensus: Britain's MI6 sits well clear of the field, prized above all for recruiting sources deep inside Russia, China, Iran and Turkey.

"The United Kingdom has by far the strongest intelligence services in Europe. MI6 is exceptional." — Robert Grenier, former CIA station chief

It is an odd thing to put a number on, and the number itself — MI6 on 251 points, France's DGSE on 169 — matters less than what the exercise says about how Europe's spy services see their own hierarchy after four years of war on the continent and a widening contest with Beijing.

How you score a secret

L'Express borrowed the format of a Eurovision or a Ballon d'Or: each of the 60 voting professionals, including 17 serving or former agency chiefs, submitted a top five. Points were tallied, and a ranking fell out the other end. MI6 topped it, followed by the DGSE, the Dutch AIVD and MIVD, Ukraine's services in fourth, and Germany's BND in fifth. Sweden, Norway, Estonia, Poland and Denmark rounded out the top ten, each drawing praise for a specific niche — Estonia for cyber and open-source work, Norway for Arctic and maritime coverage, Sweden for its rising weight in the Baltic.

Greece scored no points at all, tied with Ireland and Luxembourg at the bottom of the table. And yet the report singles Athens out for something the numbers don't capture: a standing invitation to the table whenever the subject is Turkey. Geography and decades of friction across the Aegean have given Greek services a granularity on Ankara's politics, military posture and intelligence services that larger, better-funded agencies can't easily replicate. It is the survey's most interesting footnote — proof that in this business, proximity can outrank budget.

The long game

What separates MI6 from the pack, according to the practitioners L'Express spoke to, is patience with people rather than any particular piece of tradecraft. Sources cited the agency's willingness to cultivate an informant for a decade or more before that relationship pays off — described by one voter as playing it "rather like the Russians do." Two cases surfaced repeatedly in the reporting: Alireza Akbari, the former Iranian deputy defence minister executed by Tehran in 2023 after being convicted of spying for MI6, and a Chinese civil-service couple recruited over years inside Beijing's bureaucracy. Neither case was volunteered by London — both surfaced through the states that caught the leak, which is itself a comment on how rarely this kind of penetration becomes visible at all.

"The British are out in front on the Ukrainian issue." — Ralph Goff, former head of CIA operations in Europe

The DGSE's runner-up finish came with its own compliment, less about longevity than reach. A 34-year CIA veteran, Gina Bennett, told L'Express the French service was "the most capable partner" she'd encountered in human intelligence — a reputation built on France's post-colonial networks across the Sahel, the Gulf and North Africa, terrain where London has comparatively thinner cover.

The institutional memory behind the ranking belongs partly to Sir Richard Moore, who ran MI6 from 2020 until stepping down last year — notably, a former UK ambassador to Turkey himself — and partly to his successor, Blaise Metreweli, the service's first female chief, who took over in October 2025 having previously run its technology directorate. The reputation the survey measures was built well before either of them arrived; what it doesn't measure is whether it survives the transition.

What the score doesn't show

The caveats are worth sitting with. L'Express has not published a scoring rubric, a full list of who voted, or how ties and abstentions were handled — this is a magazine's reported survey of professional opinion, not an audited index. Critics elsewhere have made the more pointed version of that objection: the ranking measures reputation, not output, and reputation tracks budget. Britain's intelligence spending runs into the billions; the Baltic services praised for punching above their weight operate on tens of millions. A service can be well regarded by its peers and still miss the thing that matters most, or be feared by its targets while barely registering in a poll of allies.

None of that makes the exercise irrelevant to anyone pricing risk in Moscow, Tehran or the Bosphorus. Perception among the people who actually trade intelligence with each other is itself a input into how fast a warning travels between capitals, and how much a partner service is willing to share before a crisis breaks rather than after. A Europe where human coverage of Russia and Iran is concentrated in one or two capitals is a Europe slower, on average, to see trouble coming everywhere else — a gap post-Brexit debates about UK-EU security cooperation have circled for years without resolving.

The next test of that gap is already on the calendar. Sanctions diplomacy around Iran's nuclear file and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and the daily calculus of what Moscow will risk next in Ukraine, are precisely the theatres where the sources MI6 is credited with recruiting would earn their keep — or be shown, uncomfortably, not to have existed at all.
