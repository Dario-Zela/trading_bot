BEYOND THE TAPE · WASHINGTON · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-05 · 4 min read

Senate unveils bipartisan stopgap funding bill through midterms

The United States Capitol, where senators voted 89-4 to advance the stopgap funding measure. — Martin Falbisoner / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Senate advanced a bill funding the government through December 11, deliberately pushing any shutdown fight past the midterm elections.

There is a particular species of Washington news that almost never makes headlines: the crisis that didn't happen. This week the Senate produced one anyway, unveiling a bipartisan bill to keep the government funded through 11 December — a deadline that, notably, sits comfortably on the far side of the midterm elections in November.

The current funding patch does not actually expire until 30 September. That the Senate is already moving, weeks ahead of schedule and well before its August recess, tells its own story about an institution that has grown wary of its own brinkmanship.

The deal and the dealmakers

The bill is the work of Susan Collins, the Maine Republican who chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee, and Patty Murray, the Washington Democrat who is its ranking member — a pairing that has become the closest thing the current Senate has to a reliable adult in the room. Collins called the measure "straightforward," adding that it "avoids any poison pills." Murray was more expansive about the calculus behind it.

"I believe that we all want to make sure that we have good funding through December 11, and hopefully, you know, we'll be able to negotiate good bills once the elections are over."

The arithmetic behind the bill is almost as telling as its content. Senators voted 89-4 on Monday to advance it — a margin that, in a chamber split 53-47 along party lines, reflects something closer to institutional relief than partisan negotiation. The bill holds spending at current levels, with carve-outs for adjustments to SNAP and WIC food-assistance programs, disaster relief and national-security accounts. It also blocks, for the duration of the stopgap, a White House budget-office proposal that would have handed political appointees final say over federal grant approvals — a provision both Collins and Murray pointed to as evidence the bill had been kept clean of ideological freight.

What it pointedly leaves out

The more interesting story may be what didn't make the bill. President Trump and House Republicans had pushed for the stopgap to carry the SAVE America Act, the president's marquee voter-eligibility legislation, and Trump had at one point demanded the Senate cancel its August recess until it passed. The Senate said no. Thom Tillis, the North Carolina Republican, argued there wasn't enough time to implement the act before the November election and that trying would invite exactly the kind of chaos Republicans would be blamed for at the ballot box. Senate Majority Leader John Thune has been similarly blunt that the votes for it, or for gutting the filibuster to force it through, simply are not there.

That a Republican-controlled Senate would hand its own president a defeat on a signature priority, in an election year, is the kind of detail that gets lost beneath the duller headline of "funding bill advances." It suggests the appropriators' instinct for self-preservation is currently outrunning the instinct for message discipline — an unusual state of affairs, and one worth watching for what it implies about Thune's leverage over his conference heading into the fall.

Why markets shrug and voters might not

For anyone pricing risk, the honest answer is that this is a non-event dressed as news, and deliberately so. Shutdowns rattle markets because they are abrupt, open-ended and hit federal payrolls, contractor invoices and economic data releases all at once — the August 2023 lapse and, more relevant here, the two funding gaps the government has already weathered earlier this year gave investors fresh memory of what that disruption looks like. A bill that removes the possibility of a repeat before December does not generate a rally; it removes a tail risk that was already only partially priced in. Prevention rarely moves a tape the way a crisis does.

The more durable story is political rather than financial. By pushing the fight to 11 December, Collins and Murray have bought Congress five extra weeks after the midterms to negotiate full-year spending bills without a shutdown deadline colliding with get-out-the-vote operations — a sequencing choice that protects incumbents of both parties from having to explain a government closure to voters days before they cast ballots. It also means the SAVE America Act fight, and whatever fiscal fights lie beneath the surface of "current levels" funding, have simply been deferred, not resolved.

The bill still needs a final Senate vote and House passage before 30 September, though the 89-4 tally makes that a formality rather than a cliffhanger. The date to circle is 11 December itself — when the same appropriators, with a midterm result now in hand rather than in front of them, will find out whether the political cover they bought themselves this week was worth the price.
