AMD posts record $11.5bn quarter but shares slide on high expectations

AMD CEO Lisa Su, who said Q2 data-centre revenue "more than doubled year-over-year" as the Helios AI rack platform tracks ahead of internal forecasts. — Gene Wang / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
AMD posted record $11.5bn revenue with data-centre sales up 107%, yet shares fell over 7% as the beat fell short of sky-high investor expectations.

AMD delivered the best quarter in its history on Tuesday — record revenue, record profitability, a beat on every headline number Wall Street tracks — and its shares fell more than 7% anyway. The disconnect says less about AMD's chip business, which is by most measures firing on every cylinder, than about how far the market's AI expectations have run ahead of even exceptional results.

The numbers were not the problem

Second-quarter revenue came in at $11.5 billion, up 50% year-over-year and a company record, comfortably ahead of the roughly $11.3 billion analysts had modelled. Data-centre revenue — the segment investors care about most, covering AMD's EPYC server chips and Instinct AI accelerators — reached $6.7 billion, more than doubling from a year earlier with 107% growth, and edged past the Street's $6.5 billion estimate. That division alone now accounts for 58% of everything AMD sells.

Profitability moved even faster than revenue. Non-GAAP net income rose 253% to $2.8 billion, and non-GAAP earnings per share came in at $1.66 against a consensus of roughly $1.60-$1.62. GAAP net income of $2.3 billion was up 163% on the year. Guidance for the third quarter — revenue of about $13 billion, plus or minus $300 million — implies roughly 41% year-over-year growth and topped the $12.5 billion analysts had penciled in.

"We delivered an excellent quarter, with record revenue and profitability as Data Center revenue more than doubled year-over-year," chief executive Lisa Su told investors on the earnings call. She said AMD's new rack-scale AI platform, Helios, is "tracking ahead of initial internal forecasts," with shipments beginning this quarter and a steeper ramp into 2027.

Then the market marked it down anyway

By the numbers alone, this reads as an unambiguous win. Shares tell a different story: AMD stock, which had already climbed roughly 140% over the prior twelve months on the strength of the AI trade, fell as much as 9% in the session that followed the report before settling down a little over 7%. That kind of reaction after a genuine double beat is unusual, and it points to a market that had priced in not just a strong quarter but a blowout one.

Two details in the print gave sceptics something concrete to point to. Gross margin, on a non-GAAP basis, came in at 56%, but the GAAP figure of 54% missed the roughly 56% some investors had modelled — the gap traced to near-term costs from ramping Helios production before volumes reach scale. Second, while Su said data-centre revenue should keep accelerating through the back half of the year and into 2027, that guidance implicitly leaned on a small number of very large customers rather than broad-based demand.

Concentration is the real story beneath the beat

AMD's own disclosures underline the point. Su told analysts that frontier AI labs — OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta among them — are buying compute at "gigawatt scale" through various cloud providers, while other customers are interested in Helios but at "a more regular scale." AMD's newest marquee deal, a partnership with Anthropic for up to two gigawatts of MI450-series GPU capacity deployed in Helios racks, illustrates the scale of these arrangements — and the risk. A handful of AI labs currently operating at a loss are, in effect, underwriting a large share of AMD's projected growth. Gaming revenue fell 31% in the quarter as console cycles wind down, and PC sales face their own headwinds from memory-market conditions, leaving data centre with even more weight to carry.

None of this makes AMD's quarter a bad one. It makes it a useful data point for a broader question hanging over semiconductor stocks: how much further can AI infrastructure spending run before it collides with the profitability of the companies doing the spending? AMD is now committing to an annual cadence of new AI rack platforms through the end of the decade, with an MI500 generation already flagged for 2027 — a bet that demand keeps compounding rather than plateauing.

What to watch

The next test is execution, not orders. Helios shipments begin this quarter, and AMD's guidance depends on that ramp reaching meaningful volume in the second half of 2026 without repeating the margin drag seen this quarter. Q4 results, expected early next year, should show whether customer diversity — the "regular scale" buyers Su mentioned alongside the gigawatt-scale hyperscalers — actually materialises, or whether AMD's growth remains a bet on three or four counterparties. Either way, Tuesday's reaction has put the market on notice that for AI-exposed chipmakers, a beat is no longer enough on its own.
