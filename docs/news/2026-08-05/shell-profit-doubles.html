Shell profit more than doubles to $9.8bn, adds $3bn buyback

Shell's Pernis refinery near Rotterdam, Europe's largest, part of the downstream network that ran at a record 102% utilisation in the second quarter — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's adjusted earnings more than doubled to $9.8bn on record 102% refinery utilisation and $5.8bn of cost cuts, funding a fresh $3bn buyback.

Shell posted adjusted earnings of $9.8bn for the second quarter, more than double the $4.26bn it made in the same period a year ago and comfortably ahead of the $8.92bn analysts had pencilled in. The company also launched a fresh $3bn share buyback — its nineteenth consecutive quarter of repurchases at that level or above — even as fighting in the Middle East knocked out roughly a fifth of its global production.

The headline number was driven less by a single windfall than by the parts of Shell's business that rarely make headlines: refineries running flat out, a chemicals division turning a corner after years in the red, and a cost-cutting programme that has now stripped out $5.8bn in structural spending since 2022. Revenue came in at $94.7bn, up 45% on the quarter, and operating cash flow topped $21.4bn.

A refinery system running at capacity

Shell's downstream business posted refinery utilisation of 102% — a record for the company, achieved by running units above their nameplate rating through debottlenecking and tighter scheduling. Chief executive Wael Sawan told analysts the figure reflects closer integration between Shell's trading desks and its plant operators, a model the company is now rolling out across its refining network rather than treating trading as a business bolted on to the side.

"These results show more than anything that Shell delivers through volatility," Sawan said on the earnings call. "Volatility is the new normal — what we have been trying to build is a company that is able to thrive through it."

The chemicals and products division backed that claim up: adjusted earnings of $2.9bn were up 53% on the first quarter, the unit's best quarterly showing in more than five years, and chemicals generated positive free cash flow for the first time in that span — a segment that has weighed on group returns since the pandemic-era demand collapse. Marketing added $1.3bn, while integrated gas contributed $2.7bn and the upstream unit $3.5bn, the latter lifted by higher realised oil and gas prices.

Cost cuts meet a Middle East supply shock

The quarter's other engine was discipline on spending. Shell has now delivered $5.8bn of structural cost savings against a $5-7bn target it set for 2028, with $700m of that coming in the first half of this year alone — putting the company on track to land at the top of its own range roughly two years early. Four-quarter operating expenditure has fallen to $35.5bn from $39.5bn in 2022.

That cost discipline mattered because the operating backdrop was not straightforward. Disruption tied to the conflict around the Strait of Hormuz forced Shell to shut its Pearl gas-to-liquids plant and associated LNG assets in Qatar, taking out roughly 25 million tonnes a year of supply capacity and affecting an estimated 550,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day — close to a fifth of the group's global output. Shell has guided third-quarter integrated gas production down to 570,000-630,000 boe/d as a result, with repairs at Pearl's second train not expected to finish before the first quarter of 2027.

That the group could absorb a supply-side shock of that size and still more than double its year-on-year earnings is, in management's telling, the point of the integrated model: trading and downstream margins cushioned the loss of upstream barrels, even as regional benchmark oil prices moved on the same disruption. It is a dynamic playing out in parallel at BP, which posted its own sharp earnings jump this week on Middle East-driven price strength, and it sits awkwardly against hopes that a Hormuz de-escalation deal could soon take some of that price support away.

Balance sheet room for more buybacks

Shell's board approved the $3bn repurchase alongside the results, keeping distributions within its stated policy of returning 40-50% of cash flow from operations through the cycle — the trailing 12-month payout ratio currently sits at 44%. Free cash flow for the quarter was $17.5bn, and gearing stood at 19%, with net debt of $42bn, or $12bn once lease liabilities are stripped out. Capital spending guidance for the year was left unchanged at $24bn-$26bn.

Shares rose around 1.3% to $89.54 in the session following the results, a muted reaction given the scale of the earnings beat — a sign, according to traders quoted around the results, that the market had already priced in strong energy earnings given where crude and refining margins have been trading. Reported earnings per share of $1.76 missed some analyst models built on a higher share count assumption, a discrepancy that produced conflicting headlines but does not change the underlying cash generation.

The next test comes at third-quarter results, due in late October, when investors will want to see the Pearl GTL outage resolving on schedule and refinery utilisation holding near record levels rather than reverting once the Middle East disruption eases. Whether the current run of buybacks — nineteen straight quarters and counting — can be sustained if oil prices soften from their current elevated levels remains the open question hanging over the stock.
