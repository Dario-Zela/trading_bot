Suppressed UK intelligence report warned ecosystem collapse threatens food security

In one sentence
A shelved UK intelligence assessment warned ecosystem collapse could trigger food shortages and price shocks — twice suppressed before a Guardian investigation forced it into view.

Somewhere in Whitehall, twice in the space of a year, someone decided that a warning from Britain's own intelligence machinery was too alarming to publish. The document in question was not about a hostile state or a terror cell. It was about hedgerows, coral reefs and the Amazon — and what happens to food prices and social order when the natural systems that grow, water and stabilise the world's crops start to fail.

That document, Global Biodiversity Loss, Ecosystem Collapse and National Security, was compiled with input from the Joint Intelligence Committee, the body that sits above MI5, MI6 and GCHQ and produces the assessments a prime minister reads before deciding what actually threatens the country. According to reporting by the Guardian's Fiona Harvey, aides to then-prime minister Keir Starmer quashed plans to release it twice — first ahead of a planned October 2025 launch, then again afterwards — reportedly for fear that Reform UK would attack the government for prioritising climate concerns over the cost of living. It only became public in January 2026, in a redacted 14-page form, after a sustained freedom of information campaign by Ruth Chambers of the Green Alliance think tank forced Defra's hand.

"A devastating picture of severe food shortages, price rises, migration, political destabilisation and possible war."

That is how Harvey characterised the substance of the assessment. It is a striking sentence to find applied to a government document about ecosystems rather than armies — and it is why the episode matters well beyond the usual audience for biodiversity policy.

What the intelligence service actually said

The redacted version, published via gov.uk on 20-21 January, rates the risk to UK national security from global ecosystem degradation as "high" under a reasonable worst-case scenario, using the same probability-and-confidence framework the JIC applies to state threats. It names six systems — the Amazon and Congo rainforests, boreal forests, the Himalayas, and Southeast Asia's coral reefs and mangroves — as being on pathways toward collapse, meaning irreversible loss of function rather than gradual decline.

The mechanism the assessment worries about is not exotic. The UK imports around 40% of its food, more than a quarter of it from Europe. Officials reportedly judged it a "realistic possibility" that by 2030 the natural systems underpinning that supply — soil, pollinators, stable rainfall, functioning fisheries — could already be degrading enough to disrupt it, with some ecosystems capable of tipping within five years. The knock-on effects described include higher global food prices, intensified competition for scarce resources, migration, a rise in organised crime around resource smuggling, and — because degraded ecosystems bring humans, livestock and wildlife into closer contact — greater pandemic risk.

Defra's public defence has been that Britain's food system is resilient by design. "We have access through international trade to food products that cannot be produced here," a spokesperson said, arguing that diversified sourcing insulates the UK from any single shock. The National Farmers' Union was less sanguine. Its deputy president, David Exwood, put it bluntly: "With an increasingly volatile geopolitical and climatic situation, we cannot rely on imports to sustain us."

A pattern of shelving, not a one-off

What turns this from a policy dispute into a governance story is the repetition. Caroline Lucas, the former Green Party leader who has tracked the episode closely, argued that the biodiversity assessment was not suppressed in isolation — that a second, related report on UK food security was also held back around the same period. "To cover up one high-level report looks odd," she said. "To cover up two looks like a pattern."

The pattern, as reported, was political rather than analytical: nobody inside government appears to have disputed the JIC's judgment. The objection was to public alarm at a moment when the cost of living, not the Amazon canopy, was the dominant political story. That is a defensible short-term political calculation and a poor way to manage a long-tail systemic risk — the two things are not the same, and conflating them is the failure worth naming.

Starmer resigned as Labour leader and prime minister in June, and Andy Burnham took office on 20 July. The decisions to shelve the report were his predecessor's, but the question of whether to publish the assessment in full — Parliament has already pressed the government on this in both the Commons and the Lords this year — now sits with him. So far, Defra has not committed to releasing an unredacted version.

What it means for anyone pricing risk

For traders, the interesting part is not the ecology. It is what the episode reveals about how systemic, low-probability-high-severity risks get treated inside the institutions meant to monitor them. A government that will not surface its own intelligence assessment of a food-security threat to its own parliament is not a government whose risk models can be assumed to be feeding into anything markets watch — sovereign debt pricing, agricultural futures, or supply-chain contingency planning. Commodity markets already have plenty on their plate this month, from oil sliding on hopes of an Iran-Hormuz deal to gilt yields at their highest since 1998 ahead of the Autumn Budget. Ecosystem collapse does not trade on any of those screens, and this episode is a reminder that the absence of a price signal is not the same as the absence of a risk.

The UK's own third 35°C-plus heatwave of the year, with rail speed restrictions already in force, is the visible, immediate face of the stress the JIC assessment describes in the abstract. The intelligence document is about the slower, structural version of the same problem — and it took a freedom of information campaign, not a government press release, to get even a redacted account of it into public view.

The next marker to watch is whether Burnham's government responds to the parliamentary pressure by publishing a fuller version of the assessment, or whether the report that took an FOI fight to surface once quietly settles back into the file. Green Alliance and opposition MPs have said they will keep pushing; the Autumn Budget, due in the coming months, will be the first real test of whether food-security risk features in fiscal planning at all.
