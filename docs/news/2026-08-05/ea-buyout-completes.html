Saudi-led consortium completes record $55bn buyout of Electronic Arts

Electronic Arts' Redwood City, California headquarters, now under private ownership after the $55bn buyout. — King of Hearts / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A Saudi-led consortium completed the largest leveraged buyout ever — $55bn for EA at $210 a share — financed with $20bn in debt at rates well above 2007's TXU record deal.

Electronic Arts stopped trading on Nasdaq this week for the last time. In its place, prior to Wednesday's open, the S&P 500 slotted in Ferguson Enterprises, the plumbing-and-building-products distributor — a small, procedural sentence that ends a nearly year-long process and a somewhat larger one: the world's largest leveraged buyout has closed.

The consortium — Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, private equity firm Silver Lake, and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners — completed its $55 billion acquisition of the Battlefield and FIFA publisher on August 4, paying $210 a share in cash, a 25% premium to EA's undisturbed price of $168.32 last September. Andrew Wilson stays on as chief executive; the company stays in Redwood City, California. What changes is who owns it, and how much debt now sits on top of it.

The mechanics of a $55bn take-private

The deal was struck almost eleven months ago, on September 29, 2025, and shareholders approved it that December. Getting from signature to close took most of 2026: the European Commission's antitrust and foreign-subsidies reviews ran to their July deadlines, and CFIUS — the US national-security review that had reportedly stalled the timetable earlier in the year — cleared the transaction in late July, weeks ahead of the September 28 outside date written into the merger agreement.

Ownership of the private EA breaks down unevenly. Regulatory filings put PIF's equity stake at 93.4%, with Silver Lake holding 5.5% and Affinity Partners 1.1% — a reminder that this is fundamentally a sovereign-wealth-fund purchase with two minority partners attached, rather than a three-way partnership of equals. PIF already owned 9.9% of EA going into the deal and rolled that stake forward.

Financing a record deal at today's rates

What makes this a genuine test of PE appetite, rather than just a large cheque from Riyadh, is the debt. The consortium put in roughly $36 billion of equity; JPMorgan committed the remaining $20 billion alone, then sold it down. In March, the bank launched an $8 billion junk-bond offering split between $5.5 billion of secured notes — priced across dollar and euro tranches at a 7.25% coupon — and $2.5 billion of unsecured notes at 8.75%. CreditSights estimated gross leverage at close of around 6 times earnings, a level that would have been unremarkable in 2007 and is considerably harder to carry with base rates where they now sit.

That 2007 comparison is not incidental. Until this week, the leverage record belonged to the $45 billion buyout of Texas utility TXU by KKR, TPG and Goldman Sachs Capital Partners — a deal that collapsed into Chapter 11 seven years later when natural-gas prices fell and the debt load proved unsustainable. No sponsor had attempted anything on that scale since, which is part of why EA's financing matters beyond gaming: banks and bondholders were willing to underwrite $20 billion of buyout debt against a games publisher's cash flows, at coupons north of 7%, and get it done. Whether that appetite reflects confidence in EA's franchises — Madden, EA Sports FC, The Sims — or simply in PIF's backing is the question leveraged-finance desks will be arguing over for the next several quarters.

Index rebalancing and what it signals

For equity markets, the more immediate mechanical effect was Wednesday's S&P 500 reshuffle, with Ferguson Enterprises stepping into EA's spot. Passive funds tracking the index were forced to sell EA-linked exposure and buy Ferguson in scale ahead of the open — the kind of flow event that shows up in volume data without much fanfare, but that trading desks price around all the same.

The more durable signal is about financing conditions rather than index mechanics. PIF's Turqi Alnowaiser called entertainment and sports "among the fastest growing and evolving sectors" for the fund's strategy; Silver Lake's Egon Durban framed the investment around what artificial intelligence "can do to enhance game development." Kushner, whose Affinity Partners is funded largely by Gulf capital, described EA as having built stories and communities that are "part of everyday life for hundreds of millions of people." The language is standard deal-closing rhetoric, but the underlying fact — a sovereign fund and two PE shops secured $20 billion in junk debt for a single acquisition, in a market that had not supported a deal this size in nearly two decades — is the part likely to be cited in the next round of buyout pitches, in gaming and well beyond it.

EA's next scheduled disclosure will be Silver Lake and PIF's first update on the private company's leverage and operating performance, likely to come alongside 2027 fund-reporting cycles rather than any public filing — a reminder that EA's finances, including whether that 6x leverage proves durable, will now be visible only to bondholders and the consortium itself.
