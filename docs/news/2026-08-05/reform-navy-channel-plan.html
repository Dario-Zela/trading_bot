Reform UK's Royal Navy Channel migrant plan sparks French anger

Nigel Farage, whose Reform UK unveiled 'Operation Fortress' to deploy the Royal Navy against Channel migrant crossings. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament (Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Reform UK's plan to deploy the Royal Navy against Channel migrant boats drew a 'declaration of maritime war' warning from Calais's mayor and skepticism from naval experts.

The pitch, delivered at a London press conference on Monday, had the cadence of a military briefing. Nigel Farage and his home affairs spokesman, Zia Yusuf, stood behind lecterns holding booklets titled Operation Fortress: Stopping the Invasion of Britain and promised what they called the largest Royal Navy operation in the Channel since the Second World War. Every small boat crossing from France, they said, would be intercepted and sent back — by warship, if necessary, before the vessel had finished landing.

Across the water, the response was immediate and pointed. Calais mayor Natacha Bouchart told France 3 that deploying the Royal Navy against migrant boats would amount to a "declaration of maritime war" — reviving a phrase she has used against British governments before, but one that now carries the weight of an explicitly hard-right proposal rather than a mainstream one. France's interior ministry went further, calling the plan a violation of French sovereignty and of international maritime law.

What Reform is actually proposing

Operation Fortress, as outlined by Yusuf, would fold the Royal Navy, Royal Marines, RAF and British Army into a single command structure alongside a new surveillance unit tasked with tracking boats as they leave French beaches. River-class offshore patrol vessels — the same ships currently used for fisheries protection — would intercept dinghies in the Channel, detain occupants, provide water, food and a medical check, then return them to France or Belgium under naval escort.

Yusuf was careful to say the plan does not involve "pushback" tactics — towing or physically barging boats away at sea, a method Britain considered and shelved in 2021 after warnings it could capsize overcrowded dinghies. Instead, the model is closer to escorted return: intercept, process, sail back. Farage insisted the approach "falls within other conventions of international law," though he did not specify which.

When pressed on how France would be persuaded to accept returned boats it has not agreed to take, Yusuf pointed to money. Britain currently pays France roughly €766 million under a bilateral arrangement to fund patrols and processing on the French coast. Reform's suggestion was blunt: renegotiate that payment, or withhold it, to force cooperation — an approach one interviewer put to him as, in effect, threatening to "get into a diplomatic argument."

A plan built for a fight it may not need to have

The timing is not incidental. Reform's announcement followed a deadly migrant surge at Spain's Ceuta enclave that has put border security back atop the European news agenda, and it lands as the party holds a wide polling lead over the governing Labour Party — though Labour has recently clawed back some ground. The next UK general election is not due until 2029, which means Operation Fortress is, for now, a manifesto marker rather than an operational order.

That has not stopped military and legal specialists from picking it apart. Tom Sharpe, a former Royal Navy commander, told reporters the logistics simply don't work: "ferrying people back to France, against the French's will, to me just doesn't add up." Interception in French or international waters without Paris's consent would strain the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea and rules on non-refoulement that Britain is bound by regardless of who occupies Downing Street. Downing Street itself noted that the Royal Navy already held primary responsibility for small-boat operations in 2022, without crossings falling — and that crossings are in fact down 43% year-on-year in 2026 under the current Border Force-led approach and the UK-France "one-in, one-out" returns deal signed last year.

Why the anger travels further than the policy

What makes the French reaction significant is less the plan's feasibility than what it signals about the terms of a relationship both governments have spent two years trying to stabilise. The existing returns arrangement — France taking back small-boat arrivals in exchange for Britain accepting asylum seekers with UK family ties — took months of negotiation and remains a pilot. A UK opposition party campaigning on unilateral naval interception, backed by threats to withhold French payments, tests exactly the kind of goodwill that arrangement depends on, regardless of whether Reform ever forms a government.

For now the practical stakes are limited: no boats have been turned back by warship, no treaty has been torn up, and the Élysée has not summoned Britain's ambassador. But French officials have made clear that any future government pursuing this path would face resistance framed in terms of sovereignty and international law, not just diplomatic irritation. The next test of the relationship is more mundane than a naval standoff — it's whether the one-in, one-out returns scheme, due for review as its pilot period runs through mid-2026, survives the political noise around it intact.
