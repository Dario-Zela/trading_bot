BP profit jumps 78% on Middle East oil surge as new CEO trims capex

A BP filling station forecourt — the retail face of a company now selling off refining and retail assets even as trading profits surge. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP's profit more than doubled to $5.7bn on Hormuz-driven oil prices, but new CEO Meg O'Neill cut production guidance and expanded asset sales rather than invest into the surge.

BP posted a second-quarter profit of $5.7 billion, more than double what it made a year earlier, as fighting around the Strait of Hormuz pushed oil and gas prices sharply higher. That much fits the pattern set by every oil major reporting this earnings season. What doesn't fit is what the company's new chief executive did next: rather than lean into the windfall, she trimmed how much oil and gas BP expects to pump this year and put more of the company up for sale.

The result is a split-screen quarter. On one side, a profit number that beat every analyst estimate. On the other, a CEO who used the moment of maximum tailwind to tell investors she doesn't expect it to last.

The windfall

Underlying replacement-cost profit — the measure BP and its peers use to strip out swings in the value of oil inventories — came in at $5.7 billion for the three months to June, up from $2.35 billion in the same period last year and up 78% from the first quarter's $3.2 billion. Net profit attributable to shareholders was $3.91 billion. Operating cash flow nearly doubled to $10.9 billion, and net debt fell by $3.1 billion to $22.3 billion.

The driver was not BP's own operations, which were actually weaker: upstream production fell 6% quarter-on-quarter to about 2.2 million barrels of oil equivalent a day, and refinery reliability slipped as regional disruption rattled supply chains. What rescued the quarter was price. Fighting has choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint that normally carries roughly a fifth of the world's traded oil and gas, and BP's trading and refining desks captured the resulting spike in margins.

BP was not alone. Shell's profit also more than doubled this quarter on the same dynamic, and in Washington, President Trump complained that Exxon Mobil and Chevron — whose own profits jumped to $14.5 billion and $12 billion respectively — were "making too much money based on a shortage." BP raised its quarterly dividend 4% to 8.66 cents a share regardless.

O'Neill's retreat

The more consequential news came from the guidance BP attached to those numbers. Meg O'Neill, who took over as chief executive in April — the first woman to run a top-five oil major and the first outside hire to lead BP in more than a century — used the results to cut BP's full-year upstream production outlook to 2.18–2.27 million boe/d, down from 2.312 million in 2025. She also confirmed a five-point plan to shrink the company: BP is marketing its US renewable natural gas unit, Archaea Energy, exiting the Bay du Nord project off Canada, selling its Gelsenkirchen refinery and Austrian retail business, and — in a formal sale process launched in late July — putting its entire UK North Sea upstream business on the block. Divestment proceeds for the year are now guided at $8–9 billion, including roughly $6 billion already banked from the sale of Castrol.

Capital spending guidance, oddly, nudged up slightly — to $13.5–14 billion from $13–13.5 billion — but only because BP is delaying some of those same asset sales to hold out for a better price, not because it's investing more to grow. O'Neill was blunt about the state she inherited. "Our performance over the past few years has not met our own expectations, let alone those of our shareholders," she said, adding that BP has "written off too much shareholder value" and needs to get "fit for the weight class we are in today." CFO Kate Thomson said BP is still chasing $3.5 billion in previously announced cost cuts that aren't "yet sufficiently visible in earnings."

Betting against the surge

The market's reaction told its own story: BP shares fell almost 4%, to $42.53, on a day the company beat profit forecasts — because investors read the guidance, not the headline number, as the signal of intent. A company confident that Hormuz-driven prices would hold, or that global oil demand had years of growth left, would be spending to pump more, not less. Instead BP is doing the opposite: shrinking its production base, selling refining and retail assets, and using a one-off pricing shock to accelerate a balance-sheet cleanup — it now expects to hit its $14–18 billion net-debt target ahead of schedule, with financial obligations down to $39–41 billion by year-end.

That is a specific kind of bet: that the Strait of Hormuz premium is temporary, that long-run demand growth is not something to build new production capacity for, and that shareholders would rather have a smaller, cleaner company than a bigger one leveraged to a conflict that could end at any time. Whether O'Neill's arithmetic holds depends largely on Tehran and Washington, not on BP.

The next test comes fast: the North Sea sale process is expected to conclude by year-end, and BP's third-quarter results — due in early November — will show whether the Hormuz premium survives into autumn or whether, as diplomatic efforts progress, oil prices resume their slide and vindicate a chief executive who declined to bank on the surge lasting.
