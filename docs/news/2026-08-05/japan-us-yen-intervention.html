Japan and US intervene to halt yen's 40-year slide

The Bank of Japan's head office in Tokyo's Nihonbashi district. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Japan and the US bought yen together for the first time since 1998 after a 40-year low, hours after Tokyo cut its growth forecast over oil prices.

For most of this year, Tokyo's response to a sliding yen has been rhetorical: officials would warn of "one-sided" moves, traders would shrug, and the currency would keep drifting toward levels not seen since before most active fund managers were born. That changed on July 31, when Japan's Ministry of Finance and the US Treasury moved together to buy yen in New York trading, after the currency touched roughly 164 to the dollar — a 40-year low. It was the first joint action by the two governments to prop up the yen since 1998, during the Asian financial crisis, ending a 28-year gap in coordinated support.

In one sentence: Japan and the US bought yen together for the first time since 1998 after a 40-year low, hours after Tokyo cut its growth forecast over surging oil prices.

A rare joint move

Japan's Finance Minister, Satsuki Katayama, confirmed on August 3 that her ministry had purchased yen in coordination with the US Treasury, which sold euros — not dollars — to help fund the operation. Central bank data suggest Tokyo spent somewhere in the tens of billions of dollars doing so; estimates of Friday's operation alone range as high as $59 billion. The yen jumped as much as 3.8% over two sessions, pulling the dollar down to around 155–156 yen from its earlier low.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent framed the move as a warning shot rather than a one-off: "Friday's coordinated foreign exchange actions countered disorderly yen movements," he said. "We will not hesitate to participate in further joint intervention." Katayama echoed the line almost word for word, telling reporters Tokyo and Washington were increasingly "aligned" on currency policy and prepared to act again. President Trump, asked about the operation, put it more plainly: "They have a weakening yen, and they wanted a little bit of help. And we're always there for Japan."

Not everyone was convinced by the mechanics. Routing the trade through euros rather than dollars struck several former officials as an odd way to defend a currency. Edwin Truman, who spent decades at Treasury, called it "weird," noting that selling a third currency is less direct than selling dollars outright. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution went further, warning that unconventional plumbing gives markets "reason to ask questions" about how serious the intervention really is. The skepticism matters because currency intervention works largely through signalling — traders who doubt the mechanism are likelier to test it again.

Oil, not just rates, behind the slide

The intervention landed one day after Japan's Cabinet Office cut its fiscal 2026 growth forecast to 0.9% from 1.3%, citing oil prices it now expects to average $92.5 a barrel, up from a January assumption of $68. The government also revised its own working yen forecast weaker, to 161.4 per dollar from 155.2 — effectively conceding, before the intervention, that it expected the currency's slide to continue. Japan imports nearly all its oil, so a weaker yen and higher crude prices compound each other: import bills were already climbing 25% in June, tipping the country's trade balance to a ¥406.9 billion deficit from a ¥122 billion surplus a year earlier.

That combination — a shrinking growth outlook and a currency in retreat — put the Bank of Japan in an uncomfortable spot. The BoJ raised its policy rate to 1% in June, the highest since 1995, and has since warned that core inflation is likely to run "clearly above" its 2% target. Normally, rate increases support a currency. This time they haven't been enough, partly because Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's expansionary fiscal stance — including a ¥3 trillion supplementary budget to cushion households from energy costs — has pushed 10-year Japanese government bond yields to multi-decade highs and revived doubts about debt sustainability. Interest payments on that debt are projected to climb from ¥13 trillion this fiscal year to ¥21.6 trillion by fiscal 2029, a trajectory that gives currency traders another reason to sell yen rather than buy it.

What the intervention does and doesn't fix

Currency intervention buys time, not direction. Mark Sobel, a 40-year Treasury veteran, called the move "unwise" unless Japan addresses the underlying causes of yen weakness — an interest-rate gap with the US that, even after June's hike, remains wide, and a fiscal position that keeps growing. Absent those fixes, he and others expect the yen to resume its decline once the immediate intervention pressure fades, much as it did after Japan's solo interventions in 2022 and 2024.

The stakes extend beyond Tokyo's currency desk. A yen that keeps weakening even after joint intervention would embolden the carry trade — investors borrowing cheaply in yen to fund purchases of higher-yielding assets elsewhere — the same dynamic that triggered a violent global unwind in August 2024 when the BoJ last surprised markets with a rate move. For exporters like Toyota and Sony, a weak yen still flatters repatriated profits, but it raises costs for the energy- and food-importing households and small manufacturers that make up the rest of the economy, which is precisely the squeeze behind this week's growth downgrade.

The next test comes with Japan's September GDP data and any signal from the BoJ on further tightening; a repeat slide back toward 160 would tell markets the intervention bought only a pause, not a floor.
