White House finalises AI evaluation framework, keeps contents secret

The West Wing of the White House, where the finalized AI evaluation framework was briefed to industry but not the public. — Geraldshields11 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The White House finished a voluntary AI safety review framework requiring 30-day pre-release model access — then said it won't publish what the review actually checks for.

The White House has finished building the rulebook that is supposed to determine whether the most powerful AI systems in the country are safe. It is not going to show anyone the rulebook.

On August 3, officials confirmed they had completed a voluntary framework requiring developers of "covered frontier models" — the most capable, closed-source systems, the ones judged to carry national-security risk — to hand the federal government access to a new model for up to 30 days before it reaches the public. The following day, staff from Meta, Nvidia, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic and a handful of smaller firms travelled to Washington for a walkthrough of the finished document. The public got no such briefing, and none is planned.

In one sentence: The White House finished a voluntary framework making AI companies submit powerful new models for a 30-day government review — then declined to say what the review actually checks for.

What the framework does — and doesn't — say

The document traces back to Executive Order 14409, signed June 2, which gave agencies 60 days to design a review process for frontier models. That deadline landed August 1. The order itself already flagged what would stay hidden: the cyber-benchmarking methodology used to test models, and the exact threshold a system has to cross before it counts as "covered," are both classified. What's new is that the White House has now decided the surrounding framework — the parts that were never classified — will also be withheld from public view.

A White House official put the logic bluntly to reporters: "Just because things are unclassified that doesn't mean we are going to broadcast them to everyone." The framework does lay out, at a general level, what participation involves — confidentiality protections, cybersecurity requirements, insider-risk controls, intellectual-property safeguards, and rules on how the government may use and must not disclose what it sees. It also explicitly rules out mandatory licensing or preclearance; a company that skips the review faces no penalty for releasing its model anyway. Open-weight models are carved out entirely, on the reasoning that once a model's weights are public, restricting it after the fact makes little sense.

So the shape of the process is public. The substance — what a model actually has to demonstrate, or fail to demonstrate, to pass — is not.

A 30-day handshake, dressed as oversight

The mechanics are already partly in motion. The Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), the Commerce Department body that replaced the Biden-era AI Safety Institute, has pre-deployment evaluation agreements with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Microsoft and xAI, and by early May had run more than 40 model evaluations, including on systems that were never released publicly. Anthropic pulled models offline in June for security hardening at the government's request; OpenAI coordinated with officials ahead of its July 9 release of GPT-5.6; Google submitted a model for review in July.

What none of that history answers is what "pass" means. A company submitting a model doesn't know, going in, which capabilities will trigger scrutiny, what score constitutes a red flag, or what happens if the government doesn't like what it sees — because the criteria live in a document nobody outside a small circle of officials and invited companies has read.

The credibility problem

Chris McGuire, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, called the decision "baffling": "We can't have secret, voluntary rules to regulate the most important tech in the world." The objection isn't really about classification — nobody disputes that cyberattack benchmarks are sensitive. It's about everything else. A safety review that the public can't inspect, evaluating standards the public can't read, applied to systems that will shape how millions of people work and communicate, asks for trust it hasn't earned the normal way — by showing its work.

The timing sharpens the point. The letter pushing back on the secrecy arrived weeks after OpenAI disclosed a breach involving Hugging Face and Anthropic reported three separate security incidents, prompting 15 organizations to sign a July 30 letter demanding an investigation. Critics, including Brad Carson of Americans for Responsible Innovation, have argued for years that public trust in AI oversight depends on the government demonstrating it holds companies to real, visible standards — not on companies and regulators agreeing privately that everything is fine.

For businesses weighing whether frontier tools are safe to deploy, and for the companies building them, the practical effect is uncertainty by design: nobody knows what "cleared the White House review" is actually supposed to mean until after they've submitted a model and found out. None of the six companies that sat through Tuesday's briefing has said anything on the record about what they saw.

What comes next

The framework is voluntary, which means its real test won't be a court challenge — it'll be whether major labs actually use it, and whether the government publishes anything at all once evaluations start running. Watch for the first model submitted under the new process, and for whether CAISI or the White House releases even a redacted summary of what a passing or failing review looks like. Until then, the framework's only firm public feature is the one everyone agrees on: it exists, and it's closed.
