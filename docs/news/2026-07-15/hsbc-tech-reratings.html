HSBC reshuffles tech coverage: downgrades IBM and Arm, doubles Intel target

IBM's Armonk, New York headquarters. HSBC cut the stock to Reduce the same day IBM posted its worst trading day since 1987. — Mark Hillary / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
HSBC downgraded IBM and Arm on valuation grounds while doubling its Intel target to $200, betting on a turnaround over two richly priced incumbents.

HSBC's technology analysts spent the past two weeks tearing up their coverage map, and the pattern that emerges is a coherent one: the bank is pulling money out of legacy enterprise IT and putting it into whichever chip or consulting story is either underpriced or actually delivering. On July 14, the same day IBM's stock suffered its worst single session since 1987, HSBC cut the century-old company to Reduce. A day earlier, HSBC had doubled its Intel price target. And on July 14 as well, it knocked Arm Holdings down to Hold. Three calls, one thesis: pay for growth you can see, not growth you're promised.

The IBM verdict, and a very bad day to deserve one

HSBC analyst Abhishek Shukla moved IBM to Reduce from Hold and cut his price target to $191 from $231 — implying roughly 34% downside from where the stock was trading before it fell further on its own. The timing looks almost choreographed. Hours after the note landed, IBM issued a preliminary Q2 warning: revenue of $17.2 billion against a Street estimate closer to $17.9 billion, and adjusted earnings of $2.93 a share, short of consensus. The stock dropped as much as 25% intraday, its steepest one-day decline on record, worse than the 23.7% slide of October 1987. Chief executive Arvind Krishna told investors plainly, "we faltered," pointing to deals that failed to close and a late-June scramble by clients to redirect capital toward supply-constrained servers, storage and memory ahead of expected price increases. IBM will give a fuller account, including updated full-year guidance, on its scheduled earnings call July 22.

Shukla's case, though, predates the miss and rests on valuation rather than a single bad quarter. IBM trades at 22.0 times its 2027 estimated non-GAAP earnings, against a sector median of 16.9 times, despite projected earnings growth of 10.7% a year through 2028 — about half the sector's 19.2%. To make the point concrete, HSBC built what it called a "synthetic IBM": for $287.56, an investor could buy a combination of SAP, Accenture, HP and IonQ shares that replicates IBM's exposure to software, consulting, hardware and quantum computing. HSBC projects that basket would generate 2030 earnings per share of $23.15, against $16.59 for IBM itself — a roughly 40% gap in the basket's favor. The quantum comparison is the most pointed piece of the note: IBM has logged only about $100 million in new quantum computing orders over the past five quarters, versus close to $600 million for IonQ. IBM's growth, Shukla wrote, is "less sustainable as it is dependent upon continued cost cutting," a contrast he draws directly against SAP and Accenture.

Doubling down on Intel

While downgrading IBM, HSBC has been getting more bullish elsewhere in hardware. On July 2, the bank raised its Intel price target to $200 from $100 — the highest on Wall Street — while keeping its Buy rating, and named Intel one of 15 stocks it sees as best positioned for AI-driven upside over the next five years. The call leans on two legs. First, server CPUs: HSBC lifted its 2026 shipment growth estimate to 25% year-over-year from 20%, and to 30% for 2027, pushing its data-center and AI revenue forecast to $24.1 billion, about 4% above consensus. Second, and newer to the thesis, is Intel's foundry business, which HSBC now folds into a sum-of-the-parts valuation and describes as "too good to ignore now," positioning Intel as an emerging alternative to TSMC as advanced packaging and front-end capacity tighten across the industry.

It is, by HSBC's own framing, a contrarian bet. Intel has spent the past several years ceding ground to Nvidia in AI accelerators and has slipped on its own process-node timelines under chief executive Lip-Bu Tan, who took over in March 2025. The stock has nonetheless run hard — up roughly 481% over the past year by HSBC's own figures cited in coverage of the note — which makes a doubled target read less like a discovery and more like a bank willing to chase a turnaround story that has already started moving. Intel reports second-quarter results July 23, the first real test of whether the foundry narrative shows up in numbers rather than slide decks.

Pumping the brakes on Arm

The third leg cuts the other way. HSBC analyst Frank Lee downgraded Arm Holdings to Hold from Buy on July 14, even as he raised his price target to $315 — a rare pairing that signals a valuation call rather than a change of heart on the business. Lee's argument is that Arm's push into "merchant server CPUs," competing more directly with Intel and AMD in data-center chips, remains a genuinely promising expansion, but that the market has already priced in more than the near-term numbers can support. He specifically cited foundry capacity bottlenecks as the binding constraint: "incremental foundry capacity allocation being the primary upside catalyst, which we believe is unlikely." At roughly 288 times forward earnings, Arm trades far above Nvidia's multiple, Lee noted, leaving "no room for operational errors or macro slowdowns." Shares fell more than 6% on the news.

The through-line

Set side by side, the three calls describe less a rotation out of "tech" than a rotation out of stories that have run ahead of what their next quarter can prove. IBM's valuation assumed a quantum and consulting franchise that, on HSBC's numbers, is losing share to smaller, more focused rivals. Arm's valuation assumed a server-CPU expansion unconstrained by the same foundry bottlenecks squeezing the rest of the chip industry. Intel's, conversely, has been depressed by years of missed execution — which is exactly the gap HSBC is betting closes as the foundry business matures. None of this is a verdict on the broader AI trade; it is a bank telling clients that price still matters even inside it. Arm reports fiscal first-quarter results July 29, one week after IBM's full Q2 call on July 22 and Intel's on July 23 — three data points in eight days that will show whether HSBC's repositioning was early or simply premature.
