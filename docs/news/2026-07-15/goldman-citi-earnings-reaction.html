Goldman surges on record profit, Citigroup slips despite strong quarter

Goldman Sachs' world headquarters at 200 West Street in Lower Manhattan. — Z4dude / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Goldman Sachs jumped 9% on a 78% profit surge while Citigroup fell 5% despite beating estimates, as investors priced in the cost of Citi's ongoing turnaround.

Wall Street's two biggest lenders posted the kind of quarters banks dream about — record profits, revenue beats across the board — and investors greeted them in almost opposite ways. Goldman Sachs shares jumped 9% on Tuesday to close at $1,140, a new 52-week high. Citigroup, reporting the same morning, saw its stock slide more than 5% even after beating earnings estimates by a wide margin.

Goldman's number was hard to argue with: net earnings of $6.63 billion, up 78% from a year earlier, on record quarterly net revenues of $20.3 billion. Earnings per share came in at $20.98, also a record, against an annualized return on equity of 23.5%. The engine was trading and dealmaking — equities revenue surged 86% to $6.0 billion, fixed income rose 6% to $6.1 billion, and investment-banking fees climbed 30% to $3.3 billion, the highest since 2021, helped by fee income tied to SpaceX's IPO. "Momentum has accelerated throughout our businesses," chief executive David Solomon told analysts, crediting the firm's ability to win mandates across trading, advisory and financing simultaneously. Goldman also returned $5.36 billion to shareholders in the quarter, including $4 billion in buybacks.

Citi's numbers were strong too — the market didn't care

Citigroup's quarter looked, on paper, nearly as good. Net income climbed 45% year-over-year to $5.83 billion, with EPS of $3.15 beating consensus by roughly 16%. Revenue was the bank's best in a decade, with double-digit growth in four of its five core businesses and 9% positive operating leverage — the kind of efficiency metric management has spent years chasing.

What investors focused on instead was everything layered underneath. Equities trading revenue, while at a record for Citi, grew more slowly than at Goldman or JPMorgan, and CFO Gonzalo Luchetti conceded the bank had "fallen behind in investing" there. Citi also flagged rising severance costs — roughly $800 million already booked in the first half of the year — tied to a workforce reduction of about 5,000 people, or 5% year-over-year, with chief executive Jane Fraser signaling more cuts and heavier AI-related spending to come. Full-year return-on-tangible-common-equity guidance held at 10-11%, unchanged, but the message that costs are rising before the payoff arrives was enough to send the stock down to $134.36 from $140.71.

The split reads as a verdict on trajectory rather than results. Goldman's quarter told investors the bank was already winning; Citigroup's told them a multi-year turnaround still has bills to pay before it shows up fully in the numbers. Both banks report again in October, when Citi's next update on headcount and equities-trading investment will show whether Fraser's restructuring is starting to close the gap — or widening it.
