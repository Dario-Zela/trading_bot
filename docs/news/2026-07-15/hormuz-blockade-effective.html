HORMUZ BLOCKADE · By C. Tanaka · 2026-07-15 · 6 min read

US Navy blockade of Hormuz shipping takes effect as strikes on Iran continue

The carrier USS Gerald R. Ford is part of an unusually large U.S. naval presence — more than 20 warships — now operating across the Middle East. — U.S. Navy photo by Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Jackson Adkins / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
U.S. resumes its Hormuz naval blockade at 4 p.m. ET alongside fresh strikes on Iran, sending Brent above $85 as Trump also floats a 20% shipping toll.

The blockade takes hold at 4 p.m. Eastern time this afternoon, and for the second time in three months the United States Navy will be turning ships away from Iranian ports and coastal waters in the Persian Gulf — a corridor that on an ordinary day carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. It is not, in the strictest sense, new: Washington ran an identical blockade from April 13 to June 18, before a fragile understanding with Tehran let it lapse. What is new is how fast that understanding came apart, and how much firepower the U.S. has now stacked behind reimposing it.

In the hours before the deadline, U.S. Central Command carried out what it described as an additional round of strikes on Iranian targets — language that matters, because it frames Tuesday's and Wednesday's bombing not as a fresh war but as the continuation of one that has been running, in bursts, since spring. The stated objective is narrow and specific: degrade the missile batteries, drone launch sites, radar installations and fast-attack boats that Iran has used to menace merchant shipping in the strait. The blockade and the strikes are, in the Pentagon's telling, two halves of the same operation — one closing the door, the other clearing the room behind it.

Why the guns are firing again

The immediate trigger was a wave of Iranian attacks on commercial vessels that began in the second week of July. The Cyprus-flagged container ship GFS Galaxy was hit on July 12, suffering severe engine-room damage; its crew abandoned ship, and one Indian sailor was later confirmed dead after the Omani navy recovered his body. Admiral Brad Cooper, the CENTCOM commander, has said Iran intentionally struck seven commercial vessels over a single week, leaving nearly a dozen civilian crew members dead, injured or missing, and that Iranian forces also fired dozens of missiles and drones toward neighboring Gulf states. "US forces are holding Iran accountable for unwarranted aggression that continues to endanger innocent lives," Cooper said.

The response has been the heaviest U.S. force posture in the region in more than two decades. Three carrier strike groups — built around the USS Gerald R. Ford, USS Abraham Lincoln and USS George H.W. Bush — are now in or near the theater, a level of concentration not seen since the opening of the Iraq war in 2003. CENTCOM's own count, posted to X as the blockade took effect, put the number at more than 20 Navy warships and hundreds of military aircraft operating across the Middle East. "American forces remain vigilant, lethal, and ready," the command's statement read.

What the blockade actually does

The mechanics carry over from the spring version. During the April–June run, CENTCOM says it redirected more than 140 compliant vessels away from Iranian ports, disabled nine ships that refused to comply, and waved through more than 50 vessels carrying humanitarian cargo. That is the model resuming Tuesday: not a wall across the strait itself, which would choke off Gulf Arab oil exports along with Iran's, but a checkpoint regime aimed specifically at traffic calling on Iranian terminals.

Layered on top of it is something with no real precedent — a demand for payment. President Trump has said the U.S. will now act as, in his words, "the guardian of the Hormuz Strait," and wants a 20 percent reimbursement on the value of cargo passing through, to be borne by the Gulf states that benefit from safe passage: Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain and Kuwait. At current freight and crude values, analysts estimate that toll could add on the order of $30–34 million to a single supertanker voyage, dwarfing the roughly $2 million transit charges Iran itself once levied on shipping in the strait. The International Maritime Organization has said there is no legal basis for such a mandatory toll, and the White House has not said how it would be calculated, billed or enforced — through shipowners, insurers, flag states or port authorities. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, offered a pointed rebuttal: whoever secures the strait should be paid something, he said, but "20 percent is of course too much. We will be fair."

The market is already moving

Oil did not wait for the 4 p.m. deadline. Brent crude, which had drifted back toward pre-war levels after the earlier ceasefire, has climbed back above $85 a barrel, up roughly 3 percent on the week as the strikes resumed. Traffic through the strait is described by analysts as grinding back down to — or below — the pace seen during the worst of the spring standoff. The scale of what is theoretically at stake is what makes the number worth sitting with: the Strait of Hormuz is barely 33 kilometers wide at its narrowest navigable channel, and the International Energy Agency has said a full closure would remove something like 10 to 14 million barrels a day from world markets, a shortfall with no historical precedent in the oil era.

Nobody is calling this a full closure yet — the blockade targets Iran-bound traffic, not the strait itself, and Gulf Arab crude is still moving. But shipping insurers do not price for "yet." War-risk premiums, which spiked and then eased during the ceasefire, are set to reprice again as underwriters mark the corridor back to active-conflict status, and that cost passes straight through to freight rates on every barrel and container that transits, not just the fraction bound for Iranian ports.

What comes next

The pattern from April holds a warning worth noting: the last blockade did not end because either side achieved a decisive result, it ended because a ceasefire arrangement briefly held — and that arrangement is precisely what unraveled this month when Iran resumed attacking merchant ships. The strikes CENTCOM is running now are explicitly framed as preparation for enforcement, not a one-off. The variables to watch from here are narrow and concrete: whether Tehran answers Tuesday's strikes with further attacks on shipping or on Gulf state territory, whether any of the five Gulf Arab states targeted by Trump's toll proposal actually pay it, and whether Brent's move above $85 proves to be the start of a sustained repricing or another spike that fades if the two sides again find their way back to a truce. The blockade itself is scheduled to run indefinitely, which means the next real data point is not a date on a calendar but the next Iranian response.
