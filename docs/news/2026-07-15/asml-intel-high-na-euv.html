ASML and Intel hit High-NA EUV production milestone on 18A node

ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven, Netherlands, where the company builds the High-NA EUV lithography systems now used in Intel's Oregon fabs. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
ASML and Intel shipped the first high-volume logic chip using High-NA EUV lithography on Intel's 18A node — matching, not yet beating, old-tool yields.

Intel's laptop chips are now, in part, made by the most expensive machine the semiconductor industry has ever built. ASML and Intel said Wednesday that a subset of Intel's new Core Ultra Series 3 processors — the chips marketed as Panther Lake — have shipped to customers with certain layers patterned using ASML's High-NA EUV lithography tool, on Intel's 18A manufacturing process. It is the first time the technology has been used in high-volume production of a logic chip anywhere in the world.

That sentence is dense with jargon, so it's worth unpacking before getting to why investors should care.

What actually happened

EUV lithography is the process of using extreme ultraviolet light to etch circuit patterns onto silicon wafers — it's how the transistors in a modern chip get drawn small enough to be useful. "High-NA" refers to a new generation of ASML's EUV machines with a larger lens aperture (0.55 versus 0.33 in standard tools), which lets the light resolve finer detail: roughly 8-nanometer features versus 13 nanometers on the older machines. Finer resolution means more transistors packed into the same sliver of silicon, which is the raw material of faster, more efficient chips — the kind Nvidia, AMD and everyone chasing AI workloads wants more of.

Intel installed the first commercial High-NA machine, ASML's Twinscan EXE:5000, at its Hillsboro, Oregon research fab in April 2024. It has since added the production-grade successor, the EXE:5200B, which pushes throughput to 175 wafers per hour and improves overlay accuracy — how precisely each patterning layer lines up with the one below it — to 0.7 nanometers. Wednesday's announcement is the first confirmation that this hardware has moved from lab experiment to something touching a real, shipping product. Intel says the 18A layers processed on the High-NA tool are now "dual-qualified" at Oregon, and yields on those layers have matched the older NXE-platform EUV tools they're meant to eventually replace.

"With increased resolution and better process control, the introduction of High NA EUV marks a substantial development in semiconductor lithography," said Christophe Fouquet, ASML's chief executive. Ann Kelleher, who runs Intel's technology development group, framed it as continuity rather than a leap: "Working closely with ASML, we will harness High-NA EUV's high-resolution patterning as one of the ways we continue Moore's Law and maintain our strong history of progression down to the smallest of geometries."

Matching yields, not exceeding them

The detail worth sitting with is that Intel is not claiming High-NA makes better chips yet — it's claiming the new tool can match what the old one already does, on a live production line, without wrecking yield. That is a real achievement, since a machine this different in its optics and stage mechanics could easily have introduced new defect modes. But it also means the milestone is about proving Intel can run High-NA reliably at all, not about the performance gains the technology is ultimately meant to deliver. Those gains are still ahead, expected to show up more meaningfully as Intel applies the tool more broadly on its next node, 14A.

The caution matters commercially, too, because the equipment is punishingly expensive. Industry estimates put the EXE:5200B at $380 million to $400 million per unit — the costliest tool ever built for chipmaking. That price is precisely why TSMC, the world's largest contract chipmaker and Intel's chief rival, has said it won't put High-NA into mass production until at least 2029. TSMC's co-chief operating officer, Kevin Zhang, told investors this year the company would rather keep extracting more from its existing EUV fleet through multiple patterning — running a wafer through the machine more than once — than absorb the cost of the new tool for its upcoming 2nm and A16 nodes.

Why it matters for the foundry bet

That divergence is the real story here. Intel has staked its comeback as a chipmaker on 18A and its successor 14A regaining the process leadership it lost to TSMC over the past decade, and on persuading outside customers to trust Intel's factories with their own chip designs — the foundry business CEO Lip-Bu Tan has called central to Intel's future. Being first to ship a High-NA product, even in a limited, layer-by-layer way, is a data point for that pitch: it says Intel's factories can run bleeding-edge tools in volume, not just in a lab. Intel's finance chief has said two prospective 14A customers are expected to make binding commitments in the second half of 2026 — a nearer-term test of whether milestones like this one actually move customers, or just move headlines.

The risk Intel is taking is the opposite of TSMC's. TSMC is betting patience on old tools saves money without costing it orders, since customers value proven yield over bragging rights. Intel is betting that being first — even at enormous capital cost, with ASML as a willing and financially interested partner — buys it credibility it can't otherwise afford to wait for. ASML, for its part, has every incentive to make this look like a triumph: it just raised full-year revenue guidance to €36 billion-€40 billion on the back of AI-driven demand, and a single flagship customer proving out its priciest machine is good marketing for the roughly 60 low-NA EUV units it still expects to sell this year, plus whatever High-NA orders follow.

The next checkpoint is less about lithography than about arithmetic: whether Intel can bring High-NA layers into 14A at a cost per wafer that outside customers are willing to pay, and whether those binding 14A commitments materialize in the second half of the year as promised. Until then, Wednesday's announcement is proof of concept, not proof of a business model.
