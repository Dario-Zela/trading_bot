Energy markets digest thinner-than-expected crude draw and Hormuz risk

Pumpjacks draw crude from the ground — even as U.S. inventory data shows stockpiles barely shrinking despite a Gulf-driven price rally. — Chris English / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Brent hit a five-week high on Hormuz blockade risk even as US crude stocks barely drew down, exposing a rally built on fear, not scarcity.

Oil traders got two signals this week, and they pointed in different directions. The geopolitical one screamed louder — but the physical one, buried in Wednesday's inventory data, is the one worth sitting with.

September Brent futures touched $85.92 a barrel, the highest since mid-June and up roughly 19% from levels seen before the U.S.-Iran conflict flared in late February. The proximate cause is the Strait of Hormuz, where President Trump this week reinstated a blockade on Iranian vessels and announced that other cargo transiting the waterway would face a 20% reimbursement fee — around $32 million for a single supertanker, against transit charges Iran itself had capped near $2 million. Traffic through the strait fell by more than half in the days that followed, to 57 transits over a single weekend versus roughly 130 vessels a day before the fighting resumed. Iran has declared the waterway closed "until further notice."

"Crude oil is fast losing its strategic petroleum reserve buffer," said June Goh, a senior oil analyst at Sparta Commodities, warning that "a violent repricing up cannot be discounted" if rhetoric doesn't cool. Bart Melek of TD Securities went further, putting a run toward $100 a barrel within reach if physical shortage risk starts to look real rather than theoretical.

A risk premium, not a demand story

Set against that, American Petroleum Institute data told a quieter and less flattering story. Commercial crude stocks drew down by just 564,000 barrels in the week ended July 10 — a fraction of the 2.7 million-barrel draw analysts had penciled in, and the second straight week the actual number badly missed consensus. The prior week's draw, 399,000 barrels, had itself come in well short of a forecast 1.5 million. Refiners are running hard into peak summer driving season, yet crude stockpiles aren't shrinking the way tight supply would demand.

The gap matters for how the current rally should be read. What's pushing Brent toward $86 is not evidence that barrels are scarce on the ground — it's the market pricing the probability that they could become scarce if Hormuz traffic stays throttled. That's a risk premium sitting on top of, not built from, the demand data. It can deflate as fast as it inflated if the two sides step back from the strait, and it can just as easily blow through $100 if a tanker is hit and stays hit.

Iberdrola's steadier ground

Away from crude, Spain's Iberdrola offered a reminder that not all of the energy complex is trading on headlines. The utility, Europe's largest by market value at more than €140 billion, raised its 2026 net income guidance to "double-digit" growth from a prior mid-to-high-single-digit range, lifted its interim dividend 8% to €0.404 a share, and reported first-half electricity distribution up 6.3% alongside roughly 2,300 megawatts of new generation capacity, mostly wind and solar. Shares were essentially flat on the week, up 0.38% Tuesday to €21.22 — a company being priced on delivered numbers rather than geopolitical scenarios, with full first-half results due July 22.

The split is the useful takeaway for anyone pricing energy exposure right now: crude is trading Hormuz, not barrels, while regulated utilities are trading fundamentals largely untouched by the Gulf. The next API and EIA prints, due the following Tuesday and Wednesday, will show whether the inventory pattern holds — and whether the strait stays as empty as it looked this weekend.
