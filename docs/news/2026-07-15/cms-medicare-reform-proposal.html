CMS proposes Medicare reforms aimed at accountable care and physician-payment overhaul

CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz, whose agency issued the proposed 2027 physician fee schedule rule on July 14, 2026. — CMS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
CMS's "transformational" Medicare press release masks a 2027 rule that actually cuts physician payment rates while sweetening ACO participation — a mixed signal, not a clean one.

The headline out of the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services this week reads like a State of the Union applause line: "transformational," a shift "from sick care to healthcare," a modernized system for the 68 million Americans on Medicare. The document underneath it is a 2027 physician fee schedule that, on the line that actually moves money, cuts payment rates.[^1]

That gap — between the press release's framing and the proposed rule's arithmetic — is the story here, at least for anyone trying to read this as a trading signal rather than a talking point.

The rule, formally CMS-1848-P, sets Medicare's 2027 physician conversion factor — the dollar multiplier used to price nearly every doctor visit and procedure billed to the program — at $33.17 for clinicians in qualifying alternative payment models, down 1.19% from 2026's $33.57. Physicians outside those arrangements fare worse: their conversion factor falls to $32.84, a 1.68% cut.[^2] Both figures include statutory raises of 0.75% and 0.25% respectively, plus a 0.53% adjustment CMS attributes to updated work-value estimates. All of that is outweighed by the expiration of a one-year, 2.5% bump that Congress wrote into the One Big Beautiful Bill Act for 2026 only. Take away a temporary sweetener and the underlying trend — rates essentially flat to declining against inflation — reasserts itself.

Where the reform label earns its keep

Set against that backdrop, the more interesting provisions are structural. CMS proposes phasing out traditional Merit-based Incentive Payment System reporting by the end of 2028, pushing clinicians toward specialty-specific "MIPS Value Pathways" — new tracks are proposed for diabetes, hypertension and hospital-based care.[^3] For accountable care organizations, the agency would raise the shared-savings rate for its top-performing BASIC track tier from 50% to 60%, while trimming a regional benchmark adjustment in the ENHANCED track from a maximum of 50% to 35%.[^2] A new billing modifier would pay ACO-affiliated practitioners 32% more for longitudinal evaluation-and-management visits — a real, if narrow, revenue lever for physician groups already inside Medicare's value-based programs.[^2] Starting in April 2027, newly approved ACOs would also gain authority to waive or reduce beneficiaries' out-of-pocket costs, a recruitment tool aimed at Medicare's roughly 476 existing ACOs, three-quarters of which shared in $4.1 billion of savings in 2024.[^2]

Reaction split along predictable lines. The American Medical Group Association said the conversion-factor update "falls well short of what it costs" members to deliver care, noting last year's increase only happened because Congress intervened. The American Academy of Family Physicians warned the reductions would "further destabilize physician practices," pointing to inflation-adjusted Medicare payments down 33% since 2001. The AMA flagged consolidation risk, saying the rule could make it "harder for independent practices to remain viable."[^3]

None of that supports the press release's cheerful framing, and none of it supports a clean short or long either — the rule cuts base rates while sweetening participation in value-based arrangements, a mixed bag that lands differently across hospital systems, physician-staffing companies and ACO-focused operators. The proposal is open for public comment through September 14, 2026, with a final rule typically following in early November ahead of the January 1, 2027 effective date. That comment window, and any lobbying-driven revisions to the conversion factor, is the next concrete data point worth watching — not Tuesday's headline.
