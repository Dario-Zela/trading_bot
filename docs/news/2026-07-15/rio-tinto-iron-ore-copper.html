Rio Tinto iron-ore shipments hit five-year high; copper cost guidance cut

A Rio Tinto shiploader loads iron ore for export in the Pilbara region of Western Australia. — CSIRO / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto's iron-ore shipments hit a five-year high, but its lower copper cost guidance owes more to a gold-price windfall than to cheaper mining.

Rio Tinto shipped more iron ore out of Western Australia last quarter than in any three-month stretch since 2020, and cut its cost forecast for copper at the same time. Read quickly, that looks like a diversified miner firing on every cylinder. Read closely, the two numbers are telling slightly different stories — one about ore actually moving through ports, the other about a gold price windfall dressed up as a copper story.

The numbers themselves are not in dispute. Pilbara shipments rose 7% year-on-year to 85.3 million tonnes in the June quarter, Rio Tinto said in its second-quarter production report released July 15. Combined with a recovery from cyclone disruption earlier in the year, that made for the strongest first-half haul out of the Pilbara since the company's 2018 record, with H1 sales up 5% to 157.7 million tonnes. Full-year guidance of 343–366 million tonnes was left unchanged — Rio Tinto is simply running ahead of the pace it needs, not resetting expectations.

"We are delivering growth as we drive performance across the group, with copper equivalent production up 3 per cent in the first half," chief executive Simon Trott said of the results.

Where the copper number actually comes from

The more interesting revision sits in copper. Rio Tinto now expects C1 net unit costs — the standard industry measure of cash cost per pound, after by-product credits — of 30 to 50 cents per pound for 2026, down sharply from the 65-to-75-cent range it had guided to previously. Copper output itself was unremarkable: first-half production rose just 1% to 442,000 tonnes, and the second quarter alone was down 7% year-on-year, even as Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia grew production 31% during its underground ramp-up.

What moved the cost line was not mining efficiency but gold. Oyu Tolgoi produces substantial gold alongside copper, and under C1 accounting, gold sales are credited against copper's cost base. Rio Tinto built its revised guidance on a gold price assumption of $4,026 an ounce, reflecting where spot trading sat at the end of June — a level that would have looked implausible a year ago and puts 2026's bullion rally squarely inside the company's copper economics. Copper itself has not been idle either: prices touched a record $6.11 a pound in January and LME contracts set fresh closing highs in May, so the underlying commodity backdrop is genuinely strong. But the specific cost-guidance cut Rio Tinto published this week is better read as a gold-credit adjustment than a signal that copper mining has become cheaper to run.

Why the distinction matters for allocation

For traders and models pricing mining-sector exposure, that distinction has consequences. Iron-ore strength is volume-driven and repeatable — more tonnes moved at broadly stable unit economics, with full-year guidance untouched. The copper cost improvement is price-driven and reversible: strip out an elevated gold assumption and the reported C1 range would look considerably less flattering, since underlying copper output actually contracted in the quarter. A model treating both revisions as equivalent evidence of operating momentum would be overweighting the copper side of Rio Tinto's book relative to what's structurally durable in it.

Rio Tinto shares rose alongside the report, and the broader read from analysts has been positive — UBS held a Neutral rating with a $177 target ahead of the release, and the market's reaction suggests investors have taken the headline numbers at face value. Lithium also had a strong quarter, up 20% to 14.6 kt LCE with early production from Sal de Vida and Fénix 1B ahead of schedule, adding a third data point to a report that, taken as a whole, favors volume and price momentum over any sign of cost discipline breaking down elsewhere in the portfolio.

The next test comes with half-year results in late July, when Rio Tinto discloses realized prices and margins rather than guidance ranges — the point at which the gold-credit effect in copper either shows up as durable cash flow or gets revealed as a forecasting artifact tied to a bullion price that has already proven volatile this year.
