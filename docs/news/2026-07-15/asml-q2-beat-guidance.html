ASML beats Q2 estimates and raises full-year guidance despite export curbs

ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven, the Netherlands, home to the world's only maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography systems. — A ansems / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
ASML beat Q2 estimates and raised full-year guidance to €43B–€45B, showing AI-driven chip-equipment demand is outrunning China export curbs.

Numbers first, then the caveats — that is how ASML likes to be read, and Wednesday's second-quarter report gave the market plenty of the former. The Dutch lithography monopolist posted net sales of €9.3 billion and net income of €2.9 billion, both comfortably ahead of what analysts had modeled, and — more importantly for a stock that trades on the future rather than the past — raised its full-year sales outlook to €43 billion–€45 billion, up from the €39 billion–€41 billion range it had guided to after the first quarter. It is the third upward revision this year.

That matters beyond Veldhoven. ASML makes the machines that print the circuitry onto every advanced chip on earth — there is no second supplier for its extreme ultraviolet (EUV) systems — which means its order book is one of the cleanest reads available on whether the AI-driven capital spending cycle in semiconductors is durable or already running hot.

In one sentence: ASML beat Q2 estimates, guided Q3 sales to €11.0B–€12.0B, and raised its full-year outlook to €43B–€45B — a signal that chip-equipment demand is outrunning the drag from export controls.

A beat that was mostly about upgrades, not new tools

The headline comparison is the one Wall Street will quote: EPS of $8.82 against a consensus estimate of $7.98, an 11% beat, on revenue of $10.84 billion against $10.28 billion expected. In euros, that was €9.3 billion in total net sales — up from €8.8 billion in the first quarter — with net income of €2.92 billion, well above the roughly €2.6 billion analysts had penciled in.

The composition of the beat is worth pausing on. ASML's own commentary attributed the outperformance largely to Installed Base Management — the servicing, upgrades and spare-parts business built around machines already sitting in customer fabs — which came in at €2.8 billion, stronger than expected. Gross margin landed at 54%, up from 53% in the first quarter, and the company sold 86 new lithography systems plus five used ones during the quarter, against 67 new systems in the prior period. None of that is as dramatic as a wave of fresh EUV orders, but it is the kind of high-margin, recurring revenue that gives management confidence to raise guidance rather than just clear a low bar.

That confidence showed up most clearly in the forward numbers. ASML guided third-quarter net sales to €11.0 billion–€12.0 billion, well above the roughly €10.1 billion consensus, with gross margin of 55%–57%. The lift to the full-year gross-margin outlook — now 54%–56%, versus a prior range in the low 50s — tells a story about pricing power as much as volume: customers are paying up for capacity, not just placing more orders at the same margin.

Export curbs are real, but AI demand is outrunning them

The guidance raise arrived alongside a genuine structural headwind. China's share of ASML's system sales has been sliding fast — from 36% of sales in the fourth quarter of 2025 to just 19% in the first quarter of 2026 — as Washington's export-control regime bites deeper into what Chinese fabs are permitted to buy. ASML now expects China to account for roughly 20% of 2026 revenue, down from about a third of sales in 2025. Pending U.S. legislation known as the MATCH Act would tighten restrictions further, this time reaching into the deep ultraviolet (DUV) tools that are ASML's higher-volume, lower-margin product line and had been less exposed to prior curbs.

That the company could absorb a swing of that size in one of its largest markets and still raise guidance says something about where the offsetting demand is coming from. CEO Christophe Fouquet described customers "accelerating capacity expansion plans," driven by AI-related investment, and the order data backs the framing: ASML plans to ship roughly 30% more low-NA EUV and DUV immersion tools in 2027 than the 2026 base of about 65 and 130 units respectively, and is studying a further 30% increase for 2028. Logic and memory customers — most visibly TSMC, Samsung and SK Hynix — are the ones filling the gap left by China, buying capacity for chips destined for AI data centers rather than consumer electronics.

The technology frontier is moving alongside the revenue mix. The same week as earnings, ASML and Intel confirmed that specific layers of Intel's 18A-node chips are now being produced using High-NA EUV — ASML's newest, most expensive class of machine, at roughly $360 million a unit — in a dual-qualified production line in Oregon. It is an early but concrete sign that the industry's next lithography generation is moving from lab demonstration into volume manufacturing, which is the point at which ASML's highest-margin equipment sales tend to show up.

What the market was pricing versus what it got

Going into Wednesday's release, options markets had priced in an outsized move — figures circulating ahead of the print ranged from roughly 8% to a claimed 7.5%, with some option-chain estimates as high as 8.36%, comparable to the volatility priced into other mega-cap reports this earnings season, such as Progressive and BNY Mellon, though those carried smaller implied swings in the 3.9%–4.1% range. The spread among estimates reflects how unusually binary the setup was: shares had already rallied more than 65% year-to-date and had gained roughly 3.5% intraday in the session before earnings, so a beat without a guidance raise risked a sharp reversal, while a third consecutive upward revision — which is what materialized — gave the rally a fundamental floor rather than just sentiment.

ASML also confirmed capital returns alongside the results: an interim dividend of €1.88 per share, payable August 5, and €1.1 billion in share buybacks during the quarter under its 2026–2028 program.

The read for the rest of the sector

The throughline across the quarter is that ASML is proving two things can be true simultaneously: geopolitical fragmentation is shrinking one large customer base, and AI-driven capital spending is more than replacing it, at better margins. For the rest of the semiconductor supply chain, that is the more useful signal than the earnings beat itself — capex intentions at the equipment layer tend to lead the industry's actual output by several quarters. The next checkpoints are ASML's third-quarter print, due in October, and whatever additional detail emerges from Washington on the MATCH Act's scope, which will determine how much further the China number has to fall before AI-linked demand has fully backfilled it.
