White House launches 'Gold Eagle' initiative for cybersecurity vulnerability coordination

In one sentence
The White House launched Gold Eagle, an AI-driven clearinghouse run with Treasury, DHS and the Pentagon to speed discovery and patching of software vulnerabilities.

The White House on Monday rolled out a new clearinghouse meant to speed up how the government and private industry find and fix software vulnerabilities — before criminals or hostile states get to them first. It's called Gold Eagle, and its defining feature is that much of the vulnerability-hunting is being done by artificial intelligence rather than human researchers.

The initiative, built jointly by the Treasury Department, the Department of Homeland Security's Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, and the Department of War, gives federal agencies and critical-infrastructure operators a single channel to report, verify and prioritize flaws discovered by AI scanning tools. It runs on VINCE — the Vulnerability Information and Coordination Environment — an existing coordinated-disclosure platform built by Carnegie Mellon University's Software Engineering Institute and best known as the backbone of the CERT Coordination Center's bug-reporting system.

"Treasury, along with our partner agencies, will continue to harness frontier AI capabilities to stay ahead of our adversaries," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in the announcement. Treasury is managing day-to-day operations of the clearinghouse.

Why AI changes the math

The program traces back to an executive order President Trump signed on June 2, which directed federal agencies to work with AI developers on secure deployment of advanced models — and pushed those companies to give the government early access to systems capable of finding software flaws. The order came after Anthropic disclosed that an unreleased model it calls Mythos, developed under an effort named Project Glasswing, had gotten good enough at code analysis to find thousands of high-severity vulnerabilities on its own, including bugs in every major operating system and browser. Anthropic has since expanded access to Mythos to roughly 150 organizations in more than 15 countries, backed by up to $100 million in usage credits, and says partners have already surfaced more than 10,000 high- or critical-severity flaws.

That capability cuts both ways. A model that can spot a serious flaw in a piece of infrastructure software can, in principle, also be used to find and weaponize one. Gold Eagle is the administration's attempt to make sure the defensive side of that equation moves at least as fast as the offensive side — collecting AI-discovered vulnerabilities from companies and researchers, verifying them, and routing prioritized fixes to the agencies and firms that need to patch them.

Officials talk up urgency, details stay thin

War Secretary Pete Hegseth called Gold Eagle "the vanguard of America's cyber defense," while DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said the partnership would "expand existing security measures," and National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross framed it as working alongside "America's brightest innovators." A White House official described the scale of vulnerability discovery underway as a "step function change" from prior years.

What the administration has not said is nearly as notable as what it has. Monday's announcement did not specify which agency runs daily operations, how many vulnerabilities have been processed so far, which companies besides Anthropic are contributing models or data, or whether participation — and patching — is mandatory for any of the critical-infrastructure operators involved. Michael Daniel, a former White House cybersecurity coordinator, noted that policymakers are still working out whether AI-discovered threats genuinely require different defensive playbooks than the vulnerability-management processes already in place, or whether Gold Eagle is mostly adding speed to a system that already exists in the form of CISA's disclosure program, the CVE system and NIST's vulnerability database.

The test of Gold Eagle will be whether it produces verified patches faster than the status quo, not whether it produces press releases faster. The next marker to watch is whether Treasury or CISA publishes any operational data — vulnerabilities processed, patches shipped, participating firms — that would let outsiders judge whether the "step function" is real or just rhetoric.
