Trump unveils 'Super Intelligence Force' to oversee AI policy

In one sentence
Trump's new 'Super Intelligence Force', led by Jay Clayton, has a 120-day reporting task but no stated budget or enforcement powers, so market impact looks limited.

The White House has created a new body to coordinate federal AI policy and given it a name borrowed from science fiction: the "Super Intelligence Force". Announced on 4 October, it has a named leader, a short list of cabinet-level members and a 120-day reporting deadline. Nothing in the published accounts gives it a budget line, regulatory authority or any way to compel a company or agency to do anything.

What was announced

Donald Trump said Jay Clayton, the Director of National Intelligence, will lead the force. According to a summary of the announcement by Straight Arrow News, its members include Vice President JD Vance, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and chief of staff Susie Wiles. Its stated job is to coordinate government engagement with AI developers, consumers, religious organisations and infrastructure providers.

It has to report within 120 days on AI risks, opportunities, security vulnerabilities and how breaches should be reported. That is a deliverable, not a power. A report due in early February can recommend rules, but it cannot make them.

Trump also signed an executive order directing federal agencies to say "super intelligence" instead of "artificial intelligence". His reasoning, as reported, was that "artificial" makes the technology sound "fake, and it is not fake, it's actually amazing." Clayton's own line was more conventional: "The risk of not being first is high."

The Washington Post framed the launch as coming after calls for an AI slowdown. That suggests the body is as much a political answer to safety critics as a regulatory step. The claim rests on that framing and on the thin remit, not on any stated administration motive.

Why the market impact looks limited

Coordinating bodies of this kind have a mixed record. They tend to hold meetings, commission reports and rely on the agencies that already have the legal authority. Without money or enforcement tools, they lose influence once the political attention moves elsewhere or budgets tighten.

What would change the picture:

  • Budget authority. A funding line, or a say over federal AI procurement, would give the body leverage over suppliers.
  • Binding rules. Reporting requirements for security breaches, for example, would turn the 120-day review into obligations for companies.
  • Chip and export policy. The members include the Treasury and Defense secretaries. If the force starts shaping those decisions, that would matter more to valuations than the name does.

None of these is in the announcement. Clayton's background is in securities law and intelligence, not the technology industry, which critics have noted. That may suit a security-focused review, but it says little about how much the body will shape policy.

The backdrop is nervous. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey said this week that "everybody is currently priced to be a winner" as AI-linked debt nears $450bn. A Washington body with no powers is unlikely to move prices on its own. A later move that gives it some could.

The rebrand has a side effect

The rename has already had a small commercial effect. BBC Technology reported a spike in demand for Slovenian domain names. The country's ".si" suffix looks like the start of "super intelligence", which has sent buyers looking for addresses. We could not independently confirm the scale of that demand.

What to watch

The first marker is the 120-day report, due around early February. The more important test comes sooner: whether the White House attaches funding, staff or a legal mandate to the force, or whether the order stays a renaming exercise.
