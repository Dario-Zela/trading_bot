Hamilton bemused as F1 timing glitch turns race into a 'Lego race'

In one sentence
A software fault that left cars stalling in the wet at Sepang overshadowed the race; Hamilton, who finished third, called it 'like the Lego race' as the FIA pushed a fix and promised a review.

The race was supposed to be the story. Instead, a software fault that left cars crawling and stalling in the wet at Sepang took over, and Lewis Hamilton, who finished third, was left describing the afternoon as 'like the Lego race'.

The glitch was in the cars, not the television broadcast. The fault hit the power-unit software and affected several drivers during the formation laps behind the safety car. Cars that should have been rolling at a steady pace instead lost power or struggled to move at normal speed.

What happened

Hamilton said he had to stop on the track, switch off the car and the engine, and restart it. 'I've never done that before,' he said. At one point five cars were reported side by side on the straight, with drivers unable to work out what was happening ahead of them.

The 'Lego race' line is a reference to the battery-powered Lego F1 cars that took part in a promotional show before the British Grand Prix in July. Max Verstappen, who is reported to have won the race, used the same comparison and put the cause down to complexity. 'These engines are so complicated and a lot of software is implemented,' he said.

The cause

According to the reports, the FIA traced the fault to an unusual combination of conditions: low engine speeds, low grip, the energy-management settings used in the wet, and the layout of one part of the Sepang circuit. Together these triggered an unintended loss of power in several cars.

The governing body issued updated software during the rain delay. It removed the energy limit in the affected sectors before the race resumed. Reports say the FIA, teams and power-unit manufacturers will now review why the problem was not caught earlier. Rain-affected running has been scarce this season, which would explain how a wet-weather combination went untested.

Why it matters, and why not very much

The fault was fixed within the same event, and nothing in the accounts suggests the result was distorted. A wet race restarted after a delay with corrected software is an ordinary, if embarrassing, outcome.

The more lasting point is about the 2026 rules. The current engines lean heavily on software to manage electrical energy deployment. That makes them more exposed to rare combinations of conditions like these than the old hardware-dominated units were. One bug on a wet afternoon does not show a systemic flaw. It does show that the software now needs wet-weather testing as seriously as the engines themselves.

The review is the next thing to watch. If it concludes the fault was a one-off gap in wet-weather testing, the story ends here. If it finds other untested conditions, the teams will want answers before the next rainy race weekend.
