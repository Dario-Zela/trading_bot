Government 'complacent' on food as a national security issue, report warns

In one sentence
More than 150 experts tell Andy Burnham the UK has no plan to feed people in a crisis and is complacent about food as a national security issue.

Britain has no plan for how to feed its people in a prolonged crisis, and the government is "complacent about food as a national security matter". That is the warning in an open letter from more than 150 experts, addressed to Andy Burnham and reported by the Guardian on 5 October. Its signatories include academics, business leaders, community groups and local food partnerships. They want food treated as a national security issue, in a period when supplies "can be weaponised".

The letter, and a 14-page report that accompanies it, is not a market-moving document in itself. It sets out a structural argument about how the UK feeds itself. That argument matters for commodity and food-sector exposure, because a shift in policy would change who holds stocks, who bears the costs and where Britain buys its food.

Where the exposure sits

The report stresses how much the UK depends on global markets for food and fertiliser. Britain imports around 40 per cent of its food. About a quarter of that comes from Europe. The country is "heavily reliant" on imports for fresh fruit, vegetables and sugar, and its animal farming depends on soy imported from South America.

The signatories name several threats to the food chain: climate shocks, geopolitical disruption, cyber-attacks and ransomware, energy outages and software failures. They also point to the way the system is built. Supermarkets and suppliers run on just-in-time delivery, with little stock held in reserve. A small number of large companies dominate the system. And, they say, the UK has no national food store, since its strategic stockpile was dismantled in the 1990s.

This is a general case about fragility and concentration, not a list of named single-supplier failures. The report does not, in the material available, name a specific trade route or supplier as the likeliest point of failure. Even so, the concerns line up with problems already visible elsewhere. UK diesel has crossed £2 a litre on fuel-supply worries, and a just-in-time distribution network runs on diesel.

The government's position

Ministers do not dispute that disruption is possible. The Cabinet Office is launching a household stockpiling campaign, and a "Prepare" website carries emergency guidance. Angela Eagle, the Environment Secretary, has recommended that households keep several days of food and water in case of power or utility failures. She is also overseeing farming improvements and reservoir-building.

The signatories' criticism is that this puts the burden on individual households rather than on the system. Advice to keep a few days of supplies does not address who holds national reserves or how imports would be rationed or rerouted.

Prof Tim Lang of the Centre for Food Policy at City St George's, University of London, said: "We need a system shift: grow more food; more regionalism, diversity and storage at multiple levels." Prof Paul Behrens of the Oxford Martin School said: "Food resilience, healthy diet and environmental benefits all point in the same direction."

What the experts want

The recommendations are institutional rather than market-facing. They include:

  • local food resilience committees;
  • an audit of import dependency and corporate concentration;
  • production realigned to nutritional needs;
  • storage schemes at several levels, rather than a single central reserve;
  • a more decentralised food supply.

The letter also notes that millions of people cannot afford a healthy diet. It treats affordability as part of resilience, not a separate issue.

What to watch

The test is whether the government moves from household advice to anything that changes costs for the sector. An import-dependency audit, a stockpiling requirement or support for domestic production would each reprice risk for importers, retailers and fertiliser and feed suppliers. None has been announced. The next marker is the government's response to the letter, and whether food resilience appears in any wider national security review. Tighter fuel and bond-market conditions would make the funding of such measures harder.
