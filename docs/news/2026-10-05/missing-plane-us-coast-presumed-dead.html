Missing plane off US coast: all six aboard presumed dead

In one sentence
The Coast Guard has suspended its search for a Canadian air ambulance that vanished between Bermuda and Boston, and all six aboard are presumed dead.

A Canadian air ambulance that vanished on its way from Bermuda to Boston has been given up for lost. The US Coast Guard suspended its search at sunset on Sunday, 66 hours after the Gulfstream G100 dropped off radar. All six people aboard are presumed dead.

The flight, callsign Heartbeat 104, was a medical transfer. According to NBC News, it was operated by Canada-based Latitude Air Ambulance. Four Canadian crew members were aboard, along with two Bermudians: a patient and the patient's son, who was travelling as a family escort. The jet left L.F. Wade International Airport in Bermuda in the small hours of Saturday and was due at Boston Logan before 1:30 a.m.

It never arrived.

The last minutes

The Federal Aviation Administration lost contact with the aircraft as it descended over the Atlantic, south of Nantucket. NBC reports the jet dropped from 24,000 feet to about 11,000 feet before contact was lost. ABC News, citing air traffic audio, reports the pilot had described electrical problems and declared an emergency. Aviation analyst Mary Schiavo told ABC the recordings suggest the aircraft was "coming in blind" as a storm moved across its path. That is informed speculation rather than a finding. Investigators have not said what went wrong.

Searchers later found a debris field roughly 20 miles off Nantucket. Coast Guard fixed-wing aircraft, helicopters, boats and cutters combed the water, supported by Massachusetts authorities and the New York National Guard. Reports differ on how large the search area was, and on exactly when contact was lost, so those figures are best treated as approximate.

Why the search stopped

Rear Adm. Matthew Lake, whose district ran the operation, said ending a search was never a simple call.

"Suspending a search is not an easy decision. We express our deepest condolences to the families."

The debris sits in comparatively shallow water, ABC reports 80 to 90 feet. That makes recovery of the wreckage, and of the flight recorders, plausible. The Coast Guard had said it planned to use sonar to scan the seabed. NBC says no black box has yet been located.

What comes next

The US National Transportation Safety Board is expected to lead the inquiry, with Canada's Transportation Safety Board taking part, since the aircraft was Canadian-registered and the crew Canadian. The first thing to watch is whether the wreck and its recorders are found. They would show whether an electrical failure, the weather, or both brought the jet down. An NTSB preliminary report would normally follow within a few weeks.

Air ambulance flights carry people already in fragile health, over open water, often at night. When one is lost, it is a loss for the families and for the medical-transport crews who fly the same routes.
