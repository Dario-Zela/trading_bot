Money stolen through hijacked UK social media accounts is up 400%

In one sentence
Reported UK losses from hijacked email and social accounts rose 417% to £6.3m, though police say much of the jump reflects a new reporting system.

Money reported stolen through hijacked UK email and social media accounts rose by more than 400% in a year. The headline figure is large. The base it rises from is small, and the police who published it say part of the jump is a change in how fraud gets recorded.

Report Fraud, the national service run by the City of London Police, logged £6.3m in losses from account hacking in the year to 31 March 2026. The previous year's figure was £1.2m, a rise of about 417%. The number of victims reporting a financial loss rose 929%, from 226 to 2,325. The Guardian first reported the figures.

A reporting change, not only a crime wave

The caveat matters for anyone drawing conclusions about banks or platforms. Report Fraud replaced Action Fraud in January 2026, after years of criticism of the older system. According to The Record, 92% of the account-hacking reports that involved a financial loss were recorded in the second half of the financial year, which overlaps with the switch. Police have said much of the increase reflects changes in reporting rather than a sudden fivefold rise in attacks.

The totals are also incomplete by construction. They rely on voluntary reports, and victims often stay silent out of embarrassment or because the sum involved is small. The real total is likely higher, and the true year-on-year change is unknown.

How the scam works

The typical case does not involve a thief draining the hijacked account's owner. A criminal takes over an email or social media profile, then poses as the owner to people who trust them. Fake event tickets are the most common bait. The Guardian cited a music fan whose Instagram account was used to advertise Oasis tickets, costing her friends £1,400. A "Hi mum" variant runs the same play over WhatsApp or text.

The victims who lose money are therefore often the friends and relatives who pay, not the person whose account was taken. Ch Supt Amanda Wolf of Report Fraud said that for most people "being hacked isn't just a cyber issue – it's personal", and that it can "quickly impact family, friends and colleagues as fraudsters exploit trusted relationships." Report Fraud recommends passkeys as a defence.

Where liability sits

The sources reviewed do not describe a new dispute over who pays. The reported material gives no evidence that liability is shifting to customers. The context is existing UK rules. Since October 2024, the Payment Systems Regulator's regime has required banks and payment firms to reimburse authorised push payment fraud, up to £85,000 per claim, with the cost split between the sending and receiving firms. Social media companies sit outside that scheme. Payments made to a scammer posing as a friend can fall inside it, but the victims in these cases are individuals who made a payment themselves, and the bank's treatment of a claim will turn on the facts.

That gap is the regulatory exposure. Lenders carry the reimbursement cost for fraud that begins on platforms that bear none of it. Banks, including Santander, which supplied data for the Guardian's report, have argued for years that platforms should contribute. A rise in reported cases, even one inflated by better recording, adds to the case for that argument.

What to watch

The numbers to watch are the City of London Police's next Report Fraud figures, which will show whether reported losses stay near £6m on a stable reporting system. Next are any moves by the regulator or government to bring platforms into the reimbursement framework. Until a full year of Report Fraud data exists, the 400% rise says more about measurement than about the scale of the threat.
