How India became dependent on Chinese imports

In one sentence
China supplies about 15% of India's imports, concentrated in pharma ingredients, electronics and energy inputs, and diversification has so far lagged the political rhetoric.

India's factories, pharmacies and phone-assembly lines run on parts that mostly come from one neighbour, and that neighbour is also its largest strategic rival. China supplies roughly 15% of India's imports by value. That figure understates the exposure, because the goods concerned sit upstream of things India makes and sells itself.

The trade gap shows the imbalance. India's merchandise deficit with China has run at around $85bn–$100bn a year in recent fiscal years, on imports of about $100bn and exports of only $15bn or so. Indian shipments to China are mostly raw materials and commodities. Chinese shipments to India are mostly finished components, machinery and chemicals.

Where the dependence sits

The concentration is uneven, and it is heaviest in the inputs that are hardest to replace.

  • Pharmaceuticals. India is a large generic-drug exporter, but it is widely estimated to source around 70% of its active pharmaceutical ingredients from China. The finished pill is Indian; the chemistry behind it often is not.
  • Electronics. Government incentive schemes have drawn smartphone assembly to India. The displays, batteries, circuit boards and other sub-assemblies are still largely imported from China, so much of the work in India is final assembly.
  • Energy and industrial inputs. Solar cells and modules, lithium-ion cells, specialty chemicals and machine tools follow the same pattern. China's tightened export licensing for rare-earth magnets in 2025 showed how quickly a single chokepoint can stall Indian automakers.

The pattern shows in the cost structure as well as the volumes. Chinese suppliers hold scale and price advantages that new suppliers in Vietnam, Taiwan or India itself have not yet matched. Buyers can find a second source, but usually at a higher unit cost.

Diversification, slowly

New Delhi has tried to respond. Production-linked incentive schemes in electronics, pharma and batteries pay manufacturers to build domestic capacity. Border clashes since 2020 have led to app bans, tighter scrutiny of Chinese investment and anti-dumping duties on a growing list of products.

The results are mixed. Import volumes from China have kept rising even as policy has turned more hostile, which suggests rhetoric has outrun substitution. Building an ingredient plant or a component ecosystem takes years, and the gap is bridged in the meantime by buying more from the same supplier.

What the exposure means for markets

The channels to markets are fairly direct. If friction hardens into tariffs, quotas or informal export curbs, Indian manufacturers with thin margins would face higher input costs they cannot easily pass on, and that falls hardest on electronics assemblers, drugmakers and solar developers. A larger import bill in dollar terms would also add pressure on the rupee through the current account, which is already sensitive to oil prices.

The risk runs the other way too. China depends on India as a large consumer market, and that mutual dependence has so far kept escalation short of a full rupture. Even so, the cost of a miscalculation falls unevenly: Indian firms have few alternatives, while Chinese exporters can redirect sales elsewhere.

The next signals will be in the trade data and in policy. Monthly customs figures will show whether the China share of imports is falling. Any change to export-licensing rules in Beijing, or to the anti-dumping docket in New Delhi, would show whether the two governments are tightening or easing.
