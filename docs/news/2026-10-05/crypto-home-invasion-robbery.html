Crypto thieves attack man at home in 'horrific' robbery

In one sentence
Masked men beat a Solihull businessman with hammers to force a crypto transfer, a violent case that shows personal exposure but, on its own, no sign of a wider or systemic risk.

A businessman in Solihull, in the West Midlands, was beaten with hammers in his own home while masked men demanded he hand over his cryptocurrency, according to an account he and his wife gave the BBC. The couple, who have asked to remain anonymous and are identified only as "James" and his wife, called the attack "horrific". She was seven months pregnant at the time.

What happened

The attack took place in December 2025. Three masked men forced their way into the house and spent about 45 minutes assaulting James, striking him on the face, head and ribs with hammers. One of the men held his wife down on a sofa and threatened her and the unborn child.

The detail that stands out is the fourth participant. The intruders were taking instructions from someone on a live FaceTime call, who told them to go through the phone. "I can hear the guy on FaceTime saying: 'Show me everything on his phone. Show me what apps he's got,'" James told the BBC. Under duress he transferred what was reported as hundreds of thousands of pounds of cryptocurrency. The men also took several Rolex watches. The exact sum has not been published.

As the men left, James heard the caller tell his accomplices: "You can have 10 grand each but I am taking the rest." That suggests a division of labour: a coordinator who knows what to look for on a phone, and hired muscle who take the physical risk for a fixed fee.

No arrests have been reported and the men have not been identified. Police believe they were in their late teens or early twenties. Crimestoppers has offered a £10,000 reward for information.

Wrench attacks, and what the numbers say

The industry calls this kind of crime a "wrench attack": no hacking, just physical coercion of the person who holds the keys. The name comes from a well-worn security joke in which an attacker skips the encryption and simply threatens the owner.

The case shows why crypto is attractive to robbers. A bank can reverse a transfer; a blockchain transaction generally cannot be undone, and the funds can be moved on within minutes. That makes the victim's phone, and the victim, the weak point.

But one case does not establish a trend. The figure circulating in reporting, attributed to Chainalysis, is about $30m stolen through such attacks worldwide in 2026 up to June, with France accounting for the most incidents after a tax-office data breach. Set against the industry's far larger losses to hacks and scams, that is small. It is also a figure from third-party summaries; the original research was not checked for this article. Nor is it clear how the Solihull attackers picked James, or whether they chose him for his crypto or for general signs of wealth. The reported FaceTime direction suggests at least some knowledge of what they were after.

What it does and does not change

This does not touch markets or institutional custody. Nothing here suggests an exchange, a custodian or a protocol failed. The exposure is personal: a visible holding, a phone with the apps on it, and a home address that can be found.

The standard advice follows from that. Do not advertise holdings. Keep a large balance behind delays or multi-person approval, so that no single phone can empty it under duress. Some wallets and exchanges offer withdrawal time-locks for this purpose. None of it helps if an attacker already knows who to target, which is why data leaks, as in the French case, matter more than any single wallet setting.

What would turn this from an isolated crime into a pattern is evidence of organised targeting in the UK, such as repeated cases with a shared coordinator, or leaked customer lists. The West Midlands investigation, and whether the caller on that FaceTime link is identified, is the next thing to watch.
