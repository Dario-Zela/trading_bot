Tory conference: tax and regulation cuts, scrapping the Environment Agency, and a pitch to gilt-wary markets

In one sentence
The Conservatives used conference to pledge tax cuts and to scrap the Environment Agency and Natural England, even as gilt yields sit at multi-decade highs and the sums stay untested.

The Conservative Party opened its annual conference with a package that mixes tax cuts, deregulation and the abolition of two environmental regulators. It did so while the UK government borrows at its highest cost in decades. In the gilt market, that combination is closely watched. The last time a UK government promised large unfunded tax cuts into a nervous bond market, in September 2022, the result was a sterling slide and a Bank of England intervention.

The party is trying to avoid that comparison. Its message is that cuts will be paid for. The market question is whether investors accept that.

The backdrop: expensive government debt

The 30-year gilt yield is above 6% and the 10-year is near 5.5%, according to this edition's markets coverage. Both are well above the roughly 5.0% and 5.7% seen in late July. Higher yields mean the Treasury pays more to refinance debt, and they cut the headroom a chancellor can use for tax cuts, whoever holds the job. A party in opposition has no budget to constrain it, but it does need to be believed.

Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, had signalled she would pursue what she called "scrapping taxes". Her plans in this area have included stamp duty and business rates. The party has said abolishing stamp duty would cost about £9bn by the end of the decade. It says the cost would be met from a £47bn pot of savings, drawn from welfare cuts, a smaller civil service and further reductions in foreign aid. Triage notes for this piece also point to hints on inheritance tax and on ending the £100,000 income threshold at which the personal allowance is withdrawn. The effective marginal rate there is about 60%, and critics of the rule call it a cliff edge. Detail on both was limited, and neither has been costed publicly in the material reviewed.

Note on attribution: some early summaries described Badenoch as shadow chancellor. She is party leader. The shadow chancellor is a separate role.

Scrapping the regulators

The most concrete structural pledge is to abolish the Environment Agency and Natural England, with their functions folded into the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs. The party argues the two bodies hold up housebuilding and add cost to projects. It says reducing headcount at the Environment Agency and bodies such as Arts Council England could save £1.6bn by the end of the decade.

For utilities and developers, fewer overlapping approvals would in principle lower compliance costs. But the pledge depends on winning an election. No timeline or replacement framework has been set out, and moving regulatory functions into a government department raises its own questions about independence and enforcement, particularly in water. For investors, the effect on water companies and housebuilders is speculative until those details appear. The party has also faced internal friction over its wider environmental stance, including reported opposition among members to its net zero plans and criticism from the former prime minister Theresa May of a plan to repeal the Climate Change Act.

'Classic old-school Conservative'

Badenoch described herself at conference as a "classic old-school Conservative". The phrase does little to settle policy. Its context is pressure from Reform UK on the party's right flank, which pushes the Conservatives towards bolder tax and regulatory promises while keeping them tied to a fiscal-responsibility label that bond investors look for. Those aims can pull in different directions. Tax cuts are popular with the voters Reform is courting. Gilt investors want to see offsetting savings that are specific and credible.

What the market will look for

There is no direct evidence yet that conference announcements moved gilts. Opposition pledges rarely do on their own. The more useful signals are indirect. One is whether the party publishes costings that an independent body such as the Institute for Fiscal Studies or the Office for Budget Responsibility can test. Another is whether the "savings" from welfare and civil-service cuts look achievable. A third is how long yields stay at these levels, which sets how much room any future chancellor would have.

The next fixed points are the government's own fiscal statements and the Bank of England's rate decisions, which will shape gilt pricing more than any conference speech. If the Conservatives want their pitch taken seriously by markets, the test is whether the sums hold up when someone outside the party checks them.
