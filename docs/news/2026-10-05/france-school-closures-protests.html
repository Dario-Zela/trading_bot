France closes up to 500 schools as student protests spread

In one sentence
France is closing up to 500 schools as student protests over class sizes and teacher shortages spread, adding a public-order test to a government already squeezed on budgets.

France is shutting up to 500 schools on Monday, fully or partially, as a wave of student protests that began in mid-September shows no sign of burning out. The government calls the closures a precaution. Protest organisers say they are a sign that ministers have not answered the complaints that started the movement.

Education Minister Edouard Geffray announced the measure on Sunday, framing it as a route to a "partial, progressive return to lessons". Closing a school to restore order is a blunt instrument. It also suggests that officials cannot guarantee safety at the gates.

How it started

The movement began on 17 September at a high school in the Paris region. It spread through the lycées, the French upper-secondary schools, on grievances that are mundane and specific: large classes, teacher shortages and absences, school days that students consider too long, and dilapidated buildings.

By Friday the education ministry counted at least 400 of France's 3,700 high schools closed. A further 560 faced protests or disruption. Twenty-four schools have been burned or ransacked, according to reports citing the ministry.

The human toll is considerable. Reports put injuries at about 170 students and 78 education staff. Arrest figures vary between outlets, from roughly 3,000 to more than 5,000, with Al Jazeera reporting that 85% of those detained are minors. The numbers have not been reconciled, and they should be treated as approximate.

Two readings

Mr Geffray has said that far-left activists "infiltrated" the movement and are responsible for "extreme violence". That is a claim from a party to the dispute, and the sources reviewed do not offer independent evidence of how many of those involved are organised militants rather than pupils.

Student organisers, who have called fresh large demonstrations for Tuesday, argue that the government has offered no "concrete measures". The two accounts are not mutually exclusive. A movement can have genuine, widely shared grievances and also attract people who want a fight.

Why markets might care, and why caution is due

The fiscal backdrop is what gives a school dispute wider interest. France is the euro area's second-largest economy, and it is attempting to consolidate its public finances. Education is one of the largest items of state spending, and complaints about staffing and buildings are in effect complaints about money. Any concession that costs something sits awkwardly with deficit targets. Any refusal prolongs the unrest.

Still, the evidence for a macro event is thin. Student protests are not strikes by workers. They do not stop trains or refineries, and nothing in the reporting so far links this movement to unions, to a budget vote, or to bond-market pricing. A spread on French government debt over German Bunds moves on deficits, coalition arithmetic and ratings decisions, not on lycée blockades. Spillover into the euro area's risk sentiment is a possibility to watch rather than an observed fact.

The precedent most French politicians remember is that youth protests can escalate when they merge with wider labour discontent, as in 2006, when student demonstrations against a youth employment contract spread across the country. Nothing yet suggests such a fusion this time.

What to watch

The first test is Tuesday's demonstrations, to see whether turnout holds or fades after the closures. After that, the question is whether ministers move beyond security measures to something on staffing or school conditions, and whether teachers' unions join in. Participation by adult workers would change the character of the story. If the numbers of arrests and injuries keep rising while the closures hold, the dispute will become a question of public order as much as education policy.
