EU ambassador says the ball is in the UK's court on the future relationship

In one sentence
The EU's ambassador says Brussels will engage once London defines what it wants, leaving trade and City-access terms stuck while the UK weighs alignment against divergence.

Brussels has told London that the next move on the future UK-EU relationship is London's. According to the EU's ambassador to the UK, the bloc will be "ready to engage" once the British government has settled what it wants from the relationship. Until then, talks on trade terms and City access stay where they are.

The message is short, but it reads as deliberate. The EU is declining to open with an offer. It is asking the UK to set out its priorities first, and it is putting that request on the public record. Details of the ambassador's remarks beyond that framing were limited in the reporting available, and no new negotiating text or timetable accompanied them.

What is actually unresolved

Two areas matter most to markets: trade in goods and services, and financial services access.

On trade, the issue is how closely UK rules track EU rules. Alignment cuts border friction and compliance costs. It also limits London's freedom to diverge. On financial services, the City's access to EU clients and counterparties depends largely on regulatory judgments made in Brussels, not on a settled treaty right. Those judgments can be granted, narrowed or withdrawn. That uncertainty has a price, though it is difficult to isolate in any single quote.

The standoff therefore reflects a real disagreement over regulatory alignment and market access. It is not just a dispute over who speaks first. Each side wants the other to show its hand on how much divergence it will accept, and what it would pay to keep access.

Why Brussels is waiting

The EU's position is simple. If London does not know what it is asking for, there is nothing to negotiate. Moving first would also hand the UK a baseline to bargain down from. Waiting costs Brussels little, since the EU is the larger market in this relationship and the UK is the party seeking changes.

For London, the incentives run the other way. Spelling out priorities means choosing between trade-offs: deeper alignment in exchange for smoother trade, or freer regulation in exchange for thinner access. Those choices carry domestic political costs. A government that is also managing fiscal credibility, with gilt yields near multi-decade highs, has reasons not to rush into a choice that could cost it either way.

What it means for markets

The cost of a stalemate is mostly one of drift. Firms cannot plan around terms that nobody has proposed. They face a standing risk of unplanned tariffs or narrower access, and they have no scheduled event that would remove it. Clarity in either direction, whether closer alignment or a clean break, would be priced quickly. Diplomatic posturing is priced slowly, if at all.

The next data point is a UK statement of priorities. Nothing in the ambassador's remarks suggests Brussels will act before one arrives. Any signal from the Conservative conference season or from ministers on whether London favours alignment or divergence would be the first real indication of which way the UK is leaning.
