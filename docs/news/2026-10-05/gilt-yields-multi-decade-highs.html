Gilt yields hold at multi-decade highs, with the 30-year above 6% and the 10-year near 5.5%

In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields topped 6% for the first time since 1998 and the 10-year hit 5.5%, a post-2007 high, as oil-driven inflation fears hit global bonds and the FTSE 100.

UK government borrowing costs have climbed to levels last seen before the financial crisis. On 1 October the 10-year gilt yield reached 5.5%, its highest since July 2007. The 30-year touched 6% for the first time since 1998. Yields eased on 2 October, with the 10-year slipping back below 5.4%. They remain high enough that the move is being read as a repricing, not a blip.

The move

The path to 5.5% was steady rather than sudden. The 10-year yield rose 23 basis points in September alone and ended the month just below 5.4%, according to Trading Economics. Earlier in the year it was nearer 4.9%. The 4.9% reading came on 26 March and was then described as approaching an 18-year high.

The immediate driver is energy. A global bond selloff, led by rising oil prices and the inflation risk they carry, has hit gilts harder than most peers. Sharecast's London market report framed the FTSE 100's slide the same way. Equities were falling alongside bonds, as higher discount rates cut the present value of long-dated cash flows. The Sharecast article itself could not be retrieved for this piece, so the equity-side detail rests on its headline and the triage summary. Neither source gave a precise FTSE 100 level or percentage fall.

The oil backdrop has been unsettled for months. Brent neared $105 a barrel in March, in what Trading Economics described as its largest monthly gain since 1990, amid the US-Iran conflict. A separate piece in this edition covers UK diesel passing £2 a litre, and another covers Houthi claims of strikes on Aramco sites. Both point to supply risk that has not gone away.

Why the long end matters

A 30-year yield above 6% is not only a borrowing-cost story for the Treasury. Long-dated gilts are the benchmark against which pension funds, insurers and property and infrastructure investors price long-duration assets. A rise of that size lowers the value of existing bonds and raises the hurdle rate for everything else. Equity sectors whose value sits in distant earnings feel it first.

The curve itself tells a story. With the 30-year about 50 basis points above the 10-year, the market is asking for extra pay to hold UK duration. That points to inflation and supply risk, not a view that growth is about to weaken. Trading Economics notes that stronger-than-expected economic data has also strengthened the case for "higher-for-longer" rates. Bond markets are therefore pricing resilience and inflation together, which is the harder combination for policymakers.

The Bank of England's room

The policy implication is the awkward one. Markets now price further tightening, not cuts. In March traders expected two to three rate rises by July 2026. By October they anticipated four by July 2027. That leaves the Bank of England little room to look through an energy-driven inflation spike. Easing into it would risk de-anchoring expectations. Tightening into it would add to the pressure on households already facing higher fuel costs. The British Retail Consortium has reported that consumer confidence "plummeted in March" on inflation fears.

Fiscal politics adds a second layer. Tory conference pledges on tax and regulation cuts were pitched explicitly at gilt-wary markets, covered elsewhere in this edition. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey's warning that "everybody is currently priced to be a winner" came as AI-linked debt neared $450bn. It sits uneasily next to yields at these levels.

What to watch

The nearest test is whether the 10-year can hold below 5.4% after its 2 October retreat, or whether a renewed oil move sends it back to 5.5%. US jobs data and the oil price, both flagged in this edition's S&P 500 week-ahead, will set the tone for global duration. The next Bank of England rate decision and the following inflation print will show whether markets' rate-rise pricing needs to move further.
