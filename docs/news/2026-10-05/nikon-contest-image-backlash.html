Tiny image sparks backlash in Nikon photo contest

In one sentence
Nikon is re-reviewing its Small World In Motion winner after scientists said the microscope video looks AI-made, exposing how vague contest rules are on AI.

The winner of a prestigious microscope-video contest is under review after other scientists said it looks AI-made. The dispute, over a clip a few thousandths of a millimetre wide, shows how hard it is for a competition to police "original work" when AI is built into the scientific imaging pipeline.

Nikon's Small World In Motion contest takes photos and video shot through powerful microscopes and has run for 15 years. This year's winning entry, with a $3,000 prize, was a short video by Dr Ning Xu of Tsinghua University in China. It purports to show cilia, the tiny hair-like structures that sweep mucus along the airways, moving in the lungs of a child with a rare disease.

What the critics saw

Scientists who looked at the footage said it did not behave like biology. Edward Phelps, an associate professor at the University of Florida, said structures "pop in and out of existence" and that cilia "appear from nowhere". Others said the cilia looked too large. Ian Donovan, a PhD student at UT Southwestern Medical Center, said the source image appeared to carry a watermark indicating it was AI-generated.

Patrick Hickey, who placed fifth in the contest, told the BBC the rule was "quite clearly that you couldn't use generative AI to produce things". Contest rules are reported to require microscope-captured footage.

Xu's defence, and a vague rulebook

Xu has acknowledged using AI "to distinguish and visualize features in the reconstructed grayscale images". He denies that it generated the experimental footage or the motion. He has said the regions beneath the cilia were rendered "mainly to improve the visual presentation, rather than provide an anatomical reconstruction".

That is a fine line, and the published rules do not draw it clearly. Amateur Photographer reports that entries are judged on originality, informational content, technical proficiency and visual impact, but that the rules do not explicitly set limits on AI use or require disclosure. Whether AI-assisted reconstruction counts as processing, as is routine in computational microscopy, or as generation is the question the contest now has to answer.

Nikon said initially: "At this time, we don't see that any rules were violated." It has since said it is "carefully re-reviewing the information provided during the initial vetting, along with additional supporting materials". Xu is reported to be "respectfully complying" with requests for technical documents. The entry remained displayed as the winner during the review.

Not the first time

The episode follows a run of similar disputes. In 2023 the Sony World Photography Awards winner revealed that the image was "co-created" with AI. This year a Hasselblad Masters finalist was disqualified after online observers spotted AI artefacts in lettering in the picture. In each case, detection came from outside volunteers, not from the organisers' own checks.

Why it matters beyond photography

For markets, the point is structural rather than about Nikon's share price. Contests, agencies and stock libraries trade on a promise that a human made the picture with a camera. If an AI-processed image can pass initial vetting and only crowd scrutiny catches it, that promise is hard to enforce. The gap invites outside verification: provenance metadata, disclosure rules and certification services. None of these has yet become a common standard in competitions. That is a prediction rather than something this case demonstrates.

The next data point is Nikon's promised update. If it sets out in writing what AI processing is allowed and what must be disclosed, it will be one of the first concrete rules of its kind from a major imaging brand.
