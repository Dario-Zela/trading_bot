Mahmood says nearly all women arriving on small boats have been raped

In one sentence
Shabana Mahmood said almost every woman in northern France has probably been raped en route to Britain, without citing evidence, as protesters and police scuffled at Dover.

The Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, has said that almost every woman waiting in northern France to cross the Channel has probably been raped on the way. She offered no figures to support it. The remark, made on a podcast, landed on a weekend when anti-migration demonstrators gathered at two south-coast ports and, in Dover, scuffled with police as people rescued from small boats were brought ashore.

What was said

Speaking to The Rest Is Politics podcast, in an interview reported by the Guardian on 4 October, Mahmood said: "You can almost guarantee that every woman that's in the north of France has probably been raped along the journey."

The wording matters. The headline version, that nearly all women arriving on small boats have been raped, compresses a hedged statement. Mahmood spoke of women in northern France, not of arrivals counted at the border, and she said "probably". The claim is still a sweeping one.

She used it to support an argument central to the government's asylum policy: that criminal gangs, rather than the British state, now decide who reaches the UK. The case for expanding sponsored, safe routes for refugees follows from that. On this reasoning, the people who make it across are those who can pay smugglers and endure the crossing, not necessarily those in greatest need.

The evidence gap

No Home Office assessment quantifying the prevalence of sexual violence on routes to northern France has been published, according to a fact-check of the interview by UK Fact Check. Official arrival statistics record age and sex, not what happened before people reached the beach. The claim is therefore neither confirmed nor refuted by the data the government publishes.

Charities and researchers have long documented sexual violence against women on migration routes, including in camps along the French coast, which is why the remark was not dismissed outright. Imran Hussain of the Refugee Council welcomed the recognition of sexual violence against refugee women, while asking why safe legal routes were being narrowed at the same time.

The numbers add context. Women are a minority of those crossing. Figures cited by UK Fact Check put adult women at about 13% of arrivals in the year to June 2026, against 72% adult men and 15% children. Arrivals in 2025 totalled 41,472, the second-highest on record. The running total for 2026 stood at 18,565 on 23 September, about 42% below last year's pace, even after the busiest single day of the year, with 781 people. These figures come from a secondary source and should be checked against Home Office releases.

On the beach

The politics around the claim were already charged. Police braced for anti-migrant protests in Southampton and Dover on 3 October, with restrictions in place and warnings against face coverings, according to ITV Meridian. In Dover, demonstrators carrying English Defence League banners marched below the cliffs, flanked by officers, while counter-protesters held signs reading "Racism hurts everyone". One report put the number brought ashore at 149; scuffles broke out between protesters and police. Hampshire Police said no arrests followed the demonstrations in its area.

The two events are separate, but they pull on the same argument. For the government, the language of vulnerability, of women preyed upon by gangs, frames crossings as a humanitarian failure to be fixed with safe routes and tougher action against smugglers. For protesters at the port, the same arrivals are a question of border control. Mahmood's remark was aimed at the first audience, but it was heard by both.

What to watch

Two things will test the claim. The first is whether the Home Office or a research body publishes evidence on the scale of sexual violence on the northern France route; without it, the statement stays an assertion by a minister, however plausible some of it may be. The second is how the government's sponsored-routes plans develop, since the claim is the moral premise for them. Weekly crossing figures, which have been running well below 2025, will show whether the argument is matched by any change on the water.
