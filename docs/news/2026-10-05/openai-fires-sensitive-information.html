OpenAI fires workers for 'mishandling sensitive information'

In one sentence
OpenAI fired three researchers for mishandling sensitive company information tied to an outside AI evaluator, but has not said what was shared and no reporting points to customer data.

OpenAI has dismissed three researchers, saying they mishandled sensitive information. The company has said little else. It has not described what the information was, how much of it was involved, or where it went. Nothing in the public reporting so far says customer data was affected.

That gap matters because of who relies on OpenAI. Companies and trading firms increasingly run production systems on its APIs, and they have a direct interest in whether the firings point to a one-off breach of internal rules or to weak controls.

What OpenAI has said

The company confirmed the departures on 2 October. "We have parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information," it said, according to reports. A spokesperson added: "Our investigation confirmed that these individuals mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures, violating our policies and breaking the trust essential to our work."

AFP, RTÉ and others name the three as Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni. OpenAI itself did not name them in the statements reported. At least two worked on safety and alignment.

The one other detail comes from the reporting rather than from a full account by the company. The conduct reportedly involved work with an external organisation that evaluates AI models. That organisation has not been identified. Neither the date of the dismissals nor the length of the investigation has been published.

What is not known

The phrase "sensitive company information" is the key one. It points to OpenAI's own material, such as model internals, evaluation results or internal documents. It does not point to user or customer records. That reading is an inference from the wording, not a confirmed fact. OpenAI has not said what category the data fell into, and none of the coverage reviewed for this piece reports customer data being involved.

So the two readings the story invites cannot yet be separated. In the narrow one, three staff shared internal material with an outside evaluator in a way the company's rules did not allow. The company enforced those rules, and the matter ends there. In the broad one, the episode shows that internal material can leave the building through ordinary working relationships with outside testers, and that the firings are the first visible sign of a weaker control environment. The evidence so far fits the narrow reading better, but it does not rule out the broad one.

The safety-debate overlay

The firings also land in a fraught context. All three had posted publicly about AI safety in recent weeks. Balesni wrote that AI carried more than a 10% risk of human extinction. Korbak voiced unhappiness with OpenAI's direction. Wang cautioned against rushing recursive self-improvement, the idea of AI systems improving their own design.

Reports stress that the three were dismissed for the information handling and not for raising safety concerns, and OpenAI's statement says nothing about their views. Still, the pairing is why some observers will read the move as retaliation. Without knowing what was shared, with whom, and under what arrangement, outsiders cannot test either account. The same silence that protects a legitimate confidentiality interest also makes the company's version impossible to check.

What to watch

Three things would settle the matter. The first is whether OpenAI or the external evaluator says what was shared and whether it was returned or contained. The second is whether the former employees, who are active on social media, give their own account. The third is whether OpenAI tells enterprise customers or regulators anything about its controls. For customers building on its APIs, the practical question is not these three people. It is whether the company's rules for outside evaluators are written down, enforced and audited, and so far OpenAI has not said.
