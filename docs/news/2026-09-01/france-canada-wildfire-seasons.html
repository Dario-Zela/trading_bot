France and Canada face historic wildfire seasons as 2026 drought persists

A wildfire burns at night near Trévillach in France's Pyrénées-Orientales region, 4 July 2026. — Frenchbluesman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
France's 2026 wildfire emissions match the extreme 2022–23 fire years and Canada has burned over 3 million hectares, as back-to-back drought seasons strain insurers and carbon markets.

Southern France was still smouldering in late August when Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez stood near Bordeaux airport and confirmed what firefighters already knew: two of their colleagues had died battling a blaze that would not stay contained. President Emmanuel Macron, surveying the damage days later, said the country had not seen fire on this scale "since the Second World War." Four thousand miles away, in the boreal forests of Ontario and British Columbia, Canadian Armed Forces helicopters were lifting residents of Eabametoong First Nation out of a fire zone that had already consumed more than 3 million hectares — an area larger than Belgium.

Both events are, on their own, local disasters. Together, with a second consecutive year of severe drought behind them, they read as something else: a pattern that reinsurers, commodity traders and climate scientists are now treating less as an anomaly and more as a baseline.

A season that rewrote the record books

France's fire season began early, forced by an 11-day June heatwave that left vegetation primed to burn. By early July, blazes in the Aude and Hérault departments near the Spanish border had already scorched hundreds of hectares; within weeks, fires generating their own pyrocumulonimbus storm clouds — a phenomenon common in North America and Australia but never previously documented in France — were igniting new fires with lightning strikes of their own making. The Landes fire near Biscarrosse alone burned more than 3,500 hectares and forced over 30,000 evacuations.

By the end of August, monitoring bodies including the EU's Joint Research Centre put the season's cumulative toll above 96,000 hectares — more than five times the twenty-year average — with some later estimates, factoring in fires still burning into early September, exceeding 160,000 hectares nationwide. The precise figure moves depending on which agency and which week is being measured, which is itself telling: France's fire-monitoring infrastructure, built for a temperate climate with a short, predictable fire season, is being outpaced by a fire season that no longer behaves that way.

The more consistent signal comes from the atmosphere rather than the ground. The Copernicus Atmosphere Monitoring Service, which tracks wildfire carbon output by satellite, found that France's year-to-date emissions in 2026 are running comparable to, or higher than, the extreme fire years of 2022 and 2023 — years that were themselves outliers until this one. France's July emissions alone hit 0.89 megatonnes of carbon, well above the previous monthly record of 0.64 megatonnes. Copernicus scientists say 2026 is on course to rank among the worst fire years of the past 24.

Canada's fourth straight red-alert summer

Canada's season tells a similar story from a different starting point. More than 3 million hectares had burned by the time the World Meteorological Organization flagged it in its August briefings, driven by the same mechanism as France's crisis: a hot, dry summer following on from an already depleted snowpack and soil moisture deficit. The federal government raised its National Preparedness Level to 4 — its highest active-response tier — after Ontario formally requested military assistance in mid-July. The Canadian Armed Forces evacuated the Eabametoong First Nation community of Fort Hope by helicopter starting 18 July, one of dozens of communities, many of them Indigenous, displaced across the country's north.

In British Columbia, the Bald Range fire forced the overnight evacuation of the entire town of Summerland — some 12,000 people — along with 8,000 more around Peachland, as fast-moving flames destroyed homes west of Okanagan Lake. Smoke from the northern fires drifted south through July, blanketing much of the US northeast and Midwest in haze, a now-familiar late-summer feature since the record 2023 season. By the August government update, the season's toll stood at 4,793 fires and 4.1 million hectares burned — below 2025's even more extreme 7.7 million hectares, but still far above the historical average, and arriving for the fourth consecutive year in which Canada has declared a top-tier national wildfire emergency.

Why insurers and traders are watching

Wildfire losses used to be a US and Australian problem for reinsurers; Europe's exposure was considered comparatively minor. That assumption is eroding. Analysts at Morningstar DBRS estimate France's 2026 wildfire losses at €10–15 billion, with only a fraction insured — Marcos Alvarez, a director there, has warned that a fire reaching a mid-sized city like Bordeaux would represent "a completely different scale of losses" for an industry still pricing European wildfire risk as a marginal line item. The European Central Bank and EU insurance regulators have separately found that only about a quarter of the continent's climate catastrophe losses between 1980 and 2024 were covered by insurance at all — a protection gap that widens every time a season like this one arrives without the capital reserves to match it.

AXA's climate research unit projects nearly 70% more high-risk fire days annually across French regions by 2050, a trajectory that reinsurance pricing models are only beginning to catch up with. Global insured catastrophe losses are still tracking toward $115–135 billion for 2026 — a fifth straight year above the $100 billion threshold, according to Swiss Re — even though the first half of the year came in comparatively light. Wildfire, insurers now say plainly, is the fastest-growing peril in their models, and the widening gap between what burns and what is covered increasingly falls on governments, agricultural insurers and, ultimately, the carbon and timber markets exposed to forests that no longer function as reliable long-term carbon sinks.

What the drought signal means

The detail that most concerns climate scientists is not the size of either season individually but their recurrence. The WMO's broader 2026 assessment describes a compound European drought — driven by low rainfall and record June heat — that has pushed river levels down on the Seine, Rhine and Danube while priming forests for ignition. World Weather Attribution's research group has gone further, concluding that Canada's run of extreme fire seasons is no longer an anomaly to be waited out but a structural shift in what an average summer now looks like. Neither France nor Canada experienced this kind of season in isolation this decade; both are now several years into a pattern where the exceptional year is becoming the expected one.

For markets, the next data points worth watching are the January 2026 reinsurance renewal season — where wildfire-exposed property lines are expected to reprice regardless of this year's comparatively light first-half global losses — and Copernicus's coming full-year emissions tally, due once the French season closes in autumn, which will confirm whether 2026 has in fact matched or exceeded the 2022–23 benchmark. Canada's fire season typically runs into September; a wet autumn could still cap this year's final hectare count, but the pattern building across four consecutive red-alert years suggests planners on both sides of the Atlantic will be modelling for a wildfire season like this one becoming the rule, not the exception.
