China's Caixin manufacturing PMI due after official gauge stays in contraction

Yangshan Deep-Water Port, part of the Port of Shanghai, a hub for China's containerised export trade — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
China's official factory PMI stayed in contraction at 49.8 in August, but the export-tilted RatingDog (formerly Caixin) survey jumped to 51.5 — a nine-month high.

China's factory sector is sending two different signals at once, and the gap between them says more than either number alone.

The official manufacturing PMI, compiled by the National Bureau of Statistics from a survey weighted toward large, state-linked firms, came in at 49.8 for August — an improvement from July's 49.2 but still below the 50 line that separates expansion from contraction. It was the second straight month in contraction, after a reading below 50 in July snapped a four-month run of growth. Sub-indices offered some comfort: production rose to 50.4 and new orders climbed to 50.6, both back above the threshold, while new export orders reached 50.1.

The private-sector gauge told a starker, better story. The RatingDog China General Manufacturing PMI — the survey long known as the Caixin PMI before a Shenzhen credit-research firm bought the naming rights from S&P Global in a mid-2025 sponsorship deal, though S&P Global still compiles the data — jumped to 51.5 in August from 50.9 in July, beating forecasts of 51.0. That extended the index's run above 50 to nine consecutive months, the longest expansion in five years.

Smaller firms, bigger orders

The RatingDog survey, which polls 430 to 650 companies tilted toward smaller, privately owned and export-oriented manufacturers, showed new export business rising at its fastest pace in six months, driven by consumer goods. New orders overall grew for a fifteenth straight month — the longest such streak since 2018 — and output expanded at its fastest clip in three months. Input costs accelerated for the first time since April on higher material prices, even as manufacturers cut selling prices for the first time this year, a sign that competition for orders remains fierce despite the volume gains.

That divergence is the story. The official survey's weaker showing reflects the state-linked, capital-intensive end of Chinese manufacturing — steel, heavy industry, large exporters exposed to tariff friction and softening domestic construction demand. The private survey's strength suggests smaller, nimbler exporters are finding buyers abroad even as the big-ticket end of the economy stays under pressure. It is a similar pattern to episodes in 2019 and 2023, when the two gauges split during uneven downturns, with the divergence tending to close only as broader demand recovers.

What it means for markets

A reading this firm from the smaller-exporter cohort complicates the case for aggressive stimulus. Commerzbank and other desks had flagged the case for further easing on the back of the weak official numbers; a nine-month expansion streak in the private gauge, alongside a services PMI due later this week, gives policymakers room to wait. Industrial metals and freight-linked equities, which move on China demand signals, are likely to track whichever reading dominates the narrative in the days ahead — and for now the export-facing story is the more encouraging one.

One caveat sits underneath the headline: business sentiment within the RatingDog survey slipped to a seven-month low even as current activity strengthened, hinting exporters are less confident the pace holds. China's official non-manufacturing PMI, covering services and construction, stayed at 49.0 in August, still in contraction. The next test comes with the Caixin/RatingDog services PMI later this week and the official PMIs for September, due August 31 — the readings traders will use to judge whether August's export strength was a genuine turn or a one-month reprieve.
