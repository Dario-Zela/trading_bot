Japanese firms deepen India pivot for a third straight year as China risks mount

Narendra Modi, whose government has courted Japanese manufacturing capital with a $66bn decade-long investment target. — Government of India / Wikimedia Commons (GODL-India)
In one sentence
Japanese FDI into India has beaten flows to China for three straight years, backed by a $66bn decade target, as Tokyo's risk-averse capital reprices Chinese exposure.

For the fourth year running, Japanese manufacturers have told their own development bank that India, not China, is the most promising place to put money to work outside Japan. That survey finding, tucked into a September release from the Japan Bank for International Cooperation, sits underneath a much larger and now well-established pattern: Japanese direct investment into India has exceeded Japanese direct investment into China for three consecutive years, and Tokyo has put a number on where it wants that trend to go — $66 billion in private capital over the coming decade.

The headline figure is a target, not a certainty, and Japanese officials have doubled it once already. But the flows underneath it are real, they are accelerating, and they track almost exactly with the geopolitical risk premium that has attached itself to China since 2020. Read together, they suggest that de-risking away from Chinese exposure — the phrase that dominated G7 communiqués for the past three years — is no longer a slogan for Japanese capital. It is a spreadsheet line.

What the numbers actually show

Net Japanese investment flows into China fell from roughly $12.5 billion in 2021 to an estimated $1.7 billion in 2025, according to data cited in East Asia Forum analysis. Over the same span, flows into India rose from $3.7 billion to roughly $7.6 billion. The gap is starkest in transport manufacturing, the sector most exposed to both the electric-vehicle transition and supply-chain politics: Japanese direct investment into India's transport sector increased more than sevenfold between 2021 and 2024, hitting close to $2 billion, while direct investment in China's transport sector fell 83% over the same period, to roughly $300 million.

Toyota and Suzuki have been the visible face of that shift, announcing a combined $11 billion in Indian manufacturing and export capacity over the past year. The most recent milestone came on 2 July, when Prime Minister Narendra Modi and his Japanese counterpart, Sanae Takaichi, jointly inaugurated Maruti Suzuki's fourth Indian plant, an 800-acre complex at Kharkhoda in Haryana built with a planned ₹35,000 crore (roughly $4.2 billion) outlay and capacity set to reach one million vehicles a year. It was one of nearly 120 agreements worth about $12.5 billion signed around that visit, Takaichi's first to New Delhi as prime minister, with more than 150 Japanese firms in attendance.

The $66 billion pledge, and its limits

The decade-long target dates to August 2025, when Modi and then-prime minister Shigeru Ishiba pledged to mobilise 10 trillion yen — roughly $66-68 billion depending on the exchange rate used — in private-sector Japanese investment into India, doubling a target first set in 2022. Takaichi, who succeeded Ishiba in October, has continued the push, framing it around economic security, artificial intelligence and defence cooperation rather than purely commercial terms.

East Asia Forum analysts caution against reading the shift as a coordinated geopolitical retreat, however. Their research characterises Japanese firms as guided primarily by market size, growth potential and commercial returns — with India's economy simply growing while Japan's domestic market shrinks under a population that has been declining for roughly 16 years. China's slowdown and the more transactional US trade environment reduce the competing pulls. Hedging against Chinese supply-chain concentration is a factor firms cite, not the only one.

The pivot is also younger than the three-year streak implies, and it followed a period of retreat. A Centre for Social and Economic Progress analysis notes that Japanese investment in India fell from a rough parity with ASEAN in 2008 to a $23 billion gap by 2024 — $28.7 billion into ASEAN against $5.3 billion into India — as Japanese manufacturers got caught between India's own China-focused screening rules and their continued reliance on Chinese components. India has since begun unwinding some of that friction: since 1 May 2026, investors with up to 10% Chinese ownership can use India's automatic approval route within existing sectoral caps, a partial easing of the Press Note 3 regime imposed after the 2020 Galwan Valley clash.

What it means for positioning

The practical read for markets is less about India as a standalone growth story — that thesis is old — and more about the pace at which a large, risk-averse, U.S.-aligned pool of capital is willing to reprice Chinese exposure. Japanese manufacturers do not move on ideology; JBIC's own surveyed firms rank India first for market potential, not for politics. That a conservative, decades-old survey now shows a four-year consecutive lead for India over China is itself a signal that the hedging thesis has moved from hypothesis to base case for corporate treasurers in Tokyo, even as China policy under Takaichi grows more openly securitised.

The next data points worth watching are the JBIC survey's 2026 edition, due next autumn, and whether India's Press Note 3 easing draws measurable Chinese component investment back in without reopening the political argument that produced the restriction in the first place. China's own Caixin manufacturing PMI, due for release imminently, will offer a read on whether Beijing's factory slowdown is deepening the incentive for Japanese firms to diversify further, or whether a stabilisation gives boardrooms in Tokyo and Osaka reason to pause before the next capital-allocation cycle.
