Dry-weather sewage discharges soar as UK water companies face renewed scrutiny

A combined sewer overflow outfall — the type of infrastructure implicated in England's surging dry-weather discharge figures. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
South West Water logged 1,299 illegal dry-weather sewage discharges in five months of 2026 — over half of 2025's total — deepening regulatory and financial risk for the water sector.

For five months of near-average rainfall across the south-west of England, South West Water discharged raw sewage into rivers and coastal waters on dry days 1,299 times. That is more than half the total recorded across the whole of 2025 — itself already a record year — and it has happened in barely a third of the time.

Dry-weather discharges are not the storm overflows most people have grown used to hearing about. Combined sewer overflows are designed to release diluted sewage into rivers during heavy rain, when treatment works would otherwise be overwhelmed — legal, if unpopular, engineering. A spill on a dry day is a different matter. There is no storm to blame, no surge of rainwater to point to. Under the terms of water companies' environmental permits, it should not be happening at all.

A first full year of data tells an ugly story

England's water companies were required, for the first time, to start reporting dry-day discharges from combined sewer overflows to the Environment Agency last year. The early numbers, gathered under freedom of information requests and reported by the Guardian's Sandra Laville, show discharges accelerating rather than levelling off: 7,280 dry-weather spills nationally in the first five months of 2026, against 8,576 for the whole of 2025.

South West Water accounts for a disproportionate share. The company serves Devon, Cornwall and parts of Dorset and Somerset — a region whose rivers and beaches are central to a tourist economy that depends on clean water almost as much as it depends on sunshine. Robert Forrester, a former Environment Agency officer with more than two decades in the role who has been submitting the FOI requests behind much of this data, put the pattern bluntly: "Dry day spills will almost always be deliberate and illegal, otherwise there would be non-stop raw sewage spilling from pipes." In other words, these are not obscure edge cases in a monitoring system — they look, on his reading, like routine practice.

The scale showed up starkly in August, when near real-time monitoring recorded South West Water discharging sewage for roughly 300 hours in a single week — 664 separate spills across 184 sites — despite just 16.5mm of rain falling in the area. South West Water said the near real-time data requires validation before it can be treated as final, a standard caveat for monitoring feeds that has nonetheless done little to quiet local anger. Ed Tyldesley, a Liberal Democrat county councillor in North Devon, has been among those pressing the company publicly over discharges in his patch.

Regulators are already circling

This is not South West Water's first collision with its regulators this year. Ofwat's investigation into the company's management of its wastewater treatment works and sewerage network — opened after finding the firm had failed to properly operate, maintain and upgrade its assets — concluded with a £24m enforcement package funded jointly by the company and its shareholders, including a commitment to spend £20m by 2030 reducing spills from specific storm overflows. Separately, in June, South West Water was fined £1.853m by the Drinking Water Inspectorate over a cryptosporidium outbreak that struck Brixham in 2024, a record penalty that DLA Piper's environmental lawyers flagged as a marker for how much tougher criminal sentencing in this area has become. More recently, Ofwat confirmed an £86m combined enforcement package covering South West Water and Anglian Water, underscoring that regulators are now treating wastewater failures as a sector-wide pattern rather than isolated lapses.

Water UK, the industry's trade body, has pushed back on how the dry-day figures are being framed, calling near real-time readings "unverified" and stressing that no spill is ever acceptable to the sector. The industry points to its own remedy: a £12bn commitment, approved by Ofwat within a wider £104bn 2025-30 investment programme, to nearly halve storm overflow spills by 2030 through new storage tanks and nature-based drainage schemes. Defra, for its part, has been less diplomatic, describing the pattern of dry-weather discharges as "totally unacceptable."

Why this matters beyond the riverbank

For investors, the arithmetic is straightforward even if the politics are not. Every enforcement package, criminal fine and mandated capital programme lands directly on the balance sheets of regulated monopolies whose returns are already capped by Ofwat's price controls. South West Water sits inside Pennon Group, a FTSE 250 utility whose shares have otherwise had a reasonably strong run over the past year. But a company under active, overlapping investigation from both Ofwat and the Environment Agency — with a criminal prosecution already on the books in 2026 — carries a different risk profile than one simply meeting its permit conditions. Sustained scrutiny raises the odds of further enforcement orders, and each one tends to arrive with a bill attached, whether paid by the company, its shareholders, or ultimately its customers through bill rises.

The wider read is that England's water sector's promised turnaround — the £104bn investment programme sold to the public as the fix for a broken system — is running up against evidence that some of the discharging happening today isn't the legacy problem investment is meant to solve, but current, ongoing non-compliance. That distinction matters for how regulators respond next, and for how much patience the public has left.

The next marker to watch is the Environment Agency's full-year dry-weather discharge dataset, expected in early 2027, which will show whether South West Water's early-2026 surge was a blip or the new baseline — and whether Ofwat's enforcement machinery moves quickly enough to get ahead of it.
