Victoria Beckham's fashion label posts first-ever profit after 18 years

Victoria Beckham, whose eponymous fashion and beauty label posted its first annual profit in 2025 after 18 years of losses. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Victoria Beckham's fashion and beauty company posted a maiden £7.3m operating profit in 2025, ending 18 years of losses once subsidised by David Beckham.

Eighteen years, £69 million of cumulative losses, and — by her own account — more than one conversation with her husband about whether to just stop. On Monday, Victoria Beckham's fashion and beauty business posted its first-ever operating profit: £7.3 million for 2025, on revenues up 15% to £129.8 million.

It is a small number by the standards of the luxury houses her label has spent two decades trying to sit alongside. But for a company that lost money in every single year since Beckham founded it in 2008 — including a £1.6 million loss as recently as 2024 — it marks the point where the business stops being an extension of her husband's earning power and starts standing on its own.

The bill David paid

The Beckham name has always done double duty as marketing and as balance sheet. Victoria Beckham Holdings Ltd went, in the company's own later description, "tens of millions into the red" over its first decade and a half, burning through funding rounds and a 2017 investment that saw NEO Investment Partners take a 30% stake for £30 million. Sir David Beckham, whose post-playing career of sponsorships, punditry and the Inter Miami ownership stake has made him one of football's highest earners, repeatedly put money in to cover shortfalls and payroll — support that outside filings and reporting have pointed to since at least 2016.

That kind of subsidy is not unusual in celebrity fashion — plenty of famous-name labels exist as vanity projects or marketing vehicles that never need to clear a profit test on their own. What makes the Beckham case notable is the scale and duration of the losses relative to the eventual payoff, and the fact that the company kept going long enough to find one.

What actually turned it around

The swing to profit was not driven by the clothes that built the brand's reputation. It came from beauty. Victoria Beckham Beauty, launched in 2019, now generates roughly two-thirds of group revenue, and its breakout product — a £104 Foundation Drops formula — sold out on launch and built a waitlist reported at more than 25,000 customers before restocking in 2026. Skincare sales more than doubled on the back of it. Sybille Darricarrère Lunel, who joined as chief executive in July 2025 from Christian Dior Couture, credited "several years of disciplined execution and strategic investment" rather than any single hit product.

It is the fifth consecutive year of double-digit revenue growth, and the company says momentum has carried into the first half of 2026. A permanent US store on Mercer Street in New York — housing fashion, beauty and fragrance under one roof — opens this month, the label's first real bet on brick-and-mortar retail in its largest growth market.

The longer read

The arc here is less about Beckham herself than about what it actually costs to build a fashion house from nothing. Luxury brands with generations of capital and distribution behind them — the LVMHs and Kerings of the world — took decades to become what they are; a celebrity name buys attention on day one but not the infrastructure, wholesale relationships or manufacturing scale that make a label self-sustaining. Beckham's business needed 18 years and a beauty line, not a runway show, to get there. "After 20 years, people don't look at my brand as a celebrity brand anymore," she said of the milestone — a line that only makes sense once the accounts back it up.

The next test is whether beauty's momentum can fund the fashion side properly, rather than simply covering for it, and whether the New York store translates a media moment — the Beckhams' Netflix documentary, released last October, brought a fresh wave of attention — into repeat retail custom rather than a one-off spike.
