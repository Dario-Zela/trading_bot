England's prisons to get more in-cell capacity as overcrowding crisis deepens

A watchtower at HMP Manchester, known as Strangeways — part of an estate now running near capacity. — Crispin Semmens / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The Ministry of Justice is converting offices and storerooms into prison cells rather than building new jails, as Burnham is warned running near-full capacity is a 'big risk'.

England's prison estate is, in the words of one former Conservative justice secretary, running "red hot" — and the new government's answer, for now, is not to pour concrete for new jails but to find spare rooms in the ones it already has.

Justice Secretary Alex Norris told MPs this week that £110m fast-tracked from existing budgets will go toward converting unused office space, laundry rooms and storage areas inside current prisons into cells, adding hundreds of places faster than any new-build programme could manage. It builds on more than 3,200 places already delivered over the past two years and sits alongside a 1,700-place prison under contract in Lancashire, part of a wider pledge to add 14,000 spaces by 2031.

In one sentence: The Ministry of Justice is retrofitting cells into existing prisons rather than building new ones, as Andy Burnham is warned that running the system near full capacity is a "big risk".

A system with nowhere left to put people

England and Wales's prison population has pushed past 87,000, against a certified normal capacity of roughly 70,000 — a gap that has forced emergency measures since last spring, including police-cell overspill and an early-release scheme for thousands of prisoners nearing the end of their sentences. That scheme, reduced from around 6,000 to roughly 5,000 releases after ministers exempted rapists and serious child sexual offenders, is due to restart in October.

David Gauke, the former Tory minister who led the Starmer government's independent sentencing review, used a newspaper interview to warn that Prime Minister Andy Burnham — who took over from Keir Starmer in July — is "taking a big risk on prison capacity" by continuing to run the estate near its ceiling. Gauke said he understood Burnham was "in a bind", but described the stresses of operating at such tight margins as liable to produce exactly the kind of crunch that triggered emergency releases in the first place.

Retrofit over new-build

The £110m announcement is notable less for its size — modest next to the multibillion-pound new-prison programme already under way — than for what it signals about sequencing. Building a prison from scratch takes years and planning permission; carving cells out of an existing laundry room or admin block can be done in months. For a government under fiscal pressure and needing capacity now rather than in 2031, that trade-off looks close to unavoidable.

Norris framed the investment as evidence of momentum rather than retreat: "This Government is serious about fixing the broken prison system we inherited... we're now investing to boost these efforts further, getting spades in the ground quicker to deliver thousands more places." The department is also directing money toward deporting foreign national offenders and considering earlier release for some prisoners serving indeterminate IPP sentences — both aimed at trimming the population rather than expanding the estate.

What's next

The early-release scheme resumes in October, the next real test of whether the retrofitted capacity buys enough headroom to avoid another round of emergency measures. Gauke's review — and his public unease — suggests the sentencing side of the equation remains as contested as the building side.
