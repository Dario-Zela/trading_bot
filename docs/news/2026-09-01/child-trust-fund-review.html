Child trust fund providers face review over £1.5bn in unclaimed pots

Child trust funds were designed as a simple, government-seeded nest egg — but hundreds of thousands were never claimed. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The FCA is reviewing all 55 child trust fund providers over whether they've done enough to trace owners of roughly £1.5bn in dormant accounts.

It began as one of New Labour's tidier ideas: give every child born after September 2002 a £250 government voucher, lock it into an investment account, and let compound growth do the rest of the work by the time they turned 18. Two decades on, a sizeable chunk of that money is still sitting there, unclaimed, and the Financial Conduct Authority wants to know why the firms holding it haven't tried harder to give it back.

The regulator has written to all 55 providers still running child trust funds, asking them to account for their efforts to trace customers who have lost touch with their accounts. Roughly 760,000 pots, worth an average of £2,000 each, remain unclaimed — a total of about £1.5bn spread across the cohort born between September 2002 and January 2011, who are now aged 15 to 23. The FCA says it will press harder on providers with large market shares or "potential indicators of consumer harm," and report back next year.

A scheme built to be forgotten

Child trust funds were, by design, easy to lose track of. Some 6.3 million children were enrolled before the scheme closed to new entrants in 2011, and a meaningful slice of those accounts were opened automatically by HM Revenue & Customs on behalf of parents who never filled in the paperwork — often because the child was in care, or the family simply missed the window. Those accounts drifted through address changes, teenage years and university moves with nobody actively managing them or, in many cases, even aware they existed.

The FCA's review found that only four of the 55 providers have voluntarily joined a shared tracing register run by the Tracing Group, a commercial outfit that has managed to map around 60% of all child trust funds. The rest have shown, in the regulator's words, little enthusiasm for making the search easier. That gap has created an opening for claims-management firms, some of which have charged up to £400 — or ongoing subscriptions — to do what HMRC's own tracing tool does for free.

"You don't need to pay someone else to claim what's rightfully yours," said Chris Knight, the FCA's director of insurance. "Tracing and accessing your own child trust fund costs nothing."

What happens next

HMRC has already started writing directly to 21-year-olds with unmatched accounts, using PAYE and student finance records to find current addresses — a tactic that trims the unclaimed pile but doesn't reach the youngest cohort, whose accounts won't mature until 2029. The Share Foundation, a charity that helps reunite people with their funds, wants the government to go further and automatically release money to account holders once they turn 21, arguing it could put more than £550m into young adults' hands, over a third of it to those from lower-income households.

For providers, the FCA's report next year is the date that matters: a finding of inadequate tracing efforts could trigger enforcement action and set a template for how firms are expected to handle other categories of dormant retail accounts, from old pensions to forgotten ISAs. For everyone else, the simplest step remains the one the regulator keeps repeating — check the free HMRC tracing tool before anyone offers to check it for a fee.
