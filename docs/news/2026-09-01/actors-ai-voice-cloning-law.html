Actors including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas call for UK law on AI voice cloning

Hugh Bonneville, one of more than 80 performers backing the Save Our Voices Now campaign — Julienmorvan / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
More than 80 UK performers, including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, are demanding a statutory right to their own voice as AI cloning tools outpace the law.

Hugh Bonneville has spent a career playing an earl who insists on doing things properly. Off screen this week, the Downton Abbey star is making a similar point about his own voice — arguing that if AI companies can clone it in three seconds and use it without asking, the law ought to say they can't.

Bonneville is one of more than 80 British performers, including Matt Lucas, Nicola Coughlan, Luke Evans and Doctor Who's Pearl Mackie, who have signed an open letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham demanding legislation on AI voice cloning. The campaign, called Save Our Voices Now, wants voice recognised as a statutory property right in UK law — on the same legal footing as a person's name or image, rather than left to a patchwork of copyright and data-protection rules that were never built with generative AI in mind.

Three seconds is all it takes

The technical premise driving the campaign is blunt: modern voice-cloning models need only a few seconds of clean audio to produce a convincing replica of someone's speech. Co-founder Alice Sockett, an audiobook narrator, has described the risk as "an existential threat to our entire industry," pointing to voice actors and narrators whose entire livelihood is the thing now being copied for free. Fellow campaign founder Peter Caulfield has been sharper still, accusing the government of "allowing AI companies to steal and profit from artists' voices without their consent" while also leaving the door open for scammers to clone people's voices for fraud.

Alongside the letter, the group has lodged a petition on the UK government's official petitions site, the mechanism that forces a parliamentary debate once a threshold of signatures is reached. The ask is specific: write voice ownership into statute, rather than relying on performers to fight infringement case by case through existing image-rights or copyright law, an approach the campaign argues is too slow and too expensive for most working actors.

A government offer that isn't a law

So far, the official response has been a promised consultation — not a bill. That gap is the real story here. Ministers have spent the summer fielding AI complaints from multiple directions: musicians and authors over copyright, financial regulators over systemic risk, and now performers over voice rights, each wanting a specific carve-out rather than a general framework. A consultation buys time to weigh those competing asks against each other, but it also means nothing changes for however long it runs, while cloning tools keep getting cheaper and better.

The UK is not moving first. Denmark has already amended its law to let citizens demand removal of unauthorised AI-generated voice or likeness content and seek compensation. In the US, Tennessee's ELVIS Act — passed in 2024 — gives residents an explicit right against unauthorised AI voice replication. Campaigners are, in effect, asking Britain to catch up rather than lead.

Not every use of a cloned voice is opposed on principle. The letter's authors have pointed to licensed examples — Gene Wilder's estate consenting to a posthumous voice recreation for a Netflix production, for instance — as proof the objection is to theft, not the technology itself. As the open letter puts it: performers are not opposed to artificial intelligence, they are opposed to theft.

What's next

The petition needs 10,000 signatures for a government response and 100,000 to guarantee a Commons debate; where it lands on that scale, and whether the promised consultation produces draft legislation or another delay, will show whether entertainment's voice-rights push gets treated as a genuine gap in AI law or shelved alongside the UK's other pending AI questions — including the transparency rules the EU has just brought into force for chatbots and deepfakes, which Britain has yet to match.
