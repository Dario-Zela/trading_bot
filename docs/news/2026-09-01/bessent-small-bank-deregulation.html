Bessent calls for lighter-touch rules to help small US banks compete

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who told G20 counterparts that post-crisis rules have left small US banks "too small to succeed." — U.S. Department of the Treasury (official portrait)
In one sentence
Bessent told G20 finance ministers that post-2008 rules turned "too big to fail" into "too small to succeed," pushing for lighter regulation of small US banks.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent used the G20 finance ministers' meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, to press a familiar argument in an unfamiliar setting: that the rules written after the 2008 crisis have done more harm than good to America's smallest banks, and it is time to loosen them.

Speaking to counterparts from the world's largest economies on August 31, Bessent framed the case less as an abstract policy debate than as an arithmetic one. Since 2010, he said, the United States has lost more than 3,600 small and community banks — roughly half of those that existed before the post-crisis regulatory wave took hold. "Dodd-Frank was supposed to end 'too big to fail,'" Bessent said. "Instead, it created 'too small to succeed.'"

The pitch to a global audience

Bessent's remarks were notable partly for the venue. G20 finance sessions have historically been government-only affairs; this one, held under the U.S. presidency, included senior executives from JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs alongside the finance ministers and central bank governors. That gave Bessent a bigger stage than a typical domestic banking speech, and he used it to argue that regional and community lenders need "the same chance to succeed as those that serve Wall Street."

His central data point was the wave of bank failures in 2023 — Silicon Valley Bank, Signature Bank and First Republic — which he said showed that heavier supervision had not prevented the largest collapses in the sector's history, even as it burdened smaller institutions that posed no systemic threat.

What has actually moved

The rhetoric sits on top of a policy program that has been building for months rather than a single new proposal. Bessent pointed to a recent easing of capital requirements for community banks, which he said could "unleash tens of billions for reinvestment into small businesses and loans for families." The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency has separately moved to cut assessments on smaller banks, reverse a plan to fold community-bank supervision into the large-bank regime, and replace fixed examination schedules with risk-based ones.

Bessent also cited a rebound in new bank charter applications as evidence the message is landing with the industry, saying more applicants sought charters in the first year of the current administration than in the entirety of the prior one.

Limited specifics, clear direction

What Bessent did not offer in Asheville was a concrete legislative or rulemaking timeline. The G20 remarks read as an escalation of tone — deploying the 3,600-bank figure and the "too small to succeed" line to a global audience — rather than a new mechanism. For markets, that leaves the signal clearer than the substance: this is now a headline priority for Treasury, one that dovetails with the administration's broader deregulatory push across financial services, but the near-term earnings impact for regional lenders depends on rules that have not yet been finalized.

The next marker to watch is the OCC's ongoing rulemaking on community bank capital and examination thresholds, along with whether Congress takes up any of the tailoring proposals Bessent has floated since taking office.
