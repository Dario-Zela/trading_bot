NHS England rolls out free seven-minute cholesterol checks at pharmacies

A blood-pressure cuff and monitor in use — the kind of point-of-care check now being offered alongside cholesterol screening in pharmacies. — Delael / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
NHS England is rolling out free seven-minute pharmacy cholesterol and blood-pressure checks after a pilot found 19% of those tested needed treatment.

Walk into one of roughly 100 pharmacies across England this autumn, and — if you are between 40 and 84, not pregnant, and have not had a blood test in the past year — a pharmacist can now check whether your cholesterol or blood pressure puts you at risk of a heart attack or stroke. The result comes back in about seven minutes, from a finger-prick sample, with no GP referral needed.[^1]

The expansion follows a pilot run jointly by Barts Health NHS Trust, UCLPartners and the charity HEART UK across 61 pharmacies in north London. Between January 2025 and March 2026, the scheme tested 2,493 people. Nineteen per cent — just under one in five — were found to need medication for high cholesterol or blood pressure, and 88 people started taking statins as a direct result.[^2] Chris Laing, UCLPartners' chief executive, put the finding starkly: one in five people tested were at high risk of a heart attack or stroke within the next decade, often without knowing it.[^1]

Why a pharmacy, and why now

The logic is arithmetic rather than novel: cardiovascular disease remains one of England's leading causes of preventable death, and statins are cheap once someone is identified as needing them. Catching risk before a first cardiac event is far less costly, in both money and lives, than treating one after the fact. NHS England says the wider ambition — of which this scheme is one strand — is to cut premature deaths from heart disease and stroke by a quarter over the next ten years.[^1]

Pharmacies also solve a access problem GP surgeries do not: no appointment, no wait for blood work to come back from a lab. David Webb, NHS England's chief pharmaceutical officer, called community pharmacy "a vital first line of defence," while Dr Rani Khatib, the health service's cardiovascular disease clinical adviser, urged that "everyone should strive to know their numbers" — a reference to the NHS's guidance that total cholesterol should sit below 5 mmol/L and non-HDL cholesterol below 4 mmol/L.[^1] Dr Sotiris Antoniou of Barts Health, who ran the original pilot, said the model "has the potential to improve outcomes and reduce inequalities," pointing to the fact that people who rarely see a GP often do visit a pharmacy.[^2]

Caution is warranted on scale, however. NHS England has not published a national target for how many of the roughly 100 launch pharmacies' catchment populations will actually take up the offer, nor a costed projection of how many statin starts the wider rollout might produce — the 19 per cent figure comes from a self-selected group of people who chose to walk into a pharmacy, not a randomised population sample, so it should not be read as the prevalence of undiagnosed risk across all adults aged 40 to 84. Health minister Alison McGovern called the approach "exactly the type of innovation we want to see," but the pilot itself continues to run until spring 2027, when a fuller evaluation is due.[^1][^2]

That evaluation — covering uptake, cost per statin start and any measurable effect on referrals to secondary care — is the next real data point. Until then, the rollout is best understood as an expansion of an unusually promising early signal, not yet a proven population-level intervention.
