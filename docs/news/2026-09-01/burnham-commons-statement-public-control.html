Burnham pledges 'more public control' in first Commons statement as PM, drawing 'war on London' charge

Andy Burnham's official portrait as Prime Minister, taken at 10 Downing Street in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham told MPs public ownership can drive growth but named no sectors, as Cleverly accused him of a regional 'war on London'.

Andy Burnham stood at the despatch box on Tuesday for the first time in sixteen years, no longer the backbench MP for Leigh but the occupant of Downing Street, and offered the Commons a proposition simple enough to fit on a placard: the state should own and run more of the country's essential services, and doing so will make Britain grow faster.

It was Burnham's first substantive test since becoming prime minister on 20 July, five weeks after winning the Makerfield by-election that gave the former Greater Manchester mayor a route back into Parliament and, in short order, to the top of his party. The statement was billed by aides as a chance to move past the mechanics of his unusual ascent — a leadership contest in which he was nominated by more than 94% of Labour MPs and faced no rival — and set out what he actually intends to do with the job.

'Public control' as growth strategy

Burnham's pitch inverts three decades of cross-party orthodoxy. Where governments since the 1980s have generally treated privatisation and deregulation as the default route to efficiency, Burnham told MPs that restoring public oversight of services such as water, energy and transport would lower long-term costs to the state and could itself be "an engine for economic growth." A No. 10 source framed the message in blunter terms ahead of the statement: "Andy is optimistic about our country's future. He has a clear plan for how we turn things round."

The difficulty, for MPs trying to scrutinise the plan and for markets trying to price it, is that the optimism arrived well ahead of the detail. Burnham did not name a sector for nationalisation, put a number on the cost of expanded public ownership, or say whether it means outright acquisition, tighter regulation, or something short of either. He paired the ownership message with a set of nearer-term, more conventional measures — a cut to VAT on energy bills taking effect in October, reduced business rates for pubs and clubs, and £110 million of new prison capacity alongside an early-release scheme to relieve overcrowding. Those are costed, scheduled policies. "Public control" as growth strategy, by contrast, is for now closer to a governing instinct than a programme, and Burnham told the House it would be made "a priority for the Government over coming months" — an admission, in effect, that the specifics are still being worked out.

For investors in utilities, water and infrastructure — sectors that have spent much of the past two years already jumpy about political risk — the vagueness cuts both ways. It rules out an immediate repricing event, because there is no target list to react to. But it also means the overhang doesn't clear: any company operating essential services in Britain now has to weigh the possibility that Downing Street's default answer to a service failure or a price spike is more state involvement, not less, without yet knowing which companies or contracts that might touch.

Cleverly's opening shot

The statement also became a proxy battle in a fight that has nothing directly to do with public ownership. James Cleverly, the former foreign and home secretary who quit the shadow cabinet on 29 August to run for London mayor, used the run-up to Burnham's Commons appearance to accuse the prime minister of waging "a war on London."

"He's got a war with London, and London needs someone to fight its corner," Cleverly said in a weekend LBC interview, describing Burnham as "basically very, very anti-London" and accusing him of "stripping hundreds of millions of pounds out of the budget of London councils."

Cleverly's real target is Burnham's devolution agenda rather than public ownership specifically, but the two threads are hard to separate in his argument: a prime minister who made his name as "King of the North" for defending Greater Manchester through the pandemic is now, in Cleverly's telling, tilting both devolved funding and state intervention toward the regions that elected him, at London's expense. Cleverly offered no figures to substantiate the claim of centralising power "in his own backyard," and Downing Street has not yet responded in kind — Labour's attention on Tuesday was trained on Kemi Badenoch's shadow cabinet reshuffle rather than Cleverly's mayoral pitch. But the accusation lands at a moment when Burnham has given critics an easy way to make it stick: a growth strategy built around greater public control, described only in the abstract, is a wide enough target that any region or sector left out of the detail can plausibly claim to be the one being shortchanged.

What the vagueness leaves open

What ties the two threads together is the same absence. Burnham has committed his government to a philosophy — more public ownership, more devolved power to the regions — without yet naming who wins and who loses under it. That ambiguity bought him a largely uncontested first outing at the despatch box, since there is little concrete for opponents to attack beyond the framing itself. It will not last. Burnham faces his first Prime Minister's Questions against Badenoch on Wednesday, where the absence of sector detail is likely to be probed directly, and the government's stated intention to make public control policy "over coming months" sets its own deadline for turning the rhetoric into a list. Until that list exists, utilities and infrastructure investors are left pricing a risk they cannot yet locate, and London's political class is left arguing about a redistribution nobody has quantified.

Gilt markets, already sitting near multi-week highs on separate rate-hike concerns, have not moved on the statement — a sign that traders are treating "public control" as rhetoric until it acquires a price tag. The next marker is Wednesday's PMQs, followed by the autumn policy announcements Downing Street has pencilled in as the moment the ownership agenda gets specific.
