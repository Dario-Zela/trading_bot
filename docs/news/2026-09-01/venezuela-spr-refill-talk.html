STANDING WATCH · VENEZUELA OIL · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-01 · 3 min read

Trump touts 'fairly quick' SPR refill with Venezuelan crude

An oil well in Monagas state, Venezuela, one of the regions covered by NABEP's expanded drilling rights. — Daniel Añez Scott / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Trump says Venezuelan crude will refill the SPR 'fairly quickly,' but sanctions status, heavy-crude chemistry and years of missing infrastructure argue otherwise.

President Trump said this week that Venezuelan crude will soon begin refilling the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, framing a newly restructured oil-supply arrangement with Caracas as a fix for a reserve he has repeatedly called dangerously depleted.

"One of the things I am going to do with the Venezuelan Oil is fill up the Strategic National Reserves," Trump wrote on Truth Social, adding that the SPR had been left "virtually emptied" under Joe Biden. The "topping out" process, he said, would begin "very shortly" and amounted to "a Gift from Venezuela to the People of the United States."

The reserve does need topping up. It held roughly 290 million barrels as of August 21 — the lowest level in nearly 44 years, and down close to 30% since January 2025, a slide that includes a 172-million-barrel drawdown under Trump's own administration.

The oil in question is meant to come through NABEP, the Venezuelan operator controlled by businessman Alejandro Betancourt, which has been granted 100-year rights over 17 oil fields holding a claimed 65 billion barrels of proven reserves. Under terms reported by the Wall Street Journal and others, NABEP is committing $100 billion to new infrastructure and plans to deploy 52 drilling rigs — 23 already sourced from Helmerich & Payne, Precision Drilling and Patterson-UTI — with a target of 1.5 million barrels a day from the existing fields plus eight new greenfield blocks. The Pentagon would take a 35% ownership stake in the venture; the State Department gets a guarantee to buy 20% of output at cost. Venezuela's interim government has pitched itself as pocketing roughly $19 a barrel, a sum it says could total $209 billion annually at full output.

The gap between rigs and reserve

That arithmetic assumes production that does not yet exist. The 65-billion-barrel figure describes oil still underground; Venezuela's actual output was about 1.25 million barrels a day last month, and Rystad Energy estimates it would take $180 billion over a decade to lift that meaningfully. Tankers have faced waits of up to 30 days at Venezuelan ports because of power outages and crude-quality problems. And most Venezuelan crude is extra-heavy, typically well below the roughly 22° API gravity the SPR's salt caverns are built to hold — cargoes bound for Bryan Mound or the other Gulf Coast sites would likely need blending before they qualify.

Sanctions add a second layer of ambiguity. Washington eased restrictions on Venezuela's oil sector after Nicolás Maduro was captured by US forces in January and Delcy Rodríguez, his former vice-president, was installed as interim president. But the general licenses issued by the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control name specific companies — Chevron, BP, Shell, Eni, Repsol and, since February, Maurel & Prom — and officials have said the broader Venezuela sanctions regime remains intact. Whether NABEP itself operates under a clear license, or under looser understandings tied to the new government, has not been spelled out publicly. Venezuela's constitution also reserves petroleum resources to the state, leaving the interim administration's legal authority to hand a foreign-backed operator century-long field rights an open question that has not been tested in court.

None of that makes the announcement meaningless — a Pentagon equity stake and a guaranteed federal offtake, if they hold up, would mark a real shift in how Washington treats Venezuelan crude. But "fairly quickly" is doing a lot of work in a deal that still needs wells drilled, pipelines rebuilt, port capacity restored and a legal framework nailed down. The next marker to watch is whether the Department of Energy issues an actual solicitation naming Venezuelan crude as an SPR purchase source, and whether OFAC extends a specific license to NABEP rather than leaving the arrangement in the current legal fog.
