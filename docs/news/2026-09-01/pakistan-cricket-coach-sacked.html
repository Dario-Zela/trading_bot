Pakistan cricket board sacks head coach, sends home seven players after England series collapse

Mohammad Rizwan, dropped from Pakistan's Test squad after scores of 12, 41, 7 and 1 against England. — Wikimedia Commons / Smile to Jannah (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
PCB sacked coach Sarfaraz Ahmed and sent home seven players, including Rizwan and Imam-ul-Haq, mid-tour in England — drawing rebukes from Afridi and Malik.

Pakistan's cricket board has never been accused of patience, but even by its own standards this was fast. Two days after England wrapped up the second Test at Lord's to take an unassailable 2-0 series lead, the Pakistan Cricket Board tore up its Test set-up: head coach Sarfaraz Ahmed was removed, bowling coach Umar Gul was sent home, and seven players — including two of the side's most established names, wicketkeeper-batter Mohammad Rizwan and opener Imam-ul-Haq — were released from the squad before a ball has been bowled in the third and final Test at Edgbaston, which starts September 9.

It is the kind of overhaul boards usually save for the inquest after a tour ends, not the middle of one. That the PCB couldn't wait even ten more days says less about this particular defeat than about an institution that has cycled through coaches and selection panels for years and has now decided the emergency is bad enough to warrant surgery mid-operation.

From bad to worse in ten days

The tour has been brutal on the scoreboard. Pakistan lost the opener at Headingley by an innings and 103 runs, then were beaten by 194 runs at Lord's — a margin comfortable enough for England to wrap the series with a match to spare. Rizwan's returns with the bat over the series — 12, 41, 7 and 1 — did little to justify his place, and the PCB's statement announcing the changes offered no explanation beyond the results themselves, according to Dawn, which noted the board did not respond to requests for comment.

Sarfaraz Ahmed's exit is the more startling piece of it. He had been in the head coach's chair for barely two series, presiding over five defeats in six Tests — a dismal record, but not an unusual one for a Pakistan side that has struggled overseas for years. Removing a coach that quickly, before he has had a full cycle to build anything, is normally a last resort. Here it looks more like the PCB deciding that visible action, any action, was preferable to riding out the rest of the tour.

His replacement, Mike Hesson, was already inside the building as Pakistan's white-ball coach — the former New Zealand coach who guided the Black Caps to the 2015 World Cup final and has run Pakistan's limited-overs side since 2025. Former Australia seamer Ashley Noffke steps up from the white-ball set-up to replace Gul as bowling coach. Notably, Hesson has dropped Rizwan before: as the architect of Pakistan's white-ball rebuild, he removed him as ODI captain and left him out of T20 selections earlier this year. The wicketkeeper's Test career is now in the hands of the same coach who has already decided, twice, that Pakistan is better off without him in the side.

Five uncapped faces for a series decider

The casualty list beyond Rizwan and Imam-ul-Haq includes Salman Ali Agha, Aamir Jamal, Muhammad Awais Zafar and Khurram Shahzad. The most contentious omission is seamer Ali Usman, who took 5 for 95 at Headingley and finished the series with six wickets at an average of 13.33 — numbers that, on their own, would usually earn a bowler a longer run rather than a plane ticket home.

In their place, Pakistan have recalled Saim Ayub and Abdullah Fazal and handed debuts, or the promise of them, to five uncapped players: Arafat Minhas, Mohammad Imran Jnr, Saad Baig, Mohammad Imran Randhawa and Razaullah. It is an unusually large influx of unfamiliar names to throw into a dead-rubber Test in England, against an attack that has just won by an innings and by 194 runs in successive matches.

Afridi and Malik say the quiet part out loud

The reaction from Pakistan's own cricketing establishment has been unusually blunt. Former captain Shahid Afridi called the timing of Sarfaraz's sacking "the most shocking decision ever by a selection committee which includes so many experienced selectors," and questioned the logic of drafting white-ball-oriented players into a Test XI in English conditions: "Even the replacements make no sense — T20 players have been included for a Test match in England? What is this thinking and approach?"

Shoaib Malik was equally pointed, objecting less to the substance of the changes than to how the players were discarded. "The players dropped from the squad are still our national cricketers who have served Pakistan," he wrote. "Phrases like 'sent on the first flight back' are disrespectful." His broader complaint — that accountability should start with the system and the selectors who built it, not with players given limited opportunity — echoes a criticism that has followed the PCB through several coaching changes over the past decade.

What it signals

Mid-tour overhauls tend to say more about the organisation making them than about the team on the field. Pakistan have now had a rotating cast of head coaches, chairmen and selection committees for years, and this is the pattern repeating: results turn bad, and rather than absorb the loss and plan properly for the next assignment, the board reaches for a lever it can pull immediately, regardless of whether that lever helps the specific match in front of it. Edgbaston is already lost as a series decider; what is really being decided is who gets to shape Pakistan's Test team through the next assignment against South Africa later this year, and whether the players sent home this week — Rizwan chief among them, at 34 with his white-ball place already under Hesson's scrutiny — have played their last Test for the country.
