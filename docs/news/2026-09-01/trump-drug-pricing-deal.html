White House reaches new drug-pricing deal with nine pharmaceutical firms

Prescription medication of the kind covered by the new pricing agreements. — J. Troha / National Cancer Institute, public domain via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nine drugmakers signed Medicaid pricing deals with the White House, but no per-drug figures or enforcement terms were disclosed, and markets shrugged.

Nine more pharmaceutical companies have signed on to the White House's drug-pricing push, the administration announced from the Oval Office on Monday — the latest expansion of a program that now touches more than a quarter of the industry but still tells markets remarkably little about what it actually costs drugmakers.

The new signatories are Alcon, Astellas Pharma, BeOne Medicines, BridgeBio, CSL, Kyowa Kirin, Sun Pharma, Teva Pharmaceuticals and UCB. Under the deals, each commits to selling covered drugs to state Medicaid programs at "most-favored-nation" prices — matching the lowest rate the company charges in any peer developed market. The list of conditions treated runs from hemophilia and Parkinson's disease to macular degeneration, glaucoma and several cancers.

That brings the total roster of companies with MFN agreements to 26, which the administration says now covers 89% of the US branded-drug market. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz joined President Trump for the announcement. Trump put a number on it: "These agreements are projected to save Americans more than $600 billion over a very short period of time," citing Council of Economic Advisers modeling that spans the next decade.

The companies also pledged a collective $19.6 billion in near-term US manufacturing investment. Four of them — Astellas, Sun Pharma, Teva and UCB — agreed to donate active pharmaceutical ingredients to a federal strategic reserve meant to cut reliance on foreign API supply: UCB is contributing 163 tons of the anticonvulsant levetiracetam, Sun Pharma 71.4 tons of the antibiotic clindamycin, Teva 45 tons of metronidazole, and Astellas 25 kilograms of the immunosuppressant tacrolimus.

The numbers that aren't there

What the fact sheet doesn't include is the part traders would actually price: per-drug dollar figures, rebate mechanics, or an enforcement timeline. Medicaid already buys at close to the lowest available rate in most cases, and two of the nine signatories — Sun Pharma and Teva — are primarily generics makers with limited room to move on price in the first place. Only one of the nine, BridgeBio, is US-headquartered.

Advocacy groups on both sides of the debate flagged the gap. Peter Maybarduk of Public Citizen called the round "a distraction from the administration's failed plan to lower US drug prices," arguing there is no public evidence any company has followed through on commitments from earlier rounds. Merith Basey of Patients for Affordable Drugs made a similar point from a reform angle, saying patients need "transparent, enforceable measures," not "short-term, voluntary agreements whose terms remain secret."

Equities barely moved on the news. Teva slipped about 1.1% before recovering some ground after hours; BeOne fell roughly 2%; Alcon, BridgeBio and the rest were largely flat — consistent with a sector that has increasingly priced MFN deals as a bounded, largely symbolic risk rather than a margin threat.

The next test of whether any of this has teeth is Medicare, not Medicaid: the administration has not released terms for how — or whether — these nine deals extend to the larger federal program, nor set a date for doing so. With midterms approaching, expect more signings before more specifics.
