Shein shares slide up to 10% in long-delayed Hong Kong debut

Exchange Square in Central, Hong Kong, home to the Hong Kong Stock Exchange where Shein made its trading debut. — Wikimedia Commons / WiNG (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Shein raised $1.7bn at a $26bn Hong Kong valuation — a quarter of its 2022 peak — but shares slid up to 10% as growth collapsed to near zero.

Shein's shares fell as much as 10% on their long-awaited Hong Kong trading debut on Tuesday, a rough welcome for a company that spent nearly two years and two rejected listing venues trying to get to a public market at all.

The fast-fashion group priced its initial public offering at HK$48.56 a share, raising $1.7bn and valuing the company at roughly $26.3bn — a fraction of the near-$100bn private valuation it commanded in 2022. Shares briefly touched HK$43.72 after the opening bell before paring some of the loss. Founder and chief executive Sky Xu and finance chief Leigh Gui rang the gong at the Hong Kong Stock Exchange to mark the occasion; the market's response was closer to a shrug than a cheer.

The only door left open

Shein's path to Hong Kong runs through two failed attempts elsewhere. A New York listing stalled amid pressure from US lawmakers, who wanted the Securities and Exchange Commission to require Shein to certify it does not use forced labour from China's Xinjiang region before any shares could trade — a demand tied to the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act and a 2023 congressional commission finding of "credible allegations" of underpaid and forced labour in Shein's supply chain. The company has repeatedly denied sourcing cotton from Xinjiang.

A London listing fared little better. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority approved a prospectus, but China's securities regulator, the CSRC, balked at the disclosure language describing Shein's ties to Xinjiang, and the standoff dragged on for more than 18 months before the plan was shelved. Hong Kong, where Beijing signed off on the listing in July, became the only venue where Shein's Chinese origins were an asset rather than an obstacle — even as the retailer, now headquartered in Singapore, has tried for years to distance its public image from mainland China.

The growth the prospectus couldn't hide

The bigger problem for investors on debut day wasn't geopolitics — it was the numbers. Shein's revenue grew 41.1% in 2023 and 20.7% in 2024; research firm Coresight expects growth of roughly 2% this year. In the first quarter of 2026, revenue rose just 1.1% to $9.05bn, and the company swung to a $99m loss from a $395m profit a year earlier. US revenue fell 14.3% after Washington ended the de minimis exemption that let packages under $800 enter duty-free — the loophole that underwrote Shein's direct-from-China shipping model. The European Union has since imposed its own €3 charge on low-value parcels, hitting a region that accounted for close to a third of Shein's 2025 revenue.

"Public investors are no longer paying for hyper-growth," said Winston Ma, an adjunct professor at NYU School of Law and former head of North America for China's sovereign wealth fund CIC. "They are underwriting a mature cross-border platform that must now defend its profit margins against trade tariffs, higher compliance costs, and regulatory scrutiny in both the US and China."

Morgan Stanley, one of the IPO's sponsors alongside Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan, had pitched investors on a "fair value range" of $39bn to $52bn, based on 18 to 24 times projected 2027 earnings and comparisons with listed rivals Inditex and H&M. The market's actual verdict, at roughly $26bn and falling, suggests buyers weren't persuaded.

A familiar wall

What Shein is running into is less a Shein-specific crisis than the standard trajectory for e-commerce platforms that scaled on cheap goods, cheap shipping and a regulatory loophole. Amazon, Wayfair and Temu have each, in turn, hit some version of the same ceiling: the easy growth from expanding into new geographies and categories eventually runs out, and what's left is a slower, thinner-margin retail business that has to be valued like one. Shein's supply-chain speed — design to shelf in as little as ten days, by some estimates — is still a genuine advantage. It is just not, on its own, worth 20-times-plus growth multiples anymore.

The near-term catalysts are narrow. Shein will report full first-half 2026 results in the coming weeks, giving the market its first post-listing read on whether the US and EU parcel-fee hit is a one-off adjustment or a durable drag. Beyond that, watch whether trading stabilises above or below the IPO price in the next few sessions — a sustained break below HK$44 would signal that Tuesday's slide was the market recalibrating, not just first-day jitters.
