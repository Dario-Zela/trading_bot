UN urges Meta-style reforms across social media platforms

UN High Commissioner for Human Rights Volker Türk, who called for Meta-style safety design to be applied "across the industry." — Dean Calma / IAEA, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UN urges Meta's child-safety redesign become industry-wide as UK and EU regulators separately escalate platform enforcement.

The United Nations wants what Meta just agreed to do under legal duress to become the industry norm — not the exception won only after a lawsuit.

On 28 August, UN human rights chief Volker Türk called on governments and tech companies to apply the same child-safety design changes Meta accepted in its roughly $18bn settlement with 51 US states, territories and Washington DC across the entire social media industry. "Children should not have to wait for a lawsuit to be safe online," Türk said. "The design changes that Meta has now agreed to implement on its social media apps align with what my office has been calling for. Safe design should be applied across the industry."

The Meta settlement, reached after states alleged the company knowingly designed features to hook young users while concealing the risks, requires the company to roll out default two-hour daily time limits for under-18s, night-mode cutoffs, restrictions on features linked to compulsive use, hidden like-and-reaction counts for younger users, and stronger parental controls. Türk's office had already laid out ten similar principles in guidance published in May titled "Getting Children's Safety Online Right" — among them, maximum data protection as a default setting, a ban on micro-targeted advertising aimed at children, age limits on addictive design features and AI chatbots, and independent oversight bodies with real legal teeth. His argument now is straightforward: rather than wait for the next multibillion-dollar verdict to force a redesign, governments should legislate the standard directly.

Two regulators already moving

The UN has no power to compel anything — it can only recommend. But the report lands as the UK and EU are separately hardening enforcement in ways that look a lot like what Türk is describing, without waiting for UN backing.

The European Commission has issued preliminary findings that both TikTok and Meta breached Digital Services Act transparency obligations, and separately found TikTok in preliminary breach of child-safety rules for letting minors' accounts default to public visibility — exposing under-18s' content to any user, including people without a TikTok account. If confirmed, TikTok faces a fine of up to 6% of its global annual turnover. In December, the Commission handed X a €120m penalty — its first full DSA non-compliance decision — over a paid "verification" checkmark it judged deceptive, a threadbare advertising repository, and blocked researcher access to platform data.

Ofcom, meanwhile, has been grinding through enforcement of the UK's Online Safety Act: five enforcement programmes and 21 investigations covering 69 sites and apps since March 2025, with fresh penalties this year against adult-content platforms over failed age-assurance checks, and live investigations into services including Chat-Avenue and Teen-Chat still open as of late August.

Why this matters more as a pattern than a policy

None of this adds up to a single binding global regime — the UN report is persuasion, not law, and the UK and EU are each enforcing their own separate statutes. But the direction is the same in all three: default settings, not user opt-ins, are the correct place to build in child protection, and companies get punished for choosing the design that maximises engagement over the one that minimises harm. For platform operators the pattern of overlapping expectations across a state settlement, an EU statute and a UK regulator raises the cost of designing one architecture and shipping it everywhere, since the strictest jurisdiction increasingly sets the practical floor.

The next test is whether the Commission's preliminary TikTok finding hardens into an actual fine, and whether Ofcom's open investigations produce more penalties before the year is out. Neither report will cite the UN by name — but both will be read as evidence of whether Türk's call for an industry-wide standard is starting to happen anyway, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, without anyone drafting the treaty.
