Kushner expresses regret over shelved World Cup ticket sell-off plan

Joshua Kushner, whose Thrive Eternal fund was set to lead the now-abandoned FIFA Forward Enterprise investment. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Josh Kushner says he regrets backing FIFA's shelved $4.2bn World Cup stake sale, which collapsed within days under a UEFA-led revolt.

The apology arrived a month late and from the wrong Kushner, in the strictest sense — though in the family business sense, exactly on schedule. Joshua Kushner, the venture capitalist brother of Donald Trump's son-in-law Jared, broke his silence this week on the collapse of a plan that would have handed his firm a slice of the World Cup itself.

"Had we known what this would devolve into, we would not have gotten involved," Kushner said, in his first public comments since FIFA's so-called Forward Enterprise scheme blew up in July. "We failed to appreciate the political dynamics of global football."

The deal that wasn't

The plan, unveiled by FIFA president Gianni Infantino in late July, was audacious even by football's recent standards of audacity. FIFA would bundle the commercial guts of the World Cup — broadcast rights, sponsorship, ticketing, licensing — into a new entity called FIFA Forward Enterprise, then sell a 20% stake to outside investors for as much as $4.2bn. Thrive Eternal, the fund founded by Kushner's Thrive Capital, was positioned as the lead buyer, valuing the new unit at around $20bn.

Infantino framed it as redistribution: the proceeds, he said, would lift funding for FIFA's 211 member associations from £5.9m to £14.7m over the 2027–2030 cycle. Kushner still defends that logic. "We stand behind the motivations of FFE," he said this week — the aim, in his telling, was simply to "direct more capital and equity equally" across the sport's poorer corners.

Who killed it

Nobody in football bought the framing. UEFA's 55 members voted to boycott FIFA events over the plan within days, calling the World Cup "not an investment product" and accusing Infantino of undervaluing the tournament — UEFA put its worth closer to £15bn than the sum implied by FIFA's own numbers — while bypassing any competitive process. CONCACAF and the Asian confederation lined up alongside them. In Washington, House Judiciary Democrats piled on with a sharper political edge, casting the arrangement as a "kickback" that put FIFA into direct business with the Trump family. FIFA shelved the plan within days of it becoming public. UEFA has since sought subpoenas for testimony and documents from Kushner and Thrive, and is weighing a formal complaint against Infantino.

What it leaves behind

For Kushner personally, the fallout looks more like a rounding error than a reckoning: less than a month after the FFE scheme died, he and former Disney chief Bob Iger struck a deal to buy the Los Angeles Lakers at a $12.5bn valuation. For FIFA and Infantino, the damage sits closer to the bone — a rare moment where the federation's own confederations turned on a monetisation scheme before it could be signed, sealed and spent.

The broader signal is the one worth sitting with as the World Cup itself approaches kickoff next June. Sports properties have spent two decades getting more comfortable selling equity stakes to outside capital — franchises, leagues, even hospitality rights. This was the moment a governing body tried to do it with the tournament's entire commercial core, and its own membership stopped it cold within a week. Whether that holds as a hard ceiling on privatisation, or just a badly timed pitch to the wrong investor at the wrong political moment, is the question UEFA's subpoenas may end up answering before the opening whistle does.
