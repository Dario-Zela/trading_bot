Nepal rescuers drill into flood-blocked hydropower tunnels as hundreds remain trapped

A Landsat satellite image captures the scar left by the glacier-collapse flood as it tore down Nepal's Trishuli valley on 26 August 2026. — Landsat 9 / USGS, processed by Wikimedia Commons user Chorchapu
In one sentence
Rescuers backed by Chinese and Indian tunnel experts have freed 350 of roughly 933 hydropower workers trapped by a Himalayan glacier-collapse flood that has killed over 950.

The mountain came down on a Wednesday morning, and by the time anyone in Kathmandu understood what had happened, a wall of ice, rock and mud had already travelled nearly 100 kilometres down the Trishuli and Bhote Koshi valleys. It buried construction camps, swept away a border crossing with China, and entombed hundreds of hydropower workers inside the tunnels they had been building into the mountainside.

Six days on, the death toll in Nepal has climbed past 950, with more than 4,000 people still missing and Chinese authorities reporting a further 16 dead and hundreds unaccounted for across the border in Tibet. The Nepal Red Cross estimates more than 90,000 people have been affected. But the number holding the country's attention hour by hour is smaller and more specific: of roughly 933 hydropower workers who were trapped underground when the flood hit, about 350 have now been pulled out alive, most of them from a single tunnel at the Upper Trishuli-1 project in Rasuwa district.

A glacier that behaved like an earthquake

Scientists studying satellite imagery say the trigger was a bedrock collapse beneath a glacier on Langtang Lirung, a 7,234-metre peak in the Langtang Himal roughly 64 kilometres north of Kathmandu. A slab of rock and ice broke free at around 5,200 metres, fell some 1,200 metres, and slammed into the Lende Khola with enough force to register as a magnitude-5.2 seismic event on instruments worldwide. What followed was not a conventional flood but a debris flow — a slurry of boulders, ice and mud moving fast enough to erase the Gyirong Port crossing into Tibet within seven minutes and to keep gathering material as it funnelled down the Trishuli and Bhote Koshi rivers into Rasuwa, Nuwakot, Dhading, Gorkha and Chitwan districts.

"A large amount of debris has been deposited, and that is posing us a big challenge to open the tunnels," Nepal Army spokesman Raja Ram Basnet said as crews resumed drilling after rain and rising river levels forced repeated pauses.

The run-of-river hydropower stations that line these valleys were built partly underground by design — their intake tunnels and powerhouses cut into rock to shield them from exactly this kind of surface hazard. That engineering choice became a trap. At Upper Trishuli-1, a 216 MW plant backed by South Korea's Korea South-East Power and the state-run Nepal Water and Energy Development Company, mud and debris sealed workers inside as effectively as it protected the turbines above.

Drilling through rock, one metre at a time

Nepal's army, working with roughly 10,000 troops and civilian volunteers, has been boring down through the tops of the fortified tunnels to reach pockets of trapped workers, pumping in air and lowering cameras before attempting to cut a way out. Progress has been slow and repeatedly interrupted: rescuers broke through into the upper section of one tunnel at Trishuli 3A on a Saturday evening, only to lose ground again overnight when rain swelled the river and reflooded cleared sections. Four bodies have been recovered from three mud-filled tunnels so far, according to the army.

What distinguishes this operation from Nepal's earlier flood disasters is who showed up to help. Kathmandu formally asked both Beijing and New Delhi to send tunnel-rescue specialists, and both did — an unusual instance of practical cooperation between two governments that otherwise compete hard for influence in Nepal. China has deployed more than 2,100 rescue personnel on its side of the border, sent nine tunnel-rescue experts into Nepal, shared satellite reconnaissance, and pledged roughly $33m in aid, alongside airdrops of equipment to crews cut off by landslides. South Korean specialists, tied to their country's investment in the Trishuli projects, have joined as well. Nepali officials have been careful to frame the assistance as narrowly technical — filling specific gaps in drilling and tunnel-rescue expertise — rather than a broader opening to either power, but the sight of Chinese and Indian teams working the same disaster site is rare enough to be noted in itself.

A hydropower sector built on high-risk ground

Hydropower supplies roughly two-fifths of Nepal's domestic electricity and has become one of its few significant export earners, with India buying more than a gigawatt of Nepali capacity and a further agreement in place to import 10,000 megawatts over the next decade — a trade relationship both governments have promoted as a rare economic win-win between the neighbours. That model depends on dozens of run-of-river plants strung along glacier-fed rivers in exactly the terrain that just failed. Nepali authorities now estimate the flood knocked out about 431 megawatts of operating capacity and damaged a further 470 megawatts under construction, spread across roughly a dozen projects — a meaningful bite out of a national grid still expanding to meet demand at home and export commitments abroad. Kathmandu has said it may need to lean on imported Indian power to cover the shortfall through the recovery period, a reversal of the export relationship it has spent a decade building.

The deeper unease is glaciological rather than financial. Langtang Lirung's glaciers, like others across the Himalaya, have been retreating and destabilising for decades as the region warms faster than the global average — a trend flagged repeatedly by climate scientists who study glacial lake outburst floods as a growing hazard for exactly this kind of high-altitude infrastructure. Nepal built its hydropower boom in the rivers below these glaciers because that is where the water and the elevation drop are. There was, by design, nowhere else to put it.

What comes next

Rescue teams say roughly 100 workers remain unaccounted for at the Upper Trishuli tunnel alone, with the broader search across a dozen sites still active as of Tuesday. The next fixed point is less dramatic than a rescue but arguably more consequential: Nepal's disaster authority has yet to publish a full damage assessment for the hydropower sector, which will determine how much capacity returns online before the dry season, when the country typically needs Indian imports even without a disaster. Any finding by investigators on early-warning gaps — Nepali officials have acknowledged there was no advance information-sharing arrangement with China on the glacier's condition — will also shape how the two governments handle the next one.
