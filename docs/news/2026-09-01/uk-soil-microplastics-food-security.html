Rising microplastics in soil flagged as UK food-security threat

A freshly ploughed field in Cornwall — UK farm soils are among the most microplastic-contaminated in Europe, researchers say. — Tony Atkin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK MPs say microplastics from sewage sludge have built up in British farmland enough to threaten food security, and regulation hasn't caught up.

Britain's fields are absorbing more than fertiliser. A report from a cross-party group of MPs, published this week, says microplastic particles — shed from packaging, textiles and farm plastics, then spread across farmland largely via treated sewage sludge — have built up in UK soils to a degree that now threatens the country's capacity to grow food.

The report, from the All-Party Parliamentary Group (APPG) on Microplastics, is blunt about where Britain stands. UK agricultural soils are among the most contaminated in Europe, it says, a legacy of decades spent using sludge from wastewater treatment as a cheap, nutrient-rich fertiliser. That sludge carries with it whatever synthetic fibres and fragments passed through the sewage system first — and those particles don't break down. They accumulate.

"We urgently need a preventative approach focused on reducing microplastic releases at source," said Alberto Costa, the Conservative MP for South Leicestershire who chairs the APPG, framing the issue as one of both public health and food security. The group is serviced by the National Federation of Women's Institutes, which has campaigned against plastic pollution for several years, and drew on research from the Revolution Plastics Institute at the University of Portsmouth.

What the evidence shows

The case against microplastics in soil has hardened considerably in recent years, moving the debate beyond marine pollution — where the problem has driven bans on microbeads and packaging rules — into agriculture. A 2023 study published in Communications Earth & Environment found that fertiliser use was a substantial driver of rising microplastic concentrations in English soils between 1966 and 2022, with some samples containing more than 8,000 particles per kilogram. Separately, research reviewed by the Guardian's environment desk this year found that microplastics can interfere with photosynthesis, with modelling suggesting global yield losses for wheat, rice and maize of between 4% and 14% — losses researchers compared in scale to those already inflicted by climate change.

Prof Cressida Bowyer, who directs the Revolution Plastics Institute, and colleague Dr Antaya March both contributed to the APPG's findings. Their broader concern, echoed in the report, is one of accumulation and uncertainty: more than 16,000 chemicals are used in plastic production, of which roughly 4,000 are known to be hazardous and most of the rest have never been tested for safety in soil or food systems.

The regulatory gap

What makes the warning politically pointed is the contrast with marine policy, where the UK has moved — microbead bans, packaging extended producer responsibility, calls for a global plastics treaty. Soil has no equivalent framework. Sewage sludge spread on farmland is regulated as a fertiliser and waste product, not screened for microplastic content, and there is no national monitoring system tracking how contamination is changing over time.

The APPG's report sets out five recommendations: legally binding targets to cut microplastic pollution, regulation of the largest sources of releases, extended producer responsibility across the plastics lifecycle, tighter rules on wastewater and sewage sludge destined for farmland, and a coordinated national monitoring system. None of these exist in UK law today. Defra has been contacted for comment on the findings, according to reporting on the report's release.

Why it matters beyond the field

The economic stakes are still speculative — no one has yet quantified a UK-specific yield hit — but the direction of the evidence is consistent enough that campaigners argue government cannot wait for certainty before acting. If soil contamination follows the marine playbook, expect the same sequence: mounting academic evidence, parliamentary pressure, then years of lag before binding rules arrive. For now, the report puts a marker down that regulators will find harder to ignore than it was even two years ago.

The next test is whether Defra responds substantively, and whether the government's forthcoming soil health strategy — still in development — incorporates microplastics rather than treating them as a future problem.
