Oil tops $86 as US and Iran trade strikes near the Strait of Hormuz

A supertanker under way — vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz face renewed mine risk after Monday's escalation. — U.S. Navy photo by Aviation Warfare Systems Operator 2nd Class William S. Stevens / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oil topped $86 after US strikes on Iran's Larak Island and Iranian retaliation against Jordan and the UAE, with a supertanker set ablaze by mines in the Strait of Hormuz.

Crude oil pushed through $86 a barrel on Monday after the United States and Iran traded direct military strikes for the first time in a month, and a supertanker caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz after hitting naval mines — the clearest sign yet that the world's most important oil chokepoint remains a live front in the conflict, not a dormant one.

WTI crude touched $86.22 a barrel, up roughly 2.8% on the session, while Brent futures climbed 3.4% to $91.09. Both benchmarks had spent much of August drifting lower on hopes that Washington and Tehran were quietly negotiating wider shipping access through the strait. Monday's exchange of fire ended that bet abruptly.

What happened overnight

U.S. forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island late Sunday, after intelligence indicated Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps units were preparing to fire rockets carrying sea mines into the strait. U.S. Central Command described the action as "limited, precise action against IRGC minelaying forces posing an imminent threat," adding that "Iran created the threat and the U.S. military eliminated it to protect civilian mariners, commercial shipping, and the free flow of global commerce." It was the first publicly acknowledged American strike on Iranian territory since late July.

Tehran did not let the strike pass quietly. The IRGC said the attack killed and wounded Iranian soldiers and civilians, called it a "strategic and fatal mistake," and by Monday morning had launched retaliatory strikes it said targeted U.S.-linked military infrastructure in Jordan and the UAE. Iran's account: missiles fired at the King Hussein and Al Azraq air bases in Jordan, which Jordan's military said intercepted eight incoming missiles, and drones sent toward Al Minhad air base in the UAE, a claim Abu Dhabi's defence ministry rejected outright, calling it "Iranian aggression." Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said the country was "not looking for war" but would "never sit still in the face of any aggression."

A tanker set ablaze

Separately, Iran's Revolutionary Guards Navy said a supertanker caught fire and lost power after striking two naval mines while transiting a route south of the strait, near Omani waters, that Tehran considers unauthorised. The IRGC did not name the vessel, disclose its flag or cargo, or report casualties, but warned that "other vessels that attempt to violate IRGC's security rules would face the same fate." Neither the mine-clearance claims made by U.S. officials nor Iran's account of the tanker incident could be independently verified — commercial transit through the strait has been intermittently disrupted for months, and independent shipping data has been hard to come by.

The strait carried roughly 20 million barrels a day in normal times, about a fifth of global petroleum consumption and closer to a quarter of all seaborne oil trade, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, with the bulk of it destined for China, India, Japan and South Korea. EIA figures show flows through the passage were already down nearly 30% in the first quarter of 2026 from the prior quarter, a reminder that the market has been pricing chokepoint risk into freight and insurance costs well before Monday's escalation.

Markets already primed for volatility

The strike landed on a market that was already recalibrating. Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish turn at Jackson Hole last week — a sharper inflation warning than he gave after the July meeting — had already pushed September rate-hike odds from roughly 36% to above 60% on CME's FedWatch, a shift covered in this edition's Warsh piece. A fresh oil shock complicates that calculus further: higher crude flows directly into headline inflation readings, giving a Fed chair already leaning hawkish less room to look past energy-driven price pressure.

Equity markets were mixed rather than routed on the news — a sign traders are treating this as an escalation within a known, months-long pattern of tit-for-tat strikes rather than a fresh war footing. Brent has swung between roughly $90 and $114 since U.S. and Israeli operations against Iran began in late February, spiking on strikes and mine threats, easing on de-escalation signals. Monday's move fits that pattern rather than breaking it, though the return of direct fire after a month of relative quiet — and the deliberate targeting of Gulf states hosting U.S. forces — narrows the space for a durable calm.

What to watch

The near-term catalysts are the ones that have moved this market all year: whether U.S. Navy vessels report further mine-clearance operations in the strait, whether Iran escalates against Gulf state infrastructure beyond Monday's claimed strikes, and whether shippers start rerouting or halting transits altogether, which would show up quickly in freight and insurance rates before it shows up in official flow data. On the policy side, the Fed's September 15-16 meeting is now a more consequential date than it looked a week ago — a rate decision landing squarely between a hawkish Jackson Hole and an oil-driven inflation impulse.
