US Army Secretary Driscoll resigns amid clashes with Hegseth

Daniel P. Driscoll, the 26th U.S. Secretary of the Army, who is stepping down September 3, 2026 after 18 months in the role. — U.S. Army / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Army Secretary Dan Driscoll resigns effective September 3 after Hegseth fired the generals running his AI and drone modernization drive.

The secretary of the Army is leaving the Pentagon, and almost nobody in Washington is treating it as a surprise. Dan Driscoll, the 40-year-old former venture capitalist who has run the Army since February 2025, submitted his resignation to the White House this week and will be out of the building by September 3. The official language, from White House spokeswoman Anna Kelly, credits him with "providing outstanding leadership during historic military operations" and "restoring an emphasis on readiness and lethality." The unofficial version, confirmed by multiple people close to the process, is that Driscoll could no longer work with Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth.

Driscoll's exit is not an isolated resignation. It is the clearest data point yet in a pattern that has been building since Hegseth took over the Pentagon: a defense secretary who has now fired, forced into retirement, or otherwise pushed out roughly two dozen generals and admirals, alongside two of the armed services' top civilian leaders. What makes Driscoll's departure notable is that he was, by most accounts, exactly the kind of appointee Hegseth's Pentagon was supposed to produce — a combat veteran, a Trump campaign adviser, a self-described "disruptor" brought in to modernize a hidebound institution. That he is leaving anyway says something about how the disruption has actually unfolded.

The break over the generals

Driscoll arrived at the Pentagon with a specific project: the Army Transformation Initiative, a plan he built alongside Gen. Randy George, then the Army's chief of staff, to push artificial intelligence and drone technology into combat formations and decentralize the purchasing decisions that had bogged the service down in procurement bureaucracy. A Yale Law graduate who did a nine-month tour in Iraq as a cavalry scout before moving into investment banking and venture capital, Driscoll framed the project in language borrowed directly from the Ukraine war. "When you look at Ukraine," he said last year, "[they] have not accepted the current version of a thing as sufficient." Under the initiative, an Army unit in Europe began building and fielding its own low-cost drones, modeled on the kind of improvised, fast-iterating production lines Ukrainian forces have used against Russia.

That project largely died with George's career. In April, Hegseth called George and told him to retire immediately — a decision George learned of mid-meeting, according to accounts from officials briefed on the call. Driscoll broke publicly with the decision, driving to George's home as soon as he found out and later telling lawmakers he considered George an "amazing, transformational leader." Two other generals tied to the transformation effort, David Hodne and James Mingus, were forced out around the same time. The European drone battalion has since been ordered to stand down and revert to a conventional infantry role. A person familiar with Driscoll's resignation said he had raised concerns with the administration directly: that Hegseth was blocking Army modernization by firing the officers responsible for carrying it out.

A pattern, not an incident

George's ouster was itself part of a longer list. Hegseth removed Gen. C.Q. Brown as chairman of the Joint Chiefs and Adm. Lisa Franchetti as chief of naval operations within weeks of taking office in early 2025. Navy Secretary John Phelan, the civilian who ran that service, was pushed out this spring alongside George. By most counts, roughly two dozen generals and admirals have now left their posts under Hegseth, with dozens more officers' promotions stalled — a scale of turnover without recent precedent in a department that normally prizes continuity of command. Sen. Jack Reed of Rhode Island, the top Democrat on the Senate Armed Services Committee, has been blunt about where he places the blame, contrasting Driscoll's record favorably against what he called "Hegseth's disastrous tenure atop the Defense Department." Even inside the West Wing, according to people familiar with a private meeting, Trump himself has expressed concern about the pace of Hegseth's firings — though not enough, evidently, to intervene on Driscoll's behalf.

The Army is now without both a Senate-confirmed civilian secretary and a permanent, confirmed chief of staff, an unusual gap at the top of the service that has roughly 450,000 active-duty soldiers and the largest procurement budget of any branch. Hegseth's preferred replacement for Driscoll is reportedly Sean Parnell, an Afghanistan veteran currently serving as the Pentagon's chief spokesman and a close Hegseth ally — a choice that would tighten, not loosen, the defense secretary's grip on Army leadership, but one that faces its own confirmation obstacles given Parnell's partisan profile and past domestic-abuse allegations, which he denies.

What it signals for defense policy

The substance of the Driscoll-Hegseth dispute is not personal chemistry; it is a disagreement over what kind of Army the United States is building. Driscoll's side of the argument was that modernization requires empowering the officers who understand drone warfare and AI-enabled targeting, and that firing them undercuts the very transformation Hegseth says he wants. Hegseth's Pentagon, for its part, has framed its personnel changes as a return to a warrior ethos and away from what it considers politicized promotion criteria. Whichever framing wins out inside the administration will shape where Army contracting dollars flow next — toward the kind of low-cost drone production Driscoll championed, or back toward legacy platforms and the primes that build them.

The next marker to watch is the Parnell nomination, if it materializes: a confirmation fight would put Hegseth's management of the Army before the Senate Armed Services Committee in a way Driscoll's quiet exit does not. Beyond that, the question is whether the Army's now-leaderless transformation office survives the transition at all, or whether the drone battalion's stand-down proves to be the template for the rest of the initiative.
