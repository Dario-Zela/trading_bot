Badenoch reshuffles shadow cabinet, elevating Griffith and Tugendhat

Kemi Badenoch, who reshuffled her shadow cabinet ahead of the Conservative Party conference. — Roger Harris / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Badenoch reshuffled her shadow cabinet ahead of conference — Griffith and Tugendhat promoted, Cleverly quit for a London mayoral bid — with no sign of a fiscal or monetary policy shift.

Kemi Badenoch spent Sunday doing what opposition leaders do best in the doldrums of a parliament: rearranging the furniture. The Conservative leader tore up her top team, installing Andrew Griffith as shadow chancellor in place of Sir Mel Stride and handing the foreign affairs brief to Tom Tugendhat, who replaces Dame Priti Patel. Sir James Cleverly, meanwhile, walked out of the shadow cabinet altogether — not into obscurity, but into a bid to become the Conservative candidate for Mayor of London.

In one sentence: Badenoch reshuffled her top team ahead of conference, promoting Griffith and Tugendhat and losing Cleverly to a London mayoral bid — a management story, not a policy one.

Out with the old guard

Stride departs after nearly two years shadowing the Treasury, a stint Conservative aides credited with helping the party "rebuild economic credibility" after the Truss-era mini-Budget wrecked it. Patel, one of the more recognisable faces of the Boris Johnson years, returns to the backbenches with what allies describe as a new international role rather than a demotion in name only. Stuart Andrew also lost his health brief to Damian Hinds. None of the outgoing trio are gone from frontline politics for good, but all three lose the shadow cabinet table.

Griffith's promotion is the more pointed of the two headline moves, if only because of the baggage he carries. He was a Treasury minister under Liz Truss during the 2022 mini-Budget that triggered a gilt-market rout and ended her premiership in weeks — a fact Labour's Bridget Phillipson was quick to weaponise, branding him "a key architect of Liz Truss's disastrous mini-Budget." Griffith's response was to lean into ambition rather than apologise for the past, promising an "economic revolution" built on lower taxes, deregulation and more domestic oil and gas production.

Tugendhat's second act

Tugendhat, a former army officer and one-time leadership rival to Badenoch, moves from security-adjacent portfolios to shadow foreign secretary — a natural enough fit given his years chairing the Commons foreign affairs select committee. Patel's exit from that brief closes out a period in which she had become a familiar voice on immigration and asylum policy; her successor is expected to bring a more Atlanticist, defence-minded tone to the role, though the party has given no indication of a substantive shift in foreign policy positioning.

Claire Coutinho steps up to shadow business secretary, taking the slot Griffith vacates, while Katie Lam moves into the housing brief left empty by Cleverly's departure.

Cleverly's exit, and why it matters more for London than Westminster

Of the three big personnel changes, Cleverly's is the one with the clearest immediate consequence — just not for national politics. In a piece for The Times announcing his move, the former home and foreign secretary wrote that London's capital "needs more than an administrator," and told Badenoch he had to be "free to speak about every aspect of London's future" to make a real run at City Hall. He has since launched his campaign on a "classically Conservative" platform of tougher policing, more housebuilding and lower taxes — though Londoners will not actually choose a mayor until May 2028, leaving a long selection race ahead among party members.

The bit that isn't there

What the reshuffle does not contain is any hint of a rethink on fiscal or monetary policy. Griffith's rhetoric about tax cuts and deregulation is standard opposition fare, not a costed platform, and nothing in the appointments touches the party's actual positioning on gilts, spending rules or the Bank of England. The move reads as pure party management: Badenoch shoring up her authority and refreshing a top team before October's conference, at a moment when polling puts the Conservatives in third place — trailing both Labour and a resurgent Reform UK, which recent surveys have shown a few points ahead of the Tories. For a party fighting to be heard at all, getting faces right matters more than getting policy right, at least for now.

The next marker to watch is the Conservative conference itself in October, where Griffith will face his first real test of turning "economic revolution" rhetoric into something resembling a platform — and where the party's polling position, not its cabinet lineup, will do the talking.
