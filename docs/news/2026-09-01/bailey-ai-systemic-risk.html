Bailey warns G20 that AI-driven valuations and AI cyber-risk threaten financial stability

Andrew Bailey, Bank of England governor and chair of the Financial Stability Board, who wrote to G20 finance ministers warning of linked AI valuation and cyber risks. — Office of the U.S. Ambassador to the U.K. / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
FSB chair Andrew Bailey told G20 ministers that AI stock concentration and leveraged cross-investment could trigger a disorderly market correction, while frontier AI's cyber-attack potential is finance's most immediate systemic risk.

Andrew Bailey has spent six years as Bank of England governor delivering warnings that markets mostly shrugged off. This one landed differently. In a letter to G20 finance ministers and central bank governors published ahead of their meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, Bailey — who since July 2025 has also chaired the Financial Stability Board, the body that coordinates global banking regulation — laid out two AI-related threats to the financial system that he said were no longer theoretical. One is a market bet that has become dangerously narrow. The other is a form of attack that regulators do not yet know how to defend against.

"For the financial system, the most immediate concern is the potential impact of frontier AI on cyber risk," Bailey wrote. But he did not treat that as separate from the valuation story. The two, in his account, compound each other: a financial system already leveraged against a handful of richly priced AI companies is also the one most exposed if those companies' computing infrastructure is attacked.

A bet concentrated in a few hands

The numbers behind Bailey's valuation warning are not new, but they have kept climbing. The so-called Magnificent Seven — Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Nvidia, Meta and Tesla — made up roughly 11% of the S&P 500 in 2015. By mid-2026 that figure had peaked above 34% before settling around 33.8%, according to market data, meaning roughly a third of America's benchmark index now rises and falls on the fortunes of seven companies, most of them betting heavily on AI. Nvidia alone was valued at more than $5.2 trillion in late August, having become the first company to cross $5 trillion.

Bailey's specific complaint is not simply that these stocks are expensive. It's how they're being financed. "The issue is not simply that investors are borrowing more, but that leverage is interacting with high valuations and market concentration," he wrote, "in particular the cross-investment between artificial intelligence companies and hyperscalers, in a way that could amplify a future market correction." That phrase — cross-investment — points at the circular financing arrangements that have proliferated across the AI buildout, in which chipmakers, cloud providers and AI labs invest in one another and sign reciprocal supply contracts, arrangements that inflate reported growth on all sides without necessarily reflecting new external demand.

Layered on top, Bailey warned, is a private credit market that has swollen to an estimated $1.5–2 trillion in assets — much of it financing exactly the sort of mid-sized firms that would be first to suffer in a downturn — alongside sovereign debt markets he described as fragile. Put simply: markets are "vulnerable to a potentially disorderly correction that could spread across borders," and AI-driven equity concentration is the piece most likely to trigger it. Not every analyst agrees the danger is imminent — Goldman Sachs has noted that, unlike dot-com-era darlings, most Magnificent Seven firms generate substantial free cash flow and pay dividends, while Bank of America calls current conditions more an "AI air pocket" than a full bubble. But Bailey's letter is notable precisely because it comes from a regulator, not a bear.

The cyber flank

The second warning is the one Bailey's own staff flagged as more urgent. Frontier AI models, he wrote, are showing "increasingly sophisticated autonomy and problem-solving abilities, as well as threat capabilities," and could "materially alter the speed, scale and economics of cyber risk" in a way that "could undermine market confidence system-wide, especially due to highly concentrated third-party service providers." That last clause is the crux: banks and asset managers increasingly rely on the same small set of cloud and AI vendors, so a successful attack against one provider's infrastructure could cascade through institutions that have no direct relationship with each other.

The warning arrives alongside a broader shift in the security industry's own language. More than 100 companies — including Anthropic, Microsoft, Google, Amazon Web Services and CrowdStrike — signed an open letter this year cautioning that AI-enabled cyberattacks are becoming more widespread and sophisticated within a matter of months, not years. Researchers have documented state-affiliated hacking groups doubling attack volumes after adopting open-source AI tools, and blockchain analytics firms have tracked a sharp rise in AI-assisted crypto theft. Bailey's contribution is to translate that into regulatory terms: he said many jurisdictions still lack protocols for managing the release and deployment of frontier models, and called on authorities to support "safe and responsible model release and deployment on a global basis" — an explicit push for coordinated AI governance rules for the finance sector, not just voluntary industry pledges.

Banks are leaning in regardless

What makes the concentration risk harder to unwind is that banks have kept increasing their exposure even as the warnings accumulate. Commercial and industrial lending commitments to AI-adjacent industries — software, semiconductors, energy tied to data-centre demand — rose from around 9% of large banks' total commitments (roughly $250 billion) in 2015 to about 13% (roughly $450 billion) by late 2025, according to Federal Reserve data. US regulators have responded: in April, the Fed, the OCC and the FDIC jointly issued SR 26-2, an update to model-risk-management rules that now requires banks to demonstrate they could unwind a critical AI vendor relationship quickly if that vendor were compromised. Whether that requirement is more than paperwork remains untested — no major institution has yet had to invoke it under stress.

What comes next

Bailey's letter does not predict when a correction might come, and the FSB has no power to force G20 members to act on either warning — it can only recommend. The practical test will be whether finance ministries translate his language into binding rules on AI vendor concentration and model deployment, something Bailey explicitly called for but stopped short of drafting himself. The next marker will be the FSB's follow-up assessment expected ahead of the G20 leaders' summit, and, more immediately, how markets react if any of the hyperscaler-AI cross-investment deals Bailey flagged start to look shakier when third-quarter earnings land later this year.
