Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole turn lifts September Fed hike odds above 60%

Kevin Warsh, who took over as Federal Reserve chair in May, delivered an unexpectedly hawkish message at Jackson Hole. — Federal Reserve Board / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Fed funds futures imply a 60%+ chance of a September rate hike after Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech, reversing dovish bets from days earlier.

Fed funds futures now put the odds of a quarter-point rate hike at the September 15-16 meeting above 60% — up from roughly one-in-three a week earlier — after Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole speech to tell markets that inflation is not behaving the way the Fed needs it to. It is a sharp reversal from where sentiment sat only days before, when several desks had penciled in a dovish tone from the new chair.

From a coin flip to a lean

Heading into the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium in Grand Teton National Park, the setup looked tilted the other way. July's jobs and retail-sales prints had come in soft, the July FOMC meeting read as cautious, and several economists were framing a dovish Jackson Hole as the more likely outcome — one that would steepen the yield curve and take some pressure off the dollar. Instead, Warsh spent his roughly 20 minutes at the podium doing the opposite: recommitting to the Fed's 2% PCE target and warning that recent progress on prices doesn't tell him underlying inflation has "meaningfully improved."

The reaction was immediate. By the following Monday, CME FedWatch data showed a 60.4% probability of a 25-basis-point hike in September, up from about 56% at the end of the previous week and well above the roughly 36% priced in before the speech. Gold fell, Asian equities dipped, and small-cap stocks — the part of the market most exposed to higher borrowing costs — underperformed for the week even as the S&P 500 closed higher.

The inflation math behind the pivot

Warsh's case rests on data that has stopped cooperating. Headline PCE inflation ran at 3.7% year-over-year in July, core PCE at 3.3%, and the Cleveland Fed's nowcast puts the August headline figure near 3.8%. Both readings sit close to double the Fed's target, and by one count inflation has now overshot 2% for 65 consecutive months. Warsh described the Fed's task as avoiding a "hall of mirrors," where policymakers and markets end up reacting to each other's expectations rather than to the inflation data itself — a pointed way of saying he isn't inclined to pre-commit to easing simply because traders expect it.

He paired that warning with a labor market he called "quite stable" and "consistent with full employment," at a 4.1% unemployment rate, and pointed to second-quarter corporate profits up 22.8% year-over-year as evidence the economy can absorb tighter policy without tipping into recession — he cited the historical pattern that downturns rarely begin while profits are still rising. Roughly half of the recent pickup in capital spending, he noted, is data-center construction tied to the AI buildout, a detail that matters because it suggests some of the economy's recent strength is structural rather than a sign of self-correcting inflation.

A Fed split, and a president watching

The hawkish turn does not reflect unanimity. Three regional Fed presidents dissented at the July meeting, all in favor of a hike that didn't happen, which means Warsh's Jackson Hole language reads less like a lone hawk staking out ground and more like the median of a committee that has already been quietly shifting. That distinction matters for how durable this repricing is: a chair leaning hawkish because the committee is already there is a different signal than a chair overriding a dovish consensus.

It also puts Warsh, who won Senate confirmation in a 54-45 vote in May to succeed Jerome Powell, at odds with the man who nominated him. President Trump has pushed publicly for lower rates and said he hopes to eventually convince people the US should have the lowest interest rates in the world. Since Jackson Hole, though, Trump has notably held back from criticizing Warsh directly, saying he won't try to talk him out of a hike — a restraint that itself says something about how seriously the White House is taking the inflation data.

Not every rate market is moving the same direction. UK gilt yields, which had priced in a chance of a Bank of England hike earlier this year, have instead pulled that expectation into 2027 as falling Brent crude prices eased British inflation concerns — a reminder that Warsh's message is a US-specific read on a US-specific inflation problem, not a global tightening cue.

What happens next

Prediction markets have not fully caught up to the futures move: Kalshi and Polymarket still show hike odds closer to 48-49%, roughly a coin flip, versus the more confident 60%-plus priced into CME futures — a gap that will close one way or the other as fresh data arrives. The August jobs report lands September 5, followed by August CPI around mid-month, both before the Fed's rate announcement on September 16. Any further upside surprise in either would harden the case Warsh made at Jackson Hole; a soft print would reopen the argument that this was one hawkish speech, not a committee-wide shift.
