NHS rolls out new 'super flu' vaccine after busiest-ever summer

An NHS accident and emergency department entrance — A&E logged its busiest months on record this summer. — Betty Longbottom / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
NHS England starts an early flu vaccine campaign retooled for the 'Super-K' strain, days after confirming its busiest-ever summer for A&E and ambulance demand.

England's health service has begun handing out an updated flu vaccine this week, a campaign that would ordinarily pass with a paragraph in the local paper. This year it arrives on the back of what NHS England is calling the busiest summer in its history — and follows a winter so severe that hospital leaders described it, at the time, as a 'worst-case scenario.'

The timing is not coincidental. Last winter's flu season arrived more than a month early, driven by a mutated strain of H3N2 known as subclade K — nicknamed 'Super-K' in the press, though NHS clinicians are at pains to note that 'super flu' is not a medical term. It doesn't mean the virus causes worse disease; it means the population had little existing immunity to it, so it spread faster than usual. By early December, hospitals in England had 2,660 flu patients a day, a 55% jump in a single week and the highest figure on record for that point in the season. Health Secretary at the time, Wes Streeting, called it 'a tidal wave of flu tearing through our hospitals.'

A record summer, not a quiet one

What followed was not the usual seasonal lull. NHS England's own performance data show A&E departments recorded three of their busiest months ever between May and July, with average daily attendances topping 80,000 for the first time in June and A&E logging 2.5 million attendances in July alone, amid two heatwaves. Ambulance crews handled 829,675 incidents that month — the fourth-highest total on record — while 999 call volumes rose 6.5% year-on-year to 898,503. Diagnostic services ran at record pace too, with 2.65 million tests and scans carried out in June, the most in any single month.

Credit to NHS staff is genuine on the numbers: four-hour A&E performance actually improved to 75.4% even as attendances climbed, and the overall waiting list fell to 7.27 million in June — the first June-on-June fall since 2011. NHS national medical director's office pointed to that combination — record demand met with improving throughput — as evidence the service is coping, not merely enduring. But 'coping' and 'coping with headroom to spare' are different claims, and it is the latter that will be tested if this winter's flu season repeats last year's early, sharp arrival.

Getting ahead of subclade K

This year's flu vaccine has been reformulated specifically to improve its match to subclade K, following World Health Organization strain-selection guidance issued in February. The rollout began on 1 September for the groups considered highest-priority spreaders and highest-risk: children aged two and three, school-age children (who receive a nasal spray rather than an injection), and pregnant women. Frontline healthcare workers, adults aged 18 to 65 in clinical risk groups, and everyone 65 and over become eligible from 1 October, with care home residents due to be vaccinated by the end of November. Those outside the eligible groups can pay privately, typically £10–£20.

Dr Amanda Doyle, NHS England's national director for primary care, framed the earlier start explicitly around last year's experience, noting that the season 'came early and was made worse by a new variant' that strained urgent and emergency care nationwide. NHS England chief executive Sir James Mackey has written to trusts underscoring that the programme is intended to ease winter pressure on beds and ambulances as much as to protect individual patients — an acknowledgment that vaccination policy here is also, in part, capacity management.

The workforce question underneath

What the vaccine campaign does not fix is the staffing base it depends on. NHS England's own workforce data put registered nursing vacancies at just over 25,600 — a 6% vacancy rate — even as some local trusts report recruitment freezes that leave newly qualified nurses unable to find posts, a contradiction that speaks to funding constraints as much as to labour supply. An ageing nursing workforce approaching retirement, tighter international recruitment rules, and pay-related attrition have combined to keep the service running close to its margin through a summer that broke attendance records and a winter that broke hospitalisation records in the same twelve-month span.

That matters beyond the wards. A health service absorbing back-to-back record-demand seasons without expanding headcount is, in effect, running a productivity experiment — NHS England cites a 2.6% productivity gain across 2025/26 — that has limits. James Murray, who became Health and Social Care Secretary in May after Wes Streeting's resignation, now inherits the test of whether an earlier vaccine campaign and marginally improved throughput are enough to prevent a repeat of December's bed-occupancy crunch, which reached 94.2% at its peak.

What comes next

The practical marker to watch is whether this winter's flu wave arrives on the old, familiar December-January schedule or repeats last year's early September-arriving pattern — UKHSA publishes weekly national flu surveillance data through the season, typically from October, and NHS England's winter sitrep releases will show whether bed occupancy and ambulance handover delays track below last year's levels. The first real test is likely by late November, once care home vaccination completes and community transmission data for the new school term comes in.
