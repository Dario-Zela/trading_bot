Gilt yields stay elevated near multi-week highs as rate-hike bets fade

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which continues APF gilt sales toward a £488bn target regardless of the rates debate. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
10-year gilt yields near 5.04–5.21% even as September BoE hike odds fall to ~15%, as oil's summer slide reprices inflation faster than rate bets can catch up.

UK borrowing costs are stuck in an uncomfortable place. The 10-year gilt yield has spent the back half of August grinding higher, touching 5.04% in the middle of the month before pushing on toward 5.21% by August 31 — its highest since May and only a whisker from the 5.16% peak hit in July, a level not seen consistently since 2008. Normally, yields that high would go hand in hand with a market braced for a rate hike. This time they don't.

Money markets are pricing barely 4 basis points of tightening into the Bank of England's September 17 decision — roughly a 15% chance of a move to 4.00% from the current 3.75%. That's down sharply from where hike bets stood a few weeks ago, when a run of stronger inflation data and a tightening MPC vote had traders taking the prospect seriously. The gap between where yields sit and where rate expectations sit is the story: something other than imminent BoE tightening is holding gilts up.

The oil channel

That something has largely been oil. Brent crude sold off hard through much of August as a US-brokered pause in the Iran conflict took hold, dragging prices down from the mid-$80s toward the mid-$70s and taking a chunk of near-term UK inflation risk with it. Traders read the move as licence for the BoE to hold off, and rate-hike odds for September collapsed accordingly — from a coin-flip in early August to today's 15%.

Gilt yields didn't follow. Even as hike bets evaporated, 10-year paper kept climbing, a divergence that points to forces beyond monetary policy: heavy Debt Management Office issuance, persistent fiscal-sustainability worries in the market, and a Bank of England that is still actively selling bonds regardless of what rates do next.

The oil story has since complicated itself further. Strikes between US and Iranian forces near the Strait of Hormuz have pushed Brent back above $86 a barrel this week — erasing most of August's decline and reintroducing the inflation risk that the sell-off had priced out. If that holds, the rationale for keeping hike odds this low gets harder to sustain, even with gilt yields already elevated.

QT keeps grinding

Underneath the rates debate, the Bank continues to run down its Asset Purchase Facility. The MPC's plan, set last September, calls for reducing the APF stock by £70bn over the twelve months to September 2026, taking cumulative gilt sales toward a target holding of £488bn. Bailey's committee has kept that programme on autopilot through the summer's rates turbulence, selling gilts on a quarterly schedule regardless of the vote split on Bank Rate — a structural source of extra gilt supply that sits on top of, and somewhat independent of, whatever the MPC decides about the price of money.

That MPC vote has itself been drifting hawkish. The committee held at 3.75% in July by a 6-3 margin, tighter than June's 7-2 split, with chief economist Huw Pill joined by Megan Greene and Catherine Mann in voting for an immediate hike to 4.00%. Bailey has pointed to inflation easing faster than expected — CPI came in at 2.6% in June before rising back to 2.9% in July on higher household energy bills — while flagging that volatile Middle East energy prices remain the biggest wildcard in the forecast.

What it adds up to

The net effect is a gilt market pricing in more caution than the rates market. Yields near multi-week highs suggest investors are demanding compensation for fiscal and supply risk that has little to do with whether Bank Rate sits at 3.75% or 4.00% next month. Falling oil eased the inflation case for a hike; a renewed spike, if it persists, could just as quickly reopen it — while the Bank's own gilt sales keep adding supply either way.

The next test comes fast. August CPI lands on September 16, the day before the MPC's decision, and will show whether energy-driven price pressure is building again now that Brent has clawed back most of its summer losses. A hot print would narrow the gap between what gilt yields and rate markets are each betting on; a soft one would leave the divergence — and the question of who's mispricing what — unresolved.
