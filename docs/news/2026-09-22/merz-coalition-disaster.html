Merz vows to hold German coalition together after 'disaster' regional election losses

Chancellor Friedrich Merz, whose CDU suffered its worst-ever regional election results in September 2026. — Sophie Hugon / European Union, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Merz's CDU was shut out of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern's parliament entirely as AfD topped 38%, deepening a coalition crisis bond markets are watching but not yet pricing.

Friedrich Merz has been chancellor of Germany for sixteen months. On the evidence of the past two weekends, a growing number of people inside his own party are wondering whether he will make it to seventeen.

On 20 September, voters in Berlin and the eastern state of Mecklenburg-Vorpommern delivered the worst regional results Merz's Christian Democrats have recorded since the party's founding in 1945. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, the CDU took just 4.9% of the vote — short of the 5% threshold needed to enter the state parliament at all. It is the first time since reunification that Germany's traditional governing party has been shut out of a Landtag entirely. The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), running on a hardline anti-immigration platform, won the state outright with 38.2%, more than doubling its previous result.

"We cannot sugar-coat the result in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern," Merz told his party's presidium afterward. "It is a disaster." It was the second time in a fortnight he had reached for that word. Two weeks earlier, in Saxony-Anhalt, the AfD had won 43.8% — the strongest showing by any party in the state since reunification — while the CDU collapsed to 17.2%, losing all 38 of its directly contested constituencies. Merz called that result then "the worst election defeat the CDU has suffered in years, or even decades."

A coalition running low on road

Merz took office in May 2025 at the head of a CDU/CSU–SPD grand coalition, the arrangement Germany reaches for when no other governing majority is available. It was meant to project stability after years of drift under his predecessor. Instead, the government has been consumed by infighting almost since it formed: the CDU's parliamentary leader, Jens Spahn, resigned in July amid a surrogacy scandal, and a subsequent cabinet reshuffle was handled badly enough that it produced the first open talk of replacing Merz just over a year into his term.

The September results have hardened that talk into something closer to a countdown. National polling now puts the AfD at 28–29%, with Merz's Union bloc trailing near 20%. Exit polling after the Saxony-Anhalt vote found barely one in ten voters approved of his chancellorship. Within his own party, an AfD state candidate, Leif-Erik Holm, predicted flatly that "the chancellor certainly won't make it to the end of the year," declaring that "the twilight of the chancellor's era has begun." AfD co-leader Alice Weidel has been blunter still, telling German broadcaster ZDF that the CDU "will never win another election" and describing her own party as experiencing "explosive growth" nationwide.

Merz's coalition partners are not making his position easier. SPD leader and vice-chancellor Lars Klingbeil said after the Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern results that "things can't go on" on the current trajectory, pressing for the 2027 federal budget to be reopened and for planned pension and care-system reforms to be softened. Berlin itself illustrated the fracture from the other direction: the CDU fell to 18.8% of the vote there, well behind Die Linke, which surged to 25.7% — its best result in the city's history — and is now positioned to lead a new left-wing coalition with the Greens and SPD. Merz has so far rejected both retreat and resignation, telling reporters he intends to govern with "backbone, steadfastness and patience." Names already circulate as possible successors — North Rhine-Westphalia's Hendrik Wüst, Bavaria's Markus Söder, Hesse's Boris Rhein — though none has signalled any appetite for the job, and Wüst has his own state election to defend next spring.

Why bond markets are watching, not panicking — yet

So far, financial markets have treated the crisis as a political story rather than a fiscal one. Ten-year Bund yields touched their highest level since 2011 around the Saxony-Anhalt vote, yet moved barely a basis point on the day itself; the DAX, similarly, barely reacted. The euro slipped roughly 0.3% against the dollar to around $1.072 after the Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern results — a modest risk-off move, not a rout. Analysts attribute the calm to Germany's institutional habit of self-correction: a credible bench of successors, a constitution that makes snap elections hard to trigger, and a base-case assumption that the coalition muddles through.

That calm is conditional, not structural. The risk investors are actually pricing is not the AfD's poll numbers but what a cornered coalition might do about them — specifically, whether Klingbeil's push to reopen the 2027 budget tips into looser fiscal policy to buy back working-class and eastern voters. That is the scenario market analysts describe as capable of producing "the first genuine German political risk premium in Bund yields since reunification," with spreads against peripheral eurozone debt widening and the European Central Bank's inflation calculus complicated just as it tries to hold policy steady.

What comes next

The immediate test is fiscal, not electoral: whether Merz and Klingbeil can agree a 2027 budget that satisfies an SPD base demanding softer reforms without spooking the bond market that has, so far, given Berlin the benefit of the doubt. Beyond that, North Rhine-Westphalia's state election next spring — fought on Wüst's own turf — will serve as the next real-world gauge of whether the CDU's collapse in the east is spreading west, or whether Germany's largest state still offers the party solid ground to stand on.
