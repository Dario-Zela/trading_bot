CNN, MS NOW and Politico sue Trump White House over press access ban

The James S. Brady Press Briefing Room in the White House West Wing, the daily stage for the access now at the center of a federal lawsuit. — Randy Florendo / Official White House Photo (public domain)
In one sentence
CNN, MS NOW and Politico sued the Trump White House on Monday, calling the seizure of their reporters' press passes unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

The last time a White House press pass ended up in front of a federal judge, the case involved one CNN reporter, one bruised ego and a viral video of an intern grabbing for a microphone. This time it is three newsrooms, a Cabinet's worth of named defendants, and a fight over whether the President can simply decide which outlets get to watch him work.

On Monday, CNN, Politico and MS NOW — the network formerly known as MSNBC — filed suit against Donald Trump, Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Communications Director Steven Cheung and Secret Service Director Sean Curran, alleging that pulling their reporters' credentials violates the First Amendment and the Fifth Amendment's due-process guarantee. "This morning, we notified the government that we are filing a lawsuit today to protect our First Amendment rights and defend the principle that the government does not decide what the press reports or publishes," the three outlets said in a joint statement.

From grievance to grounds

The dispute did not start in a courtroom. Trump announced on Friday that the three organisations were being frozen out, telling reporters the decision followed what he called two years of unfair and negative coverage rather than any single story. By Saturday it was no longer rhetorical: MS NOW's Akayla Gardner, CNN's Betsy Klein and Politico's Cheyenne Haslett each tried to walk onto White House grounds for their normal shifts and were turned back by the Secret Service, which confiscated their hard passes on the spot.

That sequence — a president naming his grievance, then agents physically stopping named reporters at the gate — is what moved the story from a press-freedom talking point to a legal complaint. The suit asks a court to restore full access for the outlets' journalists, including their right to rotate through the video pool that feeds footage to every network, not just the plaintiffs.

A lawyer, and a judge, who have done this before

There is a strong sense of rerun to the case. CNN has hired Ted Boutrous, the same First Amendment lawyer who won a temporary restraining order for reporter Jim Acosta in 2018, after the White House yanked his pass over a testy exchange at a press conference. Boutrous did not soften his framing this time: "The President's ban of CNN, MS NOW and POLITICO from the White House is a direct assault on the First Amendment and a blatant violation of due process," he said, adding that Trump "has been explicit that this ban is intended to punish reporting he doesn't like and to intimidate journalists."

Even the judge is a repeat character. U.S. District Judge Timothy J. Kelly — a Trump appointee who, in the 2018 Acosta case, ruled against the White House while noting he was simply bound by precedent he didn't necessarily like — has been assigned the new case and set a videoconference hearing for Wednesday afternoon.

That precedent has grown since 2018. In 2020, the D.C. Circuit sided with Playboy correspondent Brian Karem after the White House suspended his pass for a month over a shouting match with a guest at a press event, ruling he'd had no fair warning of a penalty that severe. The new complaint leans on that ruling to argue the same logic applies here, only with three newsrooms shut out indefinitely rather than one reporter benched for thirty days.

The argument, and who's lining up behind it

The legal theory turns on a phrase that sounds dry until you see it applied to a president naming outlets by name: viewpoint discrimination. Courts have generally held that once the White House opens press access to some outlets, it cannot then pick and choose based on how flattering the coverage is. Jameel Jaffer, director of Columbia's Knight First Amendment Institute, told NBC News the press pool functions as a "public forum" under the First Amendment, meaning the president "can't exclude journalists from it on the basis of their viewpoints" — and called the ban "pretty clearly unconstitutional."

The White House Correspondents' Association, not historically the plaintiffs' loudest cheerleader, has also weighed in. President Jacqui Heinrich warned that "a standard used to exclude one news outlet because of its coverage could be applied to any outlet in the future," a line aimed squarely at newsrooms that might otherwise sit this fight out. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press and the Freedom of the Press Foundation both used near-identical language, calling the ban a clear or blatant First Amendment violation.

MS NOW's presence in the suit adds an odd wrinkle: the network only took that name in November, after Comcast spun MSNBC and its cable siblings into a separate company, Versant Media Group, to avoid brand confusion with NBC News. Barely a year into its new identity, it finds itself as a named plaintiff in one of the more consequential press-access cases in decades.

Wednesday's hearing before Judge Kelly will not resolve the underlying constitutional question — the 2018 Acosta fight never did, ending instead in a quiet restoration of access. But a ruling either way, even a temporary one, will signal how much room courts think a president has to treat White House credentials as a reward for favourable coverage rather than a basic tool of the job.
