BitMEX to shut down permanently on 23 September

BitMEX's logo — the exchange that pioneered the leveraged perpetual swap closes permanently on 23 September 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BitMEX, the exchange that pioneered 100x-leveraged Bitcoin perpetuals, shuts down for good on 23 September after 11 years and years of US regulatory battles.

BitMEX, the exchange that introduced the world to 100x-leveraged Bitcoin perpetual swaps, will close permanently on 23 September at 04:00 UTC. The Seychelles-registered platform stopped taking new registrations in July, barred new positions from 26 August, and has spent the past month forcing customers to unwind or withdraw before the deadline — the final chapter for one of crypto derivatives trading's founding venues.

HDR Global Trading Limited, BitMEX's operator, framed the closure as the outcome of a strategic review rather than a forced exit, saying in its announcement it was proud of an 11-year run with no funds lost to hacking and that "all assets exceed liabilities." Customers who miss the deadline face a maintenance fee of 1% annually, or $50 a month, whichever is greater — an incentive to leave rather than linger.

From market leader to also-ran

Founded in 2014 by Arthur Hayes, Ben Delo and Samuel Reed, BitMEX built its name on the perpetual swap, a contract with no expiry that let traders take 100x-leveraged bets on Bitcoin's price. At its 2019 peak the exchange handled north of $1 trillion in annual volume and commanded roughly 57% of the global crypto derivatives market, with single-day turnover reaching $8 billion in mid-2018. That dominance did not survive contact with better-capitalised, more liquid rivals — Binance, OKX and Bybit among them — which offered deeper order books, more trading pairs and, crucially, far less regulatory baggage attached to their brand.

The baggage was considerable. In October 2020 the CFTC and US federal prosecutors charged BitMEX and its founders with operating an unregistered trading platform and failing to implement anti-money-laundering controls required under the Bank Secrecy Act. Hayes, Delo and Reed pleaded guilty in 2022, forfeited $10 million each and drew probation; BitMEX's corporate entities separately paid a $100 million penalty to the CFTC and FinCEN, with a federal court later ordering the founders to pay a further $30 million combined. In March 2025, President Trump pardoned Hayes, Delo, Reed and HDR Global Trading itself — the first corporate pardon on record — clearing the legal slate roughly eighteen months before the board opted to wind the business down anyway.

What's left unresolved

One loose thread is BitMEX's insurance fund, built up over a decade to absorb liquidations and prevent auto-deleveraging on losing trades. It holds roughly 3,600 BTC and just over $30 million in USDT — about $270 million at current prices — and unlike customer margin, it was never customer-owned. BitMEX rebalanced the fund in November, trimming it from a reported 32,800 BTC, and has not said publicly what happens to any surplus once liabilities are settled. The ambiguity, according to people close to the failed sale talks that ran from February 2025 until the board gave up on finding a buyer, was itself one of the obstacles to an acquisition.

The bigger signal

BitMEX was not driven out by a single regulator's order, and it says so itself. But its closing is still a data point in a broader pattern: exchanges built for the unregulated, high-leverage, know-your-customer-optional era of Bitcoin trading are struggling to compete with venues that made peace with regulators years ago and built compliance into their product from the start. Binance paid $4.3 billion to settle US charges in 2023 and kept growing; BitMEX paid $100 million, got pardoned, and is shutting down regardless. Open interest that was still sitting on the platform will now migrate to those regulated or larger competitors, a small but real liquidity event in derivatives markets over the coming days.

The exchange goes dark at 04:00 UTC on 23 September. After that, customers who left assets behind start accruing fees on a platform that no longer trades.
