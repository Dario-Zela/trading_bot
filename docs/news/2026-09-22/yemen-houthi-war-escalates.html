G7 demands Iran stop arming Houthis as Yemenis flee escalating Saudi border war

The UN General Assembly Hall in New York, where G7 foreign ministers met on the sidelines of high-level week. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
G7 ministers condemned Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia and told Iran to stop arming the group, but offered no enforcement as 125,000 Yemenis flee a captured Red Sea chokepoint.

The Group of Seven's foreign ministers gathered on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly on Monday and did what diplomats do when a war is spreading and nobody wants to commit troops or treasury to stopping it: they issued a statement. Ministers from Britain, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan and the United States condemned what they called the "unacceptable continued strikes" by Yemen's Houthi movement against Saudi Arabia, and called on Iran to end its arming and support of the group. They did not announce sanctions. They did not propose a naval task force, an arms-interdiction mechanism, or any timeline for consequences. The statement was, in essence, a demand with no attached lever.

In one sentence: G7 ministers condemned Houthi strikes on Saudi Arabia and told Iran to stop arming the group, but offered no sanctions or enforcement as 125,000 Yemenis flee a war that has handed the Houthis a Red Sea chokepoint.

A demand without a stick

The ministers' language was pointed: the flow of Iranian weapons to the Houthis, they said, represented "a dangerous pattern of escalation" that risked "undermining international trade and creating global instability," and violated existing UN Security Council resolutions. They also told the Houthis directly to "immediately cease all military actions, all threats and attacks against civilian shipping, and to return to the political process in good faith."

That last demand carries an uncomfortable history. UN Security Council Resolution 2216, adopted in 2015, already imposes an arms embargo on Houthi leadership. US naval forces have spent years intercepting dhows carrying Iranian-made missile and drone components bound for Yemen — interceptions that have done little to slow the flow. A Yemen sanctions panel meant to document embargo violations has for years been unable to get its own arms expert appointed, blocked at the Council table. Washington's top diplomat, Marco Rubio, said plainly this month that "the Houthis, in many ways, are agents and proxies of the Iranians," and that "behind some of this is clearly the Iranian hand." The G7 statement restates a position the international system has held, and failed to enforce, for over a decade.

How the Houthis got the strait

What's different this time is the ground the Houthis now hold. The current round of fighting is a spillover from the wider war that opened in February, when the United States and Israel struck Iran; the 2022 truce between the Houthis and the Saudi-backed government, already fraying, broke down over the summer. Fighting resumed in July, and by early September the Houthis had gone on the offensive down Yemen's Red Sea coast.

On 8 September, Houthi missiles and drones hit targets across Saudi Arabia's south — Abha, Jazan, Najran, Khamis Mushait — striking energy infrastructure, airports and military sites and wounding 73 people. Saudi Arabia's Jazan refinery, a 400,000-barrel-a-day facility belonging to state oil giant Aramco, was damaged badly enough that it recorded no exports the following month. The Saudi-backed government's health ministry says roughly 150 civilians have been killed since 3 September. Two days after the initial strikes, Houthi forces seized the port of Mocha; by 11 September they controlled Yemen's remaining Red Sea coastline and Perim Island, which sits in the middle of the Bab al-Mandeb strait itself. They also took the Hanish Islands further north. Houthi units are now positioned roughly 20 kilometres from the African shore, holding the chokepoint through which a large share of the world's shipping and several million barrels of oil pass each day.

Brent crude, which had been trading near $100 a barrel before the offensive, has moved in step with each escalation — gaining more than 5% after a drone strike on a Saudi pipeline forced a shutdown, then easing as Riyadh rerouted crude through ship-to-ship transfers near Oman to work around a partially closed Strait of Hormuz. War-risk insurance for Red Sea transits, already elevated from over two years of Houthi attacks on merchant shipping, remains priced for vessels to avoid the route: carriers weighing a return to Suez after the 2022–2024 disruption now face a strait effectively garrisoned by one side of an active war.

The exodus

The territorial shift set off the displacement the G7 statement gestures at only obliquely. The UN's migration agency, IOM, said at least 112,000 people were displaced inside Yemen over a two-week span in September, with the total climbing toward 125,000 as Houthi forces swept down the coast. Nearly 3,000 people, mostly women and children, crossed the Gulf of Aden to Djibouti in overcrowded boats; on at least one day, arrivals there topped 1,400 in 24 hours. UNICEF put the number of displaced children above 57,000.

Wahida Omer, 50, described crossing from the port town of Dhubab as Houthi fighters swept in: "The sea was frightening, difficult," she told the IOM. Those aboard her boat, she said, were "crying the whole way, and we arrived still crying," with nothing to eat. Another woman, newly arrived with her family, put the need more plainly: "We need everything. We need tents, we need food and all the essentials, because we were displaced and took nothing with us."

What the statement doesn't fix

The G7's language and the scenes on the Djibouti shore describe the same war from opposite ends — one measured in diplomatic phrasing, the other in boats and body counts — and the gap between them is the story. A communiqué that repeats a decade-old arms-embargo demand does not reroute a single tanker or resupply a single displacement camp; it registers concern without shifting the material facts on the water, where the Houthis now sit astride Bab al-Mandeb and Saudi Arabia's southern refineries remain within missile range.

The near-term signal to watch is not another statement but whether Washington or Riyadh moves beyond it — a coordinated naval response to reopen the strait, new sanctions designations tied to the arms flows the G7 named, or a Saudi push for a ceasefire similar to the one that held from 2022. Absent that, the relevant data points sit elsewhere: Brent's reaction to the next Jazan-scale strike, IOM's next displacement count out of Djibouti, and whether the Houthis' grip on Perim Island survives contact with anyone willing to contest it.
