AstraZeneca clears EU and NHS hurdles for Klygefa and breast-cancer drug

AstraZeneca's headquarters and R&D campus in Cambridge, UK. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca won EU backing for its rare-disease drug Klygefa and a reversed NICE decision unlocking NHS access to Enhertu for breast cancer.

AstraZeneca picked up two separate regulatory wins in the space of a week, one widening its foothold in rare-disease medicine, the other closing out a two-year standoff over one of its best-known cancer drugs. Neither is a blockbuster moment on its own. Together they say something about how the company is managing a maturing specialty pipeline against a shifting reimbursement landscape in Europe.

Klygefa clears its first EU hurdle

The European Medicines Agency's Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use recommended approval of Klygefa (gefurulimab), an AstraZeneca Rare Disease treatment developed through its Alexion unit, for adults with generalised myasthenia gravis who test positive for acetylcholine-receptor antibodies. The condition causes progressive muscle weakness through an autoimmune attack on nerve-muscle signalling, and affects a small population — estimates run to well under 100,000 patients across the major pharmaceutical markets.

A CHMP positive opinion is not the final word — that rests with the European Commission, which typically rubber-stamps the recommendation within about 60 days — but it is the substantive hurdle. The opinion rests on the Phase III PREVAIL trial, which enrolled 260 patients across North America, Europe and Asia-Pacific and found Klygefa improved daily-living activity scores by week 26 versus placebo (treatment difference of -1.6 points, p<0.0001), with benefit visible as early as the first week. Results were published in JAMA Neurology.

The drug itself is a dual-binding nanobody that blocks the C5 complement protein, administered by weekly subcutaneous injection — a self-administration profile AstraZeneca is pitching as an advance on infused rivals in the same complement-inhibitor class, which include Alexion's own Ultomiris and Soliris franchises. Filings are still under review in the US and China, so European approval, once formalised, will be Klygefa's first anywhere.

Enhertu finally gets its NHS nod

The second piece of news is a reversal rather than a debut. NICE has recommended routine NHS use in England of Enhertu, the AstraZeneca–Daiichi Sankyo antibody-drug conjugate, for patients with HER2-low advanced or metastatic breast cancer whose disease has progressed on prior treatment. Around 1,000 women a year in England are expected to qualify.

That closes out roughly two years in which Enhertu was the only breast cancer drug NICE had declined to recommend since 2018, after a 2024 rejection on cost-effectiveness grounds. The turnaround came from a new confidential commercial agreement between the companies, NICE and NHS England, layered on top of a wider methods change: from April 2026 NICE's upper cost-effectiveness threshold rose from £30,000 to £35,000 per quality-adjusted life year, alongside a revised approach to quality-of-life scoring published in late August. NICE has linked that threshold increase to the UK–US trade arrangement struck in December 2025, under which higher NHS medicine spending helped secure a zero tariff rate on UK pharmaceutical exports. Enhertu carries a list price of £1,455 per 100mg vial; the discounted NHS terms were not disclosed. The drug has been available in Scotland, which runs a separate appraisal process, since 2023.

What it adds up to

On their own, neither story moves AstraZeneca's numbers much — a rare-disease drug with a sub-100,000-patient ceiling, and a cost top-up for roughly a thousand NHS patients a year. But read together they illustrate the two levers AstraZeneca is pulling in its European business: pushing newer, high-margin specialty assets like Klygefa through first-time approvals, while using the reformed NICE threshold to unlock reimbursement for drugs already approved but previously priced out of NHS use. Watch for the European Commission's formal decision on Klygefa, expected within the standard 60-day window, and for whether Wales and Northern Ireland — where AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo say they will next seek access — follow England's lead on Enhertu.
