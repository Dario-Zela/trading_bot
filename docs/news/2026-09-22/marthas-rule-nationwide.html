Martha's Rule second-opinion scheme rolled out to every A&E in England

King's College Hospital in Camberwell, south London, where Martha Mills died in 2021 after her family's concerns went unheeded. — C Ford / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Martha's Rule, born from a 2021 NHS failure to act on a dying teenager's family, now covers every English A&E — but its mortality benefit is still unproven by trial data.

Starting today, anyone who walks into an A&E department in England — a patient, a relative, a carer, even a nurse who thinks a doctor is missing something — can pick up a phone and demand a second opinion from a critical care specialist. NHS England confirmed the expansion of Martha's Rule to every emergency department on 22 September 2026, extending a scheme that has, until now, applied mainly to hospital wards.

The rule is named for Martha Mills, who died at 13 after a bicycle accident led to a pancreatic injury, an infection, and — six days before her death in August 2021 — septic shock that went unescalated. She was being treated on the paediatric liver ward at King's College Hospital in south London. A 2022 inquest found she would probably have survived had she been transferred to intensive care sooner; the hospital's own review identified five separate points at which a critical care referral should have happened. Martha's mother, Merope Mills, a Guardian journalist, later wrote that consultants had treated a PICU referral as "a sign of weakness." Her family's concerns about Martha's deterioration were, by the coroner's account, not acted on.

From ward pilot to emergency care

Martha's Rule launched in April 2024 at around 100 hospitals, built around three elements: a 24/7 critical care outreach team that staff can already call, the same access extended to patients and families, and a structured daily prompt asking patients and relatives whether a condition has changed. It reached 143 sites by March 2025 and covered every acute hospital ward in England by September that year; maternity units followed in June 2026. Emergency departments — where deterioration can be faster and staff turnover within a shift higher — are the last major setting to be added, and NHS England says the phased rollout across all English A&Es will not be complete until March 2028.

The emergency-care version was tested first: a pilot at seven trusts over eight months logged 69 calls to dedicated helplines, some resulting in immediate surgery or transfer to intensive care. Prof Aidan Fowler, NHS England's national director of patient safety, called the expansion "a critical new lifeline" for families and staff. Across the ward-based scheme more broadly, NHS England reported 12,301 calls between September 2024 and February 2026, with 524 patients receiving an intervention — ICU transfer, a move to a high-dependency unit, or specialist review — that clinicians judged may have saved their life. Mills, who continues to campaign on the issue with her husband Paul Laity, said in May that the volume of calls showed the process "is not being overused and has obviously met a need, giving patients and families real agency."

What the numbers can't yet show

"Potentially saved" is doing real work in that 524 figure. These are retrospective clinical judgements about individual cases, not the output of a controlled comparison between hospitals that have the scheme and hospitals that don't — the NHS has not published, and does not appear to have commissioned, a trial capable of showing whether Martha's Rule moves aggregate A&E mortality. That is not unusual for a patient-safety intervention of this kind, and it does not mean the scheme is ineffective. But it does mean the case for national rollout rests on plausibility and anecdote rather than demonstrated outcome data, a gap acknowledged by researchers who have studied similar family-activated escalation systems in other countries, including Ryan's Rule in Queensland and Condition Help schemes in the United States, where a meaningful share of calls turned out to concern pain control rather than deterioration.

A study of the scheme's early years, published this year and covering what researchers call a "mature system," identified more prosaic barriers to the ones the pilot data can't capture: confusion among patients and families about when the rule applies, reluctance to use it for fear of being seen as undermining staff, and — critically — difficulty remembering an escalation procedure at the exact moment a loved one is deteriorating and attention is elsewhere. Staff reported stress of their own at having their clinical judgement subject to formal challenge. None of this argues against the rule; it argues that a phone number is a necessary but not sufficient fix for the underlying problem Martha Mills's case exposed, which was that a clear warning went unheard inside a functioning hospital hierarchy.

What's next

The practical test now is whether A&E departments — chronically understaffed and often the most chaotic part of a hospital — can sustain the same response time to escalation calls that wards have managed. NHS England has not yet published a compliance or response-time standard specific to emergency care, nor a public dataset that would let researchers assess mortality impact independently of NHS England's own case reviews. The rollout's completion in March 2028, and whatever data accompanies it, will be the point at which the question this piece can't yet answer — does the mechanism change outcomes, or does it mainly change process — gets a real test.
