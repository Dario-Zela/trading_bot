Paramount Skydance settles state lawsuits, clearing path for $110bn Warner Bros Discovery merger

Warner Bros. Television Studios headquarters in Burbank, California — the studio at the center of Paramount Skydance's $110bn acquisition. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Paramount Skydance settled with 12 state AGs and the WGA, securing an editorial-independence board for CNN and CBS and clearing the path to close its $110bn Warner Bros Discovery merger.

Paramount Skydance cleared the last major legal obstacle to its $110 billion acquisition of Warner Bros Discovery on Monday, settling lawsuits brought by a coalition of 12 state attorneys general and the Writers Guild of America that had threatened to keep the deal tied up in court into 2027.

The settlements do not touch the federal approvals the merger has already secured. What they resolve is a state-level antitrust challenge, filed in July by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and eleven counterparts, that argued the combination of two of Hollywood's largest studios and news operations would thin out film output, raise prices for consumers and concentrate political influence over CNN and CBS News in the hands of a single, Trump-aligned owner. Skydance is controlled by David Ellison, son of Oracle co-founder and prominent Trump donor Larry Ellison — a lineage that state officials and the writers' union both cited as reason for concern about editorial interference.

What the states extracted

Rather than block the merger outright, the settlement imposes a five-year menu of enforceable commitments, overseen by an independent monitor. Paramount-WBD must release 30 films annually in its first two years (20 of them wide theatrical releases), rising to 32 films a year, including at least four independent titles, by year five. Miss those targets and the company faces divestment of the Miramax library and $30 million in payments to union benefit funds. On top of that, Paramount committed to an additional $1.5 billion in domestic production spending over five years — a figure that could shift to a 40 percent domestic-production floor if expanded state tax credits arrive, or 20-30 percent if a federal film tax credit passes first.

There is also money set aside for people, not just films: a $47.5 million workforce fund for employees displaced by the merger, and a requirement to honor existing union contracts. Cable distributors get a five-year guarantee that Paramount-WBD will negotiate carriage deals independently rather than bundling leverage across the combined portfolio, and the company must keep Pluto TV free.

"The settlement is not a vote of support for this merger," Bonta told reporters in Los Angeles, adding he still does not believe the two companies should combine. He called the deal states struck "a strong solution that protects competition and consumers."

The editorial independence board

The provision likely to outlast the headlines is a News Editorial Independence Board, which Paramount-WBD must stand up within 180 days of closing to oversee CNN and CBS News. It will comprise five working or retired journalists with at least ten years' experience each, no more than two from the same political party, serving staggered three-year terms. No government official may hold approval rights over board appointments, and no Paramount-WBD executive or non-journalist employee may sit on it. Its mandate: set editorial and journalism principles for the combined newsrooms, and adjudicate complaints of reporting bias or political interference — including interference "from ownership and shareholders."

Not every state that sued got what it wanted. Connecticut Attorney General William Tong said his office had "wanted and demanded full divestiture of CNN and CBS News" and did not get it, though he credited the editorial board structure with delivering something "enforceable under the consent decree." Tong was blunter about why the fight mattered now, linking it explicitly to Donald Trump's decision last week to bar CNN, MS NOW and Politico reporters from the White House — a ban those outlets sued to overturn this week. "The complete abdication of the federal government's enforcement role in this fight left states with one arm tied behind our backs," Tong wrote. "I am deeply disappointed that we could not do more."

Press-freedom advocates share the skepticism. Seth Stern of the Freedom of the Press Foundation called the board "a weak half-measure," arguing an editorial panel answerable to a merger consent decree "poses its own First Amendment problems" — a body adjudicating news coverage is not obviously more independent than the ownership it is meant to check.

The writers' settlement, and what's next

The Writers Guild of America, which had filed its own antitrust suit, settled separately: Paramount agreed to a five-year pause on layoffs of CBS News' broadcast writing staff and a $17.5 million contribution to the guild's health fund, plus attorneys' fees. The WGA was explicit that settling was not an endorsement — it said it still "believes the merger will cause damage to writers and the industry at large," but concluded it could not sustain a multimillion-dollar trial without state backing.

Timing pressure shaped both settlements. Paramount is contractually obligated to pay Warner Bros Discovery shareholders a daily fee — roughly $7 million — for every day past September 30 that the deal remains unclosed. David Ellison has said he still expects to close in the third quarter. Warner Bros Discovery shares rose sharply, by as much as 11 percent, on news of the settlement.

What happens next is less about this deal than the template it sets. State attorneys general have shown they can extract enforceable, court-monitored production and editorial commitments from a media merger even where federal antitrust enforcement steps back — Tong's complaint about a federal "abdication" doubles as a preview of where future leverage in these fights will sit. The independent monitor's first compliance report, and whether the editorial board proves able to intervene in an actual coverage dispute rather than simply exist on paper, will be the tests of whether Monday's settlement was a genuine constraint on the newly combined company or, as its critics argue, a face-saving formality on the way to a deal that was always going to close.
