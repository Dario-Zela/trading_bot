First UK charges brought over 1994 Rwanda genocide

Westminster Magistrates' Court, where Dr Vincent Brown appeared to face the first genocide charges brought in the UK — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A London doctor once cleared for extradition on fair-trial grounds now faces the UK's first-ever Rwanda genocide charges, 32 years after the killings.

Dr Vincent Brown has lived in Islington for two decades under a name he adopted after arriving in Britain. Before that, in Kigali, he was known as Dr Vincent Bajinya. On Tuesday, the 65-year-old appeared at Westminster Magistrates' Court charged with seven offences arising from the 1994 genocide against the Tutsi — the first genocide charges ever brought before a UK criminal court.

The Crown Prosecution Service has charged Brown with one count of conduct ancillary to genocide and six counts of conduct ancillary to murder as a crime against humanity, under section 52 of the International Criminal Court Act 2001. Prosecutors allege that in April 1994, in Kigali's Rugenge district, he directed and incited acts of murder and genocide carried out by others.

"The charges relate to allegations that Dr Brown took part in the genocide against the Tutsi in Rwanda in 1994," said Frank Ferguson, head of the CPS Special Crime and Counter Terrorism Division, confirming that the evidential and public interest tests for prosecution had been met. He will next appear at the Old Bailey.

A seven-year investigation, a two-decade case

The charges are the product of a Metropolitan Police War Crimes Team inquiry that began in September 2019, after Rwanda's government formally asked British authorities to investigate individuals suspected of involvement in the genocide who were living in the UK. Commander Helen Flanagan called it a case that "involved huge effort," and said the outcome should signal that the UK "is not a safe haven" for suspects in international crimes. Other investigations into additional individuals remain open, she added.

But the 2019 start date understates how long this case has hung over Brown. He was first arrested in December 2006, one of four Rwandan nationals detained in Britain and held at Belmarsh prison while Kigali sought their extradition. After an eighteen-day hearing, the High Court ruled in 2009 that the men faced a "real risk" of a flagrantly unfair trial if sent back to Rwanda, and blocked the extradition. A further attempt to extradite failed again in 2017.

Crucially, the 2009 judges did not treat that refusal as the end of the matter. In an unusually pointed press summary, the court noted that if the men were not returned to Rwanda, "these three can still be tried here, provided the Government of Rwanda cooperates" — effectively inviting a domestic prosecution as the alternative to impunity. Parliament followed up by amending the International Criminal Court Act 2001 to let UK courts assert jurisdiction over genocide, war crimes and crimes against humanity committed anywhere from 1 January 1991, closing a gap that had made earlier prosecution impossible. It has taken a further seventeen years for that legal fix to produce an actual charge sheet.

Why the wait matters

The passage of time is itself part of the story. Thirty-two years separate the killings in Rugenge from Tuesday's court appearance; twenty years separate Brown's first arrest from formal charges. Witnesses age, die, or lose clarity of memory; evidence degrades; political relationships between London and Kigali have shifted repeatedly across that span, including strained ties over the UK's now-abandoned Rwanda asylum-deportation scheme. None of that has stopped the case — the Met's War Crimes Team says its investigation involved "numerous deployments and significant international cooperation" — but it illustrates why accountability for mass atrocities so rarely arrives on any timeline a victim could have expected.

It also underscores how fragmented that accountability remains. The International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda closed in 2015 having tried 93 people; thousands more alleged perpetrators are believed to have scattered into diaspora communities across Europe, North America and East Africa, reachable only if and when an individual national justice system decides to act. France, Belgium and the Netherlands have each secured a handful of Rwanda genocide convictions in past years; this is Britain's first attempt, and it rests on a single defendant.

Brown denies wrongdoing and has not yet entered a formal plea. The next hearing, at the Old Bailey, will set a timetable for what is likely to be a lengthy trial process — one that will test, three decades on, whether a British jury can weigh evidence from a killing spree half the world away and half a lifetime ago.
