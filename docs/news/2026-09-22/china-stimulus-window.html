China enters critical stimulus-defining phase as consumer spending stays weak

Shanghai's Pudong skyline across the Huangpu River — China's growth engine remains lopsided between industry and consumers. — Wilson Hui / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
China's factories are outrunning its shoppers — industrial output beat forecasts in August while retail sales grew just 0.4% and home prices kept falling, leaving Beijing a narrow window to decide on more stimulus.

China's economy spent August sending two contradictory signals at once, and the gap between them is now the central question facing Beijing's policymakers as they weigh whether to open the stimulus taps further before the year is out.

Factories kept humming: industrial production rose 5.2% year-on-year in August, beating forecasts and accelerating from July. Producer prices, which had been stuck in deflation for most of the past three years, turned positive too, with the headline PPI up 3.8% year-on-year — though that rebound owes more to an oil-price shock tied to disruptions around the Strait of Hormuz than to any revival in domestic demand. Strip out energy and raw materials, and the picture reverses: prices for finished consumer goods were still falling, down 0.5% on the year, according to National Bureau of Statistics data.

Consumers, meanwhile, barely moved. Retail sales grew just 0.4% year-on-year in August, missing expectations and extending a slowdown that has now run for months. Fixed-asset investment fell 7.2% in the January-to-August period, a deeper contraction than the 6.7% drop recorded through July, dragged down almost entirely by property. New bank lending told the same story from a different angle: banks extended just 60 billion yuan ($8.95 billion) in new loans in August, against forecasts of roughly 400 billion yuan and down sharply from 590 billion yuan a year earlier — a sign that credit demand from households and firms alike remains thin regardless of how cheap borrowing gets.

Property still the drag

The property sector remains the clearest source of the malaise. New-home prices across 70 major cities fell 0.17% in August from July — the 38th consecutive month of decline, though the mildest contraction since December 2025. On a year-on-year basis prices in those cities were down 3.0%, an improvement on July's 3.2% drop. Resale prices told a less encouraging story, falling 0.31% month-on-month, a faster pace of decline than July's 0.29%. In the four first-tier cities — Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Shenzhen — new-home prices fell 0.9% year-on-year, with Shanghai the lone outlier posting a 3.0% gain.

Beijing has not been idle. The People's Bank of China has pledged to keep monetary policy "appropriately accommodative" and has expanded a loan-interest subsidy scheme that covers up to one percentage point of interest on small consumer and service-industry loans, with the central government picking up 90% of the cost. The NBS itself has called for "stepping up macro-policy adjustments" and boosting domestic demand. What has been missing, so far, is anything resembling the broad-based rate cut or large fiscal package that markets have periodically priced in and Beijing has periodically declined to deliver.

Why September matters

Economists increasingly frame the coming weeks as the moment that decides the shape of policy for the rest of the year. Raymond Yeung's team at ANZ Research called September "an important policy window to revive business confidence" ahead of October's Golden Week holiday, when consumer spending data will offer the next real test of demand. Zhiwei Zhang of Pinpoint Asset Management described the economy as sending "diverging signals, as consumption and investment remain weak while industrial production maintains the momentum" — a split that leaves policymakers without a single, clean read on how urgently to act. Citigroup economists led by Xiangrong Yu framed it more starkly: the test is whether a genuine catch-up recovery in consumption materialises in September at all.

The stakes go beyond China's own growth target — a 4.5%-to-5% range for 2026 that officials reaffirmed in March, with H1 growth of 4.7% running inside it but Q2 alone slowing to 4.3%. A visible loosening, or the absence of one, will move commodity prices, Asian equity markets and the yuan, all of which have spent recent months trading on hints of Beijing's next move rather than concrete announcements.

The next hard data points arrive in early October, when September trade figures land, followed by Golden Week retail numbers that will show whether households are still saving rather than spending. Q3 GDP, due in the third week of October, will be the clearest signal yet of whether industrial strength can keep outrunning a housing market still worth watching.
