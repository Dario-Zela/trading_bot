Severe El Niño event threatens tens of millions across Asia with drought, floods and hunger

A farmer surveys a drought-affected plot in Karnataka, India — a preview of the monsoon failures now spreading across South Asia. — Pushkarv / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
WFP warns one of the strongest El Niños on record could push nearly 50 million more people into acute hunger, with Asia's drought-hit harvests already rattling rice markets and regional currencies.

Aid officials are watching the Pacific with a specific kind of dread this year. The World Food Programme says a rapidly strengthening El Niño could push nearly 50 million more people into acute hunger by the end of 2027, concentrated in a belt running from Central America through East Africa and into South and Southeast Asia — the last of these home to some of the world's most trade-exposed rice, sugar and palm oil markets.

The warning is not speculative. The World Meteorological Organization now rates this event one of only four "very strong" El Niños recorded since 1950, with central Pacific sea-surface temperatures already running more than 2°C above normal. WMO forecasters put the odds of the pattern persisting through February 2027 at close to 100% — language the agency's El Niño updates have not used before. The event is expected to peak between September and December this year, with agricultural damage from failed or delayed harvests still working through supply chains well into next year.

The hunger math

WFP's analysis, covering 45 vulnerable countries, projects the global population facing acute food insecurity rising from 225 million to 274 million — a 22% jump. Asia and the Pacific account for an estimated 8.2 million of the additional people at risk, smaller in absolute terms than Africa's toll but concentrated in economies where food already eats up a large share of household budgets. "El Niño is a massive threat to food security of millions already vulnerable," Carl Skau, WFP's acting executive director, said as the agency rolled out anticipatory-action plans across roughly 50 countries. The organisation has released more than $14 million since May to reach 500,000 people ahead of expected shocks, arguing that every dollar spent early saves $3 to $7 in emergency response later. The 2015-16 El Niño, by comparison, is estimated to have pushed 60 to 100 million people into hunger worldwide — a reminder that the current numbers could still worsen as the season plays out.

Where the drought is landing

The clearest damage so far is in India, which depends on the June-September monsoon for roughly 70% of its annual rainfall. This year's monsoon closed with a deficit of around 15% against the long-period average — the weakest season since 2009 — with the sharpest shortfalls in the northwest and central grain belt. That matters far beyond India's borders: it is the world's largest rice exporter and second-largest sugar producer, and a repeat of the export restrictions it imposed during the 2023 El Niño would tighten an already thin global rice market further. New Delhi has record stockpiles that could let it avoid new curbs this time, but traders are treating that as a possibility rather than a certainty.

Further east, Indonesia and Malaysia — which together supply roughly 90% of the world's palm oil — are facing the drought stress typical of an El Niño year, with the Philippines also forecast for pronounced drying. Rice prices have already moved sharply this year, and analysts tracking the core El Niño-sensitive commodities — rice, palm oil, sugar and coffee — have flagged potential price shocks ranging from 10% up to 50-100% in the most exposed crops, reflecting how concentrated production is in the tropical belt the pattern disrupts most.

The market transmission

The hunger crisis and the market story are, in effect, the same event viewed from different vantage points. Indonesia's rupiah and India's rupee are trading near record lows; Thailand's baht, South Korea's won, the Philippine peso and Sri Lanka's rupee are all on track for declines this year. Central banks in the Philippines, Indonesia, Korea, Pakistan and Sri Lanka have already raised rates at least once, and the direction of travel points further that way. "El Niño is just going to make inflation more sticky," said Gary Tan, an equity portfolio manager at Allspring Global Investments, pointing to South and Southeast Asian economies as the most exposed. Weaker currencies compound the problem by raising the local cost of imported food and fuel just as domestic harvests are falling short — a feedback loop that gives central banks little room to ease even where growth is soft.

What happens next hinges on two things converging over the coming months: how India's kharif harvest, now being gathered, actually comes in against that 15% rainfall shortfall, and whether New Delhi reaches for export controls again as it did in 2023. Both will show up quickly in rice futures and in the currencies of South and Southeast Asia's food-importing economies. WMO's next El Niño update, due in the coming weeks, will say whether the event has peaked on schedule or is still building — the detail that determines whether 2027 looks like a recovery year or a second act.
