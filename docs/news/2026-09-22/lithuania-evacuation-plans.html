Lithuania says it has evacuation plans and will 'fight back' against Russian threats

Lithuanian Prime Minister Mindaugas Sinkevičius, who told BBC Newsnight the country has evacuation plans ready and would "fight back" if attacked. — Ieva Ābele-Ozoliņa / Saeima, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lithuania's PM told the BBC it has civilian evacuation routes ready and will "fight back" against Russia, days after NATO jets shot down a drone from Belarus near Kaunas.

Lithuania's government has done something Baltic states have historically been reluctant to do in public: it has told its own citizens, on camera, that it has drawn up plans to move them out of harm's way.

In an interview with BBC Newsnight, Prime Minister Mindaugas Sinkevičius confirmed that Vilnius has prepared civilian evacuation routes to be used in the event of a wider conflict with Russia, while insisting that the country's first instinct would be to resist. "We will fight back — we will engage," he said, describing a population that feels "constantly threatened" by drones and other incursions crossing in from Belarusian territory. He added that the government is "thinking about a variety of scenarios, not all of them being discussed very much publicly" — among them, he said, getting elderly residents out of cities "smoothly" and without the gridlock that a panicked, disorganised exodus would produce.

It is a rare moment of candour from a NATO member that borders both Russia's Kaliningrad exclave and Belarus, a country that has become, in effect, an extension of Russian military planning. The disclosure lands ten days after the most serious airspace violation Lithuania has recorded since the war in Ukraine began.

A drone over Kaunas

On the night of 14–15 September, an explosive-laden drone crossed into Lithuanian airspace from the direction of Belarus, tracked over the Lentvaris and Trakai areas before heading toward Kaunas, the country's second city. Two Italian Eurofighter Typhoons, flying NATO's Baltic Air Policing mission out of Šiauliai air base, were scrambled and brought it down near the village of Pratkūnai, roughly 60 miles west of Vilnius. It was the first time a drone has been shot down over Lithuanian territory, and Lithuanian authorities issued an air-raid alert for residents of both Vilnius and Kaunas while the incident unfolded.

Defence Minister Robertas Kaunas said preliminary evidence pointed to Belarus as the drone's point of origin, though Vilnius has stopped short of formally attributing intent to Minsk or Moscow, pending an investigation. Lithuania's foreign ministry lodged a formal protest, and Foreign Minister Kęstutis Budrys said further measures would follow once the drone's provenance was established. It was also the second time in roughly a month that Italian air-policing jets had responded to an incursion, following a similar scramble on 14 August.

The episode sits inside a longer pattern rather than standing apart from it. Since late 2025, Lithuania has repeatedly closed Vilnius airport and sections of its border after waves of smuggling balloons drifted in from Belarus — incidents the European Union has formally characterised as a "hybrid attack" on Lithuanian airspace, serious enough that it summoned a Belarusian representative in Brussels over them. NATO's response has been to stand up Eastern Sentry, an enhanced vigilance mission along the alliance's eastern flank that in May ran joint counter-drone exercises over Lithuania and Latvia involving Romanian and Portuguese F-16s, Spanish surface-to-air missile batteries and a Romanian Patriot system.

Alliance reassurance, and its limits

NATO allies moved quickly to frame the shoot-down as evidence the system works rather than evidence it is under strain. UK Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said the episode showed "the swift and professional actions of Lithuanian and Italian forces" and that NATO had "once again demonstrated our readiness to protect our airspace," adding that the alliance stands "united, around the clock, in defence of every inch of Nato territory." NATO Secretary-General Mark Rutte has separately warned that Russia is growing "increasingly reckless" along the alliance's border states.

But reassurance from London and Brussels is not quite the same message Vilnius is sending its own public. Sinkevičius's evacuation disclosure is a tacit acknowledgment that deterrence and defence, however credible, do not remove the need to plan for what happens if they fail — and that a population living twenty minutes' flight time from hostile airspace deserves to know the state has thought that far ahead. It is a markedly different posture from the studied reticence Baltic governments have generally shown around civil-defence planning, on the theory that public evacuation talk could look like an admission of vulnerability, or even provoke the scenario it prepares for.

That calculus appears to be shifting. Lithuania has spent much of the past year normalising civil-defence messaging — expanding shelter mapping, running reservist call-up drills, publishing guidance on what households should keep on hand — and the BBC interview reads as the natural, more visible extension of that shift rather than a one-off. Whether other frontline states, Poland, Latvia, Estonia, follow Vilnius into public evacuation messaging will be one signal of how NATO's eastern members intend to manage the gap between what their populations are told and what their militaries already assume.

What to watch

Lithuania's investigation into the Pratkūnai drone's origin is expected to conclude in the coming weeks, and its findings will shape whether the EU or NATO moves toward the kind of coordinated sanctions response already under discussion after the balloon incursions. Also worth watching: whether Latvia and Estonia, which share Lithuania's exposure to Belarusian and Russian airspace pressure, follow Vilnius's lead in discussing evacuation planning publicly, or continue to keep such preparations behind closed doors.
