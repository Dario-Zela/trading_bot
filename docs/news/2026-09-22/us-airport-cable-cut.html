BEYOND THE TAPE · INFRASTRUCTURE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-22 · 3 min read

Cable cut by construction workers delays flights at major US airports

Terminal B at Newark Liberty International Airport, one of five Northeast hubs hit by Monday's cable-cut disruption. — Famartin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A construction crew in New Jersey cut a backup fiber cable, knocking out air traffic control links and delaying over 5,600 US flights.

A construction crew working on a rail project in New Jersey managed to do what strikes and snowstorms usually take credit for: ground planes up and down the Eastern Seaboard. On Monday, a fiber-optic cable serving the Philadelphia Terminal Radar Approach Control facility — the hub that guides aircraft into and out of Newark and Philadelphia — went dead. It was the backup line. The primary one had already failed earlier that morning.

The Federal Aviation Administration ordered ground stops at Newark Liberty, Philadelphia International, LaGuardia, JFK and Teterboro. More than 5,600 flights nationwide were delayed or cancelled before the day was out, according to tracking data, with roughly 1,200 of those at the three New York-area airports. Newark bore the brunt: 267 departures and 257 arrivals delayed. Close to 100 aircraft, some hours into transatlantic crossings, were diverted to other US airports rather than wait out a fix.

A circuit, then a shovel

FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford laid out the sequence plainly: the primary circuit at the Philadelphia TRACON failed first, on its own, for reasons still being examined. Controllers tried to fail over to the backup fiber — standard practice — only to find a construction crew in New Jersey had already severed it. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said on social media that an Amtrak crew doing trackside work cut the line; NJ Transit separately said its own contractors were involved and that site markers had been placed incorrectly, muddying exactly whose spade did the damage. A full fiber repair was expected to take around 13 hours, so engineers instead rerouted the circuit, and by roughly 6pm Monday operations were back to normal.

The timing was awkward. The outage landed the day before roughly 130 heads of state, including President Trump, Volodymyr Zelensky and Benjamin Netanyahu, began arriving in New York for the UN General Assembly.

Not exactly a one-off

Duffy was candid that the vulnerability was not a surprise. "Those are issues that aren't new to us," he told reporters, adding that the department has been trying to modernise FAA telecom architecture but that the work "takes money." Newark in particular has had a rough year on that front: the TRACON facility responsible for its airspace is still working with roughly 24 of a targeted 38 certified controllers, a shortfall that has already forced the FAA to cap Newark to 36 arrivals and 36 departures an hour through next summer's scheduling season. A brief radar and radio blackout at the same facility last spring sent several controllers on trauma leave. Monday's cable cut is a different failure mode — physical infrastructure rather than staffing — but it lands on an airport system that had little slack to absorb it.

It also arrives in a month when transatlantic reliability has already taken a battering. UK air traffic control suffered a second glitch inside four weeks, triggering its own bout of flight chaos on the other side of the ocean. Neither incident points to a single common cause — one is an ageing US telecom circuit, the other a UK systems fault — but together they underline how much of scheduled long-haul travel still rests on infrastructure with very little redundancy margin.

What to watch

No airline has yet quantified the revenue hit, and a single afternoon of delays, however widespread, is unlikely to move quarterly numbers for the likes of United or Delta, both of which lean heavily on Newark and JFK. The more relevant marker is the FAA's telecom modernisation programme, which Duffy has flagged as underfunded relative to the scale of ageing circuitry still in service. Whether Monday's episode accelerates that spending, or simply becomes another entry in the log, will be worth watching when the FAA next reports on Newark's operating restrictions and controller staffing levels.
