Nearly 4 million records of enslaved people across 20 former British colonies now searchable online

Slaves cutting sugar cane in Antigua, 1823 — the same colonial economy the newly searchable registers were built to police. — William Clark / British Library, via Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Nearly 4 million British colonial slave registers are now searchable by name online — a real step forward, but one incremental year in a decade-long digitisation effort, not a sudden opening of the archive.

A cotton merchant in Bristol who insured a cargo of captives in 1793. A registrar in Kingston who counted a plantation's human property every three years, like livestock, so that owners could later be compensated for their loss. An eight-day-old baby entered in a Barbados ledger in 1817 with a name, a birthdate and an owner's mark, and nothing else. Nearly 4 million such entries — covering enslaved people across 20 former British colonies between 1813 and 1840 — are now free to search by name for the first time, in a project led by the genealogy company Ancestry with the Centre for the Study of the Legacies of British Slavery at University College London.

The headline number is real and the human stakes are not overstated: this is, by volume, one of the largest bodies of evidence anywhere documenting people who were enslaved under British rule, now reachable from a search box rather than a reading-room request slip. But the framing of the story — records "searchable for the first time" — flatters a moment that is really the latest step in a slow, decade-long institutional project, not a sudden opening of a vault.

What's actually in the registers

The collection draws on what archivists call the T71 series, held at the National Archives in Kew: colonial slave registers created after Britain outlawed the transatlantic trade in 1807 but before it abolished slavery itself in the Caribbean in 1834. Worried that owners would keep smuggling captives from Africa, the government forced every colony to file a full census of the enslaved, then updated it every one to three years — recording births, deaths, sales, escapes and convictions in slave courts. The Jamaican registers alone begin with a full count in 1817, listing each person by name, sex, age and origin, African-born or Creole.

"When you start to see the names of those individuals, and their ages, and their children, and to realise how systematically this was catalogued," said Professor Matthew J. Smith, the UCL centre's director, "it transforms how you understand the system." Nicka Sewell-Smith, a professional genealogist working with Ancestry, put the practical value more bluntly: the registers are precise enough, she said, that "you can pinpoint an exact birthday for an enslaved baby" — a level of documentation family historians researching enslaved ancestors in the Americas rarely get.

The territories covered stretch well beyond the familiar Caribbean names — Jamaica, Barbados, Trinidad — to the Bahamas, Mauritius and the Cape of Good Hope in what is now South Africa, reflecting how far the plantation and settler economy that depended on enslaved labour actually extended across the empire.

The part that isn't new

What's genuinely new here is indexing, not existence. The physical registers have sat at Kew for nearly two centuries; microfilm and scanned images of the same T71 collection have been browsable, page by page, on Ancestry since well before this year under the title Former British Colonial Dependencies, Slave Registers, 1813–1834. Researchers with the patience to work through unindexed volumes, or the money to hire someone who would, have had access for years. What changed is that the pages have now been transcribed and linked to searchable names — the difference between a locked filing cabinet in a building you can visit and a card catalogue that finds the file for you.

That distinction matters because this is not an isolated event but the latest entry in a run of British slavery-archive milestones stretching back well over a decade. UCL's own Centre for the Study of the Legacies of British Slavery published its first free database in 2013, built from the records of the £20 million paid to compensate slave-owners — not the enslaved — when slavery ended; it named roughly 46,000 claimants and traced the fortunes and family lines that flowed from those payouts. In 2021 the centre announced it was deliberately shifting its own research emphasis away from owners and toward the lives of the enslaved themselves, a reorientation this project is really the culmination of. Earlier this year, a separate three-year project led by Professor William Pettigrew at King's College London published the Register of British Slave Traders, profiling roughly 10,500 of the 13,000 or so individuals who financed and ran the trafficking trade itself. Each of these has, in its moment, been reported as opening a hidden chapter of British history. Each has, in fact, added one more layer to an archive that researchers have been assembling in public view since at least 2009, when the Caribbean slave registers were first inscribed on UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

Why the incrementalism still matters

None of that undercuts the value of what launched this week. Name-level search is a genuinely different tool from a browsable microfilm reel, and the gap between "the record exists" and "a descendant in Kingston or Brooklyn can find it from a laptop" is precisely the gap that determines whether family history research — and the evidentiary spadework behind reparations claims — is realistic for ordinary people rather than funded academics. Sewell-Smith noted that potentially millions of people descend from those named in the archive; for most of them, an unindexed reel at Kew was never a practical option.

The project also lands amid a wider reckoning that the Guardian, as one of the outlets reporting it, is not disinterested in: its owner, the Scott Trust, apologised in 2023 for the newspaper's founders' ties to the cotton trade and pledged more than £10 million toward descendant communities. Caribbean governments, coordinated through Caricom's reparations commission, have spent years arguing that Britain's own archives — meticulous, state-compiled, largely intact — are themselves part of the evidentiary case against it. A register built to protect slave-owners' property claims is now, two centuries later, being read by the descendants of the people it once catalogued as that property.

The next test is less about access than interpretation: UCL and Ancestry say further tranches of colonial records, plus links connecting individual entries to the compensation database already online, are planned for release in stages rather than one archive drop. Whether that follow-through arrives on the timeline promised — and whether it starts joining the story of who was enslaved to the story of who was paid for it — will say more about the seriousness of this effort than any single headline figure.
