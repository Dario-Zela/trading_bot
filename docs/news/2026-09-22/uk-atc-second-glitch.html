Second UK air traffic control glitch in a month triggers fresh flight chaos

Edinburgh Airport's control tower, one of the sites affected by Monday's Prestwick-centre restrictions — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A second NATS control-centre fault in 13 days hit Scottish, Northern Irish and northern English flights, deepening scrutiny of UK air traffic infrastructure.

For the second time in a fortnight, controllers at a National Air Traffic Services centre lost part of their systems network on Monday morning, forcing restrictions across the airspace that funnels traffic through Scotland, Northern Ireland and the north of England. The fault, at NATS' Prestwick centre in Ayrshire, cut capacity in Scottish-controlled airspace by roughly 60 percent for several hours, delaying and cancelling flights at Edinburgh, Glasgow, Newcastle, Manchester, Belfast and Dublin before engineers cleared the restrictions by mid-morning.

NATS said the cause — a "connectivity issue with part of our systems network" — was unrelated to the failure that grounded more than 2,000 flights across the UK on 8 September, when a previously unknown software defect corrupted flight-plan data within roughly a millisecond and triggered a six-hour outage that took more than two days to fully clear. Whether or not the two faults share a root cause, the coincidence of timing has done the damage: two significant control-centre failures in thirteen days, at a state-owned monopoly provider, is now the story regulators and airlines are reacting to.

A pattern, not an incident

Prestwick handles the Scottish Air Traffic Control Centre and the Shanwick Oceanic Area Control Centre, which together manage roughly 70 percent of UK airspace and all North Atlantic traffic passing overhead. It is one of only two centres — alongside Swanwick in Hampshire, where the September 8 fault occurred — that carry the bulk of NATS' national workload. A failure at either one has no comparably sized fallback to absorb the load, which is why a single connectivity fault on Monday was enough to force controllers to throttle traffic manually rather than simply reroute around it.

Ryanair said more than 140 of its flights and over 25,000 passengers were affected, with some delays running to three hours, and renewed its call — first made after the September 8 collapse — for NATS chief executive Martin Rolfe to resign. "Martin Rolfe has presided over repeated system failures, repeated passenger disruption and repeated failures to deliver an effective back-up system," the airline said, adding that if he does not step down, Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander should remove him. NATS has not indicated any change in leadership.

The unresolved first fault

The September 8 outage remains under formal review. NATS's preliminary investigation traced it to a legacy defect in the software module that allocates squawk codes — the transponder identifiers linking an aircraft to its flight plan, altitude and speed. A routine manual code request was interrupted by a higher-priority task, a normal occurrence, but when the original request resumed the system restarted it incorrectly and produced corrupted data, cascading into the wider processing failure. NATS says a software fix is being tested and expects to publish its full Major Incident Investigation within 60 days of the event; Alexander has separately asked the Civil Aviation Authority to run an independent review of NATS's findings and its resilience investment.

That review now carries more weight. A single failure can be treated as a one-off software bug; two in a month, at two different centres, reads as evidence of underinvestment across the network rather than an isolated coding error — precisely the argument Ryanair and other carriers are making publicly.

Who absorbs the cost

The financial exposure sits mostly with airlines, not NATS. The CAA has indicated the September 8 disruption is likely to qualify as "extraordinary circumstances" under UK passenger-rights rules, which would exempt airlines from the standard fixed compensation of £220 to £520 per passenger even though they remain obliged to cover meals, hotels and rebooking. That leaves carriers holding operational costs — crew and aircraft repositioning, duty-of-care spending, schedule rebuilding — without a mechanism to recover them from the infrastructure provider whose systems failed. Ryanair says the September 8 event alone cost it disruption to more than 65,000 passengers.

A state-owned near-monopoly with limited financial liability for its own outages has correspondingly little market pressure to accelerate redundancy spending — which is the gap regulators are now being pushed to fill by other means, whether through tighter CAA oversight of resilience investment or a change to how liability is apportioned between the provider and the airlines it serves.

The next concrete marker is NATS's Major Incident Investigation report on the September 8 fault, due within 60 days, alongside the CAA's independent review of NATS's resilience spending. Both will land under considerably more scrutiny than they would have a fortnight ago.
