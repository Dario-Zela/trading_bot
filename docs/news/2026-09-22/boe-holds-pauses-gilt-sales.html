Bank of England holds rate at 3.75%, pauses gilt sales in QT overhaul

Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey, who said the QT overhaul was meant to give markets "clarity" on the path ahead. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The BoE held rates at 3.75% on a 6-3 split vote and paused gilt sales to 2027, easing long-end yields but leaving the rate path unresolved.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on Thursday, but the vote that got it there was anything but tidy. Three of the nine Monetary Policy Committee members — Chief Economist Huw Pill among them, alongside Megan Greene and Catherine Mann — wanted to raise rates to 4%. The other six held the line. It is the kind of split that tells a reader more than the headline number does: a central bank no longer sure which direction the next move should come from.

Alongside the rate decision, the Bank rewired the mechanics of quantitative tightening, the slow unwind of the gilt portfolio it built up during years of crisis-era bond buying. The MPC voted unanimously to pause gilt sales — a halt to long-dated sales entirely, and a six-month freeze on the broader programme — while it works out a multi-year path to bring the stock of gilts held for monetary policy purposes to zero by 2034. Gilt yields fell in response, with longer-dated debt catching the biggest relief.

A hold that masks disagreement

On its face, holding Bank Rate at 3.75% is the least newsworthy thing a central bank can do. But a 6-3 vote is a wide split for the MPC, and it comes with an inflation backdrop that explains the discomfort. August CPI came in at 3.1%, and the Bank's own projections now put inflation around 3.75% in the fourth quarter, rising further to just above 4% in early 2027 — a path the Bank pins largely on higher global energy prices. Governor Andrew Bailey flagged the risk directly, warning that a prolonged conflict in the Middle East could yet force the Bank's hand toward tighter policy, not looser. "Surging global energy prices have had a quite subdued impact on general inflation in the UK," Bailey said, "but it is early days."

That three MPC members — including the Bank's own chief economist — voted for a hike while inflation is already running toward 4% is a signal worth taking seriously. It suggests the committee's centre of gravity has shifted further from cutting than markets had priced only a few months ago, even as the majority judged a hold sufficient for now. Sterling slipped modestly on the announcement, a sign traders read the split as marginally hawkish overall, even with rates unchanged.

The QT pivot

The more structural news was the rewrite of quantitative tightening. The Bank still holds roughly £488 billion in gilts bought during years of asset-purchase programmes, and until now it has been running that stock down through a mix of maturities and active market sales — sales that have been blamed, rightly or not, for adding to pressure on long-dated gilt yields already strained by heavy government borrowing. Thursday's announcement pauses those sales for the next six months and halts long-dated sales outright, with the Bank instead sketching a multi-year plan running out to 2034 and floating the idea of selling gilts directly to the government rather than into the open market.

Bailey described it as an attempt to remove uncertainty rather than a shift in the inflation fight: "Today we provided clarity over the future of our quantitative tightening policy," he said, adding that the Bank and MPC had "decided to withhold a substantial part of the stock of gilts held for monetary policy purposes from sale while the remainder will be unwound over the next eight years." The vote on the QT overhaul, unlike the rate decision, was unanimous — the MPC may disagree on where rates should sit, but it agrees the current pace of bond sales was adding avoidable strain to a market already under pressure.

Gilts breathe, cautiously

The market reaction bore that out. Ten-year gilt yields fell toward 5.2% on the announcement, and 30-year yields — which had hit their highest level since 1998 only days earlier amid a broader global bond selloff — eased to around 5.70%, their lowest in more than two weeks, for a second consecutive session of relief. That is a genuine move, but it is worth keeping in proportion: a yield in the high 5% range on the long end of the UK curve is still historically elevated by any measure of the past two decades, and two sessions of easing does not undo a run of borrowing-cost pressure that has been building for most of the year.

The modesty of the relief is itself informative. If investors believed the QT pause solved the structural problem — a market straining to absorb both continued gilt issuance from the Treasury and Bank sales on top of it — yields at the long end would likely have fallen further and faster. Instead, the muted scale of the move suggests the market sees the pause as buying time rather than fixing the underlying imbalance between gilt supply and demand.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the two decisions describe a central bank hedging in both directions at once. On rates, the Bank is unwilling to cut while inflation heads toward 4%, and a meaningful minority on the MPC would rather tighten further. On the balance sheet, it is unwilling to keep pressing gilt sales into a market that is already paying the highest long-term borrowing costs in decades. Neither move commits the Bank to a clear direction — the QT pause removes one source of upward pressure on yields without addressing the deficit financing behind it, and the rate hold buys time without resolving the split on the committee.

For traders, the practical upshot is a market with slightly less duration supply to absorb in the near term and a rate path that remains genuinely two-sided, rather than the well-worn one-way bet toward cuts that has characterised BoE commentary for much of the past two years. The next test comes with the November MPC meeting and the accompanying Monetary Policy Report, when the Bank will have another two inflation prints — and any further escalation in the Middle East — to weigh against a committee that is, for now, more divided than it has been in some time.
