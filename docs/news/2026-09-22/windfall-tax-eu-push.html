Shell and BP profits reignite windfall-tax push as six EU states seek coordinated levy

A BP filling station: Shell, BP and TotalEnergies have all posted sharply higher profits since the February Middle East war disrupted oil markets. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Six EU states want Brussels to replace patchwork national windfall taxes with one bloc-wide levy on oil majors' profits; the Commission isn't committing yet.

Oil majors are once again making more money than Brussels quite knows what to do with. Shell's second-quarter adjusted earnings more than doubled year-on-year to $9.84bn, beating analyst forecasts of $8.79bn, while BP's underlying profit for the same quarter came in at $5.7bn, up from $2.4bn a year earlier. TotalEnergies posted $5.4bn, roughly double its year-ago figure. The trigger was the same for all three: the February war between the US, Israel and Iran, which briefly closed shipping lanes through the Strait of Hormuz and sent Brent crude spiking above $126 a barrel before it settled back near $100.

That spike has now reignited a fight that first flared in 2022, but with a twist. Instead of each capital legislating its own levy, six EU finance ministries — Germany, Italy, Austria, Poland, Portugal and Spain — spent the summer pressing Brussels for something new: a single, coordinated windfall-tax framework applied across the bloc rather than a patchwork of national ones.

From national levies to a joint letter

The six ministers laid out their case in a letter to Ireland's finance minister, Simon Harris, whose country holds the EU's rotating presidency. Their argument centred on refining margins rather than crude prices alone: oil companies, they wrote, are "enjoying overall profitability and margins on refined products that exceed the rise in crude oil prices," and "excessive crisis profits must be returned to consumers." Analysis by the campaign group Transport & Environment puts the number behind that claim at roughly €24bn — its estimate of what oil majors will collect this year from European drivers alone through fatter refining margins on road fuel, before accounting for jet fuel, marine bunkers or heating oil.

The request went from letter to formal agenda item startlingly fast. EU finance ministers met informally in Dublin on 18 and 19 September — days before this article — specifically to take initial soundings on the idea. German finance minister Lars Klingbeil, the most vocal advocate, told reporters: "You know that I have been fighting for this for a long time, together with other European finance ministers." Spain's Carlos Cuerpo made the distributional case plainly, arguing there "may be fairer ways to distribute this cost" than passing higher energy bills on to taxpayers.

Why fragmentation has suited the industry

Up to now, Europe's windfall-tax landscape has been a mess in the industry's favour. During the 2022–23 energy crisis, individual measures ranged from the UK's 25 percent levy — since raised to 38 percent, taking the total tax rate on North Sea profits to 78 percent and extended out to March 2030 — to Slovenia's 80 percent and Ireland's 75 percent. By 2025, most of those had lapsed: the Czech Republic, Hungary and Spain all let theirs expire, leaving Italy's regional production surcharge, raised from 3.9 to 5.9 percent in February, as one of the few EU measures still standing. The European Commission's own 2023 review of the 2022 levies found that "diverging implementation strategies across Member States have reportedly led to significant investor uncertainty," and at the time recommended winding the whole approach down rather than repeating it.

A single EU-wide rate would remove that arbitrage. It would also, industry bodies argue, apply to companies whose upstream production and refining are increasingly conducted outside the bloc — Transport & Environment itself notes that with roughly a fifth of Europe's diesel imported, a chunk of the windfall accrues in non-EU jurisdictions a Brussels levy cannot reach. That is one of the arguments Paris is using to hold back: French finance minister Roland Lescure told the Dublin meeting, "I can't support something before I've seen it," stressing that any framework would need to account for how differently each member state's energy sector is structured — a live issue in France, where TotalEnergies' profits have drawn a domestic windfall-tax push of their own from the opposition Socialists.

The Commission holds the line, for now

Brussels itself is not rushing. Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis's response in Dublin was carefully non-committal: "Member states can already implement windfall profit taxes at the national level if they decide to do so. From the Commission side, we are ready to support those member states by sharing best practices and finding a good way forward." That is a polite way of saying taxation remains a national competence, and that reopening it at EU level — which in practice requires unanimity among 27 finance ministries — is not something the Commission will force. Klingbeil is pushing back on the delay, pressing the Commission to table concrete windfall-tax models in time for October's formal Ecofin meeting.

Meanwhile Britain, outside the bloc but facing the same political pressure, has been tightening screws of its own. Chancellor Rachel Reeves announced in May that she would close the "foreign branch exemption," a structure she said let oil and gas groups — including BP and Shell —"structure their tax affairs in a way which ensures they pay little or no corporation tax on their UK energy trading profits." The Treasury expects the change to raise "hundreds of millions of pounds a year," funding cost-of-living measures tied to the same Middle East-driven price shock.

What happens next

Crude has already given back much of the war premium — Brent slid to around $100 a barrel on 21 September, its fourth straight session of losses, as diplomatic signals around a possible Trump–Pezeshkian meeting at the UN General Assembly this week eased fears over the Strait of Hormuz. Forecasters now see Brent averaging closer to $90 in the second half of the year. That undercuts the urgency behind the windfall-tax push even as it gathers political momentum: the case for taxing an emergency spike gets harder to make once the spike itself is fading. The next concrete marker is October's Ecofin meeting, where Klingbeil wants the Commission to show its hand. Whether it does — and whether Paris, still unconvinced, signs on — will determine if this becomes the EU's first real test of coordinated fiscal policy on energy, or another letter that quietly expires once oil prices do the disinflating for it.
