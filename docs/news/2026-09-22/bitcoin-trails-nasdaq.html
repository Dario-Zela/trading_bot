Bitcoin trails Nasdaq for the year as hedge debate resurfaces

Physical bitcoin coins, a common stand-in for an asset that exists only on-chain. — Jorge Franganillo / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Bitcoin is roughly flat for 2026 while the Nasdaq is up 15.5%, reviving debate over whether it still works as a portfolio hedge.

Bitcoin closed at $86,602.91 on Monday, leaving it down roughly 1% since the start of the year. The Nasdaq Composite, over the same stretch, is up 15.5%. For an asset frequently marketed to institutional allocators as an uncorrelated store of value — a hedge that zigs when tech portfolios zag — a 16-percentage-point gap in favour of the thing it's supposed to diversify against is hard to wave away.

The shape of Bitcoin's year explains the frustration. It opened 2026 near $87,520 and touched a high of $97,008 on 15 January, then went through two separate corrections: a 15% single-session drop on 6 February took it to roughly $60,000, more than half off its October 2025 record of $126,200, and a second leg down in June carried it to a year-low of $60,862. A 13.6% April rally and a slower summer climb have now brought it almost exactly back to where it started — a round trip that looks less like an uncorrelated hedge and more like a leveraged, choppier version of the trade it was meant to offset.

The correlation numbers cut the other way

Here is where the debate gets more interesting than a simple scoreboard. Grayscale Research, in a note flagged by its head of macro strategy Zach Pandl, says Bitcoin's 90-day rolling correlation with the Nasdaq Composite has fallen from around 60% to 33% since the summer, while its correlation with gold has climbed from near zero to roughly 50%. Grayscale ties the shift to what it calls the "debasement trade": US gross federal debt crossed $40 trillion around 18 August, and the firm argues investors are increasingly treating Bitcoin, like gold, as a hedge against currency and fiscal risk rather than a tech-sector proxy.

BlackRock's Robbie Mitchnick, global head of digital assets, has made a similar case publicly, arguing Bitcoin's "closest comp" is gold rather than the Nasdaq, and that its diversification value shows up during geopolitical stress or bond-market pressure — not during equity rallies driven by AI earnings, which is precisely the environment 2026 has delivered. He points to IBIT, BlackRock's spot Bitcoin ETF, which saw only modest outflows during the 50%-plus drawdown from October's high, as evidence of a stickier, longer-horizon holder base than the price action alone suggests.

The flow data underneath that claim is genuinely volatile: US spot Bitcoin ETFs took in $3.52 billion in August, their best month of the year, then swung to a $236.5 million single-day outflow on 1 September — 85% of it from IBIT — before reversing into a $731 million inflow just two days later, the largest since January.

That combination — falling correlation to tech, rising correlation to gold, and a year-to-date return still 16 points behind the Nasdaq — is not a contradiction so much as the actual state of the argument. Bitcoin may be decoupling from the trade that hurt it earlier in the year while still not delivering the offsetting gains a hedge is supposed to produce. Whether that gap closes depends largely on whether the debasement narrative keeps building: the next reading on US debt issuance and any shift in Federal Reserve rate guidance will be the more meaningful test than any single week of price action.
