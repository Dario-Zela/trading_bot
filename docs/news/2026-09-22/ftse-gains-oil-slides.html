FTSE 100 gains as oil slides for a fourth session, though banks and energy lag

A North Sea oil platform — the kind of asset behind BP and Shell's underperformance as crude fell for a fourth straight session. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
London's blue-chip index rose modestly on falling oil, while easing Middle East supply fears weighed on BP and other energy majors.

London's blue-chip index edged higher on Monday even as its two heaviest sectors — banks and energy — failed to keep pace, a split that says as much about what investors are afraid of as what they're buying.

The FTSE 100 closed up around 0.7%, near 10,740, clawing back most of Friday's 0.6% slide. The lift came as Brent crude fell for a fourth consecutive session, dropping toward $100 a barrel and extending a run that has taken more than 9% off the price over the past week. BP and Shell, the index's two oil heavyweights, each slipped roughly 1% — moving in the opposite direction to the gauge they sit in.

Why oil keeps falling

The crude slide is not about weakening demand — it is about supply fears unwinding. Satellite tracking cited by Trading Economics showed Saudi Arabia moving oil through the Strait of Hormuz at roughly 2.9 million barrels a day over the past six days, versus under 700,000 bpd in August, even with Riyadh's East-West pipeline still offline after recent attacks. Total Middle East flows have held near 17 million bpd. That resilience, combined with reports that Washington is cooler than Saudi Arabia on striking Houthi positions and that Donald Trump could meet Iranian president Masoud Pezeshkian on the sidelines of this week's UN General Assembly, has taken the geopolitical risk premium out of the price faster than most traders expected a fortnight ago.

For a market that spent August pricing in the possibility of a Hormuz disruption, that is unambiguously good news. It is also the reason BP and Shell are lagging: their earnings have been propped up by the same fear premium now draining out of the crude price.

Banks add a second layer

Energy's drag was compounded by a mixed session for financials. Some of the more internationally exposed lenders — HSBC, Barclays, Standard Chartered — rallied hard, helped by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's upbeat read on weekend US-China talks. But domestically focused banks found less to cheer after the Bank of England's rate decision last week, when policymakers held at 3.75% on a 6-3 vote and simultaneously paused gilt sales as part of a broader overhaul of quantitative tightening — a move that leaves the path for mortgage and savings rates less clear than a unanimous hold would have. With August inflation at 3.1%, three MPC members still wanted to hike, and that tension has kept the sector's gains narrower than the headline index suggests.

The read for a rules-based portfolio

The useful signal here is not the FTSE's 0.7% gain — headline index moves are the least interesting number on days like this. It is that the rally was driven by falling event risk rather than improving fundamentals, and that the two sectors most levered to that risk premium (energy) or to policy uncertainty (banks) sat it out. That is a rotation, not a broad risk-on move, and it argues for weighting sector dispersion over index-level momentum when the two diverge this cleanly.

The next test is data, not sentiment: further confirmation of Saudi export volumes through the Strait of Hormuz, and any concrete outcome from Trump-Iran contacts at the UN General Assembly this week, will determine whether oil's fourth straight losing session becomes a fifth or reverses. A snapback in crude would flip the sector story back in energy's favour and put the current divergence down as noise rather than signal.
