US diesel prices hit $6.51 as inflation and freight-cost fears build

Diesel and gasoline prices have climbed sharply as Middle East refinery outages squeeze global fuel supply. — Tony Webster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
US diesel hit a record $6.51 a gallon, up 69% on the year, as Middle East refinery outages complicate the Fed's next rate call.

US diesel prices touched an all-time high of $6.5107 a gallon on Monday, according to AAA, capping a run that has pushed the national average up more than 87 cents this month alone and roughly 69% above the $3.69 a gallon drivers paid a year ago. The fuel first broke $6 on September 11 and has climbed almost daily since, blowing past the previous record of $5.81 set in June 2022.

The proximate cause is supply, not demand. The war between Israel and Iran, now in its seventh month, has curtailed tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — a disruption the International Energy Agency has called the largest to hit the global oil market on record — while strikes tied to the separate Ukraine conflict have knocked out an estimated 5 million barrels a day of refining capacity, including Kuwait's Al-Zour plant, which has managed only a single export cargo since March versus roughly 191,000 barrels a day before the war. Crude has pushed back above $100 a barrel as a result, and one commodity strategist described the resulting diesel deficit as one "no handshake can refill."

The pain is already visible in corporate results. J.B. Hunt Transport Services warned that third-quarter earnings would fall 5% to 10% from the prior quarter, citing a roughly $10 million sequential fuel headwind on top of $25 million in added driver costs, and its stock dropped more than 13% on the news — one of the worst single-day moves in the company's four decades as a public firm. Farmers are absorbing a similar squeeze heading into harvest: a University of Illinois economist estimated the diesel spike is adding about $11 an acre to corn production costs and $7 an acre for soybeans versus last year.

A complication for the Fed

The timing matters because the Federal Reserve had only just moved. On September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee raised its target range by a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, its first hike since 2023, citing inflation that remained too far above the 2% goal — a decision taken as diesel was already grinding toward $6.06 a gallon. Diesel's role as an input cost for freight, farming and construction means further increases feed into producer prices with a lag before reaching consumers, which is why traders are watching the October 27–28 meeting as a genuine toss-up between a hold and a second consecutive hike; it is a non-SEP meeting, so no fresh dot plot will accompany whatever the Fed decides.

With no ceasefire in the Iran conflict in sight and refining capacity still offline, analysts have begun floating $7 diesel as plausible rather than alarmist. The next major data point is the October Fed decision itself, but the more immediate one is simply whether AAA's daily average keeps climbing through the end of the month.
