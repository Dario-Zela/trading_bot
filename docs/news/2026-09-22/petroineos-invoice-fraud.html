Former refinery boss accused of £334m 'fake invoice' fraud

The Lindsey Oil Refinery in North Killingholme, Lincolnshire, before its 2025 collapse into administration. — Chris / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Administrators accuse former Prax boss Winston Soosaipillai of fabricating £334m in invoices to prop up the now-collapsed Lindsey oil refinery.

UK administrators have formally accused Winston Soosaipillai, the former chairman and chief executive of collapsed refiner Prax Group, of orchestrating a fraud that saw at least £334m in fictitious invoices sold to raise cash against a strained balance sheet — one of the largest alleged frauds to surface at a major UK industrial asset in years.

The claim, filed at the High Court in London by administrators Teneo, alleges Soosaipillai instructed senior employees, named in court papers as Jegan Benedict and Jeffrey Benedict, to fabricate invoices as Prax faced mounting costs from refinery works and a deepening liquidity squeeze. Those invoices were sold into a receivables securitisation facility arranged by HSBC in 2021 and ultimately backed by roughly £739m in bank funding, with contributions from HSBC, Citi, JPMorgan and Royal Bank of Canada. Administrators say around £277m of that facility remains outstanding.

From refinery to receivership

Prax's parent, State Oil, filed for insolvency on 30 June 2025 with debts exceeding £1.5bn, triggering the collapse of the Lindsey oil refinery in North Lincolnshire — a plant that once supplied roughly a tenth of Britain's road fuel. The government stepped in with financial support to keep the site running while administrators searched for a buyer. Phillips 66 agreed in January to acquire the refinery's assets but has no plans to restart standalone operations, folding key units into its nearby Humber refinery instead. Around 125 of the site's 420 jobs were cut in the process; the remainder were guaranteed employment only until the end of March.

Administrators say they uncovered the alleged invoice fraud only after being called in, describing a "large hole" in the receivables facility once they began reconciling it against real trading activity. The claim against Soosaipillai alleges breach of fiduciary duty and misrepresentation, and separately accuses him of personally benefiting from £23.5m in loans and overseeing £7.8m in dividend payments that may have been improper given the state of the group's finances. The High Court has frozen roughly £150m of his assets.

Soosaipillai, who co-founded Prax with his wife Arani in 1999 and built it into a business reportedly worth billions, has not been reachable by administrators or officials since the collapse; associates believe he has left the UK. In a statement issued through lawyers, he has said he "always acted in good faith" to protect the refinery and called allegations of dishonesty "untrue and offensive," denying he knew of any fictitious invoices before May or June 2025.

Why the numbers matter to lenders

The episode sits alongside a separate High Court ruling this week allowing Glencore to pursue a $236m claim against the Prax estate, after the commodities trader says it was induced by fraudulent misrepresentations to keep delivering crude to Lindsey before the collapse. Together the cases paint a picture of a securitisation structure — designed to let a trading business borrow against invoices it had already issued — that banks accepted at face value for years without catching a discrepancy between paper and product.

For institutional lenders and investors who treat receivables facilities as relatively low-risk, asset-backed exposure, the Prax case is a reminder that the underlying collateral is only as reliable as the invoices behind it. Automated credit models and periodic audits are built to catch balance-sheet stress, not manufactured paperwork sitting inside otherwise plausible financial statements.

The civil claim against Soosaipillai is expected to proceed through the High Court over the coming months; a criminal referral has not been confirmed. The next marker will be whether administrators can recover meaningful sums from the frozen £150m in assets, and whether Soosaipillai — still unlocated — ever answers the claim in person.
