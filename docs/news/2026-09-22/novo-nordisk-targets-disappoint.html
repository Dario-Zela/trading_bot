Novo Nordisk shares plunge 7% as new growth targets disappoint

Novo Nordisk's headquarters in Bagsværd, Denmark, where the company built its obesity-drug franchise before Eli Lilly overtook it in market share. — © News Øresund – Johan Wessman, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Novo Nordisk shares sank over 7% after its $23bn-by-2035 pipeline plan failed to show how it beats Eli Lilly before semaglutide's early-2030s patent cliff.

Novo Nordisk shares fell more than 7% on Monday after the Danish drugmaker used its Capital Markets Day in London to lay out a decade-long growth plan that investors read as an admission: the company's dominance of the obesity-drug market is over, and what comes next will take years to arrive.

US-listed shares dropped to $39.82, while the stock's primary Copenhagen listing pared losses to close about 5.4% lower. It was the sharpest one-day move for Novo since a bruising profit warning in February, and it extends a year-to-date decline that had already run past 20% coming into Monday's session.

A decade of targets, not a quarter of relief

Chief executive Mike Doustdar, who took over the company in mid-2025 after his predecessor was pushed out amid the first cracks in Novo's obesity story, told investors the company would launch more than five "multi-blockbuster" medicines by 2030 and generate over 150 billion Danish kroner — roughly $23 billion — in risk-adjusted pipeline sales by 2035, on top of what its current drugs already bring in. Novo also said it wants to serve more than 60 million patients globally by 2030 and build manufacturing capacity to supply ten times as many people with its oral GLP-1 obesity pill.

The numbers were large. The problem, analysts said, was the timeline. Novo guided that revenue growth between 2026 and 2030 would run "in line with industry peers" — a group that includes Eli Lilly, AstraZeneca, Amgen, Merck, AbbVie and Novartis — rather than the outsized growth that once made it one of Europe's most valuable companies. For a stock still priced, however grudgingly, for a return to category leadership, in-line-with-peers was read as a downgrade in ambition.

"Investors hoped for a project 'miracle' that could turn the momentum around short term," said Per Hansen, savings economist at the Danish broker Nordnet. "For obvious reasons, that miracle does not exist." Jefferies analyst Michael Leuchten flagged a related concern: the near-total absence of deal-making. "They should be doing business development and considering adding new assets to the portfolio," he said, noting rivals have used acquisitions to plug pipeline gaps while Novo has largely built in-house.

The patent cliff underneath the pitch

The targets were also an implicit acknowledgment of a problem Novo has been reluctant to spell out in public: semaglutide, the molecule behind both Ozempic and Wegovy, drives roughly three-quarters of the company's revenue, and its patent protection in major markets begins expiring in the early 2030s. Doustdar told investors as much directly, framing the 2035 pipeline target as the plan for what replaces semaglutide once cheaper copies arrive.

That framing cuts against Novo's near-term problem, which has nothing to do with patents and everything to do with Eli Lilly. Lilly's tirzepatide drugs, sold as Mounjaro for diabetes and Zepbound for obesity, combined for $36.5 billion in 2025 sales — ahead of the $33 billion Novo drew from Ozempic and Wegovy over the same period — and Lilly has since overtaken Novo in GLP-1 market share outside the United States. A 2024 head-to-head trial found tirzepatide produced greater weight loss than semaglutide, a result that has shaped prescribing habits on both sides of the Atlantic. Novo itself has warned that 2026 sales and operating profit could decline by as much as 13% as US list prices come under pressure and patents lapse in markets including Brazil, Canada and China.

What the market is actually pricing

The gap between the two companies is now showing up directly in guidance. Lilly has told investors to expect $80–83 billion in total 2026 sales; Novo, by contrast, is bracing for its first annual profit decline in years. Against that backdrop, a 2035 target — nine years out, covering assets still in earlier-stage development — offered little for a market that has spent the past eighteen months re-rating Novo from category leader to competitive number two.

That re-rating carries weight beyond one stock. The GLP-1 obesity category is widely expected to become one of the largest single therapeutic markets in pharmaceutical history, and a two-horse race narrowing toward a single dominant player has implications for pricing power, payer negotiations and how much of that growth accrues to Denmark's largest company versus an Indianapolis rival five times its size by some 2026 sales estimates.

Novo's next real test arrives with third-quarter earnings, expected in early November, when investors will look for the first hard evidence that Doustdar's restructuring — including the direct-to-consumer push and manufacturing build-out flagged Monday — is showing up in prescription volumes rather than slide decks. Until then, the stock is likely to keep trading on a simple question the Capital Markets Day did not resolve: whether Novo can defend a shrinking share of a market it invented, or is settling into permanent second place behind Eli Lilly.
