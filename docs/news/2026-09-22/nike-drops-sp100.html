Nike drops out of S&P 100 as LVMH's Alexandre Arnault joins the retailer

Nike's market value has fallen from $264 billion in 2021 to roughly $57 billion today. — Wonderlane / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Nike exited the S&P 100 after a 78% market-cap collapse since 2021, the same week LVMH's Alexandre Arnault joined its board — two events with no stated link between them.

Nike's near-two-decade run in the S&P 100 ended before Monday's opening bell. S&P Dow Jones Indices confirmed the swoosh would be replaced in the blue-chip index by Dell Technologies, Palo Alto Networks, Arista Networks and SanDisk — four companies built on AI infrastructure, none of them selling a single sneaker. Nike remains in the broader S&P 500, but the demotion is a hard number attached to a well-known story: a company that once defined its category is now smaller, by market value, than several firms most shoppers couldn't name.

The scale of the decline is the real story here. Nike's market capitalisation has fallen from roughly $264 billion at its November 2021 peak to about $57 billion today — a 78% drop over five years, including a 36% slide in 2026 alone. The share price has gone from $179 to around $38. Over the same stretch, the S&P 100 itself gained 83%, which is the mechanical reason Nike no longer qualifies: index membership tracks relative size, and Nike simply stopped growing while its index peers did not.

A turnaround still finding its footing

The underlying business explains the multiple. Fiscal 2026 revenue came in at $46.4 billion, down 2% on a currency-neutral basis, with Greater China sales falling 17% in the fourth quarter — the eighth straight quarter of decline there. Direct-to-consumer revenue dropped 6% to $17.7 billion as Nike pulled back from the store-opening spree of the DTC era; its retail footprint shrank 4% over the past year. Wholesale, the channel Nike spent a decade trying to de-emphasise, is now doing the work of holding revenue up, rising 6% to $27.5 billion. Competitors matter too: Anta and Li Ning have taken share in China, while Hoka and On have chipped away at the premium running category Nike once owned outright.

Chief executive Elliott Hill has stuck to a measured line through the slide. "While we continue to face top-line headwinds, we're encouraged by progress in performance product and are focused on consistent execution, improved profitability and scaling our wins," he said of the results. It is the language of a company managing a long repair job, not one bracing for a single dramatic fix.

Arnault's arrival, and what it doesn't yet answer

Six days after the index exit, Nike announced that Alexandre Arnault — deputy chief executive of LVMH's Moët Hennessy division and formerly head of Rimowa and an executive at Tiffany & Co. — would join its board, effective September 15. Executive chairman Mark Parker called Arnault someone who has "earned a reputation for helping iconic global brands evolve, innovate and grow." Hill's framing pointed to Arnault's record in "innovation, digital transformation and building consumer connections."

What neither statement does is commit Nike to anything specific. This is a board seat, not an operating role — Hill remains CEO, and Nike has not said Arnault will touch pricing, product tiering or the wholesale-versus-DTC mix that's driving the current numbers. LVMH's brands compete on scarcity and price power; Nike's problem is closer to the opposite, an oversupplied, discount-prone mass market it is still working through. Analysts covering the appointment have mostly read it as a governance move — adding a director with brand-turnaround experience — rather than the opening move of a luxury pivot. The timing invites a tidier story than the facts currently support: two events in the same week, one clearly financial (the index exit, a function of market-cap math), the other clearly speculative (whether an LVMH executive's board presence changes strategy at all).

The next real test of that question comes with Nike's fiscal first-quarter results, due in the coming weeks, where China trends and DTC margins will show whether Hill's "scaling our wins" has started to show up in the top line — a more reliable signal than any index reshuffle or board appointment on its own.
