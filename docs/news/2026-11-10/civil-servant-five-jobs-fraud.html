Civil servant who secretly held five jobs convicted of fraud

In one sentence
A civil servant reported to have held five government jobs at once faces fraud charges over almost £350,000; the verdict is unconfirmed, and the case tests public-sector oversight of remote work.

A civil servant who is reported to have drawn salaries from several government departments at once has, according to the headline that prompted this piece, now been convicted of fraud. The case file that has been published is a trial record, and it is more revealing about how the arrangement worked than the verdict itself. The conviction could not be independently confirmed. The coverage found describes Southwark Crown Court hearing the prosecution case in mid-October, with no verdict reported.

The allegations

The defendant is Kashim Chowdhury, 54 or 55 depending on the report. He faced nine counts of fraud, which he denied, along with any dishonest intent or intention to make a dishonest gain. Prosecutors told the court he earned almost £350,000 from eight employers between February 2020 and September 2023. These included Tower Hamlets Council, the Home Office, Defra, the UK Health Security Agency and the Department of Health and Social Care. By May 2023 he was reportedly being paid for five full-time roles at once. At one point, the court heard, his contracts added up to 183 hours of work a week.

The prosecution said he exploited the home-working habits that settled in during the pandemic. He allegedly told employers he needed to work from home to care for an unwell mother. He was also signed off sick from some jobs with "mixed anxiety and a depressive disorder", but did not give the sick note to every employer.

The make-up

The detail that travelled furthest was a video call. A manager, the court heard, saw him coughing and blowing his nose while wearing what prosecutor Lucy Organ called "laughable" white make-up. It is the sort of image that makes a fraud case memorable, and it also shows how thin the checks were. A call, a sick note and a plausible domestic reason were apparently enough to satisfy several employers in turn.

The defence painted a different picture. He described the work as his "lifeline" and gave evidence about his mother's declining health in 2022. Online reaction, by some accounts, leaned towards sympathy for a worker accused of hustling. That sentiment says little about the law, which turns on whether the employers were deceived.

How it was found

The most useful detail for public bodies is how the scheme came to light. One report says the National Fraud Initiative flagged it to Defra. The NFI is a data-matching exercise that compares payroll and other records across public bodies, so the discovery came from outside the individual employers. None of them appears to have noticed the others. Whether any of them could have done so is the question the case raises. The published material does not settle it.

That matters because the significance depends on a distinction the record does not yet draw: whether this is one person's dishonesty or a control failure. Departments that do not share payroll data, and that rely on trust once someone is working remotely, may have little chance of spotting a second salary. On the evidence so far, the match, not a manager's suspicion, did the detecting.

What to watch

The verdict and, if there is a conviction, the sentence will show how seriously the court treats the losses to public money. Any review by the departments involved would show whether they intend to change how they verify outside employment, or leave detection to periodic data matching. Similar cases, including a council worker found guilty of fraud after holding four public-sector jobs at once, suggest the pattern is not unique.
