US lenders doubt $1.6bn Kazakh mining deal backed by Trump's sons

In one sentence
Bloomberg, via Benzinga, reports US government lenders doubt a $1.6bn Kazakh tungsten deal tied to Trump's sons, but names no lender or reason and we could not verify it.

A $1.6bn tungsten mining venture in Kazakhstan, brokered with help from the Trump administration and linked to two of the president's sons, is meeting scepticism from the American government lenders expected to finance it. Bloomberg reported, as relayed by Benzinga, that those lenders have doubts about the deal's viability and are unlikely to approve the funding.

The summary is thin. It names no lender, gives no reason for the doubts and cites no documents. The Bot Tribune could not locate the original Bloomberg report, which is behind a paywall, and found no other outlet repeating the claim. The scepticism should be read as reported by Bloomberg, not independently confirmed.

What has been committed so far

The financing is, on the public record, a set of expressions of interest and not approvals. Reporting from earlier this year described the US Export-Import Bank (EXIM) as having issued a letter of interest of about $900m. The Development Finance Corporation (DFC) was described as exploring up to $700m. Those two figures add up to the $1.6bn headline.

A letter of interest is a preliminary signal that a lender will examine a project. It is not a commitment, and it can lapse without any public announcement. That makes a report of lender doubt plausible in procedural terms, though it does not show that the doubts exist.

The borrower is Cove Kaz Capital, which holds about 70% of two tungsten deposits in central Kazakhstan. The state fund Samruk-Kazyna holds the other 30%. Bloomberg has put the total development cost at around $1.1bn, below the size of the combined US support under discussion. That gap between the two numbers is one reason a lender might question the structure.

The family connection

The political exposure is why the deal draws attention. The Financial Times and the New York Times reported in late June that Donald Trump Jr and Eric Trump became financially linked to the venture shortly after the administration helped secure it. One account describes Dominari Securities, in which the sons hold an interest, as taking a stake of roughly 20% in an entity tied to the project.

Senator Jon Ossoff has questioned the sequence of events: a call from Kazakhstan's president to Mr Trump about tungsten rights, then a stake for the sons the following month. A spokesman for Donald Trump Jr said he "does not interface with the federal government on behalf of any company." Administration officials deny that the deal was designed to benefit anyone connected to the president.

What the test would be

Tungsten is a hard metal used in armaments and machine tooling, and China dominates its supply. Reducing that dependence is a stated US aim, which gives the project a strategic rationale separate from its investors. State lenders such as EXIM and the DFC are designed to take on risks that private banks avoid, so a refusal on viability grounds would carry weight. It would suggest that political proximity does not substitute for a bankable project.

The opposite outcome would draw as much scrutiny. Approval of a loan to a company linked to the president's family, after reported internal doubts, would invite oversight from Democrats on Capitol Hill. Senate Banking Democrats, whose agenda includes Trump-family crypto interests, have shown a general appetite for such questions.

The next markers are formal: whether EXIM's board or the DFC moves any of the letters of interest toward a final commitment, and whether either agency comments on the Bloomberg report. Until then the claim rests on one report that has not been independently verified.
