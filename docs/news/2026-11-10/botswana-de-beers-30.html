Botswana to raise De Beers stake to 30% under proposed Anglo American sale

In one sentence
Botswana says it will double its De Beers stake to 30% under Anglo American's proposed sale to a Gareth Penny-led consortium, though binding documents are yet to be signed.

Botswana will double its holding in De Beers to 30% if Anglo American's proposed sale of the diamond producer goes through. President Duma Boko announced this on Monday, according to Reuters-based and AP reports. The state already owns 15% of the company. A buyer group led by Gareth Penny, a former De Beers chief executive, would take most of the 85% Anglo is selling, and Gaborone would get a much larger say in what it becomes.

The deal is not done. Anglo American says it will keep working with Botswana, other governments and the Global Diamond Consortium towards binding transaction documents. Until those are signed, the 30% is a stated intention inside a proposal.

What has been announced

Boko's statement settles a question that had drifted for months. In July, Botswana said Anglo had picked a preferred buyer and that it was weighing its right of first refusal and an "optimal structure". Officials then said it could join the consortium or act alone. Earlier reporting had Botswana seeking a majority stake, with estimates for its end-state holding ranging from about 25% to 49%. Angola had signalled a rival interest.

The 30% figure ends that range, at least for now. Boko also said Namibia and Angola would take part in the transaction, which suggests a deal with several African governments as co-owners. The reports I reviewed do not give the size of the Namibian and Angolan holdings, so I have not estimated them.

The price is also uncertain. Bloomberg-sourced reporting in July put the figure at roughly $1bn for the Anglo stake. Another report this week cited a $4.9bn valuation for De Beers as a whole. The two are not necessarily in conflict, since they may measure different things, but the reports do not reconcile them. The final terms will say which number holds.

Why Gaborone cares

Botswana's interest is fiscal before it is strategic. Diamonds have been the backbone of its economy for decades, supplying a large share of export earnings and government revenue. That rests on the Debswana joint venture, which mines the country's diamonds and is owned equally by the government and De Beers. A weaker diamond market has strained the budget, and Gaborone has little reason to leave its largest industry in the hands of a new owner it does not influence.

A 30% stake changes the position in two ways:

  • Governance. Thirty per cent would give Botswana a larger board presence and more weight on decisions about sales, mine investment and the future of the business.
  • Revenue. Dividends rise with the stake. More important, a bigger shareholder has more leverage in negotiating how revenue is shared between the mine and the seller.

It would still be a minority holding, not the control that earlier reports said Botswana wanted. The consortium would hold most of the remainder, and the 30% gives Gaborone influence, not a veto.

What the buyers bring

Penny ran De Beers's parent business from 2006 to 2010. Reported backers of the consortium include Signet and Richemont, and sightholders such as Pluczenik and Diarough. The mix points to a buyer built around the diamond trade, with retailers and dealers among its owners. Anglo's earlier statements favoured bidders with diamond experience. Whether that pattern would serve independent buyers is a question for the deal documents.

The model matters beyond Botswana. If producing countries take large, formal stakes in the company that sells their stones, other governments may seek similar terms. Namibia's and Angola's participation points that way.

What to watch

Botswana's minister said in July that the sale would likely be finalised in the last quarter of 2026, subject to government approval. The next markers are the signing of binding documents and the disclosure of the price and stake splits. Anglo's exit from De Beers is the last big step in its portfolio reshaping, so its next results and any announcement will show whether the deal holds together.
