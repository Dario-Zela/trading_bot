Palantir lobbied minister to widen AI tools' reach to NHS GP records

In one sentence
The Guardian reports Palantir pressed a minister to widen its AI tools' access to GP records; the claim is unverified here, and the surrounding NHS data disputes are well documented.

A report in the Guardian says Palantir, the US data-analytics company, pressed a government minister to widen the reach of its AI tools to patients' GP records. The Bot Tribune has not been able to read the original article or find a second source. The minister's name, the date of the contact and any supporting documents are therefore not given here, and neither a government nor a Palantir response has been located. What follows is the claim as reported, set against the public record on Palantir and NHS data.

What is alleged

The reported claim is that Palantir lobbied a minister to extend its tools from the hospital and operational data it already handles into primary care. If that happened, it would involve a different and more sensitive dataset. GP records hold a lifetime of diagnoses, prescriptions, mental-health notes and family details. Hospital data on bed capacity, waiting lists and theatre scheduling is much narrower.

Lobbying a minister is lawful and routine, and meetings with companies are normally disclosed in ministerial transparency returns. The questions are what was asked for, whether the request was recorded, and whether anyone acted on it. None of those is answered in the material available here.

The existing footprint

Palantir already holds a central NHS contract: the Federated Data Platform (FDP), reported to be worth about £330m.[^1] NHS England has said the platform will not combine GP records nationally.[^2] That assurance is the baseline against which any push to widen access would be judged.

Other recent reporting shows why the issue is live:

  • The Financial Times reported that Matthew Swindells, joint chair of four north-west London hospital trusts, privately encouraged colleagues to integrate GP data into the FDP. Freevacy's summary of that report describes him as also advising Palantir.[^3] That is one account from one outlet.
  • The Guardian has reported that NHS England decided to let external staff from contractors, including Palantir, access identifiable patient data through a new administrative role. Labour MP Rachael Maskell called the move "dangerous".[^4]
  • The Guardian has also reported that health officials fear Palantir's reputation will hinder delivery of the £330m contract.[^5]
  • Trade press reports that a health minister has declined to commit the government to using the contract's break clause.[^6]
  • Separately, the Guardian reported that Palantir directly contacted a different minister, Tom Pursglove, urging use of its software against benefits fraud. The Department for Work and Pensions told the paper in response to a freedom of information request that its ministers had not met Palantir.[^7] That is a different department and a different subject, but it shows the company approaching ministers directly.

Why the dataset matters

Three questions follow, and the reporting available here does not settle any of them.

Privacy. Patients can opt out of some NHS data uses, and health officials have reportedly acknowledged that mistrust of Palantir is pushing people to opt out.[^8] Wider access to GP records would test that trust further. Whether it required new legal authority is a question for the Department of Health and Social Care and the Information Commissioner's Office.

Procurement. The FDP contract was awarded for specific purposes. A vendor asking a minister to widen its remit raises the question of whether the change would be competed, varied within the existing contract, or handled some other way.

Access to ministers. Disclosure rules show who met whom. They do not show what was asked of the minister or what came of it. Any record of this meeting, and any follow-up, would help establish whether the Guardian's account holds up.

What to watch

The first test is whether the government or Palantir responds on the record, and whether the original reporting is backed by documents. The department's next quarterly transparency release of ministerial meetings should show whether such a meeting is recorded. Parliamentary questions from MPs who have already challenged the FDP, such as Ms Maskell, are the likeliest route to a formal answer. The government's decision on whether to keep the FDP contract, which is reportedly still pending, would show whether any wider role for Palantir is being considered at all.
