England to ban phones from pupils' bags and pockets during the school day

In one sentence
From September 2027 England's schools will be expected to keep phones out of pupils' bags and pockets all day, closing the 'no see, no hear' loophole, with unions warning of costs.

England's schools are being told to go a step further on mobile phones. Classroom bans are already widespread, and since June the government's guidance has asked schools to keep pupils phone-free for the whole school day. Now ministers want phones kept off pupils' bodies altogether: from September 2027, a pupil should not have a phone in a bag or pocket between arriving at school and leaving it.

Education Secretary Lucy Powell announced the change on 9 November, as reported by the BBC. Her message to parents and pupils, she said, was that "school is for learning and friendship, not scrolling". The government's argument is that access to a phone "risks pulling children away from their learning and from each other".

From 'no see, no hear' to 'not on you'

The guidance became statutory in June 2026, and schools were expected to follow it from the start of the autumn term. It made schools and trusts responsible for keeping the school day phone-free, covering lessons, the gaps between them, breaktimes and lunch. Schools must have regard to the guidance and depart from it only with good reason. Ministers have been careful not to call it an outright legal ban.

The loophole the new rules close is a common one. Many schools run a "no see, no hear" policy: the phone may stay in a blazer or rucksack as long as it is silent and out of sight. Under the tougher line, a phone that stays on the pupil, even switched off, no longer complies.

Campaigners say the older approach has not held. Charlotte Ashton of Generation Focus told the BBC that "no see, no hear" policies were not working and that pupils were still "TikToking in the toilets".

Who does the policing

The government is not prescribing a method. Schools choose how to comply. According to the Department for Education, some use sealed pouches or lockers. Others collect phones at the start of the day, and some bar them from the premises.

That flexibility is also where the cost sits. School leaders' unions broadly accept the aim but not the bill. Pepe Di'Iasio of the Association of School and College Leaders said the guidance "does at least provide some clarity", but that leaders "cannot simply make mobile phones disappear". He added that the plan is "not feasible unless the government puts in the funding required to meet these costs". Paul Whiteman of the NAHT acknowledged the "safety issues" and "unhelpful distraction" phones can bring, while warning that stronger measures "come at a cost".

The costs are practical. Storing hundreds or thousands of devices securely takes equipment and staff time. Pupils who travel alone, or who have medical apps or family arrangements that depend on a phone, will test whatever exceptions schools allow. The reporting does not say how the government expects schools to handle those cases, and it mentions no new funding.

Politically, the change has little opposition. Shadow education secretary Laura Trott said she was "delighted that Labour have finally listened to us" and backed a proper smartphone ban in schools. Her criticism was of a lack of "joined up thinking" about new technology allowed into classrooms. The government has also announced a review of safety standards for technology used in schools. The Children's Commissioner, Dame Rachel de Souza, has supported strong action on distraction.

A patchwork across the UK

Education is devolved, so the four nations are moving at different speeds and in different ways. Scotland plans legislation to restrict personal phone use in schools and ran a consultation over the summer. In Northern Ireland, Education Minister Paul Givan launched a 12-week consultation in August on whether to ban phones in schools by law. In Wales, the Senedd voted against a national statutory ban, leaving headteachers to set their own rules within official encouragement to restrict use.

England's approach sits between a binding statute and pure local discretion. The guidance is statutory but the means are left to schools, and compliance will be tested through the schools system rather than the courts. The phone question also sits alongside a separate measure: a social media ban for under-16s is due in March, covering platforms such as Instagram, Snapchat and TikTok.

What to watch

The 2027 date gives schools about ten months to decide how to store or exclude devices. The first practical tests are whether the government attaches money to the policy and how far inspectors judge schools on enforcement. The reports reviewed do not say whether the 2027 change will be issued as revised guidance or follow a consultation, and that is worth confirming. Until the final text appears, the point at which a pupil's pocket becomes a school's business remains undefined.
