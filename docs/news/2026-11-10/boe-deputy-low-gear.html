Bank of England deputy warns young Britons are stuck in a 'low-gear economy'

In one sentence
BoE deputy governor Clare Lombardelli says young people bear the cost of a 'low-gear economy' with 4.7% unemployment, and that a December rate rise is a live possibility.

A Bank of England deputy governor has tied the weak jobs market for young people to the interest-rate outlook. In doing so she has turned a social concern into a monetary-policy one. Clare Lombardelli, deputy governor for monetary policy, says young Britons are bearing the cost of what she calls a "low-gear economy", and that a further rate rise is possible as soon as December. The account below rests on the Guardian's report of the speech, which was due to be delivered in London on Tuesday. The full text had not been checked against the Bank's own publication.

A market that has stopped moving

Lombardelli's diagnosis is a labour market in which firms neither hire nor fire. "It's what I call a low-gear economy," she said, according to the Guardian. Employers are holding on to existing staff and freezing recruitment as the Middle East war pushes up energy prices and clouds the outlook.

The figures, as reported:

  • UK unemployment has risen to 4.7% this year.
  • Youth unemployment has risen faster and is at its highest outside the Covid pandemic since 2015.
  • The number of young people not in education, employment or training is close to one million.
  • Redundancies remain low, the "low hire, low fire" pattern.

The pain falls on new entrants because that is where hiring has dropped most. Vacancies have fallen furthest in hospitality and retail, the sectors where many people get a first job. Employers surveyed by the Bank now regard hiring a young worker as "a higher risk than it used to be". "What you see is the cost falling on young people," Lombardelli said.

Why a central bank cares

The usual market reading is that rising unemployment loosens wage pressure and eventually helps bring inflation down. Lombardelli cast doubt on that. "You can have higher unemployment without it exerting a downward pressure on inflation," she said. Her reasoning is that firms are hiring only for hard-to-fill roles, so the jobs being lost or never created are not the ones that set the price of labour. Slack at the entry level therefore does little to cool wages.

That matters for how the Bank weighs a weak labour market against an energy-driven price shock. Under this reading, rising joblessness does not argue for lower rates, because it does not relieve the inflation problem.

The rate path

The Bank raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4% last week, its first increase in more than three years. Lombardelli voted with the majority. She said a further quarter-point rise could be warranted as early as the December meeting and that "every meeting is live for us". She put no probability on it. According to the Guardian, markets price December at roughly even odds.

She made the outcome conditional on the Iran conflict, energy prices and the wider data. Governor Andrew Bailey and other Monetary Policy Committee members have also raised the prospect of another rise. The Guardian reports that oil has slipped below $100 a barrel, which would ease some of the pressure. Related coverage in this edition tracks the Iran talks and the oil move.

What to watch

Two dates matter. The first is the Bank's December meeting, where the 50/50 pricing will be tested against the labour data released beforehand. The second is the report on young people and work from Alan Milburn, which the government is expecting next week and which will show whether Whitehall is treating the youth jobs squeeze as a policy problem of its own. The speech's main signal for rates is that youth unemployment is not, in the Bank's current thinking, a reason to hold off.
