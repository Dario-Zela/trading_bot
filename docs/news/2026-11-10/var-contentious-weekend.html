VAR under scrutiny after a contentious Premier League weekend

In one sentence
A BBC review of a contentious Premier League VAR weekend revives the question of whether the technology or the officials are at fault, though its specific incidents are unconfirmed.

Another Premier League weekend, another round of argument about video assistant referees. A BBC Sport review of the latest set of contentious decisions has reopened the question that has trailed the system since it arrived in 2019: is the technology failing, or are the people operating it?

The Bot Tribune has not been able to read the BBC's incident-by-incident analysis. This piece therefore does not describe individual decisions from the weekend, and it names no matches or officials in connection with them. What can be said is where the debate stands.

A familiar argument

The case that the technology is not the problem rests on how VAR works. It does not make decisions. It flags a possible clear and obvious error to the on-field referee, who can overrule it or accept it. Offside lines, goal-line checks and contact replays are largely settled. The disputes tend to cluster in judgment calls: whether a challenge was a foul, whether contact was enough to matter, and whether an error was "clear and obvious" or merely arguable.

That shifts the question from hardware to people. Officials differ on where the threshold sits, and a threshold that moves from week to week is what supporters notice and clubs complain about.

The season has already offered an example. Reports from September said the VAR team from the Manchester derby, Matt Donohue and Blake Antrobus, was left out of the following round after a goal was awarded to Erling Haaland. Pro Ref, the body that runs match officials, issued a statement on the call. Howard Webb, its chief refereeing officer, was reported to be under pressure over the weekend's incidents.

The numbers cut both ways

The league's defenders point to the figures. The Key Match Incidents panel, which reviews disputed calls, recorded 18 mistakes last season, down from 31 in 2023-24, according to an ESPN report. A fall of that size suggests the system is improving.

The counter-argument is that a count of errors does not capture how they feel. A slow review, or a decision that looks inconsistent with one made a fortnight earlier, damages trust regardless of the tally. Former referee Keith Hackett, in his weekly Flashscore column, has called one four-minute review of a Nottingham Forest goal "unacceptable".

What to watch

The next test is whether Pro Ref explains this weekend's calls publicly, as it did after the derby, and whether any VAR teams are stood down. The Premier League's next Key Match Incidents panel will show whether the officials' own review agrees with the BBC's verdict. That answer will matter more to clubs than the argument over technology.
