Ukraine talks resume with 'some flexibility' on both sides ahead of Abu Dhabi round

In one sentence
US officials say Russia and Ukraine are privately flexible on territory before Abu Dhabi talks, but no concession is named and public positions on Donetsk have not moved.

Ukraine and Russia are said to be privately softer on the central question of territory than their public statements suggest, with US officials saying so on the eve of a two-day round of talks in Abu Dhabi. The round is reported to run on 10 and 11 November. Donald Trump has said it offers a chance of a breakthrough. No concession has been named and no text has been agreed, so for now the claim is an early signal and not a result.

What has been said

The optimism comes from officials who are not named in the reporting. According to accounts carried by Reuters, the BBC and Axios, one US official said both sides are showing some flexibility on a possible exchange of territory. That flexibility is private. The same official said the public positions have not moved: Moscow still demands the whole of Donetsk region, while Kyiv proposes freezing the line where the armies now stand.

The White House described itself as "cautiously optimistic", in Axios's account. One official said it was the first time they had seen "real desire on the Russian side to finish". That is a statement about intent, and intent is not a concession. Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner are leading the American side. Kushner is reported to have said the round could at most build momentum towards the next phase, which is a lower bar than Trump's talk of a breakthrough.

The gap between those two framings is the story. The president's language is the most public and the most upbeat. The people closest to the process are more measured, and Ukrainian officials reportedly do not expect a definitive result from the first session.

The record so far

This is not a fresh start. US-brokered trilateral talks opened in Abu Dhabi in January, the first publicly acknowledged direct negotiations between Moscow and Kyiv on the American plan. Al Jazeera reported that the January round ended without a breakthrough, with territory the main obstacle. The core question then was whether Ukraine would withdraw from the part of Donbas it still holds, as Russia demanded in exchange for a ceasefire.

A second round followed in early February. Kyiv's delegation called the first day "substantive and productive", yet Volodymyr Zelensky accused Russia of violating a Trump-brokered pledge to stop attacks on energy facilities. Talks in Geneva later that month also closed without a result. This week's meeting is reported to be the first trilateral round since then.

The pattern is consistent: warm language at the opening, an unchanged map at the close. Similar signals of movement have appeared before without producing a signed text.

What would count as movement

The agenda gives some indication of where a real shift could show up. One session is reported to cover territory, another ceasefire monitoring and postwar security. Security guarantees for Ukraine, possibly modelled on NATO's Article 5, are expected to feature. Witkoff and Kushner have also invited NATO, the EU and several major European powers to separate discussions on the sidelines. That suggests Washington wants European backing for any guarantee before it is offered.

Three things would turn the reported flexibility into news. The first is a stated position on Donetsk that differs from the public one: a defined zone, a demilitarised strip, or a monitored line. The second is a joint statement or draft text, even a partial one on ceasefire monitoring. The third is a follow-up date with a defined agenda, as opposed to a general commitment to keep talking.

The stakes reach beyond the battlefield. A credible path to a ceasefire would bear on European gas and power prices, on Black Sea grain shipments and on the security calculations of every NATO member in the east. For that reason markets and governments will react to hints well before there is anything to sign. Without a named concession, that reaction would run ahead of the evidence.

What to watch

The first signs will be the statements issued after the second day, on 11 November. Reporting on whether Moscow or Kyiv has accepted any wording on a line of contact will matter more than Trump's description of the mood. Any announcement of a date for the next round, and of who attends from Europe, will show whether the private flexibility has survived contact with the table.
