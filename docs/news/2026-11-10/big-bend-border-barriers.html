Supreme Court lets Big Bend border construction proceed

In one sentence
The Supreme Court, with Justice Jackson alone noting she would deny the request, paused a judge's order so Big Bend border construction can resume while litigation continues.

The Supreme Court has cleared the way for border-barrier construction in the Big Bend region of Texas, including parts of Big Bend National Park, while a lawsuit over the project is still being heard. The order came on Monday, 9 November. It means the wall can go up before any court rules on whether the government had the authority to build it.

The justices acted through an unsigned, four-page opinion. Only Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson noted that she would have denied the government's request, and she gave no reasons. The Court granted the administration's request to pause a lower-court order. It did not grant the separate request to reverse that order outright.

How the case got here

The dispute turns on a 1996 immigration law. The Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act lets the Homeland Security secretary waive legal requirements, environmental statutes among them, to speed up construction of border barriers and roads. Courts may hear challenges to those waivers only if they allege a constitutional violation.

In 2026, the department issued three waivers covering the Big Bend region, which takes in areas of the national park and almost a quarter of the US-Mexico border. Those waivers were signed first by Kristi Noem and then by her successor, Markwayne Mullin. The plaintiffs are an environmental group, a local landowner and a group formed to preserve a church. The case is captioned Mullin v. Friends of the Ruidosa Church.

On 2 October, US District Judge Kathleen Cardone in El Paso temporarily barred construction in the region until the government complied with environmental and other applicable laws. In a written opinion on 14 October she found the plaintiffs likely to succeed on their argument that the secretary lacked the authority to issue such sweeping waivers under the major questions doctrine. That doctrine holds that Congress must speak clearly before an agency claims power of vast significance. She also held that the claim could be heard despite the statute's limit on court challenges. On the park's place in the wider border picture, she noted that the Big Bend sector accounts for more than a quarter of the border but only a small share of migrant apprehensions, between 0.3 and 2.2 percent according to her findings.

The administration took the matter to the Supreme Court on 21 October. Solicitor General D. John Sauer asked the justices to pause the order and to reverse it summarily. The government's filing also argued that the delay was costly, putting the loss at nearly half a million dollars for each day construction was on hold.

What the Court said

The majority wrote that the government "is exceedingly likely to prevail on its argument" that the district court lacked the power to hear the major-questions claim. According to reports of the opinion, it also pointed to what it called Congress's "clear preference for expeditious border construction," which it said outweighs "any aesthetic, recreational, and environmental harm."

The stay is temporary. It lasts while the government's appeal proceeds, and if the Court declines to take the case it ends automatically. That leaves open the possibility of a later merits ruling. But construction can proceed in the meantime, and it is hard to restore a landscape once it has been cut.

Why it matters

The order is not a ruling on whether the wall is lawful. It is a ruling on who gets to proceed while that question is open, and the answer was the government. The Court's reasoning on jurisdiction points the same way. If the lower court probably could not hear the claim at all, the plaintiffs' route to challenge the waivers narrows considerably.

For park managers and conservation groups, the practical stakes are wildlife movement and the Rio Grande corridor, where physical barriers can interrupt animal passage and alter flooding patterns. Other environmental challenges to waived border projects will now be read against this order, even though it settles nothing on the merits.

The next step is the government's appeal and whether the justices agree to hear the underlying case. The plaintiffs have not yet said how they will respond. Details such as the length of barrier planned inside the park were not available in the reporting reviewed.
