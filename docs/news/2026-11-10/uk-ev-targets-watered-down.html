Government expected to water down EV sales targets this week

In one sentence
BBC Politics reports ministers will cut UK electric vehicle sales targets this week, but the revised figures are unconfirmed and earlier reports pointed to 50% by 2030.

Ministers are preparing to soften the rules that force carmakers to sell a rising share of electric vehicles in Britain, according to BBC Politics. The report says a reduction in the targets is expected this week. The revised figures have not been confirmed, and the government has not published a proposal.

What the mandate requires

The zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate sets a legal share of new car sales that must be zero-emission, rising each year. The share was 22% in 2024. It reaches 80% in 2030 and 100% in 2035. Manufacturers that miss the share face penalties, though the rules include flexibilities such as credit trading between companies.

The scheme is a sales quota on manufacturers, not a subsidy and not a ban on driving petrol cars. That matters for the reported change. Loosening the quota would not alter what motorists may buy or drive. It would alter how hard carmakers must push electric models into showrooms, and how much they must invest to do so.

What has been reported

The BBC report is the basis for this article, and its detail is thin. Earlier press coverage, from around June, was more specific. Several trade and national outlets, citing The Sunday Times, said the 2030 requirement could fall from 80% to 50%. They said the shift followed lobbying from carmakers and unions who warned of job losses. Reports differed on whether the 2030 end-date for selling new petrol and diesel cars would stay. The Guardian was reported as saying it would. Other outlets suggested it could be dropped.

These reports were unofficial and have not been matched to a published text. Whether the 50% figure is what ministers are now preparing is not established. Neither is whether the 2035 target would change, or whether any change would go out for consultation first.

Reaction to the earlier reports split along familiar lines. The union Unite welcomed a cut. The charging industry body ChargeUK warned that any reduction would be a backward step. Car industry commentary described the reports as a victory for manufacturers. The sector has argued that demand has not kept pace with the quota, and that discounting to hit the numbers is eroding margins.

What a weaker target would change

The first effect would be on investment. A carmaker planning around 80% electric sales in 2030 has to commit now to battery sourcing, model development and factory retooling. A 50% target lowers that requirement, but it also removes certainty that companies had factored into long-lead decisions. Some firms that have already spent heavily may resent a retreat that lets rivals who lagged off the hook.

The second effect is on infrastructure. Charging networks have expanded on the premise that millions more electric cars are coming. Lower sales would slow the case for new public chargers. It would also slow grid connections and the second-hand electric market that follows new-car sales by a few years.

The third is climate policy. Road transport is the largest source of UK domestic greenhouse gas emissions. The advisory Climate Change Committee has treated rapid electric car uptake as central to the legally binding carbon budgets. Cutting the 2030 share would leave a gap that other sectors, or later years, would have to fill. The government has not yet said how.

There is a counter-argument that deserves weight. Quotas that outrun consumer demand can force price cuts and cost manufacturers money. A slower ramp could, in principle, protect jobs while the market catches up. The question is whether that gain outweighs the loss of a signal the industry has planned around.

What to watch

The immediate test is the text itself. The things to look for are the 2030 and 2035 percentages, whether the petrol and diesel end-date survives, and whether the changes arrive as a consultation or a final decision. Responses from the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders, ChargeUK and the Climate Change Committee should follow quickly. They will show how far the industry reads the move as relief and how far as a lost certainty.
