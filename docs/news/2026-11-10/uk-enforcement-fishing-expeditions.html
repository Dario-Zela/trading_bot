Mahmood's new immigration enforcement agency accused of 'fishing expeditions'

In one sentence
Freedom of information figures show two-thirds of UK immigration enforcement visits in 2025 ended without arrests and most were warrantless, but the Home Office denies 'fishing expeditions'.

Britain's expanded immigration enforcement operation, which the Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, has championed, is under fresh criticism over how it chooses its targets. Figures obtained under freedom of information laws suggest most of its visits end without an arrest, and most are carried out without a warrant. A former immigration officer calls many of the raids "fishing expeditions". The Home Office rejects that description.

The figures come from an investigation published on 9 November by the Guardian and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism. This article draws on a repost of that investigation, not the original. The numbers below are as reported and have not been independently checked.

What the figures show

The requests covered 2025. They record 23,538 enforcement engagements, a category that includes workplace raids, home raids and spot checks. That is about 60 a day. Of these, 15,578, or 66.1%, ended with no arrests.

Warrants were also rare. Around 74% of visits in which officers entered premises were made without one. Only 25.81% used a warrant. Officers can lawfully enter licensed premises, such as restaurants and bars, without a warrant under the Licensing Act 2003. They can also enter with the occupier's consent, which happened in more than 12,000 cases last year.

The source of the tip-offs matters too. About 89% of "immigration crime" leads came from the public or from Crimestoppers, and only about 2% came from police.

The operation has also produced results the government is keen to cite. Arrests of people working illegally have roughly doubled since Labour took office. The Home Office said in August that more than 17,000 such arrests had been made since July 2024.

The critics

Kevin Barker, a former immigration officer who now advises businesses wrongly accused of employing unauthorised workers, told the investigation that raids are often "fishing expeditions" built on tenuous tip-offs. Warrants cannot be sought on old or uncorroborated intelligence, he said, "but it doesn't stop them".

Mothin Ali, the Green Party's deputy leader, called the findings alarming. "This is racist harassment dressed up as immigration enforcement," he said.

The investigation also describes a raid in late October 2025 on the Sultanz restaurant in Camborne, Cornwall. Its owner, Samsu Ali, said staff scattered as officers arrived: "everyone runs to the kitchen, front door, back door, everywhere." A worker was arrested and later released after his sister brought his ID. Officers reportedly had no warrant.

The official response

The Home Office "vigorously disputed" the charge. It says intelligence is thoroughly examined before any raid. Ms Mahmood has argued that tougher enforcement removes a key incentive for irregular migration. Opposition parties make a similar case when they say that the prospect of work acts as a pull factor for Channel crossings.

The two sides are measuring different things. A two-thirds rate of no arrests can be read as speculative targeting. It can also be read as the ordinary yield of tip-offs from the public, where many leads come to nothing. The figures alone cannot settle that. The warrant data is harder to dismiss, but it largely reflects powers Parliament has already granted.

What to watch

The government is expanding the apparatus. Reports say enforcement funding has roughly doubled and staffing is set to grow by 60%, with "tens of thousands" more raids, arrests and deportations as the aim. If those plans go ahead, the share of visits that end without an arrest, and the share backed by a warrant, will become the measure of whether the operation is targeted or speculative. Further disclosures, and any parliamentary questions on the ratio, are the next test.
