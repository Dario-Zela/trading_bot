Headteachers consider leaving early after new Ofsted regime

In one sentence
Heads say Ofsted's year-old report-card framework is pushing them toward early exits, though evidence is survey-based and national departure data is still awaited.

A year after England's school inspectors began issuing report cards in place of single-word verdicts, some of the people who run the schools say they are thinking about leaving sooner than planned. Headteachers have told the Guardian the revised Ofsted framework is pushing them toward earlier exits. That turns an argument about how to inspect into a question about who will be left to lead.

What changed

Ofsted's new framework took effect on 10 November 2025 for schools, early years settings and further education providers. It ended the one-word judgments ("outstanding", "good", "requires improvement", "inadequate") for schools. In their place came a report card using a five-point grading scale across several areas, including inclusion, and curriculum and teaching. Safeguarding is judged separately, as "met" or "not met". Each card also sets out recommended actions.

The rollout was staged. Voluntary inspections began on 10 November, and the normal schedule was not due to start before 1 December. The Department for Education has defended the model, saying report cards give parents and teachers a clearer and more transparent picture of what is working and where change is needed. A High Court challenge to the reforms brought by the head teachers' union NAHT was dismissed.

What heads are saying

There is no national count of heads leaving early because of the new regime, and the evidence is mostly survey-based and self-reported. A September 2025 poll of 1,080 leaders by the Association of School and College Leaders found 65% of heads thought the new inspections would be worse for their wellbeing than the old system. It also found 74% were not satisfied with the new inspection system. An independent survey of 100 heads found them "overwhelmingly negative", with some vowing to quit; one primary head said the process would "definitely bring forward" their retirement. A UCL-linked study found that 76% of respondents thought Ofsted had a negative effect on retention, and 30% said they were themselves considering leaving after their most recent inspection.

The warnings have sharpened over the past year. In September, a union demanded a halt to graded inspections over fears they pose a risk to life. Earlier, the Guardian reported serving heads threatening to stop working as part-time inspectors unless Ofsted delayed and revised the changes. Ofsted relies on about 900 such inspectors, mostly serving heads and senior leaders. Ofsted's response is that its first priority is to assess children's education and welfare and report back to parents.

Why it matters beyond the staffroom

The link between inspection and headteacher exits is older than this framework. Research from the London School of Economics' Centre for Economic Performance found that a negative Ofsted result significantly raises the chance a head leaves the profession. That work concerned the earlier grading system, so it shows the mechanism rather than proving the new framework is making it worse.

The stakes are practical. A vacant headship is costly to fill, and the schools most likely to receive harsh verdicts are often those already struggling to recruit. If a framework meant to be fairer instead accelerates retirements, the loss falls hardest where leadership is hardest to replace.

What to watch

The test will be hard data rather than testimony. The Department for Education's School Workforce Census records headteacher movements each year, and its next release should show whether leaving rates have moved since the framework began. Ofsted's own evaluation of the first full year of report cards, and any change to the schedule of graded inspections, will show whether the watchdog accepts that the problem is real.
