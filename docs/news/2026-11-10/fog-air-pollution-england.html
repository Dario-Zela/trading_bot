Fog traps emissions and pushes air pollution up over English cities

In one sentence
Dense fog over England has trapped traffic and heating emissions near ground level, lifting urban air-pollution readings, though the scale and whether it is unusual remain unverified.

A band of dense, slow-moving fog across England this weekend did more than snarl roads and airports. It also held the exhaust of traffic and heating close to the ground, and urban air-quality monitors are reporting higher readings as a result.

The Met Office issued a yellow warning for fog across parts of England and Wales, running from 6pm on Sunday 8 November to 11am on Monday 9 November. Reports of the warning describe cover across much of southern England, outside the south-west, and the Midlands. Visibility could fall to around 50 metres. The fog was expected to clear only slowly on Monday.

Why fog and dirty air go together

The link is physical rather than mysterious. Fog tends to form under high pressure, when winds are light and the air near the surface is cool and still. A temperature inversion, in which warmer air sits above colder air, acts as a lid. Pollutants from road traffic, domestic heating and industry cannot disperse upwards or sideways, so concentrations build up near their sources. Forecasters at Netweather have described how stable high-pressure conditions of this kind can raise pollution levels.

Road transport is the main contributor in towns and cities. Campaign group ClientEarth has said in comment on past episodes that calm weather lets traffic pollution accumulate.

What the readings do and do not show

This brief cannot yet say how large the rise is. The original Guardian report was not available for checking, and the sources found do not give consistent figures, named monitoring sites or the pollutants involved. The likeliest candidates are nitrogen dioxide and fine particulate matter, which usually rise together in stagnant air. That is an inference from the mechanism, not a measured finding.

Nor is there evidence here that the readings are exceptional. Stagnant-air episodes are a recurring winter feature in English cities, and a sharp short-term rise is normal under such conditions. A record reading, a breach of a legal limit or a formal health advisory would change the picture. None has been established.

What to watch

The test is how quickly the fog lifts. Once winds pick up or the inversion breaks, concentrations normally fall within a day or so. Daily readings from the government's UK-AIR network and the Met Office's updated forecasts will show whether this was a brief spike or a prolonged episode. The timing also lends the story some context: the Royal Society of Chemistry is marking 70 years since the Clean Air Act on 11 November, passed after the London smog of 1952.
