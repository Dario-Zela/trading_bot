TfL to launch new public-sector bus operator in London

A London bus on route 6, which becomes the first service run by TfL's new publicly owned operator in late 2027 — Oxyman / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
TfL will run one bus route (of roughly 670) publicly from late 2027 — a real change in kind, but far short of the 'major milestone' billing.

London's buses have been in private hands since 1994, when the old London Buses Limited was carved into a dozen units and sold off for £233m. That changes, at least at the margins, from late 2027. Transport for London has unveiled Buses for London, a new publicly owned operator that will run its own routes rather than simply contracting them out — the first time the capital has owned and operated its own bus service in three decades.

Mayor Sadiq Khan called it a chance to "reimagine" the network. TfL's director of buses, Lorna Murphy, went further, calling the announcement a "major milestone" and "an important first step in exploring how a publicly owned bus company could support the future of the capital's transport network." Unite, whose regional officer Nadine Edwards has campaigned for exactly this for years, welcomed it as an end to a "race to the bottom culture that impacts drivers and services."

What's actually launching

Strip away the framing and the concrete plan is this: one route. Route 6, which runs between Willesden and Victoria station and is currently operated by Metroline, switches to the new public operator when it launches in late 2027. Four more routes are promised "over the coming year," starting service from 2028. The new operator's first depot will be Park Royal in west London, leased until 2033 and initially housing around 100 zero-emission buses, developed jointly with the Old Oak and Park Royal Development Corporation.

Set against the scale of London's bus network, that is a small opening move. TfL currently contracts out roughly 670 routes to eight private operators — Arriva London, Go-Ahead London, Metroline, Stagecoach London and others — running a combined fleet of close to 8,800 buses. One route and 100 buses amounts to a little over 1% of the network. The other four routes due by 2028 will not meaningfully change that arithmetic.

Milestone or gesture

None of which makes the move meaningless — TfL already specifies routes, fares and service levels under its existing tendering model, so London's bus "privatisation" was always a lighter-touch arrangement than the deregulated free-for-all seen in the rest of England. Owning and operating a route outright, rather than paying a contractor a fixed price to run it, is a genuine change in kind, not just degree, and gives TfL a live comparison point against its private contractors on cost and reliability.

But the announcement carries none of the numbers a reader would need to judge whether it works: no projected cost of standing up the new operator, no comparison of per-route cost against the private tenders it might eventually replace, and no target for how far the public operator could expand beyond the initial five routes. TfL's own language — "exploring how a publicly owned bus company could support the future" — reads as a pilot dressed in the vocabulary of a turning point. Predictably, industry voices have already pushed back, arguing London's franchised bus model already works and that Khan is solving a problem that does not exist.

The real test arrives in late 2027, when Route 6 actually changes hands. Whether the other four promised routes materialise on schedule in 2028 — and whether TfL publishes any figures comparing the public operator's costs to its private contractors — will determine if this becomes the template for reversing three decades of contracting-out, or a single symbolic route that stays that way.
