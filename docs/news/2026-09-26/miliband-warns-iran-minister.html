Miliband warns Iran minister UK 'will not tolerate' hostile activity on British soil

In one sentence
Miliband warned Iran's foreign minister at the UN that Britain won't tolerate hostile activity on UK soil, but offered no new measures or evidence of a specific trigger.

The photograph is what everyone will remember: Ed Miliband, Britain's foreign secretary, shaking the hand of Abbas Araghchi, Iran's foreign minister, on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York. But the substance of the encounter, held on Thursday, was a pointed warning — delivered face to face, in the room, rather than through a diplomatic note.

Miliband told Araghchi that the UK "will not tolerate intimidation, threats or hostile activity on UK soil," according to the Foreign Office, and specifically raised suspected Iranian links to attacks on Britain's Jewish community, including an incident over Rosh Hashanah at Lauderdale Road Synagogue in Maida Vale, where hooded youths set off fireworks. He also urged Araghchi to pursue "de-escalation and diplomacy instead of prolonging the war" — a reference to the wider Iran-Israel-US conflict — and pressed for the release of Craig and Lindsay Foreman, the British couple sentenced to ten years in Tehran on espionage charges they deny.

"Not tolerate intimidation, threats or hostile activity on UK soil — including attacks on the British Jewish community."

A pattern, not a single incident

The warning did not come out of nowhere. MI5's director-general, Ken McCallum, told an audience last October that the service had tracked more than twenty potentially lethal Iran-backed plots on British soil in a single year — a run of assassination and kidnap attempts against dissidents, journalists and Jewish community targets that has been building since 2022. The Foreman case has simmered in the background for 20 months, a recurring irritant in a relationship that Miliband's own department has tried, since his appointment by Prime Minister Andy Burnham in July, to manage rather than sever entirely.

What's missing from Thursday's account is specificity about a trigger. The Foreign Office statement bundles together the synagogue incident, the Foreman detention and the broader pattern of plots without identifying a single new event that prompted this particular warning at this particular meeting. That ambiguity matters for reading the story correctly: this reads as an accumulation of grievances aired in a scheduled diplomatic encounter, not a response to a fresh provocation.

Immediate reaction, no policy shift

The meeting itself drew swifter and louder reaction than its content. Tom Tugendhat, the shadow foreign secretary, accused Miliband of "legitimising a regime that has often tried to harm our people," citing Iran's arming of the Houthis and its record against domestic protesters. Alicia Kearns, a shadow Foreign Office minister, went further, calling the encounter a "propaganda win for the Iranians" and saying Miliband "owes Parliament, British Jews, and the Foreman family an explanation."

Araghchi, for his part, used the meeting to press Iran's own grievance — telling Miliband that Britain, France and Germany should be "held accountable" for failing to take a firm stance against what Tehran calls the US-Israeli war on Iran. There is no indication either side left with anything concrete: no new sanctions, no announced security measures, no Iranian commitment on the Foremans. The Foreign Office also confirmed the pair discussed reopening the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint for global oil shipments, though again without any resulting agreement.

What to watch

The test of whether Thursday's language amounts to anything is whether it is followed by action — new sanctions designations, a change in the Foremans' case, or a formal MI5 briefing tying a specific plot to Tehran. Absent that, the exchange sits alongside a long run of similar UK warnings to Iran: firm in tone, thin on consequence.
