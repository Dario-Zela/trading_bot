HSBC plans return to India equity broking after 13-year absence

HSBC's building in Fort, Mumbai, part of the bank's long-standing India footprint. — DesiBoy101 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
HSBC is rebuilding cash-equities and institutional broking staff in India to capture the country's retail-investor IPO boom.

HSBC is rebuilding a business it dismantled 13 years ago. The London-headquartered bank is hiring senior staff for cash-equities and institutional broking in India and could relaunch retail broking within months, according to people familiar with the plans — a reversal of its 2013 decision to shut the unit and cut roughly 300 jobs.

In one sentence: HSBC is rebuilding cash-equities and institutional broking staff in India to capture the country's retail-investor IPO boom.

Why now

The economics that drove HSBC out of Indian broking in 2013 — thin volumes, price wars among discount brokers, a retail base that barely traded — have inverted. India added 23.77 million demat accounts in the twelve months to August 2026, taking the total past 237 million, with roughly 3.27 million opened in August alone, the strongest single month since January. Mainboard IPOs climbed from 80 to 109 in the past fiscal year, raising ₹1.77 trillion ($20bn-plus), and bankers including Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan expect the pipeline — headlined by anticipated listings such as Jio Platforms and the National Stock Exchange — to keep running through 2026.

HSBC already holds a dormant broking licence through its subsidiary HSBC InvestDirect Securities (India), which it plans to use rather than apply for a fresh one — the fastest route back into a market it once decided wasn't worth the compliance overhead.

What the bank is actually rebuilding

The hiring is split between two functions: cash equities, which handles execution and trading for clients, and institutional broking, which serves large domestic and foreign funds rather than individual investors. Retail broking, aimed at India's newly minted demat-account holders, is the piece likely to follow — HSBC is reportedly looking at a largely digital, phone-based offering rather than the branch-heavy model it ran before 2013.

That earlier operation collapsed under its own cost base: HSBC's retail brokerage and depository arm was closed in 2013 with roughly 300 job losses, part of a wider retreat by foreign banks — Citibank, Standard Chartered and Deutsche Bank among them — into narrow institutional-only coverage. HSBC is now the largest foreign bank in India by balance sheet and has been expanding its affluent and wealth-management footprint there, including approval this year to add 20 branches, taking its network to 46.

Not the only one back at the table

HSBC isn't alone in reconsidering an exit it once thought was permanent. Barclays shut its Indian equities business around 2016 after a late, costly entry into a fee-compressed market, and has separately been preparing to restart equity capital markets coverage there roughly a decade on. The pattern points less to any single bank's strategy shift than to a market that foreign lenders spent the 2010s writing off as unprofitable — and that retail money has since made too large to ignore.

What to watch

The test now is execution rather than intent: whether HSBC's retail broking product actually launches on the timeline sources describe — "within the next few months" — and whether it can compete on price against domestic discount brokers that built their entire model around the retail volumes HSBC is chasing. India's IPO calendar for the remainder of 2026, and whether the current wave of new demat accounts converts into sustained trading activity rather than one-off subscriptions, will shape how much of that retail opportunity is still on the table by the time HSBC is ready to serve it.
