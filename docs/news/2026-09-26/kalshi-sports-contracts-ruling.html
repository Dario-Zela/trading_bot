Appeals court rules states can regulate Kalshi's sports prediction contracts

The Potter Stewart United States Courthouse in Cincinnati, seat of the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, which ruled against Kalshi on September 25. — Warren LeMay / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
The 6th Circuit found Kalshi's sports-event contracts are not swaps and so fall under state gambling law, deepening a circuit split that could reach the Supreme Court.

A federal appeals court in Cincinnati has handed prediction-market operator Kalshi its second defeat in six weeks, ruling that Ohio and Tennessee are free to regulate the company's sports-event contracts as gambling rather than as federally sanctioned financial derivatives. The decision, issued Friday by a three-judge panel of the 6th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, widens a split among federal courts that now looks all but certain to land at the Supreme Court.

What the court decided

Kalshi lets customers buy "yes" or "no" contracts on the outcome of an event — an election, an economic data release, or, increasingly, a football game — and settles them for a fixed payout. The company has built its legal defense on a single claim: these are swaps, a category of derivative that Congress placed under the exclusive jurisdiction of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission when it passed the Commodity Exchange Act. If that claim holds, no state gambling regulator can touch them.

The 6th Circuit didn't buy it. Writing for the unanimous panel, Circuit Judge Julia Smith Gibbons found that "Kalshi has not shown that its sports-event contracts satisfy the statutory definition of a 'swap' so as to fall within the scope of the CFTC's exclusive jurisdiction." The court went further, holding that even if the contracts did qualify, the Commodity Exchange Act neither expressly nor implicitly preempts Ohio's or Tennessee's gambling statutes. That second finding matters: it means Kalshi's usual fallback argument — federal law occupies the field regardless of how the contracts are labeled — doesn't survive either.

The ruling consolidated separate suits out of Ohio and Tennessee. In Ohio, a district judge had already sided with the state in March; Attorney General Dave Yost posted afterward that Kalshi's preemption theory got a flat "Nope." Tennessee's path was messier — a district judge initially granted Kalshi a preliminary injunction, agreeing the contracts looked like swaps, before that decision unwound on appeal. Tennessee Attorney General Jonathan Skrmetti framed the outcome bluntly: "Kalshi attempted an end run around Tennessee law to avoid any of the rules or taxes associated with sports gambling. They failed."

A split that keeps widening

The 6th Circuit is now the second appeals court in two months to side with the states. The 9th Circuit ruled on August 28 that Nevada could regulate Kalshi's contracts on the same theory — sports bets, not swaps. That put it squarely at odds with the 3rd Circuit, which ruled in April that the CFTC holds exclusive authority over the products and blocked New Jersey from enforcing its gambling law against Kalshi while the case proceeds.

New Jersey didn't wait for the split to resolve itself. On September 2, the state's attorney general, Jennifer Davenport, and the interim director of its Division of Gaming Enforcement, Mary Jo Flaherty, filed a petition asking the Supreme Court to take up the 3rd Circuit case, calling the conflict among circuits "direct, acknowledged, and irreconcilable." The 6th Circuit's ruling adds a third data point in the states' favor and gives the justices one more reason to grant certiorari rather than let lower courts sort it out contract by contract, state by state.

The stakes extend well beyond sports wagering. Sports contracts account for more than 90% of activity on Kalshi's platform and roughly 89% of its 2025 revenue, according to the company's own disclosures — meaning the legal theory under dispute underpins nearly the entire business, not a side product. Kalshi spokesperson Dani Lever said the company disagrees with the 6th Circuit's decision and does not expect it "to survive further legal review," adding that the ruling "shows exactly why a state-by-state patchwork doesn't work." That patchwork is precisely the problem: with three circuits now offering three different answers, an operator registered with the CFTC in Washington can be a licensed derivatives venue in New Jersey and an unlicensed bookmaker in Ohio, Tennessee and Nevada, simultaneously and legally, until someone above the circuit courts says otherwise.

Why traders should care

For a platform valued in recent funding rounds near $10 billion and increasingly used by quantitative traders running arbitrage and hedging strategies across event contracts, a state-by-state legal patchwork is close to unworkable. Compliance costs rise if Kalshi has to seek gaming licenses, pay state betting taxes and submit to state-level oversight in some jurisdictions while operating a federally exempt product in others. Liquidity could fragment along state lines if some states force local users off sports contracts altogether. And the CFTC's own jurisdictional reach — a live question since the agency's 2024 defeat in its attempt to block Kalshi's election markets — is now bound up with a second, separate fight over what counts as a swap in the first place.

More than 20 states have active litigation over prediction-market regulation, and 44 attorneys general wrote to the CFTC in July arguing the agency lacks authority over sports-related event contracts at all. That volume of state activity, paired with a now three-way circuit split, is the classic setup for Supreme Court review. The justices have not yet said whether they will hear New Jersey's petition; a decision on cert is the next concrete marker to watch, likely before the court's term winds down. Until then, Kalshi's sports markets remain legal in some states, contested in others, and blocked outright in Ohio, Tennessee and Nevada — a live experiment in what happens when federal preemption theory collides with fifty separate gambling codes.
