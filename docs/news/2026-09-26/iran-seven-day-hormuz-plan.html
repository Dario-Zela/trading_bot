Iran offers seven-day plan to reopen Strait of Hormuz and restart nuclear talks

Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi, who detailed the seven-day proposal to US contacts at the UN General Assembly in New York. — Foad Ashtari / Mehr News Agency (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Iran's foreign minister proposed a seven-day plan to reopen Hormuz and resume nuclear talks, but Washington still wants both settled as one package, not in sequence.

Iran's foreign minister has put a new number on the table: seven days. That is how long Tehran says it would take to reopen the Strait of Hormuz to normal shipping and sit back down with Washington over its nuclear programme — provided the United States first stops fighting, lifts its naval blockade, waives sanctions on Iranian oil sales and unfreezes Iranian assets abroad.

Abbas Araghchi laid out the plan at a private gathering on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York on Thursday, according to people in the room, including Trita Parsi of the Quincy Institute think tank. It is the most concrete diplomatic move either side has made since a previous ceasefire fell apart in July, and it arrives as American voters head toward pumps averaging $4.46 a gallon — the highest of the year — seven weeks before the midterm elections.

What Iran is offering

The mechanics, as described by Araghchi, are sequential rather than simultaneous. Fighting would stop across all fronts, including Lebanon, within the first day or two. Over the following four to five days, the US would be expected to lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports, waive sanctions restricting Iranian oil exports and guarantee no further strikes. The Strait of Hormuz — the 33-kilometre channel through which roughly a fifth of the world's daily oil supply and a similar share of global LNG normally passes — would reopen on day six. On day seven, Araghchi said, "the talks with the US will be started for the final deal on mutually agreed subjects."

That ordering is the crux of the dispute. Iran wants the strait settled first and the nuclear question addressed afterward, as a separate track. The Trump administration has insisted the opposite: that Hormuz, sanctions relief and the nuclear file all be negotiated as a single package, with no reopening of the strait until Tehran has made verifiable commitments on enrichment. Secretary of State Marco Rubio's response on Wednesday was carefully noncommittal — "if there's an opportunity to exchange ideas and messages, we will" — which is diplomatic language for not yet.

A second attempt at Islamabad

The proposal is not being read in isolation. It closely mirrors the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding, signed by both presidents on 17 June after an earlier round of fighting, which set a 60-day window to negotiate a final settlement and included its own — vaguer — provisions on the strait. That agreement collapsed within three weeks. On 7 July, Iranian forces struck three commercial vessels transiting Hormuz, including a Qatari LNG tanker that caught fire, after accusing them of ignoring Iran's approved shipping routes. Washington answered with airstrikes on Iranian territory within a day, and the ceasefire was effectively dead.

The US then imposed its own naval blockade and began charging shipping companies for safe-passage guarantees, on top of the disruption Iran's attacks had already caused. Traffic through the strait, which normally runs at 88 to 130 vessel transits a day, fell to roughly ten on a single day counted in late July. The International Energy Agency has called the resulting disruption the largest in the history of the global oil market. Seven months into the war, the compressed seven-day version of the Islamabad script looks less like a fresh idea than an attempt to salvage the old one before it is forgotten entirely — this time with a tighter clock and, Iranian officials hope, more urgent politics on the American side.

The politics behind the clock

That urgency is deliberate. Parsi, who relayed Araghchi's private remarks, said the foreign minister suggested a fast deal would hand President Trump a win before the midterms: lower pump prices, an end to a war that has dragged on far longer than promised, and a nuclear track he could still claim credit for shaping. "If Trump calculates this arrangement is worthwhile and can bring political benefit before the midterms and reduce gas prices, perhaps we'll see flexibility not seen in previous weeks," Parsi said — while cautioning that deep mutual suspicion remains, including US concern that Iran wants the breathing room chiefly to sell more oil and ease its own economic strain.

Trump has publicly rejected the idea that the electoral calendar drives his Iran policy, telling reporters this month that gas prices would fall only "right after" the midterms and that the war would end "immediately after the election." On the underlying trade-off, he has been blunt: elevated prices are "a very inexpensive price to pay" for preventing Iran from acquiring a nuclear weapon, he said in September — a line unlikely to reassure voters who have watched pump prices climb toward $6 a gallon in California. Whether that political calculus shifts is precisely what Araghchi appears to be betting on.

What would actually move markets

For oil markets, the distinction between a real sequencing breakthrough and another failed memorandum matters more than the seven-day framing itself. Brent crude jumped more than 9% in a single session in July when the US blockade was announced; a credible reopening of Hormuz, verified by tanker movements rather than communiqués, is the kind of event that could unwind a meaningful share of the war-risk premium now priced into crude and refined products. A repeat of June — vague language, quick collapse, renewed strikes — would do the opposite, reinforcing the view among traders and insurers that Gulf shipping risk is now structural rather than episodic.

Washington has not formally responded to the seven-day plan, and Rubio's guarded comment suggests no immediate answer is coming. The next marker to watch is whether the two sides establish direct contact at working level in New York before the General Assembly session closes, and whether Trump — with midterms now under two months away — signals any willingness to decouple Hormuz from the nuclear file, the sequencing gap that sank the last deal and is blocking this one.
