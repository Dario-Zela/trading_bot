Glasgow City Council pauses plan to fire and rehire 23,000 staff

Glasgow City Chambers, seat of the council that proposed dismissing and rehiring nearly its entire workforce. — Michael D. Beckwith / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Glasgow City Council paused plans to fire and rehire 23,000-plus staff after union pressure and Scottish government intervention forced fresh pay talks.

For a few days this week, roughly everyone who empties Glasgow's bins, staffs its schools, runs its libraries and answers its phones was staring down the same letter: you are being sacked, and then — if all goes to plan — immediately rehired on different terms. Glasgow City Council paused that plan on Friday evening, after three trade unions and Scotland's deputy first minister spent the day trying to talk the authority back from a move that would have touched practically its entire workforce.

The fire-and-rehire threat was never really about Glasgow wanting to shed staff. It was the endgame of an equal pay saga that has already cost the council roughly £770 million in settlements to women workers — mostly in care, cleaning and catering roles — who spent years paid less than men doing comparably rated jobs. The council's fix is a new pay structure meant to close that gap for good. On its own numbers, 63% of staff would gain financially under the new system; about 11% would see pay fall. It was the mechanics of getting there — sacking 23,000-plus non-teaching staff and rehiring them on the new contracts from 1 January — that turned a technical pay exercise into a citywide standoff.

Talks between the council and the GMB, Unison and Unite unions broke down on Wednesday. GMB and Unite said they had secured an extra £110 million to sweeten the new salary structure and wanted to put it to a member ballot. Unison disagreed sharply, arguing the money was earmarked for "assimilation and arrears" rather than protecting the minority facing cuts, and refused to ballot on it. Its education convener, Sylvia Haughey, accused the council of "bullying tactics" on air, while insisting her union "had not walked away from anything." GMB organiser David Hume made the opposite case: after seven years of negotiation, he said, members deserved the chance to vote on what was on the table.

A pause, not a peace

What moved the council was less the unions' rhetoric than the optics of the letters themselves — thousands of low-paid workers, many of them women who had already waited years for redress on equal pay, opening dismissal notices days before Christmas planning would normally start. Deputy First Minister Jenny Gilruth chaired the intervention, and the council's workforce convener, Councillor Allan Casey, confirmed a pause had been agreed, putting a previously shelved Acas conciliation package back on the table for "further intensive negotiations" starting next week. The council's communications director, Colin Edgar, called the whole affair a "hellish situation" and promised no worker would see a break in service.

A joint statement from the STUC, on behalf of all three unions, welcomed "the lifting of the threat of fire and rehire" — carefully worded, since nothing has actually been resolved. The pay dispute, the £110 million question of who it covers, and the fate of the 11% facing cuts are all exactly where they were on Wednesday. What has changed is the leverage: an SNP-led council that calculated it could not survive the headlines of mass dismissal notices going out to its own workforce. Other cash-strapped town halls eyeing similar contract resets will be watching how Glasgow's rescheduled talks land — the tactic worked once as a threat, but using it for real may prove a different matter entirely.
