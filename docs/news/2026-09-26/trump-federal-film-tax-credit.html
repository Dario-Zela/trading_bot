Trump calls for federal film and TV production tax credit

The Hollywood sign above Los Angeles, epicenter of a production sector that has shed more than 40,000 jobs since 2022. — Adrian104 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump's push for a federal film and TV tax credit, backed by a study projecting 143,000 new jobs, threatens to reshape competition among states already expanding their own incentive programs.

President Trump's call for a federal tax credit on film and television production has done something Washington rarely manages on entertainment policy: it has both parties agreeing, and a industry-commissioned study to point to.

A study released September 15 and commissioned by the Motion Picture Association projects that a federal production incentive could add more than 143,000 jobs and lift U.S. production spending by $125 billion by 2035 — pushing total annual spending to $277.5 billion from a projected $152.2 billion without one. Charles Rivkin, the MPA's chairman and chief executive, called the prospect "a gamechanger for our industry," one that would touch "people in all 50 states."

The timing matters. Trump posted his support on Truth Social in early September, urging "Republicans and Democrats" to "immediately craft Legislation to save the Movie, Television, and Entertainment Business in America" — a pivot from his earlier threat of tariffs on foreign-made films toward a subsidy instead. Los Angeles County has shed more than 40,000 entertainment-sector jobs since 2022, as studios chase cheaper labor and richer incentives in the UK, Canada, Australia and elsewhere. Sixty-five countries now offer some form of film tax credit, according to the MPA study.

The bill taking shape

Legislation is already drafted. The Motion Picture, Television and Entertainment Revitalization Act, introduced with bipartisan sponsors — Reps. Nathaniel Moran (R-TX), Linda Sánchez (D-CA), Brian Jack (R-GA) and Laura Friedman (D-CA) in the House, Sens. Tim Scott (R-SC) and Adam Schiff (D-CA) in the Senate — sets a base 20% transferable credit on U.S.-resident labor costs, including post-production and visual effects. Add-ons could push the effective rate to 30%: a 5% bonus for independent productions, another 5% for filming substantially in rural opportunity zones or federally declared disaster areas, and a further uplift for productions that spread work across ten or more states. Eligible productions must cost more than $1 million and film at least 75% of principal photography days domestically; live sports, talk shows, news and corporate video are excluded. The MPA's modelling assumes an effective date of January 1, 2027.

Rep. Friedman put the political framing bluntly: the bill "tells every country that has been outbidding us for American work that we are done losing."

States, already exposed, brace to compete twice

The harder question for markets is what a federal credit does to the 35-plus states that already run their own incentive programs, several of which have just expanded them. California's new Tax Credit Program 4.0 raised its base credit to a refundable 35–45% of qualified spend, with a $750 million annual cap. New York lifted its program to $800 million, with a 30% base rate plus a 10% upstate bonus. Georgia runs an uncapped 20–30% transferable credit. North Carolina recently tripled its per-project caps. None of that legislative activity looks like a retreat in the face of federal competition — it looks like states hedging in case the federal credit stacks on top of, rather than replaces, their own.

California Gov. Gavin Newsom, no natural ally of the administration, responded to Trump's post with "Amen! Let's make it happen," and had floated his own $7.5 billion federal-credit concept months earlier, modelled on California's state program. The convergence is notable: a Democratic governor whose state has borne the brunt of production flight and a Republican president who spent much of the year threatening tariffs on foreign-made films now backing the same subsidy-based fix.

What it means for capital allocation

For studios and streamers, a federal credit would not replace the location math so much as add a new variable to it — a national floor incentive stacked under whatever state or country program a project also qualifies for. That raises the return threshold state incentive offices need to clear to keep marginal productions from moving anyway, and it gives production accountants a federal baseline to build models around before committing budgets. It also shifts some of the fiscal cost of competing for production activity from state treasuries to the federal one, at a moment when Washington is already running large deficits.

The bill now heads toward the House Ways and Means Committee, where the details — rate levels, caps, the disaster-zone and multi-state uplifts — will be negotiated line by line. The MPA study's 2027 effective-date assumption is aggressive for a tax bill that hasn't yet had a committee markup; the next marker to watch is whether Ways and Means schedules one before year-end.
