Gilt yields hold near multi-year highs as BoE flags November hike, unwinds bond stockpile

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Gilt yields eased slightly to 5.35% but remain near 19-year highs as BoE eases QT supply pressure even as deputy governors flag a likely November hike.

UK gilt yields eased a touch this week but are still camped near territory not seen since before the financial crisis. The 10-year yield stood at 5.35% on Friday, down slightly from a peak closer to 5.4% earlier in the week, but well above the 5.295% it hit on 10 September — itself the highest level since 2007. The reason yields won't come down in any lasting way has less to do with bond supply than with what the Bank of England is expected to do with interest rates in November.

In one sentence: Gilt yields eased slightly to 5.35% but remain near 19-year highs as a Bank of England policy shift on QT and hawkish rate signals pull in opposite directions.

A softer touch on the bond stockpile

At its meeting on 16 September, the Monetary Policy Committee voted 6–3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% — with chief economist Huw Pill and external members Catherine Mann and Megan Greene pushing for an immediate rise to 4%. Alongside that, the Bank announced a significant overhaul of quantitative tightening: the pace of balance-sheet reduction drops from roughly £70bn a year to £46bn, of which only £20bn will come from active gilt sales, the rest from bonds simply maturing. More striking, the Bank paused all gilt sales outright until April 2027 while it consults the Treasury and the Debt Management Office on selling stock directly to the DMO rather than through market auctions. It also stopped selling the longest-dated gilts entirely, retaining roughly £120bn of paper maturing in 2049 and beyond — partly to back future banknote issuance, partly to stop leaning on a part of the curve that was already under strain.

The market read this as an Operation Twist of sorts — less supply pressure at the long end, in exchange for a longer overall path to a fully unwound £488bn Asset Purchase Facility by 2034. Thirty-year gilt yields fell around 11-12 basis points on the news; the 10-year eased by 7-8bps. For a few days, quantitative tightening was doing the market's work for it, not against it.

The rates story pulls the other way

That relief didn't last. On 24 September, two deputy governors reasserted the hawkish case. Sarah Breeden, who oversees financial stability, said it was "increasingly appropriate" for Bank Rate to respond as inflation risks crystallise, warning that "the larger and longer the shock, the more likely it is that we'll see material second-round effects." Clare Lombardelli, the deputy governor for monetary policy, went further, saying firms' capacity to absorb higher energy bills is limited and that policy is "increasingly likely to need to tighten if elevated energy prices persist, absent clear evidence of disinflation."

The backdrop is the war in Iran, now in its seventh month, which has kept Brent crude near $107 a barrel and pushed August's headline UK inflation to 3.1%. Ofgem's price cap for October to December rises to £1,723, and the Bank expects it to climb again in the first quarter of 2027. Since the conflict began in late February, gilt yields have risen by more than 80 basis points — more than any other G7 government bond market bar Italy's, and roughly double the increase seen in US Treasuries or German Bunds over the same stretch.

Markets have responded by pricing a November hike at close to 70-80% probability, with a further move by February seen as similarly likely — a sharp turn for a Bank that had been cutting rates earlier in the cycle and now finds itself the outlier as the Federal Reserve continues to ease.

What it means for positioning

The net effect is a rare split decision: the Bank easing pressure on gilt supply with one hand while its own deputies talk up the odds of tighter policy with the other. For sterling, the higher-yield story has not translated into support — GBP/USD has struggled to hold gains even as gilt yields climbed, with investors treating the rise as compensation for risk rather than a reason to buy the currency. That combination — elevated and volatile gilt yields, a central bank still hawkish on rates despite easing off on active sales, and a currency that isn't rewarded for any of it — is the shape of UK duration risk heading into the Bank's next decision on 5 November, when the market will find out whether Breeden and Lombardelli's rhetoric becomes a vote.
