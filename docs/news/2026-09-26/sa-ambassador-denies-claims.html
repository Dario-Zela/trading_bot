New South African ambassador to US denies 'white genocide' claims on arrival

Roelf Meyer, South Africa's ambassador to the United States and a former apartheid-era negotiator, pictured in 2017. — Fredrick Omondi / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
South Africa's Washington envoy Roelf Meyer told the BBC that Trump-era 'white genocide' claims about Afrikaners 'do not exist,' betting candor over silence.

Roelf Meyer has spent three decades being the Afrikaner who negotiated apartheid's end rather than mourned its passing. This week he used that biography as a rhetorical weapon, telling the BBC in a fresh interview that the idea of a genocide against white South Africans — a claim that has shaped a year of US-South Africa relations — simply "does not exist."

Meyer, 78, took up his post as South Africa's ambassador to Washington in April, presenting his credentials to President Trump at the White House in May after diplomatic ties between the two countries had collapsed. His predecessor, Ebrahim Rasool, was expelled from Washington last year. Trump has repeatedly asserted, including in an Oval Office ambush of President Cyril Ramaphosa in May, that white Afrikaner farmers face a coordinated campaign of violence. Meyer's response to hearing those claims, he told the BBC, was disbelief: "Is he talking about us?"

"I'm an Afrikaner from South Africa myself, and I can say that I've never experienced something of that kind."

He went further, arguing the narrative has been "discredited by every political party in the country, including by those representing Afrikaners" — a pointed line from a man who spent the early 1990s as the National Party's chief negotiator, working across the table from Ramaphosa to dismantle white-minority rule.

A dispute over numbers, not just narrative

The factual record backs Meyer's framing more than Trump's. South African police data shows Black South Africans make up the overwhelming majority of crime victims nationally, including in farming areas. Of the roughly 19,700 murders recorded in South Africa between April and December 2024, only 36 were linked to farms or agricultural holdings, and just seven of the victims were farmers themselves. Analysts and rights groups have said the pattern in farm attacks — robbery-linked, opportunistic, spread across the whole population — does not meet legal or scholarly thresholds for persecution, let alone genocide.

That has not stopped the Trump administration building policy on the claim. The White House has cast its stance as offering "a lifeline for Afrikaners," running a dedicated refugee pathway that has brought in nearly 13,000 white South Africans since launch, with plans to admit up to 17,500 more over the next year — even as the broader US refugee cap has been slashed. Campaigners sympathetic to the cause have planted thousands of white crosses on the National Mall in Washington to commemorate farm-murder victims, a visual echo of a 2020 protest video Trump once presented, misleadingly, as evidence of mass graves.

Criticism of the narrative is not confined to Pretoria. Pastor Rudi Swanepoel, an Afrikaner clergyman, has dismissed the persecution framing as a reaction to "loss of privilege" rather than genuine danger, and has questioned publicly why Afrikaners alone were carved out for expedited refugee status.

What's next

Meyer's task now is to see whether plain rebuttal changes anything in a relationship built around the claim rather than despite it — the refugee program, the aid freeze under Executive Order 14204, and the tariff truce all sit downstream of it. The next marker will be whether Washington's refugee intake pace slows as the 17,500-person ceiling is approached, or whether the political utility of the narrative keeps it moving regardless of what the ambassador — or the crime statistics — say.
