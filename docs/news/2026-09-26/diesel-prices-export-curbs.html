UK diesel prices near record as White House weighs US export curbs

A diesel pump nozzle at a filling station — UK pump prices are within a couple of pence of the 2022 record. — Rama / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0 FR)
In one sentence
US diesel export curbs under White House review — and a possible fresh UK pump-price record — point to the same tightening diesel market on both sides of the Atlantic.

UK diesel is a couple of pence from an all-time high, and the White House is publicly arguing with itself about whether to make the underlying shortage worse or better. Both facts are the same story: a fuel that everyone assumed had gone back to being boring after 2022 is once again a point of political exposure on both sides of the Atlantic.

The average UK price of diesel stood at 198.32p a litre this week, according to the RAC — within striking distance of the 199.09p record set on 25 June 2022, and already the most expensive diesel in Europe, 12p ahead of Finland and the Netherlands. RAC head of policy Simon Williams said the record would "almost certainly" fall within days as retailers pass through the cost of new supply. A 55-litre fill-up for a family car now runs to £109; hauliers, who run through hundreds of litres a week, put the added cost at roughly £250 a week per lorry.

Washington's blunt instrument, sheathed

The proximate cause is not a UK story at all. US diesel has been trading above $6.50 a gallon, a record driven by supply constraints tied to the wars in Ukraine and against Iran, and the political cost is landing on the Trump administration ahead of the midterms. Politico reported on 23 September that the White House was preparing a 90-day ban on US diesel exports to keep more fuel at home. Diesel futures fell as much as 6% within hours of the report — then rallied back much of that move when a White House official denied a formal ban was imminent.

Energy Secretary Chris Wright supplied the more interesting detail: rather than ruling out intervention altogether, he is publicly breaking with the export-ban framing while pushing for the same outcome by other means. "Definitely doesn't work" was his verdict on a blunt ban, which he warned would cut refiners' incentive to run at full throughput and could push up gasoline and jet-fuel prices in the process of trying to fix diesel. Instead he is asking refiners to voluntarily cut export volumes — a request that at least one person involved in the talks told reporters raises its own antitrust questions, since coordinated output cuts among competitors sit close to a line regulators normally patrol. Wright has also alluded to a second, unspecified measure under consideration, saying only "there's another one, too, but I can't talk about it yet."

The trade that matters

For traders, the signal is less about which specific mechanism Washington lands on and more about the fact that a middle path is now openly on the table. A flat export ban is easy to price — futures already demonstrated that with a 6% air-pocket in a single session. Voluntary curbs, informal jawboning and an undisclosed "other" measure are harder to price precisely because they're designed to be reversible, deniable and adjustable in response to the pump-price headlines of any given week. That is a wider distribution of outcomes for diesel-crack spreads and refiner margins than the market had been assuming when this was framed as a binary ban-or-no-ban question.

The read-through for UK forecourts is indirect but real. Global diesel is priced off the same seaborne and refining balances that US exports feed into; any reduction in US cargoes reaching international markets — voluntary or not — tightens supply that European buyers compete for, adding to the pressure already pushing UK pumps toward a fresh record. A White House trying to solve a domestic pump-price problem by keeping diesel home is, in effect, exporting some of that problem to everyone else who buys diesel on the water.

What to watch next: whether Wright's voluntary-cuts approach produces measurable export declines in US Energy Information Administration weekly data, and whether the RAC's 199.09p mark actually falls this weekend. Either would confirm whether policy or plain scarcity is the bigger driver of a fuel that hasn't been this politically charged since 2022.
