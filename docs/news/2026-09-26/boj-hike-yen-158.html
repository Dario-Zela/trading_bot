Bank of Japan's rate hike fails to lift the yen as it nears 158/dollar

The Bank of Japan's headquarters in Nihonbashi, Tokyo. — Fg2 / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The BoJ's fastest tightening since 1990 hasn't stopped the yen sliding toward 158/dollar, as the wide US-Japan rate gap keeps carry trades alive and intervention risk rising.

The Bank of Japan is now raising interest rates faster than at any point since 1990. The yen doesn't seem to have noticed. Eight days after the central bank's latest hike, the currency was trading near 158 to the dollar — weaker, not stronger, than before the decision, and within striking distance of the 160 line that forced Tokyo and Washington into a rare joint currency intervention just two months ago.

The disconnect is the story. A central bank tightening at its fastest clip in over three decades is failing to arrest a currency slide, and the reason has less to do with Japan than with the size of the gap between Japanese and American borrowing costs — a gap the BoJ cannot close on its own.

A hike, and then a shrug

On September 18th, the BoJ's board voted 7-2 to raise its policy rate by 25 basis points to 1.25%, the highest level since 1995. Board members Toichiro Asada and Ayano Sato dissented, preferring to hold. It was the sixth increase of Governor Kazuo Ueda's tenure — a record pace for any BoJ governor in at least half a century — and it came just three months after the previous move, the shortest gap between hikes since 1990.

The justification was straightforward: underlying inflation is sitting close to the BoJ's 2% target, wage growth is broadening, and officials feared it would overshoot. "Underlying inflation is now quite close to 2%, and our goal has become to stabilize it," Ueda told reporters afterward. Pressed on whether more hikes were coming, he added: "We shouldn't rule anything out. If we were to do big rate hikes or consecutive rate hikes, that would depend on whether Japan sees very big inflation risks."

The yen's initial reaction was to weaken. Traders had priced in a chance the BoJ would pair the hike with sharper hawkish signaling on the pace of future moves; instead they got a quarter-point rise and a governor unwilling to commit to a trajectory. Within days the currency had drifted back toward three-week lows.

The arithmetic the BoJ can't fix alone

The deeper problem is one of relative motion. Two days before the BoJ's meeting, the Federal Reserve delivered its own first rate rise in more than three years, lifting the federal funds target to 3.75%-4.00% under new chair Kevin Warsh. "The plain fact is that inflation is too high and has been for too long," Warsh said, and the Fed's dot plot showed most officials expecting at least one more hike by year-end.

Even after Japan's latest move, the gap between the two policy rates sits well above two and a half percentage points — still enough to reward borrowing cheaply in yen and parking the proceeds in dollar assets. That carry trade has been a persistent drag on the currency since 2022, and it doesn't unwind just because the BoJ narrows the gap by a quarter point at a time while the Fed is narrowing it from the other direction. Ueda has also been explicit that currency stabilization isn't the BoJ's job: "We don't guide policy to control currency moves or stabilise currency rates at a range," he said — a statement of orthodox central-bank doctrine that leaves the yen's level to whoever is willing to intervene directly.

Intervention risk, revisited

That's exactly what happened once already this year. On July 31st, Japan's Ministry of Finance and the US Treasury carried out their first coordinated yen-buying operation since 1998, with Tokyo spending roughly ¥8.45 trillion (some $53-59 billion) and Washington adding an estimated $26 billion of its own. The yen jumped from around 164 to 155.2 per dollar in the immediate aftermath — its strongest level since May — before the effect faded within two weeks as underlying rate pressures reasserted themselves.

The currency's renewed approach to 160 has revived that conversation at the highest levels. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama disclosed on September 25th that President Trump had personally raised concerns about yen weakness with Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi during a sidelines meeting at the UN General Assembly three days earlier. Takaichi, according to Katayama, told Trump directly that an undervalued yen is "problematic." The exchange reaffirmed the two governments' commitment, first made alongside July's intervention, to counter "disorderly" currency moves — diplomatic language that traders read as a warning shot the closer the pair gets to 160.

Takaichi's complication

The political backdrop makes the BoJ's task harder still. Takaichi took office promising an aggressive fiscal expansion — a two-year suspension of the consumption tax on food, worth roughly ¥5 trillion a year, and a public-and-private investment push exceeding ¥370 trillion through fiscal 2040 aimed at AI, semiconductors and energy. That spending has to be financed with additional government bond issuance, which itself pressures the currency, even as Takaichi has a documented preference for the weaker yen that helps her export-oriented growth agenda. Economists have flagged the tension this creates for the BoJ's independence: a central bank trying to defend the currency's value while the fiscal authority is, in effect, working against it.

Ueda has publicly defended the BoJ's independence and insisted rate decisions will track the inflation data, not the exchange rate or the government's spending plans. But every basis point of Takaichi's deficit spending adds to the case that Tokyo needs Washington's cooperation, not just its own tightening, to keep the yen off the intervention trigger.

The next test comes fast. The BoJ's board meets again October 30-31, and Ueda will face the same question in sharper form if the yen has already tested 160 by then. The Fed's own next move — its dot plot points to at least one more hike this year — will matter just as much for the rate gap as anything Tokyo does. Katayama and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have both signaled readiness to intervene again; the open question is whether a second round would hold any longer than the first.
