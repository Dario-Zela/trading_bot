Russia widens attacks on Ukraine's data centres and internet providers, Zelensky says

In one sentence
Russia has widened drone strikes to Kyiv's data centres and internet providers, killing four and cutting service to 100,000 households, in what Zelensky calls a deliberate campaign against 'ordinary life.'

The strike came at the end of a week Kyiv had already spent without reliable internet. On Friday, a Russian drone hit the Inkom business centre in the capital's Solomianskyi district, killing four people and tearing into a data centre operated by Datagroup, one of Ukraine's larger telecoms firms. It was not an isolated hit. Two days earlier, on September 23 and 24, Russian Geran-5 attack drones — a jet-powered, long-range design derived from Iran's Shahed — had already struck a cluster of data centres and internet exchange points across the city, cutting service to roughly 100,000 households in Kyiv and Kyiv Oblast, with knock-on outages reaching Vinnytsia and Khmelnytskyi.

President Volodymyr Zelensky framed the pattern as deliberate. "The Russians are constantly expanding their escalation operation: American and other businesses, data centres, internet providers — for Russia, all ordinary life is simply a target," he wrote, adding that the attacks affect "people's ability to stay connected, study, work." He said he had since discussed "additional decisions" with defence officials to harden critical communications infrastructure — though he offered no specifics on what that would involve.

A new column on the target list

Ukraine's internet backbone has, until recently, sat somewhat apart from the war's main axes of destruction — the power grid, the railways, the heating plants that Russia has bombed methodically through three previous winters. That appears to be changing. Over the two days of strikes, Russian forces hit UTELS' switching facility, the CityHost and MiroHost data centres, the Pavutyna network hub, and a central traffic-exchange point used by New-Telco and United DC — the latter two named explicitly by Russia's defence ministry, which claimed they carried military intelligence and security-service traffic. Those claims could not be independently verified. Kyivstar and Parkovyi were also affected, according to Ukrainian officials.

At the 1-IX internet exchange point, engineer Anatolii Frolenkov described a shift in intent rather than just intensity: "This is the beginning of a phase where, since September, they have started taking it out systematically." Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, was blunter, telling reporters Russia had "targeted major Ukrainian data centers, seeking to disrupt the flow of information... it is not a military target."

The human cost has run alongside the infrastructural one. Separately from Friday's four deaths, a 14-year-old Israeli citizen was killed when a residential building was hit in the Pecherskyi district, and Kyiv's mayor, Vitaliy Klitschko, put the total injured from the overnight barrage at 59. In at least one case the disruption compounded the danger directly: outages knocked out air-raid alert systems for some of the 100,000 affected households, meaning the same strikes that severed connectivity also silenced the warning network for whatever came next.

Resilience, at a cost

What has kept Kyiv from a full blackout is architecture built for exactly this contingency. Oleksandr Olshanskyi, founder of MiroHost — one of the data centres hit, and by his own account 90–95% destroyed in the strike — put the physics plainly: "Destroying all internet in a simple way is impossible — you'd have to use nuclear or chemical weapons or destroy 70–80% of buildings in Kyiv." He added that the intent looked less like military necessity than psychological pressure: "The enemy's goal is to cause damage, inflame panic, and heighten nervousness."

That resilience is itself the product of years of quiet capital reallocation. Olshanskyi said roughly 95% of MiroHost's clients now run infrastructure abroad or in the cloud, insulated from anything short of a strike on a foreign data centre. Most providers restored service within hours to a day, aided by network decentralization — Datagroup, for instance, rerouted traffic across other facilities to bring Friday's outage down to about two hours. But redundancy of this kind isn't free: it means duplicated hosting costs, foreign cloud contracts paid in hard currency, and physical assets at home that insurers increasingly have to price as war-zone risk rather than ordinary commercial property. Each repaired data centre is also a repeat customer for reconstruction financing that Ukraine's private sector has to source largely on its own, since war-risk coverage for digital infrastructure remains thin even among international insurers still willing to write policies in the country.

What the shift signals

The pivot toward data centres and internet providers fits a broader pattern of Russian strikes moving up the list of civilian-facing targets as the battlefield stalemates and winter approaches — echoing the energy-grid campaigns of prior years, but aimed now at the digital plumbing of a wartime economy that runs increasingly on remote work, digital banking and cloud services. For companies operating in or servicing Ukraine, the practical read is that connectivity, not just power, now belongs on the list of infrastructure that can vanish overnight, with implications for how outsourcing contracts, insurance terms and even foreign-investment decisions get written.

The next test comes as Kyiv moves into its fourth wartime winter: whether Russia pairs strikes on data centres with the kind of coordinated grid attacks seen in 2022 and 2023, which would multiply the strain on backup systems already absorbing losses, or whether this remains a narrower campaign against telecoms in isolation. Ukrainian officials say they are examining new protective measures for communications infrastructure; what those entail, and how quickly they can be funded and built, will shape how much of this resilience is repeatable the next time the drones come.
