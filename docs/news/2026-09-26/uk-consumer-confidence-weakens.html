UK consumer confidence weakens ahead of Autumn Budget, BRC warns

A sale sign in a Warehouse storefront on Oxford Street, London — discretionary retailers are seen as most exposed to weakening household sentiment. — Edwardx / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BRC data show UK economic and personal-finance sentiment fell sharply in September, and the retail body wants the Autumn Budget to ease cost-of-living pressure.

British households turned markedly gloomier about the economy and their own finances this month, according to the British Retail Consortium's latest sentiment tracker — a shift that has handed the Treasury an unwelcome data point three weeks out from the Autumn Budget.

The BRC's Consumer Sentiment Monitor, compiled with Opinium from a poll of 2,000 UK adults conducted between 8 and 11 September, found expectations for the state of the economy over the next three months fell to -34, down from -28 in August. That snapped four consecutive months of improvement. Sentiment on personal finances weakened almost as sharply, dropping to -15 from -9. Expectations for retail spending eased to +5 from +8, while expected household saving slipped to -7 from -5. Overall spending intentions held flat at +15.

A four-month run broken

The scale of the reversal is what stands out. Confidence had been climbing steadily since the spring, and the September survey marks the first monthly decline since that run began. The BRC said the drop was sharper among women respondents, and linked the shift to two converging pressures: household bills rising faster than shop prices, and consumers bracing for a jump in energy costs this winter. That combination — squeezed budgets now, a known cost shock arriving in a few months — is doing more to unsettle sentiment than any single data release could on its own.

"Consumer confidence in the economy stumbled after four months of improvement, with a similar trend for sentiment around personal finances," said Helen Dickinson, the BRC's chief executive. "With the Budget looming large, consumers want to see Government prioritise bringing down the cost of living. Retail will play its part, but the Chancellor must not compound the triple blow from escalating employment costs, energy bills, and business rates."

Budget options narrow

The BRC's ask is specific: a freeze on the business rates multiplier, so retailers are not forced into a further round of price rises to cover higher property taxes, alongside relief on employment costs and energy bills. The framing matters for the Treasury's room to manoeuvre. A government already navigating a tight fiscal position now faces a retail lobby and a household survey both pointing the same direction — toward measures that visibly protect disposable income rather than ones aimed primarily at investment or long-term growth.

That narrows the Chancellor's options less through arithmetic than through politics. A Budget seen to ignore deteriorating household sentiment carries its own risk, regardless of what the fiscal headroom permits.

What it means for positioning

For traders, the read-through is toward consumer discretionary weakness rather than a broader recession call — spending intentions overall were flat, and the retail-spending expectation, while down, stayed positive at +5. But the divergence between resilient headline spending and falling confidence in the details (personal finance, saving, the economic outlook) is the kind of gap that typically shows up first in discretionary categories — clothing, homewares, big-ticket items — before it reaches food and essentials. UK defensive and staples-heavy retail names look better insulated than general merchandisers exposed to discretionary swings.

The next checkpoint is the Budget itself, expected within weeks, where the size and shape of any business-rates or cost-of-living measures will show whether the government has taken the BRC's warning as a genuine constraint or a lobbying position to be weighed against other priorities. The BRC's October sentiment reading, likely due in the second half of that month, will be the first indication of whether the Budget moved the needle — or whether the slide continues into the crucial pre-Christmas trading period.
