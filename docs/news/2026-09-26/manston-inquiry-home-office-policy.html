Manston asylum inquiry hears overcrowding and racism linked to Home Office policy, as ex-ministers face questions

In one sentence
An inquiry heard that Home Office decisions, not an unforeseen crisis, drove dangerous overcrowding and racist abuse at the Manston asylum centre in 2022.

Four years on from the boat crossings that overwhelmed it, the Manston asylum processing centre in Kent is back in the news — this time in a hearing room, not a headline about queues on the beach. The independent inquiry into what happened at the site in autumn 2022 held its second preliminary hearing on Friday, and the picture emerging is not one of a system buckling under unforeseeable pressure. It is one of choices made, and remade, at the top of government.

Manston was built to hold up to 1,600 new arrivals for a maximum of 24 hours — a triage point, not a shelter. By November 2022 it was holding roughly 4,000 people, some for as long as 33 days. Counsel to the inquiry, Claire Dobbin KC, has told hearings that sixty families were crammed into eleven rooms in a barracks block with no hot water or showers, and that at points 81 people — babies, children, teenagers, adults — shared a single toilet. There was no soap. Diphtheria spread among detainees. The inquiry has also heard that "very racist language was used towards detainees by some staff," alongside allegations of people being locked in isolation vans as punishment and detainees attempting self-harm with barbed wire.

One death sits at the centre of the case: Hussein Haseeb Ahmed, a 31-year-old Iraqi man, fell ill with diphtheria and was, according to evidence heard so far, kept in handcuffs in hospital until roughly two and a half hours before he died.

Not an accident, a decision

What distinguishes this inquiry from a routine post-mortem of an administrative failure is where the evidence points. Witnesses have described Number 10 as having "clearly played a decision-making role" in pausing transfers of asylum seekers from Manston into hotel accommodation — the very decision that let the population balloon past anything the site could hold. That reframes the crisis: not a logistics failure overtaken by an unexpected surge in Channel crossings, but the foreseeable result of a policy choice to keep people on site rather than move them on, made and sustained at ministerial level.

That is why the witness list reads like a Conservative cabinet reunion. Chaired by Sophie Cartwright KC, the inquiry expects to hear from three former home secretaries — Priti Patel, Suella Braverman and Grant Shapps — plus former immigration minister Robert Jenrick, five other ex-immigration ministers, and former prime ministers Boris Johnson and Rishi Sunak, both in office at different points during the 2022 crisis. Dobbin has said the conditions in which children were held "raise very serious questions" about what ministers were prepared to tolerate.

What's next

Cartwright has confirmed only that directions following Friday's hearing will set a start date, venue and witness timetable for the substantive oral hearings — the point at which Patel, Braverman and the rest will actually be questioned under oath. Separately, Home Office officials have already been charged with conspiracy to steal and misconduct in public office over conduct at the site. Between the criminal case and the inquiry's eventual findings, Manston is shaping up as a rare instance where a chaotic news cycle from 2022 gets a formal, on-the-record reckoning — one that could yet reach further than a single facility's failings, into the machinery of who signs off on policies that leave people penned in barracks with one toilet between eighty-one.
