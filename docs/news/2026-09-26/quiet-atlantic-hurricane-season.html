CLIMATE · HURRICANES · By S. Whitfield · 2026-09-26 · 4 min read

Atlantic hurricane season records its quietest start since satellite era began

In one sentence
No Atlantic hurricane had formed by September's climatological peak — the quietest start on record — while an intense El Niño drove record Pacific activity.

Not one Atlantic hurricane had formed by September 24 — the traditional climatological peak of the season — a lull not seen since before satellites began watching the ocean for storms. Seven systems have earned names this year, from Arthur to Gonzalo, but none has crossed the 74 mph threshold that turns a tropical storm into a hurricane. Only two full seasons on record, 1907 and 1914, ended with zero hurricanes at all, and both predate reliable observation. By one measure of storm energy, this year's Atlantic has been running at roughly 8% of its usual output for the date.

"It's ramping up to be perhaps the strongest El Niño on record," said Brian McNoldy, a hurricane researcher at the University of Miami. That single climate pattern, more than any other factor, explains why the ocean on one side of Central America has gone quiet while the other has turned violent.

One basin muzzled, the other unleashed

El Niño works by warming the eastern and central Pacific, which alters the jet stream and, critically for the Atlantic, strengthens upper-level winds over the Caribbean and tropical Atlantic. That wind shear tears the tops off budding storms before they can organize a warm core — "it destroys the hurricane structure," as several meteorologists have put it this season. Warm water alone was never enough; shear is the piece that has been missing.

The same warming that calms the Atlantic supercharges the eastern Pacific, and this year it has done so emphatically. Three Pacific hurricanes have reached Category 5 strength, tying a record last set in 2002 — also an El Niño year. NOAA forecaster Matthew Rosencrans counts four more named storms than usual across the eastern and central basins combined, with the season's accumulated cyclone energy running 54% above the historical average. Hawaii has borne the brunt: Hurricane Lala became the first storm to strike the Big Island directly since 1871, damaging or destroying more than 500 homes and causing an estimated $168 million in infrastructure losses. Hurricane Lowell followed with Category 5 winds in early September, and Hurricane Nolo was bearing down on the islands with flooding rain as of this week. "Unlike anything I can recall," Hawaii Governor Josh Green said of the run of storms.

A pattern insurers are already pricing

The redistribution is not, on the evidence so far, proof of a permanent shift — El Niño's Atlantic-suppressing, Pacific-boosting seesaw is a well-documented and recurring mechanism, not a novel one. What is unusual is its intensity, and the speed with which capital markets are reacting to the calm. The catastrophe bond market has swelled to a record $65.6 billion outstanding, with $17.3 billion issued in the first half of 2026 alone. Spreads have compressed accordingly: the average premium investors demand over expected losses fell to 3.74% in the second quarter, the first sub-4% reading in twenty quarters, while Guy Carpenter's benchmark property catastrophe rate index is down 16% for the year. Florida reinsurance renewals in June priced 15-20% cheaper than a year earlier.

That repricing rests partly on a genuinely quiet Atlantic loss year — but cat bonds are typically structured around multi-decade historical odds of landfall, not single-season conditions, and the Atlantic season still runs through November 30. NOAA's official outlook, unchanged since the spring, still allows for two to six hurricanes before the season closes; a late Gulf or Caribbean storm remains entirely possible even after a historically slow start. If insurers are extrapolating this year's calm into next year's pricing, and El Niño swings of this magnitude become more frequent under a warming climate, the historical baselines underpinning catastrophe models may need revisiting well before the next quiet season arrives.

The next test comes quickly: October and early November are prime window for late-season Atlantic development, and any renewed hurricane activity — or its continued absence — will shape how reinsurers price Gulf Coast and Caribbean risk heading into 2027 renewals.
