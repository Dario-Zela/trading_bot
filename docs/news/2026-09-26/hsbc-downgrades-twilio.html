HSBC downgrade sends Twilio shares tumbling

Twilio's logo, taken from the company's press kit. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC cut Twilio to Reduce on margin concerns over Meta's Muse AI agent, days after two other banks raised targets on the same story.

Twilio shares fell 6.8% on Friday to $279.36 after HSBC cut its rating on the communications-software firm to Reduce from Hold, breaking with a Wall Street consensus that has otherwise turned more bullish in recent days.

The move stands out less for its rarity — single-firm downgrades happen every week — than for its timing and its argument. Twilio stock has surged roughly 30% since September 8, when Meta's new consumer AI agent, Muse, launched and set off a scramble among investors to identify infrastructure winners. The stock has nearly tripled over the past year. Just three days before HSBC's call, TD Cowen raised its price target to $300 from $260 and Rosenblatt lifted its own to $290 from $275 — both firms reiterating Buy ratings on the same AI-agent thesis that HSBC now says has run too far.

The bear case, in specifics

HSBC analyst Sameer Lam held his price target at $211 — more than $40 below the Street's average of $254.59 — while downgrading the stock two notches. His argument is not that Muse is a nonevent for Twilio. It's that the market has misjudged which parts of the business benefit.

Investors, Lam argues, have started treating Twilio as the plumbing behind Muse and similar consumer AI agents, and have priced in a share of the upside to match. His view is that Meta keeps the most lucrative layer — the proprietary voice technology — in house, leaving Twilio to handle the commodity work: phone number routing and standard SMS verification, the kind of business that has always carried thin margins. More message volume, in other words, doesn't necessarily mean more profit for Twilio if the messages routed are the low-margin kind.

Why one downgrade moved the stock this much

A rating change from a single bank rarely produces a near-7% one-day move on its own. What made this one land is the gap it exposes: Twilio's rally has been built substantially on a narrative — that AI agents will drive a new class of premium, Twilio-priced traffic — rather than on disclosed contract terms or updated revenue guidance from Twilio itself. HSBC's note is the first widely circulated attempt to price that narrative against the mechanics of how Muse-driven traffic actually reaches Twilio's network, and it landed on a stock trading at a level that left little room for a competing read to be ignored.

That also means the disagreement is falsifiable in a way vague sentiment shifts are not. If Twilio's next earnings report shows message volumes rising alongside average revenue per message, HSBC's low-margin-plumbing thesis weakens. If volumes rise while margins stay flat or compress, the bulls at TD Cowen and Rosenblatt will have some explaining to do.

What's next

Twilio does not report third-quarter earnings until late October, which leaves nearly a month in which the stock's direction depends on positioning and further analyst commentary rather than company data. Investors weighing the two camps have, for now, only Lam's routing argument on one side and the broader AI-agent enthusiasm reflected in TD Cowen's and Rosenblatt's targets on the other — a genuine split in view, not a pile-on.
