Fed's Hammack warns bond-yield rise reflects 'unsustainable' fiscal path and AI capex demand

Cleveland Federal Reserve President Beth Hammack, who said the US is on an "unsustainable fiscal path" as bond yields climb. — Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Cleveland Fed's Hammack says unsustainable US deficits and AI capex financing, not lost inflation credibility, are driving Treasury yields to multi-decade highs.

Cleveland Federal Reserve President Beth Hammack told a conference at her own bank on September 25 that the recent climb in long-term Treasury yields is not the kind of thing the Fed can simply cut its way out of. Real interest rates, she said, have risen faster than inflation expectations — a distinction that matters, because it points to demand for capital and worries about government borrowing rather than a loss of faith that the Fed will eventually tame prices.

"We're reasonably well anchored from an inflation expectations perspective," Hammack said, according to Bloomberg's account of the remarks. But she was blunter about the other side of the ledger: "The US is on an unsustainable fiscal path."

Two forces, one yield curve

The 10-year Treasury yield has pushed to around 5.17%, its highest since mid-2007, while the 30-year has traded near 5.47%, with an intraday print of 5.501% — a level last seen in 2004. Thirty-year fixed mortgage rates have followed, drifting back toward 7%.

Hammack's framing split the causes into two buckets. One is fiscal: persistent federal deficits mean the Treasury keeps auctioning more debt into a market that is demanding more compensation to hold it. The other is corporate, and newer — the financing needs of the artificial-intelligence buildout. "Demand for investment linked to AI is competing for capital in the bond market," she said, adding that "longer-term questions remain over how AI could affect inflation."

The scale involved explains why she raised it. Global AI-linked bond issuance is on pace to approach $570 billion in 2026, roughly four times last year's rate over the same stretch, as hyperscalers—Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle—commit a combined $700–725 billion to capital spending this year. Nomura has estimated that big tech borrowing now equals roughly a quarter of the Treasury's net issuance of notes and bonds to private investors, up fivefold from 2025. The six largest tech companies account for an estimated 8.6% of duration-weighted US investment-grade credit outstanding — data centers, chips and power infrastructure are long-lived assets, and hyperscalers are financing them with long-dated debt that competes directly with Treasuries for the same pool of investors.

A hawk who now has a vote

Hammack's diagnosis carries more weight than it might have a year ago, because she is a voting member of the FOMC in 2026 and has already used that vote once to push against the consensus. At the July 28–29 meeting she dissented in favor of raising the federal funds rate, arguing inflation had run too high for too long and that the committee needed to act to speed its return to the 2% target. She was careful this week to separate her own analysis from the Fed's job: "the Fed is the decision maker on monetary policy," she said, even as she stays "mindful of financial conditions."

Her remarks also land inside a changed Fed. Kevin Warsh was confirmed as chair in a bruising 54-45 Senate vote in May and took the gavel from Jerome Powell on May 22, arriving with a reputation for tighter inflation discipline than his predecessor. A Fed led by a self-described inflation hawk, with a hawkish regional president holding a vote, is a different animal from the one markets spent 2024 and 2025 pricing for steady cuts.

What it means for the rate path

The practical point of Hammack's remarks is that some of the upward pressure on yields sits outside the Fed's control. If deficits and AI-related borrowing keep drawing on the same pool of savings, long-term rates can stay elevated even if the Fed lowers its short-term policy rate — a decoupling that would blunt the transmission of any easing into mortgage costs, corporate borrowing and the rest of the real economy. That is a structural argument, not a cyclical one, and it is harder to solve with monetary policy alone.

The next test comes at the FOMC's October 27–28 meeting, where futures markets will be watching whether Hammack's caution finds allies or stays a minority view. Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement, due in the same window, will show whether the government's own issuance plans add to the supply pressure she flagged — or whether Washington starts treating the fiscal side of the equation as urgently as the market already has.
