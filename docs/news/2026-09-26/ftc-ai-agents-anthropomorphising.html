FTC chair vows to keep resisting 'anthropomorphising' of AI agents

FTC chairman Andrew Ferguson, who says developers — not AI agents — should bear responsibility for harm. — Federal Trade Commission / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
FTC chair Andrew Ferguson says he'll keep resisting talk of AI agents acting on their own, while flagging delivery, rideshare and airline pricing algorithms as his top consumer-protection worry.

Andrew Ferguson, the Federal Trade Commission's chairman, spent part of a stage appearance in Austin this week doing something regulators rarely bother with: talking down the very technology his agency is supposed to police.

Speaking at the Reuters Momentum AI conference on September 25, Ferguson said he would keep pushing back against the habit — common among AI companies and journalists alike — of describing software agents as if they have minds of their own. "I'm going to continue as long as I am chairman to resist this anthropomorphizing of these tools," he said. His argument: when an AI agent does something harmful, the fault lies with whoever built or instructed it, not with the tool itself. "If someone tells a tool to do something, and the tool does it, I don't think we would say, 'Oh, what do we do about the tool?'"

It's a jab at a narrative that has gained currency as AI firms describe systems occasionally acting in unexpected ways. Ferguson said that when the FTC has looked at audit trails in such cases, the systems were simply carrying out the instructions they'd been given — not breaking loose with, in his phrase, "wills and desires of their own." The practical upshot is a liability theory: developers and deployers, not the code, sit in the FTC's crosshairs.

Where the FTC is actually looking

The philosophical point was really a setup for the news. Asked where he personally worries most about AI's effect on consumers, Ferguson named three sectors: delivery apps, rideshare services and airlines. All three have faced years of suspicion that pricing algorithms tailor fares and fees to what a given customer — or a given moment of demand — will bear, rather than posting one price for everyone.

Airlines have particular reason to feel the heat. Delta Air Lines drew criticism last year after an executive suggested the carrier was using AI tools to help gauge how much individual customers were willing to pay for a ticket, a comment that reignited long-running fears about "personalized pricing" in air travel. Ferguson's comments suggest that episode has stuck with regulators.

He said the FTC plans to request data directly from companies in these sectors as part of a forthcoming study, and that the agency "would not hesitate" to bring enforcement action where firms use personalized or algorithmic pricing without disclosing it to consumers. That is a narrower ask than a crackdown on the practice itself — dynamic pricing is legal, and airlines have used yield-management software for decades. The line the FTC is drawing is around disclosure: whether customers know a price was shaped by their own data.

A regulator picking its lane

Ferguson has been consistent in arguing the FTC should not become a general-purpose AI regulator without Congress telling it to. Instead, he has framed the agency's role as applying existing consumer-protection and data-security law to specific harms — deceptive claims, undisclosed data use, breaches — wherever AI happens to be involved. That includes, he suggested, treating a company's failure to disclose a data breach caused by an AI agent the same way the FTC would treat any other breach disclosure failure.

The anthropomorphizing comments and the pricing comments are, in that sense, two sides of the same position: don't treat the AI as a novel legal subject, and don't treat AI-enabled pricing as a novel legal problem either. Both, in Ferguson's telling, reduce to conduct by companies that existing law already covers.

What comes next

The near-term step is the data request to delivery, rideshare and airline operators, which will feed into an FTC study rather than an immediate enforcement action — Ferguson was careful to frame this as an ongoing area of focus, not a new case. For companies like DoorDash, Uber, Lyft and the major carriers, the practical question is how their pricing algorithms document and disclose the data they use, since that is where Ferguson says the agency's attention — and any future action — will land.
