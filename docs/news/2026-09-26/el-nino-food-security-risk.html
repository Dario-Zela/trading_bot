CLIMATE · EL NIÑO · By S. Whitfield · 2026-09-26 · 4 min read

El Niño intensification raises drought and flood risk across key food-producing regions

Drought-stressed corn in Navasota, Texas — El Niño's rainfall swings put grain-belt yields worldwide back in focus. — USDA photo by Bob Nichols / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The WMO says a near-certain, potentially record-strength El Niño threatens grain-belt yields from Southeast Asia to East Africa, pushing food-security risk into commodity pricing.

The UN's World Meteorological Organization says the El Niño now building in the Pacific has an almost 100% chance of persisting through February 2027 and could become one of the strongest on record — a forecast so unequivocal that WMO chief Celeste Saulo said it was the first time the agency had issued an El Niño update with this degree of certainty. UN Secretary-General António Guterres put it more bluntly: "El Niño is being supersized before our eyes."

For traders in wheat, corn and soy futures, the forecast matters less as a weather story than as a repricing signal. El Niño shifts rainfall and temperature patterns across the world's grain belts in fairly predictable ways, and the scale of this one raises the odds that at least one major producing region takes a real hit to yields just as global stockpiles are already stretched thin by years of climate volatility.

Where the risk concentrates

The Niño 3.4 index — the standard measure of Pacific warming — averaged 1.5°C above normal from May to July, hit 2°C in July, and peaked near 2.6°C in August, with some subsurface Pacific waters running 8°C above normal. WMO's models point to above-normal temperatures across nearly the entire land surface of the planet through November, layered on rainfall patterns that follow the classic El Niño script: flooding in some places, drought in others.

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization has flagged Southeast Asia's rice belt as particularly exposed. India, Vietnam and Thailand — the world's three largest rice exporters — are among the countries most likely to see planting and yields disrupted. In East Africa, forecasters expect a two-stage pattern: drought curtailing crops in Ethiopia, Sudan, South Sudan, Uganda and western Kenya through the northern autumn, followed by a flood-inducing deluge toward year-end. Southern Africa faces its own heat-and-drought risk. South America's picture is more mixed — Bolivia, Ecuador and Peru are expecting above-average rain, which forecasters say could lift South American soybean output even as it complicates Brazil's planting calendar and shortens the window for its second corn crop.

The U.S. Corn Belt is, for now, the outlier. Warmer, wetter conditions forecast for the Midwest could support rather than threaten yields, and some analysts expect U.S. soybean and hard red winter wheat prices to soften if that plays out. Durum wheat is a different matter: the EU's Joint Research Centre has warned that a strong El Niño scenario could push global durum prices higher regardless of what happens in North America.

The market and humanitarian response

FAO and the World Food Programme have already launched their first-ever joint anticipatory action appeal tied to an El Niño event, seeking $202 million to protect 8.8 million people across 22 high-risk countries — funding cash transfers, drought-tolerant and flood-resistant seed, livestock support and flood defences before the worst of the disruption lands. The agencies estimate every dollar spent this way avoids roughly seven dollars in later losses. UN officials separately project nearly 50 million additional people could face acute hunger as the event unfolds.

For commodity desks, the signal is less about any single number than about dispersion: a strong El Niño tends to widen the range of outcomes across regions and crops simultaneously, which is precisely the condition that lifts hedging demand and options volatility even before a single harvest is in trouble. Markets have been here before — the 2015-16 and 2023-24 events both produced sharp, short-lived spikes in agricultural futures — but WMO's own comparison points are the ones from 1997-98 and 2015-16, both among the most disruptive on record.

Scientists caution that human-caused warming has not been shown to intensify El Niño itself, but it does amplify what the event delivers, since warmer oceans and a hotter atmosphere hold more heat and moisture to feed both the droughts and the floods. Researchers have not established that El Niño's frequency or strength is increasing at all, only that when the naturally occurring the event does not is the process of intensification.

The next marker to watch is WMO's updated outlook expected in December, when the event's trajectory into its northern-hemisphere-winter peak should become clearer — along with the first real read on how Southeast Asia's rice harvest and Brazil's planting season are holding up.
