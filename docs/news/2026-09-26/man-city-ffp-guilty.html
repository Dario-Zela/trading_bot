Manchester City found guilty on 114 of 115 FFP charges; rivals weigh damages claims

The Etihad Stadium, Manchester City's home ground, at the centre of a decade-long financial fair play investigation. — Dom Fellowes / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
An independent panel found Manchester City guilty on 114 of 115 FFP charges, with sanctions and rival clubs' compensation claims still to be decided.

An independent panel has found Manchester City guilty on 114 of the 115 financial charges brought against it by the Premier League — a verdict eight years and one four-year investigation in the making, and one that leaves English football's richest club facing sanctions that could range from a fine to a points deduction severe enough to threaten relegation.

The ruling, delivered Friday, closes the fact-finding phase of a case that began in December 2018, when leaked documents published by Der Spiegel first alleged City had disguised owner funding as sponsorship income. The Premier League formally charged the club in February 2023, covering conduct across a nine-year window from 2009 to 2018: inflated sponsorship deals, undisclosed payments to managers and players, and — among the more serious allegations — a failure to cooperate with the investigation itself. City contested every charge. It won on exactly one.

"The Premier League process remains ongoing, with significant elements to be completed, and subject to strict confidentiality."

That is the entirety of City's public response so far — a statement notable for what it doesn't say. The club has not denied the verdict, and is expected to lodge an appeal within the mandatory 14-day window, a process that could run another three months. But for a club that spent years insisting the case was politically motivated and would ultimately clear its name, going from full denial to procedural silence marks a shift in tone.

A verdict with no punishment yet attached

What makes Friday's ruling unusual is that it settles guilt without settling consequence. The independent commission has yet to determine sanctions, and the Premier League's own handbook gives it a startlingly wide berth to choose from: an unlimited fine, a points deduction, or, at the extreme end, expulsion from the league. Precedent offers little guide. Everton and Nottingham Forest were docked points in 2023 and 2024 for breaching profit-and-sustainability rules — six and four points respectively — but neither case involved anything close to 114 proven breaches spanning a decade, nor allegations of obstructing an investigation.

That scale is the reason lawyers inside the game have floated points deductions running into the dozens rather than single digits, though nothing is confirmed and City's parallel appeal means any sanction could be contested for months, possibly years, before it bites. The club has not denied the underlying facts so much as reserved its right to argue the process itself was flawed — a strategy that served it well in 2020, when the Court of Arbitration for Sport overturned a two-year UEFA ban on procedural grounds even as it left a smaller finding of non-cooperation intact.

This time the accuser is the Premier League itself, City's own competition, adjudicating under its own rules — which narrows the room for a similar escape route, since there is no external body left to appeal to beyond the Premier League's own appeal board.

Rivals move from threat to claim

While City weighs its next legal move, four of its closest rivals have already made theirs. Manchester United, Arsenal, Liverpool and Tottenham Hotspur each served City with legal notices in 2024, preserving their right to sue for damages tied to seasons in which they missed out on Champions League qualification, prize money or sponsorship revenue they argue was distorted by City's rule-breaking. Those notices — filed ahead of the six-year limitation period that governs civil claims in England — were speculative when lodged. They are not any longer.

Lawyers advising some of the clubs have put potential claims above £100 million each, with people close to the process suggesting a successful case could be worth more than £200 million to individual claimants once lost broadcast revenue, gate receipts and commercial spillover are factored in. There is no precedent in English football for a club successfully suing a domestic rival over competitive advantage gained through rule-breaking — nothing like it has reached a courtroom before, largely because nothing like a 114-charge guilty verdict has existed before. Any formal litigation is expected to wait until City's appeal is exhausted, which could push a resolution well into 2027 or beyond.

What the verdict is worth

City's owners, Abu Dhabi's Sheikh Mansour and the wider City Football Group, built the club into one of the most commercially valuable properties in sport largely on the back of the success this case scrutinises: ten Premier League titles since 2008, a Champions League win in 2023, and sponsorship contracts — a Puma kit deal reportedly worth more than $1.5 billion over its life, an Etihad Airways front-of-shirt deal approaching £1 billion — that depend on City being seen as a legitimate, sustainably run institution rather than a state-backed project that bent the rules to get there. Sportico valued the club at roughly $5.2 billion this year, sixth-highest among football clubs globally.

The more consequential audience for Friday's verdict may not be City's rivals or its sponsors, but every other state-linked or ownership-concentrated club now operating under similar financial oversight regimes — in England and across Europe. A regulator that spent eight years building a 115-charge case and secured a conviction on 114 of them has demonstrated it will pursue enforcement to the end, even against a club with the resources to contest every stage. Whether that translates into a sanction commensurate with the scale of the findings — rather than one eroded by appeal after appeal — is the question the Premier League now has to answer, and the one every rival club, sponsor and regulator elsewhere in sport will be watching most closely.

The next fixed point is City's appeal filing, due within 14 days of Friday's ruling, with a hearing expected to conclude within roughly 12 weeks. Sanctions will not be set until that process runs its course.
