US backs Musk's bid to overturn €120m EU fine against X

Elon Musk, whose X platform was fined €120 million by the European Commission under the Digital Services Act. — Debbie Rowe / The Royal Society, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The US Justice Department has asked to join Elon Musk's court challenge to a €120m EU fine on X, turning a platform-regulation dispute into a transatlantic legal fight.

The US Justice Department has asked to formally join Elon Musk's legal fight against a €120 million European Union fine on X, turning what had been a private regulatory dispute into a state-to-state confrontation over who gets to police American tech platforms.

The intervention, filed Thursday with the EU's General Court in Luxembourg, does not challenge the substance of the fine so much as Brussels' right to impose it at all. Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate, who heads the department's Civil Division, said the European Commission "inappropriately attempted to expand its regulatory authority to reach American companies not present or operating within its jurisdiction," adding that Washington "will not tolerate the European Commission engaging in regulatory over-reach."

What X was actually fined for

The penalty, announced on 5 December 2025, was the first financial sanction levied under the EU's Digital Services Act, a 2022 law governing how large online platforms handle content moderation, advertising and data transparency — distinct from the bloc's competition-law regime, though it carries similarly serious exposure. The Commission split the €120 million three ways: €45 million over X's paid-for blue checkmark, which regulators said made it harder for users to judge who they were actually talking to; €40 million for restricting outside researchers' access to platform data; and €35 million for shortcomings in its public advertising repository. The investigation had run since December 2023, with X and Musk personally named jointly and severally liable. X filed three separate appeals in February, now proceeding under case numbers T-114/26 and T-121/26.

Brussels holds its line

The Commission's response, delivered by spokesperson Thomas Regnier on Friday, was pointedly unbothered. "We are enforcing our legislation objectively, transparently and with a solid case," he said, adding that the EU is "ready to defend our position in court" and does not "need to be informed about anything" regarding Washington's move. Officials were also careful to ring-fence the dispute from the broader transatlantic relationship, telling reporters that "nothing will change because of a DSA case."

That framing may prove optimistic. The DOJ's filing lands alongside a running campaign by senior Trump administration figures — Secretary of State Marco Rubio has repeatedly described the DSA as a censorship tool aimed at American platforms — that has made EU digital regulation a recurring irritant in US-Europe relations. Whether the DOJ is even permitted to join the case is itself unresolved: intervention before the General Court requires showing a "direct legal interest" under Article 40 of the court's statute, a bar governments have cleared in trade and sanctions disputes but rarely in competition or platform-regulation cases brought against a private company.

Why it matters for markets

For investors in US tech names with EU exposure, the immediate financial stakes are modest — €120 million is a rounding error against X's balance sheet, and the DSA allows for further daily penalties of up to 5% of average worldwide turnover only if remedial orders are later ignored. The more consequential signal is procedural: a foreign government has now asked a court to weigh its diplomatic objections alongside the legal merits of a case brought against a company operating under that court's jurisdiction. If the General Court admits the US as a party, or even simply entertains the sovereignty argument at length, it invites other governments — and other companies — to treat EU enforcement actions as negotiable through state pressure rather than settled through adjudication. That is the kind of uncertainty that shows up in compliance budgets and legal reserves for any large platform doing business in Europe, well beyond X.

Musk has form here: X cut off the Commission's own advertising account on the platform within days of the original fine being announced, a retaliatory gesture that did little beyond signalling how personally he has taken the case.

The General Court has not set a date for ruling on the US intervention request. That procedural decision — whether Washington is even allowed a seat at the table — will arrive before any substantive hearing on the fine itself, and will be the first real test of how much weight geopolitics now carries in Brussels' enforcement machine.
