FBI hacked by ShinyHunters; personal data on thousands of agents allegedly stolen

The J. Edgar Hoover Building, FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C. — Ajay Suresh / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
ShinyHunters claims a 2-3TB breach of FBI systems via an Oracle PeopleSoft zero-day, exposing agents' home addresses and medical records, as payback for an FBI advisory.

The FBI is investigating a claim that has become depressingly familiar in 2026: an extortion gang says it walked into a federal system through unpatched enterprise software, and walked out with terabytes of data on the people who work there.

The group calling itself ShinyHunters told the security outlet BleepingComputer it exploited a previously unknown vulnerability — a zero-day — in Oracle PeopleSoft, the human-resources and applicant-tracking software many large organizations use to manage employee records. From there, the group says it moved laterally into FBI-managed infrastructure hosted on Amazon's GovCloud, the segregated cloud region built for US government workloads, and pulled between 2 and 3 terabytes of data. A sample reviewed by 404 Media reportedly includes records on roughly 5,000 agents.

The FBI has not confirmed a breach occurred. A spokesperson told TechCrunch only that the bureau "is aware of claims regarding unauthorized activity affecting FBIjobs.gov and is currently investigating." That carefully hedged language is standard for an active investigation, but it leaves the core claims — the zero-day, the scope, the GovCloud pivot — resting entirely on the word of the people who broke in.

What was allegedly taken

ShinyHunters says the haul spans records tied to the FBI's human-resources, criminal-justice and Medlink systems — the last of these covering medical evaluations for agents and applicants. Beyond names, the group claims to have home addresses and phone numbers, in some cases extending to agents' spouses.

That combination matters more than a typical customer-data leak. Home addresses of law-enforcement personnel are the raw material for swatting and physical intimidation — tactics ShinyHunters itself has been accused of using against its victims. Medical evaluation records, meanwhile, are exactly the kind of leverage a hostile intelligence service would want on an officer it hoped to coerce or discredit. Security researchers told Axios the incident appears consistent with a genuine intrusion, even though none of the technical specifics — the zero-day, the exfiltration volume — have been independently verified by outside firms.

An oddly personal motive

What sets this apart from ShinyHunters' usual playbook is the stated reason for it. The group has spent the past two years running a criminal ecosystem — sometimes overlapping with Scattered Spider and Lapsus\$ under the banner "Scattered Lapsus\$ Hunters" — built on data theft and extortion, from the 2024 Snowflake customer breaches to the 2025 wave of Salesforce and Salesloft intrusions that touched more than 700 organizations. Those campaigns were financially motivated: pay up, or the data goes public.

This one, the group insists, is not about money. It says the attack is retaliation for an FBI FLASH advisory issued in the second quarter of 2026 that described ShinyHunters' harassment tactics — including threats and swatting against victims and their families — and urged targets not to pay ransoms. ShinyHunters is demanding the bureau retract the advisory within a week. Whether or not that demand is met, the episode shows an extortion group treating a law-enforcement warning as a personal insult worth burning capability over — using a zero-day, rather than selling or hoarding it, purely to make a point.

The Oracle problem, again

The more durable story here may be about the software, not the attackers. This is not Oracle's first bad year. In October 2025, the Cl0p ransomware group exploited a separate zero-day — CVE-2025-61882, with a maximum-severity CVSS score of 9.8 — in Oracle E-Business Suite, another enterprise resource-planning product, compromising at least 103 organizations across finance, manufacturing and the public sector before CISA forced an emergency patch deadline. PeopleSoft and E-Business Suite are both decades-old Oracle application suites that remain embedded in government and corporate back offices precisely because they are hard to rip out.

Oracle has not yet issued a statement on the ShinyHunters claim; the company was contacted by multiple outlets, including TechCrunch and BleepingComputer, and has not responded publicly as of this writing. If a second Oracle zero-day in a year turns out to be real, it hands regulators and federal auditors a pattern rather than an isolated incident — and a harder question about why systems handling agents' home addresses and medical files were reachable from the internet at all.

What's next

The FBI's investigation is the near-term catalyst: confirmation or denial of the zero-day, the AWS GovCloud pivot, and the true scope of the data taken would settle a story that currently runs entirely on the attackers' own claims. Watch also for whether Oracle issues a patch or advisory for PeopleSoft — the kind of disclosure that would, on its own, corroborate ShinyHunters' account regardless of what the bureau says. And if ShinyHunters' one-week deadline passes without the FBI retracting its advisory, the next move — quiet or loud — will say a good deal about whether this was retaliation for its own sake or the opening move in something with a price tag attached.
