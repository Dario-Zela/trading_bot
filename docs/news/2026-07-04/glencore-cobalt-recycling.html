Congo's cobalt quota shift puts Glencore's recycling business in focus

Artisanal miners carry sacks of ore at a cobalt mining site in the Democratic Republic of Congo. — International Institute for Environment and Development, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.5)
In one sentence
Congo's clawback of unused cobalt export quotas has fed a theory that Glencore's recycling arm gets more strategic — but the numbers to prove it aren't out yet.

The Democratic Republic of Congo has told cobalt miners a familiar rule: use the permit or lose it. On June 29, the country's mining regulator, ARECOMS, said any first-half 2026 export quota left unused by June 30 would be clawed back and folded into the state's "strategic quota" — reserved for projects of "national interest," including local processing and value-addition schemes Kinshasa has been pushing for years. Only shipments already logged in the customs system by July 5 still qualify under the old first-half allocations; the tighter rules take effect July 1.

That has revived a trade that's been running hot since Congo lifted its outright cobalt export ban last October: reading DRC policy for winners further down the battery supply chain. One theory making the rounds — including from Insider Monkey, via Yahoo Finance — is that a tighter primary-supply regime makes Glencore's battery-recycling arm more strategically valuable. The logic holds up mechanically. Whether the evidence backs it yet is a separate question.

What Congo actually changed

Congo controls more than 70% of global cobalt reserves and has spent two years tightening its grip on how much of it leaves the country. The 2026-27 framework caps total exports at 96,600 tonnes a year, with 87,000 tonnes divided among commercial miners on a pro-rata basis and 9,600 tonnes routed straight to the state's own reserve. This week's move doesn't raise that ceiling — it just makes sure nobody sits on an allocation they don't use, and it hands ARECOMS more room to redirect volumes toward domestic processing. The regulator also warned it can strip quotas entirely from firms that miss deadlines, resell allocations to third parties, or process unauthorized material — a reminder that Kinshasa treats these permits as leverage, not paperwork.

The backdrop is a cobalt market that has already repriced hard. LME cobalt has risen roughly 160% from its February 2025 lows to around $26 a pound, driven by the export controls and by a $12 billion U.S. strategic-stockpile push. Fastmarkets expects the new volumes released into the market this year to cool prices somewhat, even against a projected 10,700-tonne supply deficit.

Where Glencore actually sits

Glencore is one of two dominant players inside Congo's quota system, with a combined 2026 allocation of about 22,800 tonnes — roughly 23.6% of the national cap — anchored by its Kamoto Copper Company mine, the world's largest single cobalt operation. That's the primary-supply side. On the other side, Glencore built a recycling arm last August by buying Li-Cycle out of bankruptcy for an initial $40 million, picking up four operating spoke facilities in North America, a large plant in Magdeburg, Germany, and the unfinished Rochester hub project, now rebranded Glencore Battery Recycling.

What's missing from the case for a strategic re-rating is data: no published recycled-cobalt output figures tied to this quota change, no demand projections, no price signal specific to secondary material. The claim that Congo's paperwork shuffle makes Glencore's recycling footprint "more strategic" is an inference about mechanics, not yet a measured shift in economics.

The next test is whether Fastmarkets' predicted price cooling shows up in the data once the reassigned volumes actually move, and whether Glencore's next earnings update breaks out recycled-cobalt volumes separately from mined tonnes — the first hard number that would let the recycling thesis be checked rather than assumed.
