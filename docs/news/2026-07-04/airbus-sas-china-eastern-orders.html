Airbus lands record orders from SAS and China Eastern

An Airbus A330-900neo — the same variant both SAS and China Eastern have ordered — pictured in Cebu Pacific livery — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
SAS ordered 18 Airbus A330-900s in its largest-ever fleet deal as it emerges from bankruptcy, while China Eastern separately locked in dozens more Airbus widebodies and narrowbodies worth billions.

Two years ago, Scandinavian Airlines was a ward of a U.S. bankruptcy court, its future ownership an open question and its fleet plans frozen. On June 30th, at a ceremony in Copenhagen, it signed the largest aircraft order in its history. Four thousand miles east, China Eastern Airlines — one of the world's biggest carriers by fleet size — had already put ink on a separate, even larger Airbus order weeks earlier. Together the two deals mark one of the busiest stretches in Airbus's commercial calendar this year, and a fairly unambiguous signal that airlines are still willing to commit billions of euros to fleets years before a single new jet lands on a runway.

In one sentence: SAS ordered 18 Airbus A330-900s in its largest-ever fleet deal as it emerges from bankruptcy, while China Eastern separately locked in dozens more Airbus widebodies and narrowbodies worth billions.

SAS bets big on the other side of bankruptcy

SAS filed for Chapter 11 protection in the United States in 2022, a restructuring that dragged through most of 2023 before resolving in October that year with a new ownership consortium: Castlelake, Air France-KLM and Danish investment firm Lind Invest, alongside a reduced but retained stake for the Danish state. The equity split that emerged — roughly 32 percent Castlelake, 26 percent the Danish state, 20 percent Air France-KLM and 9 percent Lind Invest — also set SAS on a path out of Star Alliance and into SkyTeam, aligning it commercially with its new part-owner.

What that restructuring didn't immediately produce was a fleet plan with real money behind it. That changed on June 30th, when SAS chief executive Anko van der Werff and Airbus sales executive Benoît de Saint-Exupéry signed a firm order for 18 A330-900 widebodies — the aircraft maker's newest long-haul jet, built on the A330 airframe but re-engined with Rolls-Royce Trent 7000s for roughly a quarter less fuel burn than the aircraft it replaces. Airbus and SAS have described the 18-jet order as anchoring a wider long-haul renewal that could eventually stretch to 40 widebody aircraft, with a list-price value north of $10 billion once the full program is included — though neither side has published a delivery timetable, and list prices routinely overstate what airlines actually pay once discounts are applied.

Van der Werff called the jets central to the carrier's "long-term fleet strategy," language that reads less like a slogan than a statement of relief: after three years of restructuring, SAS is once again in a position to make a decade-long capital commitment rather than simply keep the lights on. De Saint-Exupéry, for his part, pitched the A330-900 as "the undisputed benchmark for efficiency and versatility in its category" — the kind of line every manufacturer offers about its own aircraft, but one that happens to be borne out by order books: the A330 family has now logged more than 1,950 orders from 133 customers worldwide.

China Eastern's much bigger number

If SAS's order is notable for what it says about a single airline's recovery, China Eastern's is notable simply for its size. On June 26th the Shanghai-based carrier confirmed an order for 25 A330-900s worth an estimated $9.35 billion to $9.5 billion at list prices, with deliveries running from 2029 through 2033. That followed an even larger deal struck in March: 101 A320neo-family narrowbodies valued at $15.8 billion, delivering between 2028 and 2032. Add the two together and China Eastern has committed to 126 new Airbus aircraft in a single year, worth in the region of $25 billion before discounts.

The airline's own explanation is prosaic rather than triumphant: it is retiring aging equipment. China Eastern currently flies 28 A330-200s and 24 A330-300s, and plans to retire at least 53 A320s between 2028 and 2032 as leases expire and airframes age out. In its statement, the carrier said the new jets would "optimize fleet structure and route network, improve operational and service quality, and reduce unit operating costs" — corporate phrasing for a straightforward proposition: newer aircraft burn less fuel and break down less often, and China Eastern intends to keep growing into a Chinese aviation market that has kept expanding through the recovery years.

Why two airlines, one week apart, matters more than either alone

A single large order tells you about one airline's balance sheet. Two, arriving within days of each other from carriers on opposite sides of the world and opposite ends of the financial-health spectrum, tell you something about the market. SAS is a mid-sized European flag carrier just out of insolvency proceedings, still proving to lenders and passengers alike that it has a future. China Eastern is a state-linked giant with no such credibility problem, expanding because Chinese outbound and domestic travel has kept climbing. That both concluded, independently, that now is the moment to commit tens of billions of dollars to aircraft that won't arrive for years is a reasonably clean signal that airline management teams — not just analysts — believe the demand recovery has legs.

The market has, in a smaller way, already registered the news. Airbus shares (ENXTPA:AIR) were trading around €204-€206 as of Thursday, up roughly 4.5 percent over the past week and about 18 percent over the past month — a rally analysts have partly attributed to a cluster of order wins rather than any single one, with the SAS and China Eastern deals joining other recent commitments in the order book. It is worth being precise about what that order book represents, though: list-price valuations rarely survive contact with negotiated discounts, and none of the delivery slots for SAS's 18 jets have even been scheduled yet. An order is a promise to buy, not a sale booked in revenue.

What comes next

The more interesting test will be execution rather than announcement. Airbus's near-term challenge is less about winning orders — its backlog is already among the largest in its history — than about building and delivering aircraft on schedule, a persistent bottleneck across the industry given supply-chain strain on engines and fuselage components. For SAS, the next marker to watch is whether Airbus and the airline actually publish delivery dates for the 18 A330-900s, which would convert Thursday's symbolic signing into a concrete production commitment. For China Eastern, the test is simpler: whether Chinese travel demand keeps justifying a fleet that, by 2033, will look very different from the one flying today.
