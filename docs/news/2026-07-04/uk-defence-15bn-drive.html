Investors eye Labour's £15bn defence spending drive

A Challenger 2 tank on patrol near Basra, Iraq — the vehicle class Britain's new Defence Investment Plan is funding a successor to. — Graeme Main / UK Ministry of Defence, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Labour's £15bn defence spending pledge sent BAE Systems up 6% and QinetiQ trading up 71% on IG, but most procurement contracts are still unawarded.

Sir Keir Starmer stood in front of a hangar full of hardware on 30 June and did something governments rarely do convincingly: he put a number on rearmament. An extra £15 billion for defence, layered onto a near-£300 billion four-year settlement, pushing total military spending toward £80 billion a year — roughly 2.7% of GDP — by 2029. For an industry that has spent a decade lobbying for exactly this kind of multi-year commitment, it landed as confirmation rather than surprise.

The market's answer came within hours. BAE Systems shares rose more than 6% on the day, touching 2,006p and adding upwards of £3 billion to its market value across two trading sessions on volume above four million shares. Rolls-Royce, which builds the reactors for the Royal Navy's submarines as well as military jet engines, moved up alongside it. The FTSE 350 Aerospace & Defence index gained 2.5% that Tuesday, more than three times the broader FTSE 100's advance.

Where the £15bn actually goes

The plan itemises its spending more than most defence announcements bother to. £8.6 billion is earmarked for the Global Combat Air Programme, the Tempest fighter jet being developed with Italy and Japan. £63 billion covers the nuclear deterrent and submarine programme over the plan's life. Munitions get £11 billion, autonomous naval and drone systems more than £5 billion, and a further £220 million funds Project NYX — armed drones designed to fly alongside Apache attack helicopters. Armoured vehicles are a comparatively modest line item: just over £500 million for a Land Rover successor and a new 6x6 troop carrier, replacing kit like the Challenger-era vehicles pictured above.

That level of detail is unusual, and it explains why the primes — BAE, Rolls-Royce — moved so cleanly. Their exposure to submarines, jets and munitions maps directly onto the biggest budget lines. It is a different story further down the supply chain, where contracts for the smaller items have not yet been awarded and won't be for months.

What retail money is actually chasing

Brokers reported a rush of ordinary trading activity alongside the institutional move. Interactive Investor logged a 56% jump in daily defence stock buy orders; IG saw trading in BAE Systems and QinetiQ climb 34% and 71% respectively. QinetiQ is a useful caution here — the stock has swung wildly this year, having fallen more than 20% earlier in 2026 on guidance flagging contract delays in the UK and US, before the announcement pulled buyers back in. Smaller, more speculative names such as the US-listed drone maker Red Cat have also seen a pickup in retail interest, on the logic that if the government is buying autonomous systems, someone has to build them.

The caution analysts keep repeating is about price rather than policy. BAE now trades around 27 times earnings, against roughly 12 times four years ago — a re-rating that has already priced in several years of order growth. Babcock, after last year's outsized rally, has lagged the sector in 2026, a reminder that even confirmed budget increases don't lift every defence name equally or on the same timetable.

The policy signal is now about as clear as these things get. What is not yet settled is which second- and third-tier suppliers land the actual contracts under Project NYX, the 6x6 vehicle programme and the wider drone build-out — decisions expected to filter through procurement announcements over the coming year, and the point at which this story stops being about sector sentiment and starts being about specific balance sheets.
