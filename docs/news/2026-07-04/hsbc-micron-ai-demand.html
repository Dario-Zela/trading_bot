HSBC stays bullish on AI demand despite Micron's mixed print

A Micron Technology campus; the memory maker's fiscal Q3 revenue jumped 346% year over year on AI-driven demand. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC raised its Micron target to $1,700 on record AI-driven earnings, even as a price-fixing lawsuit and insider selling knocked the stock off its highs.

Micron Technology just delivered one of the biggest earnings beats in its history, and its share price has spent the two weeks since trying to figure out what to do with that information. HSBC, for its part, has made up its mind. The bank's semiconductor analyst, Ricky Seo, raised his price target on the stock for the fifth time this year — to $1,700 from $1,100 — arguing that the earnings prove artificial-intelligence demand for memory chips is still running well ahead of what the industry can supply.

"Booming general server demand is driving a stronger and longer memory cycle," Seo wrote, in a note that has followed the same template each time he has lifted his number this year: demand for memory is outrunning supply, and prices are holding as a result.

The question the note doesn't quite answer is why, if that demand is so durable, Micron's stock has spent the past week falling.

A blowout quarter, an uneasy market

On the numbers alone, Micron's fiscal third quarter was close to unimpeachable. Revenue came in at $41.46 billion against Wall Street's roughly $35.7 billion estimate, and adjusted earnings of about $25 a share cleared consensus near $20.50. Data-centre revenue alone topped $25 billion for the quarter — an annualised run rate north of $100 billion — while gross margin jumped to 84.9%, up from 74.9% the prior quarter and just 39% a year earlier. Guidance for the current quarter came in near $50 billion, versus roughly $43.6 billion expected, and the company said it had formalised around $100 billion in multi-year, take-or-pay supply contracts. Chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra said there was "no line of sight" to supply catching up with demand before 2028.

The stock responded exactly as that would suggest, at first — up 15% after hours and 17% the next morning to an all-time high of $1,255. Then it gave almost all of it back. By July 2, shares had fallen as low as roughly $1,007, a single-day drop of nearly 7%, pulled down by three things that had nothing to do with the memory cycle Seo is betting on: a class-action lawsuit filed in late June accusing Micron, Samsung and SK Hynix of conspiring to limit chip production and fix prices; more than $45 million of stock sold by Mehrotra himself, which investors read as a signal of executive caution near the top; and a broader selloff across Asian memory makers, compounded by news that SK Hynix is weighing a US listing and that Apple may source memory from a Chinese supplier for devices sold in China. A separate wave of selling followed reports that Meta is preparing to sell AI computing capacity to third parties — taken by some investors as evidence that even hyperscalers now have compute to spare. Micron's relative-strength index had touched 90 in June, a level unseen since 1995, before any of this. Even after the pullback, the stock remains up well over 200% for the year.

The case HSBC is still making

None of that changes HSBC's underlying model, and it's worth being precise about what that model rests on. Seo has framed the current upswing as a four- to five-year memory upcycle, longer than the two-to-three-year cycles that have historically defined the industry, on the logic that each new generation of AI accelerator needs several times more memory than the last. Micron's own disclosures back part of that: HBM3E and HBM4 capacity is booked through calendar 2027, with orders already stretching into 2028, and the company projects the total addressable market for high-bandwidth memory growing at roughly 40% a year through 2028, from about $35 billion last year to around $100 billion. HSBC initiated coverage on Micron in April with a Buy rating and language that has aged well commercially even if the stock hasn't cooperated lately, describing AI-driven memory demand as "unlike anything the semiconductor industry has experienced before." The bank's broader macro team has made a similar case beyond Micron, listing an "AI rally far from peak" among its watch items for the second half of the year even as it flagged other pain trades for investors to worry about.

Conviction versus the tape

What the past two weeks illustrate is less a contradiction than a gap — between an operating story that is, by the numbers, extraordinary, and a stock price that also has to absorb legal risk, insider signalling and competitor read-throughs that have nothing to do with server demand. A price-fixing lawsuit doesn't disprove that AI workloads need more memory; it does raise the cost of being wrong about supply discipline in an industry with exactly three major producers. Insider selling by a chief executive doesn't refute a four-year upcycle thesis; it does complicate the argument that there's no valuation ceiling in sight. And a sector-wide reaction to Meta's compute-reselling plans speaks to a separate anxiety entirely — whether hyperscalers themselves are running ahead of their own demand.

Analyst upgrades that follow a strong print are the least surprising event in markets; five upward revisions in seven months from the same analyst is a trend line, not a fresh data point. The more useful signal sits in Micron's own supply disclosures — bookings through 2027 and 2028, and a management team saying it sees no near-term fix for the shortage. Those numbers will be tested again when Micron next reports, and sooner than that in how SK Hynix and Samsung price their own memory contracts through the rest of the year. Until then, the gap between what the balance sheet says and what the share price is doing is the more interesting story than the target price itself.
