Analysts cut Oatly targets again on growth concerns

Oatly's Barista Edition oat milk, the product line at the center of the company's North America volume recovery. — Ajuliette102 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Analysts trimmed Oatly's price targets again on growth and margin doubts, but even after the cuts, every major target still implies large upside from the stock's roughly $9.50 price.

Oatly Group's stock has spent the past year sliding from pandemic-era froth toward penny-stock territory, and this week's round of analyst recalibration is less a single event than another data point in that slide. Simply Wall St trimmed its fair-value model on the oat-milk maker to US$17.75 from US$20.33 — a 12.7% cut — while Morgan Stanley and Barclays both nudged their own price targets lower. None of it moved the share price much, because the stock was already trading near $9.50, roughly half of even the reduced targets.

"In one sentence: Analysts keep trimming Oatly's price targets on growth and margin doubts, but even after the cuts, every major target still sits well above the stock's roughly $9.50 price."

The math behind the cut

Simply Wall St's discounted-cash-flow revision is instructive because the growth assumption itself barely moved — revenue growth actually ticked up slightly, from 4.19% to 4.80%, in the updated model. What did the damage was the discount rate, which rose from 6.56% to 7.78%, alongside a small dip in projected net margin, from 6.21% to 6.03%. In plain terms: the model isn't saying Oatly will grow much slower. It's saying the cash flows it eventually produces are worth less today, because the market now demands a higher return for the risk of waiting.

That risk premium tracks with what Wall Street's sell-side has been doing independently. Morgan Stanley cut its target to $12.50 from $14.50 on April 30, keeping an Equalweight rating — notably, not because Oatly's quarter was weak. The company had just beaten on revenue by 6.4% and topped gross-margin expectations by 188 basis points. Morgan Stanley's own note pointed instead to compressed multiples across small- and mid-cap consumer peers, rebasing its valuation to 0.7 times sales. Barclays, meanwhile, cut its target to $14 from $15 while keeping an Overweight rating — a smaller trim, and one that still implies the stock roughly needs to hold, not fall further.

What the numbers actually show

Oatly's first-quarter results, reported April 29, gave both bulls and bears something to point to. North America revenue rose 3.8%, and the company flagged its first positive volume growth in that region since the fourth quarter of 2024 — a real signal after several years of retreating shelf space. Greater China told the opposite story: revenue there fell 6.4% on a constant-currency basis as out-of-home competition intensified, even though retail sales in the segment doubled year-on-year. Company-wide, Oatly guided to the low end of its $25 million-to-$35 million adjusted EBITDA range for the year, citing Middle East-related cost pressures, and still isn't expected to reach profitability in 2026. Full-year 2025 revenue came in at $862.5 million against a net loss of $152.8 million.

The gap that matters more than the cut

What the target trims obscure is a wider disconnect: three separate valuation exercises — a DCF model, an Equalweight sell-side call, an Overweight sell-side call — all still land far above where the stock actually trades. Oatly shares closed near $9.50 against a 52-week range of $8.01 to $18.84, meaning even Morgan Stanley's more skeptical $12.50 implies roughly 30% upside, and the Simply Wall St fair value implies closer to 85%. Analyst consensus, per S&P Global's six-analyst panel, still carries a Buy rating.

That gap is the more useful read than the headline cuts themselves. It suggests the market is pricing in more execution risk — persistent China losses, a debt load Morgan Stanley flagged as a constraint, and a profitability timeline that keeps slipping — than the models are willing to underwrite. Oatly is not unique in this: consumer-growth names that rode pandemic-era demand for alternative proteins, direct-to-consumer subscriptions and similar categories have spent 2025 and 2026 being re-rated toward more conventional multiples, and Oatly's oat-milk category, once framed as a structural shift in dairy consumption, is now being priced more like ordinary packaged food.

What's next

Oatly reports second-quarter results on July 22, which will show whether North America's return to volume growth extends into a second quarter and whether China's retail gains can offset out-of-home losses. Until then, the target cuts read as routine model maintenance rather than a signal of fresh trouble — the more consequential question is whether the July print starts closing the gap between what analysts say Oatly is worth and what the market is currently paying for it.
