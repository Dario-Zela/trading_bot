UK construction stays in contraction as weak miners weigh on a muted market

A UK housing construction site — housebuilding was the weakest sub-sector in June's PMI data. — Acabashi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UK construction stayed deep in contraction in June, though the decline eased slightly from May's six-year low, offering a tentative bottoming signal.

The bad news for Britain's builders was that June was still a contraction. The mildly encouraging news was that it was a smaller one than May's.

The S&P Global/CIPS UK Construction PMI edged up to 38.4 in June from 38.2 in May — the latter a six-year low — leaving the index deep below the 50 mark that separates growth from decline. It has now sat below that line for months, and Monday's release did little to change the broader picture: a cyclical sector still shrinking, just marginally less quickly than before.

In one sentence: UK construction stayed deep in contraction in June, though the decline eased slightly from May's six-year low, offering a tentative sign the downturn may be bottoming.

Where the pain is concentrated

The headline figure masks a split. Commercial construction was the one bright spot, its downturn softening compared with May. Housebuilding was not so lucky: activity fell more steeply than the month before, hitting a fresh low for the year, as fewer new projects broke ground and buyers stayed cautious. Civil engineering fared worse still, recording its weakest reading since the earliest days of the pandemic.

Tim Moore, economics director at S&P Global Market Intelligence, noted that new work "decreased to the least marked extent since March, despite widespread reports of challenging market conditions" — a sign that order books, while still shrinking, are shrinking more slowly. Employment fell for an 18th straight month, and firms cut back further on subcontractors, even as softer demand eased some of the supply-chain strain that had pushed up costs earlier in the year. Raw material, wage and transport costs still rose.

There was a genuine upturn in mood, at least on paper: 38% of firms surveyed expect activity to increase over the coming year, against 19% expecting a further decline — the most optimistic reading since March, and a reversal of May's six-month low in sentiment. Kiran Raichura of Capital Economics summed up the case for cautious relief: "The construction sector appears to have stabilised in June as, after three straight months of falls, the headline construction PMI rose marginally."

A muted market takes note, barely

The data landed on a session that had little appetite for good news of any size. The FTSE 100 closed down 27.26 points, or 0.3%, at 10,651.77, as weak gold and precious-metals miners — Fresnillo off 2.4%, Endeavour Mining down 2.5% on a softer bullion price — dragged alongside a 2.5% fall in AstraZeneca. The FTSE 250 slipped 0.2% to 23,504.22. Construction's soft figures added to the drag rather than driving it; London's real spark came from easyJet, up sharply after its board signalled support for an improved takeover approach from Castlelake valuing the airline above £5 billion — a reminder that even a listless day can carry one loud exception.

What it means, cautiously

Construction is one of the more reliable early tells on where an economy is headed, since it reacts fast to financing costs and business confidence. A PMI that stops falling — without yet rising above 50 — is consistent with a downturn losing momentum rather than one reversing. That distinction matters for anyone pricing UK growth, sterling or rate expectations: stabilisation is not the same as recovery, and housebuilding's fresh low argues against reading June as an all-clear.

The next test arrives with July's construction PMI, due in early August, alongside Bank of England commentary on whether a bottoming cyclical sector changes the calculus on rate cuts.
