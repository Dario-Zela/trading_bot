UBS to pilot US banking services ahead of wealth-client rollout

UBS's historic headquarters on Bahnhofstrasse in Zürich, Switzerland. — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UBS plans to pilot everyday US banking with its own staff from December, ahead of a 2027 rollout to wealth clients holding $2m–$10m in assets.

UBS Group is preparing to test everyday banking — checking accounts, savings products, the basic plumbing of personal finance — on its own staff before offering it to the wealthy clients the Swiss bank actually wants to keep.

According to reporting cited by the Financial Times, UBS could begin opening accounts for US employees as early as December, using the internal rollout to work out pricing and product design ahead of a wider release to wealth management clients from the middle of 2027. It is a small, deliberate step, but it points at a larger shift: a bank built around advising the ultra-rich is now trying to hold onto their checking accounts too.

The charter behind the plan

The pilot follows a regulatory milestone reached in March, when the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency approved UBS's application to convert UBS Bank USA into a nationally chartered institution. That charter is what makes the expansion possible: it lets UBS offer checking and savings accounts, mortgages and other lending products alongside the investment advice that has long been its core US business.

UBS is not chasing mass-market retail banking. The push targets clients holding between $2 million and $10 million in investable assets — a tier just below the ultra-high-net-worth households UBS traditionally serves, but large enough in aggregate to matter. The bank estimates it already serves around 700,000 US households, and reckons roughly $150 billion of those clients' deposits currently sit at rival banks, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley among them. That figure is the real subject of this story: money UBS already has a relationship with, parked somewhere else.

Why banking, why now

The logic UBS executives have offered, per the FT reporting, is retention rather than expansion for its own sake. A wealth client whose mortgage, checking account and payroll all sit with a competitor is a client with more reasons to eventually move the whole relationship. Bundling everyday banking into the UBS platform is meant to raise the switching cost — and to capture deposit and lending revenue UBS is currently leaving on the table.

That rationale is plausible, but it is also modest. Nothing in the reporting so far describes a retail banking build-out aimed at the broader public, branch networks, or a challenge to the mass-market franchises of Bank of America or Wells Fargo. This looks like a wealth-management moat-widening exercise wearing a banking-license costume, not a strategic pivot away from UBS's core identity as an advisor to the rich.

What's still unknown

The employee pilot's actual size, the products it will test beyond checking and savings, and the pricing UBS is weighing have not been disclosed. Nor is it clear how aggressively UBS will market the eventual client-facing product once it launches in 2027, or whether it will undercut incumbent banks on fees to win deposits. The timeline also sits alongside a leadership transition: chief executive Sergio Ermotti is reportedly expected to step down in April 2027, roughly when the wealth-client rollout would be getting underway — a detail that adds some uncertainty about how firmly the strategy is locked in versus how much latitude a successor might have to adjust it.

The next concrete marker is the December start date for employee account openings, assuming it holds. Confirmation of pricing, product scope, or an accelerated timeline would tell more about UBS's ambition here than the charter approval itself did.
