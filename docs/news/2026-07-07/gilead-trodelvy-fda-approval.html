Gilead's Trodelvy wins FDA nod for first-line breast cancer use

In one sentence
FDA approved Trodelvy for first-line metastatic triple-negative breast cancer, but Gilead now must win share from a rival ADC that got there first and an entrenched Keytruda regimen.

Gilead Sciences has spent the better part of a decade trying to turn Trodelvy, an antibody-drug conjugate it paid $21 billion to acquire in 2020, into the kind of oncology franchise that can eventually rival its HIV business. On June 24, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration gave that effort its biggest lift yet, clearing the drug for first-line use in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) — moving it from a treatment of last resort to one doctors can reach for immediately after diagnosis.

The distinction matters more than it might sound. Later-line approvals — the kind Trodelvy has held since 2020 — only reach patients who have already failed one or more prior regimens, and in an aggressive cancer like TNBC, many never get that far. "For more than twenty years, patients with mTNBC had limited choices in first-line treatment," said Dietmar Berger, Gilead's chief medical officer, in the company's announcement. A first-line label puts the drug in front of nearly every eligible patient at the moment of diagnosis, not just the fraction who survive long enough to need a second option.

What the trials showed

The approval rests on two Phase 3 studies. In ASCENT-03, Trodelvy given alone cut the risk of disease progression or death by 38% compared with standard chemotherapy in patients who are not candidates for immunotherapy, with a median duration of response of 12.2 months versus 7.2 months. In ASCENT-04 — run jointly with Merck's Keytruda (pembrolizumab) — the combination cut that risk by 35% against Keytruda-plus-chemotherapy in patients whose tumors express high levels of PD-L1, extending median response duration to 16.5 months from 9.2.

That second arm is the more commercially important one in the near term, since PD-L1-positive tumors already had Keytruda-based chemotherapy as an entrenched standard of care dating to 2020. Sara Tolaney, an oncologist at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute who worked on the trials, called the approval "a practice-changing first-line treatment option for all patients across PD-L1 status" — a claim the FDA's dual clearance, spanning both PD-L1-positive and PD-L1-ineligible patients, is structured to support.

A narrow market, tightly contested

Triple-negative breast cancer accounts for roughly 15% of all breast cancer diagnoses and disproportionately strikes younger, premenopausal, and Black and Hispanic women. The metastatic first-line population Gilead is now targeting is small in absolute terms — on the order of 10,000 to 15,000 new U.S. cases a year — which caps how much any single approval can move Gilead's top line. Trodelvy already generated $402 million in the first quarter of 2026, up 37% year over year, largely from its existing later-line indications; the first-line label adds addressable patients but not a new market from scratch.

The more immediate complication is timing. AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo's rival TROP2-directed conjugate, Datroway (datopotamab deruxtecan), won its own first-line approval in the PD-L1-ineligible mTNBC population on May 22 — a full month before Trodelvy. Both drugs work by the same mechanism, attaching a chemotherapy payload to an antibody that targets the TROP2 protein on tumor cells, and both are now competing for the same monotherapy niche. Analysts have already begun framing the rivalry as an echo of the T-DXd-versus-T-DM1 contest that reshaped HER2-positive breast cancer treatment — a category where being second to market with a similar efficacy profile did not stop a drug from winning share, but did mean fighting for it rather than owning it.

Merck, for its part, is not simply content to be Gilead's combination partner — it has its own TROP2-directed conjugate, sac-TMT, in 17 separate Phase 3 studies across tumor types, a pipeline that could eventually put it in direct competition with the drug it currently pairs with in ASCENT-04.

What the market is pricing in

Gilead shares closed at $127.86 on June 26, up 3.25% on the week and a fresh 12-month high, lifting the company's market capitalization to roughly $158.8 billion. That is a modest, sentiment-driven bump rather than a repricing of the oncology business — Gilead still carries a Zacks Rank #3 (Hold), and at close to 29 times forward earnings it already trades above its industry average, meaning the stock was not exactly cheap heading into the news.

The approval itself does not resolve the open question for Trodelvy: whether it can take meaningful share from a market that now has three well-funded first-line options — Trodelvy, Datroway, and Keytruda-based chemotherapy — chasing a patient pool that isn't growing. The next signal will be prescribing data. Gilead reports second-quarter earnings in late July, and the figure to watch is not the approval headline but whether first-line Trodelvy volumes show up meaningfully in that quarter's oncology revenue line, or whether physicians default to whichever TROP2 conjugate reached their formulary first.
