Barclays pulls $960m leveraged loan on weak demand

One Churchill Place, Barclays' global headquarters in Canary Wharf, London. — Acabashi / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Barclays pulled a $960M leveraged loan for Sound Inpatient Physicians after investors balked even at a 5-point spread, a small but telling sign credit appetite is cooling.

Barclays has withdrawn a $960 million leveraged loan it was arranging for Sound Inpatient Physicians, one of the few syndicated loan deals to be pulled from the US market this year. The hospital staffing firm's debt sale failed to clear the bar with investors ahead of a June 30 deadline — even after Barclays dangled a margin of 5 percentage points over the benchmark rate, a rich coupon by recent standards.

In one sentence: Barclays pulled a $960 million leveraged loan for Sound Inpatient Physicians after investors balked even at a 5-point spread, a small but telling sign that credit appetite is cooling.

The loan was meant to refinance debt taken on as part of a 2024 restructuring, when Sound Physicians — the hospital staffing group — struck a deal with a group of private lenders that handed it $124 million in fresh capital, a discount on its existing obligations, and maturities pushed out to 2028. This latest attempt to term out that debt through the broadly syndicated loan market was supposed to be the easy part. Instead, it stalled.

A pulled deal, not a dead one

Barclays isn't abandoning the financing outright. Bankers are expected to revise the terms — likely a wider spread, tighter covenants, or both — and bring it back to the market at a later date. That is the standard playbook when a syndication misfires: rather than force a deal through at a price that alienates the arranging bank's balance sheet, lenders regroup and resell it once the terms better reflect what investors are actually willing to pay for the risk.

Still, the fact that a deal needed pulling at all is the more interesting data point. Leveraged loan withdrawals are rare in a market that, for most of the past two years, has absorbed nearly anything issuers brought to it. Deals rarely fail outright; they get repriced on the way in, or occasionally flexed wider mid-syndication. Pulling one from the market and taking it back to the drawing board is a sharper signal.

The broader credit picture

The Sound Physicians stumble fits a pattern that has been building through the first half of the year. Leveraged loan issuance is running roughly a third behind last year's pace, according to market data reviewed by Bloomberg and PitchBook, with refinancing volume down more than 40% year-over-year. Retail fund flows into loan funds hit a three-year low earlier in the year, and market participants surveyed by credit research desks now expect new-issue spreads to widen a further 25 to 50 basis points over the next six months, particularly for borrowers in weaker sectors or with thinner credit profiles.

Hospital staffing is not an obviously fashionable sector for lenders right now. Firms in the space carry reimbursement risk tied to Medicare and private-payer rates, and Sound Physicians' own 2024 restructuring — a debt-for-liquidity swap with its lender group — is the kind of history that makes syndicate desks read the fine print twice.

None of this amounts to a credit crunch. One pulled deal is one pulled deal. But leveraged loan syndication is often an early tell for where risk appetite is heading before it shows up in equity markets or high-yield spreads, because loan investors — CLOs, retail funds, separately managed accounts — are pricing default and recovery risk directly, deal by deal, with no benchmark index to hide behind.

The next test comes whenever Barclays relaunches the Sound Physicians paper. The terms it settles on — wider spread, tighter call protection, more covenants — will say more about where leveraged credit stands than the original pull did. Watch also whether other arrangers facing similarly soft books choose to flex terms rather than withdraw; a string of pulled deals, rather than one, is what would turn this from a data point into a trend.
