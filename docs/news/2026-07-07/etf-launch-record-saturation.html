ETF issuers flood the market: record 214 launches in June, Corgi adds two dozen more

Record ETF issuance in June concentrated in leveraged, thematic and buffer strategies — products built for a trade, not necessarily to last. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ETF industry launched a record 214 new funds in June, led by leveraged, thematic and active strategies — and many are built to catch a trade, not to last.

The exchange-traded fund industry launched 214 new products in June, the most in any single month on record, according to data cited across ETF industry trackers. It is the latest marker in what has become a structural shift in how issuers compete: rather than a handful of broad-market funds fighting for assets, the market is now flooded with narrow, often leveraged, bets designed to catch a specific trade rather than build a durable franchise.

In one sentence: The ETF industry launched a record 214 new funds in June, led by leveraged, thematic and active strategies — and many of them are built to catch a trade, not to last.

No single issuer illustrates the pattern better than Corgi, a Denver-based, AI-driven fintech founded in 2025. On July 2, the firm listed 24 more ETFs on Cboe's BZX exchange — 15 leveraged 2x-daily funds, mostly tracking individual stocks, plus nine structured buffer funds in its new "July Series." That capped a six-week run in which Corgi alone brought roughly 119 new funds to market, including a single-day launch of 35 ETFs in May that was itself a record at the time.

A record built on niches, not breadth

The 214 June launches were not evenly spread across the fund universe. Bloomberg Intelligence analysts Eric Balchunas and Andre Yapp, who track the space closely, have described the pace of 2026 issuance as historic even by an industry accustomed to breaking its own records every year since 2021. The month's debuts leaned heavily on three categories: leveraged and inverse products, thematic bets tied to narrow trends — AI infrastructure, memory-chip supply chains, even a fund tracking companies supporting the autism community — and actively managed strategies that let smaller issuers differentiate on process rather than price.

The common thread is that none of these categories require broad market appeal to justify a launch. A leveraged single-stock ETF needs only a slice of day-trader flow to generate meaningful fee revenue relative to its size; a thematic fund needs only a headline cycle. What they generally do not have going for them is durability. Industry data compiled by Morningstar and cited across trade publications shows that in 2023 alone, 244 ETFs closed with an average lifespan of 5.4 years and average assets of just $54 million — well below the roughly $50 million threshold that more than 85% of closures since 2021 never cleared.

That threshold is getting harder to reach, not easier. Bloomberg Intelligence data shows the average age of an ETF liquidated in 2026 has fallen to about one year and nine months, down from three years and six months in 2025 and four years and eight months in 2024. More than 40 ETFs were liquidated in just the first two months of this year, versus 33 in the same period of 2025. Issuers, in other words, are giving new funds less time to prove themselves before pulling the plug — a discipline that cuts both ways: it protects issuers from carrying dead weight, but it also means an increasing share of June's 214 launches are effectively on a clock.

Corgi's bet on volume over selectivity

Corgi's approach is the purest expression of the volume strategy. Rather than launching a handful of funds designed to become flagship products, the firm has built a pipeline that can list dozens of narrowly targeted ETFs in a single week. Its 15 new leveraged funds each carry a 0.45% expense ratio — pitched, per the company, as the lowest net expense ratio of any U.S.-listed 2x-daily long ETF tracking the same underlying stock. The nine buffer funds, built using FLEX options to cap gains while cushioning a defined slice of downside over a July 2026–June 2027 outcome period, carry a 0.40% gross and 0.30% net expense ratio, extending Corgi's buffer lineup to 27 funds across three monthly series.

"We believe leveraged ETFs have been expensive for far too long — we're re-rating the entire cost structure with our batch of leveraged products," said Nicolas Laqua, Corgi's chief executive, in a statement tied to an earlier wave of single-stock launches in late June. The company's existing funds give some sense of scale: its Founder-Led ETF (FDRS) holds roughly $80 million in assets after listing on Nasdaq in December, while its leveraged sibling FDRX has gathered about $18 million. Neither figure is small for a young issuer, but neither is the kind of asset base that supports dozens of additional launches indefinitely — particularly once Corgi's low-cost pricing invites imitation.

That is the tension sitting underneath the record numbers. Corgi is competing on price and speed, betting that being first and cheapest into a niche — a single stock's 2x-daily exposure, a specific buffer window — earns enough flow to matter before a rival undercuts it. It is a viable strategy for an AI-driven operation that can manufacture funds cheaply. It is a much harder one to sustain if regulators, competitors, or simply investor fatigue catch up before the assets do.

What the flood signals

Taken together, the June numbers and Corgi's launch pace point to the same underlying dynamic: issuers are no longer trying to win the ETF market with a few large, broad products. They are trying to win it by being everywhere a retail trader might look — every single stock with a following, every buffer window, every thematic headline — and accepting that most of those bets will not survive. For algorithmic and systematic strategies that trade around these products, the effect is concrete: liquidity keeps splitting across an expanding shelf of near-identical, thinly traded wrappers, which widens spreads and raises slippage risk in exactly the leveraged and thematic corners where crowded, fast-money trades already carry the most execution risk.

The next data points worth watching are the closure figures rather than the launch figures. Bloomberg Intelligence's liquidation tracker for the third quarter will show whether 2026's shrinking fund lifespans are catching up with June's record cohort, and whether Corgi's low-cost, high-volume model can gather enough assets across two dozen niches to avoid becoming part of that count itself.
