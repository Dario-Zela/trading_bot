Fed's Waller and prospective chair Warsh talk tough on inflation as small-cap borrowing costs bite

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher J. Waller, whose Rome remarks tied rate policy to Fed credibility rather than fiscal relief. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Waller says the Fed won't cut rates to ease deficit financing or touch its 2% target for credibility reasons, even as small-cap borrowing costs hit six-year highs under Warsh's hawkish start.

Christopher Waller has spent five years as the Federal Reserve's most reliable hawk-when-it-matters voice, and on Monday, at a Bank of Italy conference in Rome, he used that credibility for a blunt message aimed as much at Washington as at markets: the Fed is not going to hold rates down to make the government's debt cheaper to service, and it is not going to relitigate its inflation target just because doing so might be more sensible.

"Monetary policy has not and will not be conducted for these purposes," Waller said, rejecting the idea — increasingly popular in some corners of the fiscal debate — that a central bank sitting on trillions in Treasury holdings has an incentive to keep its foot off the brake. The remark lands at a specific moment: interest costs on federal debt have become one of Washington's fastest-growing budget lines, and the temptation to lean on the Fed to ease that burden has not gone unnoticed by economists watching the deficit math.

A target he'd redesign, but won't

Waller's most revealing comment, though, was less about deficits than about the Fed's own architecture. Asked about the 2% inflation target itself, he said he would prefer it be framed as a range rather than a single fixed point — a design several other central banks already use, precisely because it gives policymakers room to describe uncertainty honestly rather than defending a number to the second decimal place. But he added that changing the target now would not be credible, effectively closing the door on his own preference. The distinction matters: it tells markets the target isn't fixed because 2% is optimal, but because moving off it now, mid-inflation-fight, would look like retreating under pressure rather than refining a framework.

That credibility argument was reinforced days after Kevin Warsh took the Fed's gavel. Warsh was confirmed by the Senate 54-45 — the most divisive vote in the Fed's history — and sworn in on May 22, succeeding Jerome Powell. Asked whether Warsh needed to "recommit" to the 2% goal, Waller said he had never been anything but committed to it, brushing aside any suggestion that a change in chair might mean a change in mandate. The message, delivered by an FOMC member with no chairmanship at stake, reads as an attempt to insulate the target from the politics swirling around Warsh's arrival — Warsh himself was confirmed over Trump administration objections that the Fed's rates were too high, and has since taken a more hawkish tone than his critics expected.

Inflation risk "flipped around"

Underneath the institutional defense sits a genuine shift in Waller's own reading of the economy. A year ago he was among the more dovish voices, favoring cuts as the labor market cooled. Now, he says, "risks have flipped around": the labor market seems to have stabilized, while inflation "has been taking off" — a turn that, in his telling, changes how the Fed has to think about policy from here. That reversal is why the deficit-financing and target-range comments carry weight rather than reading as boilerplate independence talk: a governor who thought inflation was beaten would have far less reason to draw hard lines around the 2% goal. Waller's shift also came with a process argument — if the Fed's "reaction function," its rule for how it responds to incoming data, isn't well understood by markets, he said policymakers need to talk about it more, though he stopped short of saying where the gap is or what specifically needs fixing.

Where the higher-for-longer stance actually bites

The practical cost of that stance is showing up furthest from the FOMC's marble hearing room, in the balance sheets of small, leveraged companies. Interest expense now consumes roughly 31% of EBITDA for Russell 2000 constituents — more than double where it stood in 2020 — against just 6.7% for S&P 500 companies, itself down from 9.5% six years ago. Roughly 30% of small-cap debt floats with short-term rates, compared with about 7% for the large-cap index, and close to 40% of Russell 2000 members are still unprofitable. That combination — floating-rate debt, thin or negative earnings, and a Fed chair whose early tenure has pushed markets toward pricing a higher-for-longer path rather than the cuts once expected under Powell — is precisely why small-cap borrowing costs are sitting at six-year highs even as large caps barely feel the squeeze.

The through-line across Waller's remarks is that credibility, not fiscal convenience or Wall Street's preference for cheaper leverage, is what actually constrains the Fed's decisions. He would design the target differently if starting from scratch; he won't touch it now, because touching it now would cost more than it fixes. That leaves Warsh — barely six weeks into the job, confirmed by the narrowest Senate margin on record and already reading as more hawkish than the administration that nominated critics expected — with little room to lean on rates to relieve either the government's financing costs or small-cap balance sheets. The next test of that stance comes July 14, when June consumer price data lands, the last major inflation read before the FOMC's July 28-29 meeting decides whether "higher for longer" gets another confirmation.
