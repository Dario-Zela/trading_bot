C3.ai and Shell expand predictive maintenance partnership

Shell's Pernis refinery in the Netherlands, one of the global sites where C3.ai's predictive maintenance software monitors equipment. — Steven Lek / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
C3.ai and Shell extended their eight-year predictive maintenance deal to include AI-driven root-cause fixes, but disclosed no numbers — leaving the stock's 70% slide unanswered.

C3.ai and Shell have been working together since 2018, quietly wiring up sensors and software across the oil giant's refineries and plants to catch equipment failures before they happen. On June 4, the two companies said they're extending that arrangement — again — this time pushing the technology from spotting problems into actually diagnosing and helping fix them.

The existing system already watches more than 13,000 pieces of equipment across Shell's global operations, flagging anomalies like a pump vibrating oddly or a valve running hot before it fails outright. The new phase adds what C3.ai calls agentic AI — software agents that don't just flag the anomaly but attempt root-cause analysis and suggest remediation, built on C3.ai's Reliability and Agentic AI platforms running on Microsoft Azure.

"This expanded partnership with Shell proves what's possible when Enterprise AI is fully operationalized at global scale for predictive maintenance — reducing unplanned downtime and delivering hundreds of millions of dollars in economic value," said Stephen Ehikian, C3.ai's president, in the announcement.

A win the numbers don't back up

That's the kind of line that reads well in a press release and says almost nothing to an investor. The "hundreds of millions" figure describes value delivered over eight years of partnership, not new money attached to this expansion. Neither company disclosed contract value, deployment timeline, or how many additional sites are involved. For a company that needs a catalyst, this isn't one.

And C3.ai needs catalysts. The stock has fallen roughly 70% from its 52-week high of $30.11 last July to around $9 now, after a rough year: a 26% workforce reduction announced in February, a fiscal 2026 loss of nearly $500 million, and founder Thomas Siebel returning to the CEO chair in May after briefly handing the job to Ehikian. Revenue guidance for the year lands between $246.7 million and $250.7 million — down sharply from the prior year, with quarterly declines as steep as 46%. The company's other marquee industrial account, a joint venture with Baker Hughes worth roughly a fifth of revenue, was just extended through 2028, suggesting C3.ai is leaning hard on its handful of anchor relationships to steady the ship.

The bigger squeeze

The backdrop that makes this announcement matter more than it should: C3.ai built its business on being a specialist — enterprise-grade AI for industrial reliability, not a chatbot. That pitch is harder to sell now that Microsoft, OpenAI and others push generalist models into the same enterprise accounts, often bundled cheaply into software Shell already pays for. Keeping Shell inside the C3.ai tent, and pushing it toward pricier agentic features, is a defensive move as much as a growth one.

The next real test isn't this press release — it's C3.ai's first-quarter fiscal 2027 results, expected around September, which will show whether Shell's expanded scope and the Baker Hughes renewal are translating into actual bookings, or just headlines.
