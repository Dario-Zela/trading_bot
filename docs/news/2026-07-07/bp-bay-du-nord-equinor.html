BP sells Bay du Nord stake to Equinor in Canadian portfolio clean-up

In one sentence
BP is selling its roughly 37% non-operated stake in Canada's Bay du Nord offshore oil project to partner Equinor, which becomes sole owner ahead of a 2027 investment decision.

BP is walking away from one of Canada's most closely watched offshore oil prospects, selling its non-operated stake in the Bay du Nord project to Equinor, the Norwegian major that has run the development since discovery. The deal hands Equinor full ownership of a project that could eventually produce up to a billion barrels — and it is the clearest signal yet of how BP now screens its upstream book.

In one sentence: BP is selling its roughly 37% non-operated stake in Canada's Bay du Nord offshore oil project to partner Equinor, which will become sole owner as it works toward a 2027 investment decision.

BP's holding amounted to an average 37.2% working interest spread across ten licenses in the Flemish Pass Basin, about 500 kilometres northeast of St. John's, Newfoundland. Financial terms were not disclosed, and the sale still needs regulatory clearance. BP will keep its full interest in two adjacent exploration licenses, EL 1166 and EL 1170, so this is an exit from Bay du Nord specifically rather than a retreat from the Newfoundland and Labrador shelf altogether.

The logic of non-operated exposure

Gordon Birrell, BP's executive vice president for upstream, framed the sale in the company's now-familiar register: "BP is exercising strict capital discipline, allocating it to the opportunities that create the most value for BP." The phrase does a lot of work. Non-operated stakes carry the capital exposure of ownership without the control that comes from running the project — BP could not set the pace of spending, the technical approach, or the timeline, all of which sat with Equinor. In a portfolio review focused on returns, that combination of risk without control is an easy category to prune, particularly for an asset years away from first oil.

Bay du Nord has already tested patience once. Equinor shelved a final investment decision in 2023 as costs escalated and oil markets wobbled, and the project has since been rescoped: current plans point to a roughly $16 billion development that could unlock as much as a billion barrels through follow-on tie-ins, well above the 300 million barrels once envisioned for the initial phase. Equinor is now aiming for a fresh investment decision in early 2027, and Equinor's Philippe Mathieu said the buy-in of BP's stake "reflects our confidence in the project," adding that Equinor will "seek opportunities to bring in partners as part of the project's further development" — an indication that full ownership is a waypoint, not necessarily the end state.

What it says about Canadian deepwater

Bay du Nord was billed as Canada's first true deepwater development and the fifth field in the province's offshore, a project Newfoundland and Labrador's government has tied to as much as $6.4 billion in royalties and taxes over 25 years under a 2024 benefits agreement, alongside a $200 million commitment from Equinor toward a local fabrication dry dock. That provincial enthusiasm hasn't wavered — Premier Tony Wakeham cast the sale as evidence of confidence in the project rather than a warning sign. But BP's exit is a data point in the other direction: an international major concluding that its capital is better spent on assets it operates directly, even as the project's economics have only gotten larger and later.

The test now falls to Equinor. Its FID, expected in early 2027, will show whether a rescoped, costlier Bay du Nord clears the bar international capital is currently demanding of Canadian offshore projects — and whether the partners Equinor says it wants to bring in decide the risk-adjusted returns look better from outside BP's shoes than they did from inside them.
