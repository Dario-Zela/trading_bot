Oil extends five-day slide as US-Iran signing nears; $300bn private investment fund proposed

Satellite image of the Strait of Hormuz and Musandam Peninsula — through which roughly 20% of global oil exports transit daily. — NASA / MODIS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brent has shed more than $30 from its conflict peak in five straight sessions as traders front-run Iranian supply unlocked by a deal set to be signed Friday at Bürgenstock, Switzerland.

Oil headed for a fifth consecutive daily loss on Tuesday — its longest losing streak in 10 months — as traders priced in an imminent US-Iran accord expected to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and flood markets with Iranian crude. Brent retreated to around $78.57 a barrel, near a three-month low; West Texas Intermediate approached $76. The prompt spread, a measure of near-term supply tightness, collapsed to just 20 cents in backwardation — a number that, a month ago, would have seemed implausible.

The speed of the reversal says something about how aggressively the geopolitical premium had been priced. From the February 28 outbreak of the US-Israeli conflict with Iran, Brent had surged more than 45%, peaking above $113 a barrel. That premium is now being systematically unwound in anticipation of Friday's signing at Bürgenstock — the Swiss lake-district resort, perched above Lake Lucerne, where the US and Iran are due to formalise a memorandum of understanding. Switzerland's foreign ministry confirmed the venue and date on Tuesday, lending the first official European imprimatur to a process that had, until recently, been trading on rumour.

The deal grants Tehran immediate sanctions waivers for oil and fuel sales upon signing, along with restored access to banking, transportation, and insurance services for crude transactions — the direct mechanism behind the market's selloff. Market participants expect gradual transit resumption rather than overnight normalisation; US naval escorts may initially slow vessel movement through the 21-mile-wide chokepoint. But the direction of travel is clear, and traders are not waiting to see the barrels.

A $300 Billion Signal

The deal's most consequential structural detail — reported Monday by Reuters — is the proposed Reconstruction and Development Fund: a $300 billion private investment vehicle that would activate only once a final agreement is signed. The fund carries no government money, grants, or war reparations. It is designed as a commercial vehicle channelling capital into Iran's war-damaged economy across energy, logistics, manufacturing, and transportation. More than half — upward of $150 billion — has already been committed by companies in the United States, Gulf states, Asia, South America, and Africa, according to a source familiar with the negotiations. Tehran had initially sought $400 billion in direct war reparations; the concept evolved into this private structure instead, giving both sides an economic incentive to close and sustain the deal.

Against that backdrop, a sharp inventory surprise provided little counter-pressure. The American Petroleum Institute reported US crude stocks fell 8.33 million barrels in the week ending June 12 — nearly double the 4.5 million barrel analyst estimate, and only modestly below the prior week's 9.12 million barrel draw. The data would ordinarily signal a tightening domestic market. Instead, traders looked past it entirely: the prospect of Iranian export volumes returning to global markets swamped the domestic signal.

The next real test arrives Friday. The Bürgenstock MOU triggers Iran's immediate export rights; follow-on talks toward a comprehensive accord are expected to begin in Switzerland the same weekend. A breakdown in that second round — or a reprise of the dispute over deal terms that erupted earlier this week, when Washington and Tehran offered competing accounts of what had been agreed — could snap the supply premium back into place. For now, the market is pricing Friday's signing as a done deal, and surrendering four months of war risk at $79 a barrel.
