Trump invokes Defense Production Act to address munitions supply chain gaps

A Tomahawk Land Attack Missile launches from USS Arleigh Burke (DDG-51). CSIS estimates the U.S. has expended more than half its Tomahawk inventory during the Iran campaign. — Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Carlos M. Vazquez II / U.S. Navy (Public Domain)
In one sentence
Trump invoked the 1950 Defense Production Act on June 11 to address munitions bottlenecks exacerbated by the Iran war, which has consumed more than half of some critical U.S. stockpiles.

President Donald Trump signed a memorandum on June 11 invoking the Defense Production Act—a Cold War-era emergency statute last used for weapons allocation decades ago—ordering Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to pursue voluntary agreements with private manufacturers to address what the White House describes as systemic failures in the American munitions supply chain. The memo was posted to the Federal Register on June 16.

The language in the document is unambiguous. Trump declared that "conditions exist which may pose a direct threat to the national defense or its preparedness programs," citing "limited production capacity, fragile supply chains, long-lead dependencies, and related production bottlenecks." Three sub-systems stand out as most constrained: solid rocket motors, igniters, and guidance systems — the components found in nearly every precision munition in the U.S. arsenal.

The Iran War's Toll on Stockpiles

The invocation did not emerge from abstract planning concerns. The U.S. military campaign against Iran has consumed munitions at a rate that has alarmed both the Pentagon and lawmakers on Capitol Hill. An April analysis by the Center for Strategic and International Studies found that the U.S. may have expended more than half of its inventory of four critical munitions during operations against Iran — among them the Tomahawk cruise missile, which has been in continuous production since the 1970s but which defence planners had never expected to cycle through at this pace.

The DPA — enacted in 1950 during the Korean War — gives the president broad authority to direct private industry to prioritise government orders, and crucially, it allows companies to enter into collaborative production arrangements that would otherwise risk antitrust scrutiny. Under the June 11 memorandum, Hegseth has the power to negotiate those so-called voluntary agreements with manufacturers, bringing defence contractors into discussions about how to expand capacity across the supply chain.

The political messaging around the invocation has been notably discordant. Hegseth, in testimony to Congress in April, acknowledged that munitions replenishment could take "months to years." Yet he has publicly pushed back against the "crisis" framing, telling reporters that reporting on a shortfall was "a manufactured story that the media wants to peddle." The DPA invocation — a step with few peacetime precedents for conventional munitions — sits awkwardly alongside that position.

Republican Senator John Cornyn put the matter more plainly, saying the Pentagon is "running short of funding they need in order to acquire the weapons" and pledging to back increased defence spending even without Democratic votes. The administration is simultaneously pursuing a record $1.5 trillion defence budget through reconciliation, with Hegseth having pitched lawmakers on $350 billion specifically to replenish depleted stockpiles.

The DPA invocation sets up a near-term test of whether voluntary industry agreements can move fast enough to matter. Assistant Secretary of Defence Michael Cadenazzi noted that the Pentagon has been drawing defence contractors into supply chain conversations since at least September — suggesting the problems predate the Iran conflict and that the June 11 memo is the formal legal escalation of a process already quietly underway. The next concrete signal will be whether those voluntary agreements produce binding production commitments from major contractors in the weeks ahead.
