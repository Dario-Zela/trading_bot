European equities inch higher as US-Iran deal provides modest sentiment lift

The trading floor of the Frankfurter Wertpapierbörse — Germany's Frankfurt Stock Exchange handles around 90% of German equity share trading — Thomas Richter / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
European equities edged higher as the US-Iran peace deal cut geopolitical risk, but an ECB rate hike and 3.2% eurozone inflation capped any meaningful rally.

European stock markets logged modest gains on Tuesday as investors absorbed the implications of the weekend's US-Iran peace deal — a development that removed a significant tail risk from global markets but proved insufficient to drive a broad-based rally while inflation remains stubbornly above central-bank targets.

The Euro STOXX 50 added around 0.3% and the pan-European STOXX 600 edged up a similar margin by the close, with industrials and financials leading the advance. Banking names saw outsized moves — UniCredit gained more than 3%, Intesa Sanpaolo around 2% — alongside industrial bellwethers such as Schneider Electric and Safran. Energy stocks declined: lower oil prices, the very thing the peace deal delivered, compressed sector earnings expectations and weighed on names including TotalEnergies.

The underlying catalyst was clear enough. A preliminary agreement reached at the weekend between Washington and Tehran ended direct hostilities, set the terms for reopening the Strait of Hormuz to shipping, and established a 60-day ceasefire window for nuclear negotiations. Approximately 21 million barrels of oil pass through the strait daily — roughly one-fifth of global petroleum supply — and its effective closure since the conflict escalated had added a persistent energy premium to European costs. With that risk now substantially reduced, markets had reason to celebrate.

Relief tempered by reality

The problem for equity bulls is that much of the good news had already been priced in. European stocks had moved steadily higher in the days preceding the deal's formal signing as diplomatic signals accumulated and oil retreated. Tuesday's gains represent the residual lift, not the initial surge.

The more fundamental constraint did not dissolve with the peace announcement either. Eurozone inflation ran at 3.2% year-on-year in May — the highest reading since September 2023 — with energy costs up 10.9% and services inflation climbing to 3.5%. Core inflation, stripping out food and energy, sits at 2.5%, well above the European Central Bank's 2% medium-term target.

The ECB responded at its June 11 meeting by raising all three key interest rates 25 basis points — the first hike since 2023 — with the new levels taking effect on Tuesday itself. The deposit facility rate now stands at 2.25%. President Christine Lagarde offered no path toward near-term cuts, stressing that the Middle East conflict "is generating inflation pressures" and that the rate decision "is robust across a range of scenarios." The Council's commitment to a meeting-by-meeting approach without predetermined guidance gave bond markets little to run with, and equities felt that ceiling.

The logic is straightforward: a peace deal that steadily lowers energy prices is genuinely constructive for European growth. But falling oil mainly offsets an extraordinary cost shock rather than delivering new stimulus. It does not make monetary policy less restrictive. The ECB's new deposit rate still bites at a time when growth projections have been revised down to just 0.8% for 2026.

What to watch

With a 60-day ceasefire window now running, markets will track whether Iran's nuclear programme dismantlement advances on schedule — the pace of compliance will determine how quickly Hormuz traffic fully normalises and energy prices recalibrate. The ECB's next meeting in late July is the earliest point at which the Council could signal a pause; eurozone June CPI data, due at month-end, will be the first real test of whether the commodity-price retreat is feeding through to the headline numbers that matter to Lagarde's governing council.
