HSBC signs multi-year AI deal with Google Cloud as banks lean on hyperscalers

In one sentence
HSBC's multi-year Google Cloud AI deal—targeting 200+ use cases and $100M+ per project—raises the question of whether banks are trading differentiation for convenience.

HSBC on Wednesday signed a multi-year deal with Google Cloud to build out artificial intelligence capabilities across the bank, marking the most concrete step yet under chief executive Georges Elhedery's push to make AI central to the group's strategy.

The partnership gives HSBC access to Google's Gemini language models and collaboration with Google DeepMind engineers. The bank already runs about 600 applications on Google's infrastructure, so this is an extension of an existing relationship rather than a wholesale platform switch. Over the next two years, HSBC expects to enable more than 200 new AI-driven use cases. Each priority project, according to the bank, is expected to deliver more than $100 million in revenue gains or cost savings.

"A partnership like this one with Google Cloud helps us empower our colleagues with the tools they need to be future-ready, and supports our work in building a simple, agile, faster and more personal HSBC." — Georges Elhedery, Group CEO, HSBC

Three areas have been named upfront: hyper-personalised wealth management, in which AI-generated insights would be layered on top of relationship managers' advice; financial crime risk management, where the bank says agentic AI could double the speed at which it flags risks across roughly one billion transactions monitored monthly; and internal productivity tools that cut meeting-prep and administrative time for frontline staff.

The build-vs-buy calculation

The announcement fits a pattern visible across the industry. Major banks are increasingly treating cloud providers — Google, Microsoft, Amazon — as primary AI infrastructure rather than building proprietary systems. The logic is straightforward: training frontier models and maintaining the GPU clusters they run on is expensive and slow, and no bank has Google DeepMind's research depth in-house.

But the calculus carries risks that regulators have begun flagging loudly. Germany's financial watchdog warned last December that banks' ties to a small number of AI infrastructure providers create "vertical and horizontal connections which are hard to see into from the outside." The Financial Conduct Authority has flagged hyperscaler concentration as a "live issue" and signalled guidance on human-in-the-loop requirements is likely this year. When JPMorgan, Goldman and Bank of America all route AI workloads through the same three cloud providers, a serious outage or policy change at any one of them is no longer a single institution's problem.

For HSBC specifically, the deal's strategic logic is also tied to competitive differentiation — or the risk of losing it. If every large bank plugs into Gemini for wealth advice, the advice stops being a differentiator.

Elhedery has been candid about the workforce dimension of all this. Earlier this year he acknowledged that AI will "destroy certain jobs and create new jobs," while announcing plans to train all 200,000 HSBC staff on AI tools. The bank has separately been examining cuts of around 20,000 roles — roughly 10 percent of headcount — over the next three to five years. HSBC appointed David Rice as its first Chief AI Officer in April, giving the initiative a dedicated seat at the C-suite table.

What to watch

Neither the investment size nor the contract value has been disclosed, which makes it difficult to assess how seriously HSBC is betting compared with, say, its existing technology budget. The $100 million-per-project figure is a projection, not a commitment. The meaningful test will come in HSBC's next set of results: whether Elhedery can point to concrete efficiency gains, or whether the partnership remains, for now, an announcement in search of a scorecard.
