Gold miners, nuclear and homebuilders lag well behind oil's Hormuz-deal recovery

Sunrise Dam gold mine open pit, Western Australia — gold miners have borne the steepest losses of any sector since late February. — Calistemon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
GDX −26%, NLR −15%, ITB −10% since February: the Hormuz deal that sank oil below $80 cannot fix the rate environment that broke these sectors.

Oil is back below $80 a barrel. The Strait of Hormuz has been reopened. The geopolitical premium that ran crude toward triple digits has deflated in a week. For energy markets, the Trump-Iran deal looks like vindication. For investors in gold miners, nuclear stocks, and homebuilders, it looks like cold comfort.

The VanEck Gold Miners ETF (GDX) is down 26.39% since February 27. The VanEck Uranium and Nuclear ETF (NLR) has lost 15.01% over the same stretch. The iShares U.S. Home Construction ETF (ITB) is off 9.70%. Together they sit among the worst-performing sector groups in the U.S. equity market since late February — while the S&P 500 has gained roughly 10%.

The contrast is pointed. Lower oil should help at the margin: cheaper energy reduces mine operating costs, eases construction input prices, and takes some inflation pressure off the Federal Reserve. But the proximate reason all three sectors fell is not oil. It is rates.

The rate-shock transmission

The Iran conflict sent WTI crude above $100 in May. That pushed U.S. consumer-price inflation to 4.2%, well above the Fed's 2% target and enough to squeeze out the rate-cut pricing that had supported long-duration equity plays through early 2026. The 10-year Treasury yield climbed above 4.5%, compressing valuations on sectors whose cash flows sit years into the future.

Homebuilders are the clearest case: mortgage costs track the 10-year, and builder earnings multiples compress when the discount rate rises. Nuclear stocks face a similar dynamic — most of the capacity additions being built or planned will not generate revenue for five or more years, making them effectively long-duration instruments priced off current yields. Both had already sold off on rate concerns before the Iran crisis; the energy shock accelerated the move.

Gold miners are a more complicated story. They took what analysts described as a double hit: gold prices fell roughly 17% from their pre-conflict highs as haven demand unwound, while elevated energy costs ate directly into margins. Mining operations are fuel-intensive, so crude above $90 squeezed the spread between revenue and cost just when revenue was already falling. Rising Treasury yields then added a second layer of pressure, raising the opportunity cost of holding non-income-producing metals and weighing on the underlying commodity alongside the equities.

The Hormuz deal partially addresses all three of these conditions. Cheaper oil means lower mining costs and cheaper construction materials. The 10-year yield has already eased back toward 4.44%, its lowest level in nearly three weeks. But markets are waiting for confirmation that the Fed will act — the central bank is widely expected to leave the federal-funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% at its next meeting under new chair Kevin Warsh, with cuts not priced until Q3 or Q4 at the earliest.

Sectors that lag on the way down tend to lag on the way up until a catalyst forces a re-rating. For GDX, NLR, and ITB, that catalyst is not the Hormuz deal itself. It is the next CPI print, and whatever signal Warsh sends about the pace of easing.
