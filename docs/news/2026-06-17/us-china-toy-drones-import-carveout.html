US removes Chinese toy drones from import ban list in quiet trade carve-out

A small quadcopter drone — the type of inexpensive toy the FCC exemption nominally covers, though its strict criteria exclude almost all commercial models. — Zimin.V.G. / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The FCC carved toy drones out of its China import ban, but criteria so strict that even DJI's lightest model fails mean it changes almost nothing.

America's communications regulator quietly issued an exemption last week that, on paper, reopens the door to Chinese drone imports. In practice, the door is barely ajar.

The Federal Communications Commission's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau issued Public Notice DA 26-588 on June 15, acting on a June 12 determination from the Department of War. The ruling carves toy drones out of a sweeping import prohibition — but the definition of "toy" is so engineering-specific that it describes a product category almost no manufacturer currently produces.

The backstory: in December 2025, the FCC added all foreign-manufactured uncrewed aerial systems to its so-called Covered List — effectively blocking any new Chinese-made drone model from receiving the equipment authorisation required for legal US sale. The move targeted DJI and Autel Robotics most directly, though it applied categorically to foreign production regardless of brand. The ban did not ground existing fleets; drones already authorised before December 22 remained legal to operate and sell.

The fine print

The Department of War found that national security risks are not posed by drones that lack "range, endurance, sensing, payload, connectivity, and data collection and storage" capabilities. The FCC's resulting criteria translate that principle into a checklist with no room for interpretation.

A qualifying toy drone must weigh under 150 grams, carry no camera or sensor capable of data collection, operate within 100-metre line-of-sight only, fly no longer than ten minutes per charge, reach no faster than ten metres per second, and remain below a 300-foot altitude ceiling. It must have no wireless or network capability, no GPS or satellite navigation of any kind, and no brushless motors. It must be explicitly marketed as a toy — and it cannot come from any company listed under Section 1709 of the FY2025 National Defense Authorization Act, which names DJI and Autel by implication.

Stacked together, those requirements eliminate virtually every aircraft sold by a credible drone manufacturer. DJI's lightest current model, the Neo, weighs 135 grams — technically under the limit — but it ships with a 12-megapixel 4K camera, GPS, brushless motors, an 18-minute battery, and a seven-kilometre wireless transmission range. It fails on at least five counts. The Mini 5 Pro misses on weight alone before the other criteria apply.

The practical effect is that the exemption covers, as one analysis put it, roughly a powered foam glider.

As for the broader trade picture: this ruling does not signal a softening of the underlying restrictions. The Commerce Department did withdraw a separate, sweeping drone-import rule in January — partly in deference to diplomatic messaging ahead of a Trump-Xi meeting — but the FCC's Covered List remains intact. Eleven non-Chinese manufacturers have received targeted exemptions since December; DJI has received none.

DJI is pursuing a Ninth Circuit legal challenge, Case 26-1029, arguing the FCC produced no public evidence that its products pose the claimed threats. The government's decision to precisely enumerate what makes a drone "non-threatening" may, paradoxically, strengthen that argument by implying everything outside the narrow toy definition is threatening by definition alone.

The next meaningful data point arrives in court: the Ninth Circuit hearing is expected to be scheduled later this year, and any ruling there would carry far more weight than a quiet regulatory carve-out that the market has already priced as irrelevant.
