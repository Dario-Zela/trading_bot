Washington seeks AI partnership with Brussels on regulation and supply chains

Jacob Helberg, US Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs and architect of Pax Silica, the initiative the EU formally joined on June 23. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The EU signed Pax Silica on June 23, joining a US-led supply-chain pact for AI and chips, but divergent regulatory approaches remain unresolved.

The European Union formally signed on to Pax Silica on June 23, bringing the bloc — along with Germany and Greece — into a US-led pact designed to secure the technology supply chains underpinning AI. The news emerged publicly through a Bloomberg report on June 25, but the substantive story had been building for months: what started as a diplomatic overture is now a network of 18 signatories, with Argentina, Chile, Costa Rica, Kazakhstan, and Panama expected to join within days.

Pax Silica was launched in December 2025 at a Washington summit orchestrated by Jacob Helberg, the US Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs and the initiative's principal architect. It covers the breadth of the AI supply chain — critical minerals extraction, semiconductor fabrication, software, energy infrastructure, and logistics. The framework is non-binding, a political declaration rather than a treaty, but it carries a concrete economic signal: under the broader EU-US trade agreement that accompanied accession, the bloc committed to purchasing at least $40 billion worth of American AI chips.

"There's no grouping that's purpose-built to manage the AI economy," Helberg has said — the pitch being that existing multilateral bodies were designed for an earlier era and lack the technical specificity to coordinate supply chains for frontier computing.

The regulation question

The headline coupling of "regulation" with "supply chains" points to a genuine friction inside the partnership. The US position, as articulated by Helberg, is that governments should approach AI "through the lens of opportunity" rather than restriction — what he calls "innovation sovereignty" over "digital sovereignty." The EU, meanwhile, is in the middle of phasing in the AI Act, the world's first comprehensive AI legislation; its second tranche of obligations takes effect in August 2026.

Those two regulatory orientations are not obviously compatible. Pax Silica presupposes a permissive environment for frontier AI development. The AI Act mandates specific compliance burdens on the highest-risk systems. Washington's interest in a joint regulatory framework is, in effect, a nudge toward lighter-touch governance from a bloc that has invested considerable political capital in the opposite approach.

France has been the loudest dissenter inside the EU, calling the pact an attempt to "colonize Europe" and framing it as a threat to European technological sovereignty. The European Commission's official line — that the declaration "will not interfere with EU internal decision-making" — is technically accurate but politically careful: it defuses the sovereignty argument without resolving the underlying tension.

For context: the Netherlands' ASML, which controls critical extreme-ultraviolet lithography equipment without which advanced chip fabrication is not possible, adds genuine leverage to the European side of the equation. The partnership is not simply Brussels signing up to Washington's terms.

The immediate test is whether the regulatory dimension produces anything concrete before August, when the AI Act's next obligations hit. If the US and EU move toward shared standards or mutual recognition — rather than parallel but divergent frameworks — that would be the news. Until then, Pax Silica is a real and expanding supply-chain coalition bolted to a regulatory aspiration that still lacks a mechanism.
