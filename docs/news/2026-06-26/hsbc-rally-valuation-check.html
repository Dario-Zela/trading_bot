HSBC's 73% yearly run: momentum story or correction waiting to happen?

HSBC's global headquarters at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC's 73% yearly gain looks increasingly like macro bet-matching rather than operational step-change, with P/E at 15x against an 11x peer average and rate tailwinds fading.

HSBC Holdings has added 73% to its share price over the past twelve months, making it one of the more conspicuous performers among FTSE 100 constituents. The stock closed at £14.45, year-to-date gains sit at 21.3%, and the three-year run totals 188.2%. That last figure helps explain the nerves: compounding at this pace eventually prices in a version of the future the business has yet to deliver.

The short-term read is already softer. Weekly returns have flattened to 0.2%, which on its own proves little, but is consistent with a stock consolidating after an extended re-rating rather than one extending its thesis.

The Valuation Problem

The fundamental picture is genuinely ambiguous, and analysts know it. One modelling approach — discounted excess returns — puts intrinsic value at roughly £22.50, implying the stock is still 35% below fair value. A competing earnings-multiple framework flips that conclusion: HSBC currently trades at 15.46x earnings, against a modelled fair ratio of 10.85x. European banking peers average closer to 11x. The spread between these estimates — fair value narratives ranging from £7.73 to £22.50 depending on input assumptions — is wide enough to accommodate almost any investment thesis, which is not a comfort to a buyer at current prices.

Q1 2026 results provided limited traction either way. Profit before tax reached $9.4 billion, broadly flat year-on-year, with return on tangible equity at 17.3% — in line with the bank's stated target of 17% or better for the full year. Net interest income of $8.9 billion slipped from Q4 2025, partly on day-count and a one-off item. The bank is targeting $1.5 billion in structural cost reductions. Wealth management revenue grew 21% in 2025, a genuine bright spot tied to rising affluence across Hong Kong and Southeast Asia. None of this clearly explains a P/E ratio sitting roughly 40% above the European peer average.

Rate Tailwinds Reversing

The macro story that powered much of the re-rating is now moving against the bank. The Bank of England cut base rate from 5.25% to 3.75% between August 2024 and December 2025; HSBC's own economists forecast further reductions to approximately 3.00% by year-end 2026. Net interest margin — 1.60% in Q1, up one basis point year-on-year but down four from the prior quarter — will feel further pressure as the rate cycle continues lower. The offset the bank is betting on, wealth and capital markets fee income, is structural and real, but accumulates slowly.

The secondary risk sits in Asia. HSBC's outsized exposure to Hong Kong commercial real estate and mainland Chinese credit conditions remains a tail risk that would hit provisions quickly if conditions deteriorate. Consensus price targets sit around 1,427p — barely above the current level — suggesting the Street is not positioned for much additional re-rating from here.

What to Watch

H1 2026 results, expected late July or early August, are the next hard data point. The market will be watching net interest margin for signs of meaningful compression below 1.55%, and any revision to capital return guidance — the Q1 dividend was held at $0.10 per share. Progress on the $1.5 billion cost programme will also matter: that is where management has the most direct control in an environment where rate and macro variables are moving against them.

A 73% gain in twelve months commands attention. The sector tailwind was real, the structural Asia pivot is credible, and the balance sheet is solid. But at a P/E materially above peers, with rate tailwinds fading and a macro story that could shift quickly, the margin for error at £14.45 is narrower than the headline return implies.
