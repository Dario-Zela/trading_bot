Ex-Nestlé chief Freixe pursues legal action to recover bonus stripped after dismissal

Nestlé's global headquarters in Vevey, on the shore of Lake Geneva. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Freixe, fired as Nestlé CEO after an undisclosed affair with a subordinate, is in pre-litigation talks to claw back his 2025 bonus and three years of forfeited share awards.

Nine months after his abrupt dismissal from one of the world's largest food companies, Laurent Freixe is fighting back. The former Nestlé chief executive — ousted in September 2025 following an undisclosed relationship with a direct subordinate — has entered pre-litigation settlement negotiations with the Swiss food giant, seeking recovery of a pro-rata 2025 bonus and three years of unvested share awards that were stripped when he left.

Nestlé was categorical at the time: no severance, no bonus, nothing beyond a base salary through the date of departure. The board cited violations of the company's code of conduct, which it said Freixe had breached by failing to disclose the relationship during two separate internal investigations. Philipp Navratil was appointed successor the same day.

Freixe's legal team is now contesting both the grounds and the manner of that exit. His attorney, Vanessa Chambour of the Lausanne firm PSF 12, put it plainly: "After nearly forty years of unfailing service, he was removed without being heard and stripped of compensation he had legitimately earned, a punitive process that has unfairly damaged his reputation and his ability to pursue his career." Nestlé, represented by Homburger AG, has declined to comment. The specific financial sums at stake have not been disclosed, though Freixe's predecessor as CEO earned around CHF 9.6 million annually in total compensation, suggesting the unvested share tranche alone could represent a material claim.

A test of Nestlé's resolve

For institutional shareholders, the case carries weight beyond the individual. Nestlé's decision to deny all severance was itself described at the time as highly unusual by corporate governance observers — a deliberate signal that code-of-conduct breaches at the top would carry real financial consequences. A negotiated settlement that returns a portion of the withheld compensation, particularly the share awards, would blunt that signal.

The episode draws comparisons to the Tidjane Thiam affair at Credit Suisse, which unraveled in early 2020 and became the most prominent management dispute in Swiss corporate history of that era. Freixe's case has the ingredients to rival it: a 39-year career ending in acrimony, a public dispute over what was earned, and a company whose governance credibility is already under scrutiny after two CEO changes in as many years.

Freixe remained listed as CEO on his LinkedIn profile for more than six months after the dismissal, and stayed on Nestlé's board — reportedly against the company's wishes — until the April 2026 annual general meeting, when he was formally removed. The extended overlap has done little to ease tensions.

The next significant marker will be whether pre-litigation talks produce a quiet resolution or tip into formal proceedings under Swiss law. Nestlé's appetite for a public courtroom dispute — with attendant document disclosure and reputational exposure on both sides — is unclear. What is clear is that the outcome will be read by governance-focused investors as a measure of whether the company's zero-severance stance was principle or posture.
