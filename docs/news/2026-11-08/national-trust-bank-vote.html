National Trust members vote to seek a banking partner more in tune with its environmental policies

In one sentence
National Trust members have voted to seek a bank with environmental policies closer to the charity's own, though the margin, target bank and any timetable are unconfirmed.

A conservation charity with more than six million members has been asked by those members to look at who holds its money. National Trust members have voted to seek a banking partner whose environmental policies sit closer to the Trust's own, according to the Guardian. The vote turns the charity's governance process into a lever on its own lender.

The public reporting is thin. The Guardian report does not, in the material available, give a vote count or margin. It does not say which bank is the target, or whether the Trust has committed to a formal switch or a timetable. Those gaps limit how much weight the result can bear.

A long-running campaign

The vote follows years of pressure over the Trust's relationship with Barclays, which campaigners have described as a conflict with the charity's climate commitments. In 2022 a member resolution asked the Trust to end its banking ties with Barclays by February 2024, with exceptions for existing credit agreements. The board recommended voting against, arguing that moving the banking of an organisation of the Trust's size and range of operations carried "considerable cost, risk and distraction". Members rejected the motion: 86,854 voted it down, in an AGM in which more than 127,000 members took part.

Afterwards the Trust said it was not conducting a formal review of its banking arrangements, while saying that banks needed to do much more on financing the fossil fuel industry. Protesters, including Christian Climate Action, kept turning up outside later AGMs. In 2024 members backed other climate-focused measures, among them a stronger response to the climate and ecological emergency and a plan to make half of café menu items plant-based.

Setting those votes side by side, members have been more willing to back policy statements than to back an instruction that carries operational cost. A vote to "seek" a better-aligned partner is also softer than an instruction to leave a named bank. It commits the board to look, not necessarily to move.

What it can and cannot show

The Guardian report is the only source, and the claim that the vote has wider implications for UK lenders is not supported by anything in it. A single charity's current account is small beside the balance sheets of the big banks. Barclays' own market valuation is moving on analyst target cuts, not on charity governance (see the related coverage in Markets).

The more useful reading is narrower. Institutional customers, from charities to universities and local authorities, can attach conditions to their banking, and lenders can see the reputational cost of ignoring them. Whether that changes lending policy depends on how many institutions act together, and one vote does not show that.

What to watch

Three things would settle the matter: the published vote tally and margin, any statement from the Trust's board on how and when it will test the market, and any response from the bank concerned. The Trust's next annual general meeting, where the board would be expected to report progress to members, is the natural point at which the vote's weight will become clear.
