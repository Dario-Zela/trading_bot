Israel's president tasks Eisenkot with forming a government

In one sentence
Israel's president has handed Gadi Eisenkot the first attempt at forming a government, but he must still reach 61 seats without leaning openly on Arab-led parties.

Israel's president has asked Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief and now the leading figure of the opposition, to try to form the country's next government. The choice follows an election that left the incumbent camp short of a clear path. It puts the first attempt at building a majority in the hands of a challenger rather than Benjamin Netanyahu.

The president is Isaac Herzog. The mandate was widely expected: Herzog had said he would name his choice on Sunday, 8 November, and the BBC reports that it has gone to Eisenkot. Under Israeli law the president must assign the task within seven days of the official publication of the results, which puts the outer limit at 11 November.

Why the president's choice matters

Israeli voters do not pick a prime minister directly. The president consults every elected party and gives the first chance to the candidate who appears most likely to command 61 of the Knesset's 120 seats. The mandate is therefore not a prize for finishing first. It is the president's judgment about which bloc can actually add up.

That judgment appears to have rested on the recommendations. Reporting from the consultations says Eisenkot's party, Yashar, secured recommendations from 67 lawmakers. The Democrats were among those backing him, and Raam, an Arab party, also recommended him. Its leader said the party had recommended Eisenkot "to receive the mandate to form the new government". Other opposition figures, including Avigdor Liberman and Naftali Bennett, had been reported as favouring him for prime minister.

A recommendation is not a coalition. It shows who a party would rather see try, not whom it will sit in cabinet with. Seat totals for each bloc were not available at the time of writing and should be checked against the official results.

The arithmetic problem

Eisenkot's difficulty is built into his support. Some of the backing comes from Arab-led parties, yet he has ruled out forming a government with Israel's two Arab-led parties. Netanyahu's Likud has already warned against a government that depends on them. If Eisenkot assembled a majority only with the Arab parties' numbers, he would risk losing support from parts of the wider opposition.

The usual way through is to draw in defectors from the other camp. One report said a religious party had dropped Netanyahu, which is the kind of movement that could change the sums. How far that goes is unconfirmed.

What happens next

The nominee gets 28 days to form a government, with a possible 14-day extension. If he fails, the president can turn to another candidate, and repeated failure leads to fresh elections, a route Israelis have taken several times in recent years.

The things to watch are concrete. One is whether any religious or right-leaning party commits to sit under Eisenkot. Another is how he treats the Arab parties that endorsed him. The third is whether the 28-day clock runs close to expiry before a cabinet list is signed. The outcome decides who sets Israel's security and fiscal agenda for the next government.
