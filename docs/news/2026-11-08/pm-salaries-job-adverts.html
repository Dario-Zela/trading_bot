PM urges firms to publish salaries in job adverts ahead of new law

In one sentence
Downing Street is asking employers to publish pay in job adverts voluntarily ahead of a planned legal duty that is still at consultation stage, with no start date confirmed.

Downing Street has asked employers to start putting salaries in their job adverts now, ahead of legislation that would make it compulsory. The request is voluntary. It comes from the prime minister's office and was reported by BBC Politics. The law it anticipates is not yet in force.

The request is a form of persuasion. By asking firms to move early, the government is trying to shape recruitment practice before the rules arrive. The BBC report, the only source for the request itself, gives no figures, dates or named ministers. This article does not add any detail to it.

What is on the table

The policy behind the request is a consultation on pay transparency in recruitment. According to law-firm and trade summaries, it proposes that every employer in England, Scotland and Wales publish pay information in job adverts. The consultation closed on 27 October 2026. Respondents were asked whether the duty should require a pay range, a specific salary or a benchmark rate. They were also asked what other details would be proportionate.

The consultation is described as part of a wider first phase of work on equal pay and pay discrimination law. Ministers say they want to keep the burden on employers low. They also point out that many firms already publish pay ranges without being compelled to.

No confirmed start date has been published. The proposal has not been enacted, and its final form, including whether it will name a range or a single figure, depends on the government's response to the consultation. That response had not appeared in the sources checked for this article.

Why ask early

The idea has been tried before. In March 2022 the government urged firms to disclose pay voluntarily, with a pilot in which participating employers listed salaries and stopped asking candidates about their pay history. Surveys since then suggest voluntary disclosure has gone only part of the way. One analysis by the job site Adzuna found around six in ten adverts stating a salary, while a separate study of 50 common roles found only 39 per cent did.

Against that record, a second voluntary appeal looks like groundwork. Employers who change their templates and applicant-tracking systems now will have less to adjust once a legal duty applies. The government also gets a measure of how far firms will go on their own. That is an inference from the timing and the earlier pilot, not something the government has said.

The stated aim of disclosure is to narrow gender and other pay gaps. Candidates who see the pay on offer are less likely to be anchored by an employer's opening figure. Ministers also argue that disclosure helps employers evaluate roles before recruiting, and saves time by filtering out candidates whose expectations do not match the pay. Recruiters would see the most immediate change, since they handle much of the advert drafting.

What to watch

The next marker is the government's response to the consultation. It should settle three things: whether adverts must carry a range or a fixed figure, how the duty would be enforced, and when it would start. Until then the prime minister's request is an invitation. How many large employers accept it will be an early sign of how contested the final rules are likely to be.
