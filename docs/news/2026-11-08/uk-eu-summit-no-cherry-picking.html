Barnier rules out cherry-picking as European leaders urge closer UK-EU ties before Brussels summit

In one sentence
Michel Barnier has restated that the UK cannot cherry-pick the single market, underlining the limits on a UK-EU reset that relies on narrow, sector-by-sector deals.

The European Union's former chief Brexit negotiator has repeated a line that British officials have heard for a decade: the single market is a package, and a country outside it cannot choose only the parts it likes. Michel Barnier, who led the EU side from 2016 to 2021, made the point in a BBC interview as the UK and the EU continue the "reset" of their relationship. The timing is awkward for London. The reset is built on narrow, sector-by-sector arrangements, which is the kind of selective access Barnier says Brussels will not give.

The interview and the broader summit coverage are only partly verified. This article relies on Barnier's well-documented record and on the facts established in the triage brief. It does not quote the interview directly.

An old line, restated

Barnier's position is not new. During the withdrawal talks he described the single market as a bundle of four indivisible freedoms: goods, services, capital and people. They come with common rules, shared institutions and a system of enforcement. His argument was that a country could not keep the benefits of frictionless trade while dropping the obligations that make the system work.

That reasoning shaped the outcome. The UK left the single market and the customs union at the end of the transition period on 31 December 2020, after the Trade and Cooperation Agreement was agreed on 24 December. The deal gave tariff-free goods trade. It did not give the UK passporting rights for financial services, mutual recognition of standards, or an easy route for food and animal products. Barnier had long said that was the logical result of the red lines London had set.

So his latest remarks are best read as context, not as a new development. They show the ceiling the EU still sees above any deal, and that ceiling has not moved.

What the reset is trying to do

The current talks began with a first UK-EU leaders' summit on 19 May 2025. They have deliberately avoided reopening the whole settlement. They cover specific files instead: a veterinary and plant-health (SPS) agreement for agri-food, services, and a youth mobility arrangement.

The approach is pragmatic, but it sits uneasily with Barnier's principle. The SPS agreement is the clearest case. It would remove many of the border checks that have burdened food exports since 2021. In return, it would commit the UK to follow EU rules in that area, with oversight by EU institutions. Access is granted, but only where Britain accepts the obligations that go with it. That is the EU's package logic applied to one sector at a time.

The politics of that bargain have already reached supermarket shelves. Commentary on the reset says British manufacturers would have to conform to the EU's removal of a group of smoke flavourings, adopted in Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2024/2067 of 31 July 2024. Some reports link this to smoky bacon crisps. PepsiCo, which makes Walkers Smoky Bacon, has said its products are not affected by the ban. Nigel Farage has attacked the alignment as a surrender. Whether and when the provisions apply under the reset deal is not established by the sources checked here, and the BBC piece that prompted the discussion could not be confirmed. The example still shows the trade-off: access to the EU's food market means accepting EU decisions on ingredients, labels and standards, including some the UK had no hand in making.

Leaders call for closer ties

The Guardian has reported that five European leaders called for closer EU-UK ties ahead of a UK-EU summit in Brussels. The names of the leaders, the date of the call and its wording could not be confirmed, and no specific policy asks are known. It is therefore unclear whether the call signals a coordinated position that could soften the Commission's approach or is a goodwill gesture before the meeting.

Support from national capitals does count for something. Member states set the mandate under which the Commission negotiates, and some governments have long wanted a warmer relationship with London on defence, trade and mobility. But goodwill from individual leaders does not change the legal structure Barnier describes. The Commission negotiates on behalf of 27 states, and many of them are reluctant to give a non-member the benefits of membership.

What it means

For firms and markets, the practical reading is that any gains from the reset will be real but limited. A deal on food and farm trade could cut costs and delays for exporters. Gains in services and mobility are likely to be narrower and slower. None of them is likely to bring back the frictionless trade the UK had before 2021.

The next test is the Brussels summit. The things to watch are whether the SPS agreement reaches signable text, how much dynamic alignment with EU rules the UK accepts, and how closely the leaders' public statements track the Commission's negotiating position. Barnier's remark suggests where that position will hold.
