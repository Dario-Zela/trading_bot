New Zealand election delivers its tightest result in years

In one sentence
New Zealand's governing coalition holds a one-seat majority in the preliminary count, but special votes, overhang seats and the centrist Opportunity Party could still decide who governs.

New Zealand's general election on Saturday ended without a settled answer. The governing coalition of National, ACT and New Zealand First held a one-seat majority in the preliminary count, but a large pool of uncounted ballots, a new centrist party and an unusually big Parliament mean the result can't yet be called. Prime Minister Christopher Luxon said as much on the night. "I have to tell you right now it is too close to call," he told supporters in Auckland.

The contest was always going to be decided by arithmetic rather than by headline vote share. New Zealand uses a proportional system, mixed-member proportional (MMP). Each voter casts an electorate ballot and a party ballot, and seats are allocated so that Parliament roughly mirrors the party vote. No party wins outright. Governments are assembled from blocs, and small parties can end up deciding who governs.

The count so far

At about 97.6% of the vote counted, the NZ Herald's projection put the coalition on 67 seats in a 130-seat Parliament: National 42, ACT 13 and NZ First 12. The left bloc stood on 56: Labour 35, the Greens 16 and Te Pāti Māori 5. A seventh group, the centrist Opportunity Party, was on 7 seats with about 5.4% of the party vote. Al Jazeera, citing the same preliminary count, reported the coalition on 67 seats, one more than the 66 needed. It gave the size of the House as 131, so outlets differ on that detail.

The difference between a majority and a hung Parliament therefore comes down to a handful of seats. Several electorate races show how thin the margins are. National's Ryan Hamilton won Hamilton East by 456 votes. In Wairarapa, National's Mike Butterick led by 161 votes with about 86% counted. In Mt Roskill, Labour's Michael Wood led by 285 votes with 82% counted.

The night also cost the government some senior figures. National's Chris Bishop conceded Hutt South to Labour's Ginney Andersen. The Herald reported that Finance Minister Nicola Willis, Paul Goldsmith and Gerry Brownlee were also likely to lose their seats, though list placings and final counts may change that.

Overhang, special votes and a new kingmaker

Three things could still move the result.

Overhang. An overhang arises when a party wins more electorate seats than its share of the party vote entitles it to. It keeps them all, and Parliament grows. The Herald reported that National was expected to win six surplus electorate seats and Te Pāti Māori four, enlarging the House. Pollster David Farrar called it the "big wildcard". Greens co-leader Chlöe Swarbrick, who led in Auckland Central, said the overhang showed "the system has been gamed".

Special votes. Roughly 14% of ballots remain uncounted, including overseas and out-of-electorate votes. These have historically favoured the left. Farrar said the count would need to reach about 80% before a trend was clear. Luxon put the pool at around 300,000 votes. Final results are due on 27 November, and the government stays in caretaker mode until then.

Opportunity. The party, led by Qiulae Wong, looks set to enter Parliament by clearing the 5% threshold, though ACT's David Seymour said it was unclear whether it would stay above the line. If the coalition's majority erodes, Opportunity is the obvious partner for either side. Luxon had ruled out working with it. Campaign chair Simeon Brown did not repeat that, and said only that National was in "a strong position". Farrar suggested a hung Parliament would give Luxon a "perfect excuse" to talk to the party.

Labour leader Chris Hipkins did not concede. "It may take us a little bit longer to figure out exactly what it is that you've said," he told supporters.

Why it is close

The campaign turned on household budgets. Former MP Peter Dunne called it "much more of a cost-of-living election", and grocery prices dominated. National promised a return to budget surplus and a bigger pension scheme. Labour promised cheaper healthcare and transport. Neither won over enough of a weary electorate to break the deadlock.

What comes next

The first marker is the special-vote count, which will show whether the coalition's one-seat lead survives, and the official declaration on 27 November. If the coalition falls short, the talks that follow could be long. Dunne warned of possible weeks of negotiation. There is no legal deadline for forming a government, though Parliament must sit by early January. The question to watch is whether Luxon reverses his position on Opportunity, and what the party would ask for in return.
