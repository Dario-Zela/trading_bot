Met chief says policing needs reform to cope with modern criminals

In one sentence
Met Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley again says the 43-force model cannot handle fraud and cybercrime, having previously proposed 12–15 larger forces and more flexible hiring.

Sir Mark Rowley, the Metropolitan Police Commissioner, has again argued that Britain's policing model was not built for the crimes it now faces. A general complaint of that kind carries little weight. It matters only if it is tied to a specific gap and a specific remedy, and on this subject Rowley has supplied both over the past year or more.

The account of this week's remarks that reached this newsroom was a short summary of BBC Politics coverage, with no figures, dates or direct quotes. The detail below therefore comes from Rowley's earlier public statements, as reported by UK media. It should be read as the established position behind the latest warning, not as a report of what he said in this instance.

The structural argument

Rowley's core claim is that England and Wales' system of 43 territorial forces has not been fit for purpose for at least two decades. He has described it as a patched-up version of the arrangements set by a royal commission in 1962. In a Sunday Times article in July 2025, he called for a reduction to 12–15 larger forces, and called it the first serious reform of the model in more than 60 years.

The case rests on the nature of the offending. Fraud and cybercrime do not respect county lines, let alone national borders, yet each force largely builds its own capability. Rowley has said these crimes need "really niche skills" that are hard to hold in dozens of small organisations. He argues that fewer, bigger forces could share technology more easily and spend less on governance and back-office support.

A workforce question

The second strand concerns who does the work. Rowley has called for more flexible ways to recruit and employ people, so that forces can bring in digital and financial-crime specialists without forcing them through the traditional route of a uniformed constable's career. That is a narrower, more immediate proposal than merging forces, and it would not need legislation on the scale a restructuring would.

He has also offered a fallback. If the government balked at full consolidation, it could fund and strengthen existing partnerships focused on serious organised crime, fraud and terrorism. Or it could "go the whole hog" and create regional forces.

What is missing

The gaps matter as much as the proposals. The coverage reviewed does not show a detailed government response, and the main objections to a merger are not set out. Those usually concern local accountability, the cost and disruption of reorganisation, and the risk that a larger force becomes more remote from the neighbourhoods it polices. Rowley himself has also acknowledged that the Met, as a modern organisation, lacks capabilities in data and technology, which suggests that size alone is not the whole answer.

The test of whether this is a policy or a complaint is what the Home Office does next. Ministers would need to say whether they favour consolidation, regional forces or a funded partnership model, and whether any of it would be written into a police reform bill. Until they do, the Commissioner's warning remains an argument, not a plan.
