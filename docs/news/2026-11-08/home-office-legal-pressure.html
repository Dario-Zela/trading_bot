Home Office faces legal and political challenges over EU settled status, Turkish removals and an asylum kidnapping case

In one sentence
The3million has taken the Home Office to the High Court over a policy that told almost 1,500 EU nationals their settled status was 'granted in error', without appeal rights.

The Home Office is being taken to the High Court over a policy that has told almost 1,500 people with post-Brexit residence rights that they should never have been given them. The campaign group the3million lodged the claim this week. It says the department is removing rights granted under the EU Settlement Scheme (EUSS) without the appeal rights and proportionality checks that the Brexit Withdrawal Agreement requires.

Two other Home Office stories from the same Guardian politics coverage sit alongside it: a reported rise in removals of Turkish nationals, and a court ruling against the department in a case involving a kidnapped asylum seeker. They are separate matters, and what is known about each differs sharply. The settled-status claim is the best documented.

The 'granted in error' policy

The EUSS gave EU citizens living in the UK before 31 December 2020 either pre-settled or settled status. The challenge concerns a policy under which the Home Office later decides that a grant was made "in error" because the applicant had not shown they were a "relevant EEA citizen" at the time. A Home Office minister has reportedly said 1,473 people have been contacted, which is the basis for the "almost 1,500" figure.

The3million's case, as reported, is that this is a decision in substance, whatever the department calls it. Because it removes a right the Withdrawal Agreement protects, the group argues, the safeguards of an appeal and a proportionality assessment must apply. It asks the court to declare the policy unlawful under the Withdrawal Agreement and/or common law, and to order the relevant provisions removed from Home Office guidance.

The group sent a formal pre-action letter in August 2026, and the Home Office declined to withdraw the policy. A challenge of this kind had been reported on 3 September, when the group said it was crowdfunding the case with Wilson Solicitors and barristers from Garden Court Chambers. The Home Office has said affected people can seek to remain through other immigration routes, stay until any pre-settled status expires, and apply for settled status, with appeal rights if that application is refused. On the claim itself, a spokesperson said the department "could not comment while legal proceedings were ongoing."

The human detail is specific. Some Brazilian-born Italians were told their status was an error because they had applied without an Italian passport, stating only that they were Italian citizens. A 33-year-old engineer in London, resident since 2018, was told he should not have received settled status and that his Brazilian wife could not stay; that decision was reversed in recent weeks after objections. In October the Home Office accepted that evidence of Italian citizenship by descent could show someone was Italian from birth, and agreed to review affected decisions. Monique Hawkins, the group's acting chief executive, said the department was "effectively denying people the protections they were entitled to", and compared the approach to refusing someone a trial because they were thought clearly guilty.

Why the courts may be receptive

The3million has been here before. In 2022 the Independent Monitoring Authority, the body set up to police EU citizens' post-Brexit rights, won a judicial review over pre-settled status. The High Court held that residence rights could not simply lapse because someone failed to reapply, and the Home Office changed its approach. That ruling does not decide the present claim, which turns on a different mechanism, but it shows a court willing to read the Withdrawal Agreement as limiting how far the department may remove status by administrative route.

The Home Office's October concession on descent-based Italian citizenship also narrows the dispute without ending it. The underlying question remains whether a grant can be treated as void years later without any right of challenge. This summary relies on secondary reports of the claim; the court's permission decision and any hearing date have not been confirmed.

Turkish removals and a kidnapping ruling

The other two threads are thinner. Campaigners are reported to say that a sharp rise in the removal of Turkish nationals echoes the Windrush scandal. The report available gives no figures, no period and no named campaigners, so the comparison cannot be tested here. There is relevant history: in 2018 ministers closed the Ankara Agreement route to settlement for Turkish businesspeople, which campaigners said affected about 12,500 people, and the 2019 Karagul case found procedural unfairness in some refusals. Nothing confirms that the current reports concern that group.

The kidnapping ruling is likewise unverified. The court, the date and the claimant have not been confirmed. A High Court judgment in 2025, R (AAM) v Secretary of State for the Home Department, quashed a Home Office refusal of a trafficking claim made by a Syrian man who had been abducted in Libya, because the department had reasoned that kidnapping could not amount to trafficking. Whether this week's ruling is that case or a later stage of it is not established.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the High Court grants permission for the settled-status claim and sets a hearing date. The Home Office's review of the Italian cases will show how much of the policy it is prepared to concede before then. A ruling for the3million would affect how any future status is withdrawn from EU nationals: through a decision carrying appeal rights, or by administrative correction.
