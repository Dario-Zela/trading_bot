Commerce Department cleared Nvidia H200 sales to about two dozen Chinese firms, NYT reports

In one sentence
The Times reports Commerce cleared H200 sales to about two dozen Chinese firms, including at least two with no apparent working premises, raising questions about licence vetting.

The US Commerce Department has cleared Nvidia's H200 AI chips for sale to roughly two dozen Chinese companies. According to a New York Times investigation published on November 7, at least two of those approved firms show no sign of a working office. One is a Beijing company whose listed address is an unfinished shell. The other is a firm whose listed office looked deserted when reporters visited in September. The findings put the vetting behind the approvals under scrutiny, not just the policy of selling the chips at all.

The Times's account has not been independently confirmed. This article relies on the Times report as described by secondary outlets, and on a Commerce statement and Nvidia comment quoted in that coverage. The full list of licensees has not been published. The "roughly two dozen" figure comes from people familiar with the approvals.

What the Times found

The first firm is New Matrix, which Commerce approved this year to assemble H200 chips into AI servers. Its listed address is in an industrial park on the northwestern edge of Beijing. There, a Times reporter found what the paper described as a hollow gray structure with wires dangling from the ceiling where light fixtures should be. The company was created 17 months ago. Chinese corporate records link its owners to Wise Road, a private equity fund the US placed on its entity list in 2024. New Matrix did not respond to requests for comment.

The second is Maginfra. Reporters found its listed office apparently deserted in September. Analysts told the paper they believed it to be an offshoot of Inspur, a company on the entity list for working with the Chinese military. Maginfra did not respond either.

The Times also pointed to two other licensees, Zhejiang Yindun Cloud Technology and Shanghai Jiatang. Their owners have ties to firms that serve the Chinese military, the paper reported. Neither answered requests for comment. The Times did not say these two lacked a physical presence.

Two caveats matter. Both firms with no apparent premises are described as assemblers, not end users, so they would build servers rather than run the chips themselves. And the suggestion that New Matrix is a front for companies tied to Wise Road comes from two analysts the Times consulted. The paper did not establish who ultimately controls it. No charges or license revocations have been reported.

Is it a gap or an oddity?

By the Times's own approximate count, two of about two dozen licensees, roughly one in twelve, showed no apparent physical presence. That is a small sample, and an empty address is not proof of wrongdoing. Chinese firms can use registered addresses that differ from where they work. But the pattern is what makes the story more than a single odd listing. The firms are new, their ownership traces to entities Washington has already blacklisted, and the approvals were granted anyway.

If an applicant can be cleared without anyone confirming that it occupies a real building, the licensing process is checking paperwork and not much else. That is an inference from the Times's findings, not something Commerce has conceded.

The Bureau of Industry and Security, the Commerce unit that handles export licences, said it carefully reviews all applications for national security risks. It cited $324 million in export-violation penalties in 2025 and more than 90 new enforcement officers hired this year. Those numbers describe enforcement capacity. They do not say whether these two applicants were visited or checked.

Liza Tobin, a former China director at the White House National Security Council, told the Times she had "a ton of concerns" that neither Commerce nor Nvidia is doing enough due diligence on whether licence terms are followed.

Nvidia spokesman John Rizzo said "it makes no sense for the Chinese military to depend on American technology." That answers a question about military use. It does not address whether the named licensees exist as operating businesses.

How the approvals came about

President Trump allowed H200 sales to China on December 8, 2025, with the US government taking a 25% cut of revenue. The chips are routed through the US, so a 25% tariff applies. Commerce moved to case-by-case reviews of licences in mid-January. Earlier reporting in May, citing anonymous sources, said about 10 Chinese companies had been cleared, including Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and JD.com, with Lenovo and Foxconn approved as distributors. The Times now puts the total at roughly two dozen, including Baidu, BYD and Tesla Shanghai. Each was cleared to buy tens of thousands of chips, and none has yet reached its quota.

The commercial stakes so far are modest. H200 shipments to China-based customers were under 1% of Nvidia's data-centre revenue in the quarter ended in July. Beijing is the other brake. It reportedly approved about 400,000 H200 chips for Alibaba, ByteDance and Tencent in January. Separate reports say an estimated 600,000 chips sit in Hong Kong awaiting Chinese import approval that has not come at scale.

What to watch

The nearest date is November 18. Three House committee chairs have asked Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang to brief them on that day. The Times article ties the request to separate reporting on a US memo linking Huang to a suspected Chinese spy, which the Financial Times first reported. Any questions about licensing diligence would likely come up in the same room.

Beyond that, the useful signals are concrete. One is whether Commerce reviews or suspends the New Matrix and Maginfra approvals. Another is whether it publishes the licensee list. The third is whether Nvidia's next earnings report shows China data-centre sales moving from a rounding error to something that matters. If it does, the question of who is on the licence list will carry more weight than it does today.
