Rise in NHS birth centre closures is narrowing maternity choice, experts say

In one sentence
Experts say NHS birth-centre closures are narrowing where women can give birth, but the verifiable figures are from 2019 and no current national count was confirmed.

Fewer women in England may be able to choose a midwife-led birth close to home, according to experts who say a run of NHS birth-centre closures is narrowing where women can give birth. The sharper claim in the original headline, that this amounts to a denial of women's rights, is the experts' characterisation. It is not an established finding. The more defensible story is the shrinking of choice of place.

One caution first. The most detailed reporting this article could verify is a Guardian investigation by Denis Campbell, published on 11 February 2019. It is not a current count. No up-to-date national tally of closures, with dates and named units, could be confirmed, so the figures below are historical and dated as such.

What the reporting found

The 2019 Guardian report said NHS bodies had shut eight birth centres in England. The Royal College of Midwives (RCM) put the number of midwife-led units at about 160. Roughly 60 are freestanding and about 100 sit alongside hospital obstetric units.

The named cases show a mix of causes:

  • Cossham, Bristol: closed temporarily in October 2018. About 350 babies a year were born there. The trust said it lacked the staff to run two units safely, and some local campaigners doubted it would reopen.
  • Trowbridge, Wiltshire: births fell from 291 in 2015-16 to 109 in the first nine months of 2018-19.
  • Paulton, Somerset: births fell from 154 to 94 over the same period.
  • Friarwood, Pontefract and Castleford: about three or four births a week. The chief executive of Mid Yorkshire Hospitals NHS trust, Martin Barkley, said it was "right to review its existence".

The official reasons were staffing and low usage, not a national policy decision. Helen Blanchard, North Bristol's interim director of nursing and quality, called the Cossham closure "a temporary measure" and said the priority was safety. This matters for interpretation. Low use can be a consequence of limited staffing and referral practices as much as a cause of closure, a point the evidence here does not settle.

What the experts said

Elizabeth Duff, a senior policy adviser at the NCT, called the closures "a restriction of the choice that women have been promised for many years by government ministers". Sean O'Sullivan of the RCM said closing a birth centre "will inevitably limit choice of place of birth". The Care Quality Commission's survey at the time found women's ability to choose had declined in some parts of England.

These are expert opinions on choice. The phrases "shocking" and "denial of rights" were not verified against a named speaker, and are not repeated here as fact.

Women in England have been promised a choice of birth setting since 2007. The case for protecting that choice partly rests on the 2011 Birthplace study in the BMJ. It found that for healthy women with low-risk pregnancies, births in midwife-led units were not associated with worse outcomes than in obstetric units, though first-time mothers planning a home birth had higher rates of adverse outcomes.

What to watch

The test is data. NHS England's maternity statistics and the CQC's maternity survey can show whether the number of midwife-led units, and the share of births in them, are falling. Local consultations on individual units will show whether closures are temporary, as in the Cossham case, or permanent. Until a current count is published, the scale of any recent rise remains unconfirmed.
