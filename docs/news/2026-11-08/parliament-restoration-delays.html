Commons Speaker calls Parliament restoration delays 'frustrating'

In one sentence
Speaker Sir Lindsay Hoyle says his biggest frustration is that Parliament is 'not getting on with the work' of restoration, as MPs warn costs could reach £40bn.

The Speaker of the House of Commons has said publicly what many inside Westminster say in private: the repair of the Palace of Westminster is not moving fast enough. Sir Lindsay Hoyle told the BBC that his "biggest frustration" with the restoration of the Victorian building is that "we're not getting on with the work."

The remarks, reported by the BBC and relayed by Xinhua on 7 November, come from the person who chairs the Commons and sits on the bodies that oversee the building. That makes them more than a backbencher's complaint. They are an on-record admission that the programme is slipping.

What the Speaker said

As reported, Hoyle described the approval process for essential renovations as a form of self-restraint on Parliament, saying more time is spent on surveys than on repairs. He also blamed the law. It is, he said, "very frustrating when the Act of Parliament prevents you carrying out the major repairs that you need to." That points at the statutory framework governing the programme, not just at contractors or civil servants.

He also questioned why a fire-suppression mist system has still not been installed throughout the building, although he has called for one for several years. That detail matters because fire is the risk that most concentrates minds about the Palace. The wider restoration is a decades-long engineering question. A sprinkler-type system is a discrete job, so the delay is easier for a layperson to judge.

The exact wording of the Speaker's full remarks should be read against the BBC original, which this account relies on only at second hand.

The numbers behind the frustration

The context is a warning from the Commons Public Accounts Committee that the project has reached "crunch time." According to the reported summary, the committee said the work could cost up to £40 billion and take as long as 61 years. Its chairman said each additional year of delay costs taxpayers £420 million, and raised the prospect of a fire on the scale of the 2019 Notre-Dame blaze in Paris.

Those figures are the top of a range, not a forecast. The costed proposals published in February 2026 set out the choice. One option is a full decant, in which MPs and peers move out and work from temporary chambers and offices while the building is repaired. The other is a phased approach that keeps Parliament in place but stretches the programme out. The first is faster and cheaper in the long run but disruptive. The second is more convenient for members but carries a longer exposure to the building's decay.

Will it force a change of plan?

A Speaker's frustration does not by itself change a programme. Decisions on decant, funding and the legislation that governs the work rest with the two Houses and the government, and MPs have a long record of avoiding a choice that requires them to leave the building.

Still, Hoyle's intervention shifts the argument. It ties the delay to a specific, fixable obstacle in the form of the Act, and it adds a public voice to the committee's cost warning. If the Speaker is willing to say the legal framework is itself the brake, pressure builds for ministers to bring forward amending legislation or a decision on the decant question. The next markers to watch are the government's response to the Public Accounts Committee report and any move to put the options to a vote in both Houses.
