Activist circles McCormick as Unilever's food exit puts brand autonomy in play

McCormick & Co. World Headquarters, Hunt Valley, Maryland — the global base for what would become a $20 billion flavour company — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Activist Toms Capital has built a stake in McCormick as the spice maker pursues its $44.8B Unilever food deal, signalling shareholder pressure on integration delivery.

When activist investors circle a company mid-acquisition, the signal is usually the same: they think the deal works, and they're positioning for the upside — or ready to push if management loses its nerve. Toms Capital Investment Management, the New York firm run by Benjamin Pass, built a significant stake in McCormick & Co (NYSE: MKC) during the second quarter of 2026, after the spice maker announced its $44.8 billion combination with Unilever's food division on March 31.

The size of Toms Capital's position has not been publicly disclosed. The firm — which manages approximately $2.7 billion in discretionary assets and has operated since 2017 — tends to take concentrated stakes and advocate for change quietly, without agitating publicly unless pushed. Its arrival at McCormick, just weeks after the largest deal in the company's history, points to a firm that has done its arithmetic and likes what it sees.

A $45 Billion Flavor Bet

The transaction would marry McCormick's existing herbs, spices, and hot sauce portfolio with a collection of some of the world's most recognised condiment and cooking brands: Hellmann's mayonnaise, Knorr stock cubes and seasonings, Maille mustard, Cholula hot sauce, Frank's RedHot, and Marmite. When complete — expected in mid-2027, pending shareholder votes and regulatory clearance in multiple markets — the combined company would generate roughly $20 billion in annual sales and operate across approximately 100 countries, with five core markets prioritised for value capture: the United States, China, the United Kingdom and Ireland, France, and Mexico.

The deal's architecture is notable. McCormick is paying $15.7 billion in cash, but the share component — $29.1 billion in stock — leaves Unilever shareholders owning 55.1 percent of the enlarged group, with Unilever PLC retaining a further 9.9 percent stake it intends to sell down. McCormick shareholders, in other words, will hold a minority position in their own company once the transaction closes. Global headquarters stays in Hunt Valley, Maryland; a secondary European listing and international headquarters will be in the Netherlands. Brendan Foley remains CEO.

McCormick has already moved quickly on integration planning: Andrew Foust, the company's Americas president, was named Chief Integration Officer in April. The target is $600 million in annual savings within three years — a number that will concentrate analyst attention throughout 2026 and into the close. Analysts point to input cost pressure, private-label competition, and regulatory complexity as the principal risks alongside execution. Integrating acquired portfolios of this scale has historically been the source of value destruction in comparable deals, and the $600 million synergy figure sets a clear benchmark against which Foust's team will be measured.

When Brands Change Hands

The brand autonomy question is not hypothetical. As Unilever tidies its portfolio, a parallel fight is playing out at what used to be its ice cream division. Ben Cohen, co-founder of Ben & Jerry's, has been publicly campaigning for the brand's sale to socially-minded investors since the December 2025 listing of The Magnum Ice Cream Company — the standalone entity spun off to house Unilever's ice cream portfolio, which generated €7.9 billion in revenue in 2025.

Cohen's argument is familiar: that Ben & Jerry's founding commitment to social activism is incompatible with the margin demands of a listed conglomerate. The 2000 acquisition agreement purportedly guaranteed the brand's operational autonomy; in practice, that guarantee has proved difficult to enforce. Cohen says Magnum is stifling the very characteristics that gave the brand its identity, and wants it sold outright.

The relevance to McCormick is not exact — Hellmann's and Knorr are not mission-driven companies in the Ben & Jerry's mould — but the structural tension is the same. When distinct brands move under new ownership, the new parent faces pressure to streamline, standardise, and extract margin. Hellmann's built its consumer loyalty partly through a differentiated sustainability and provenance message. Knorr has its own regional identities in 90-odd countries. Rationalising those across a single flavour platform without eroding what makes the brands worth buying is precisely the kind of integration question that activists scrutinise — and that management will have to answer publicly.

Toms Capital's next disclosure will matter. If the firm crosses U.S. reporting thresholds, a 13D or 13G filing would reveal both the actual scale of its position and, in the case of a 13D, its stated intentions. McCormick's integration updates — the first substantive ones likely to arrive at the next earnings call — will give the market its first read on whether the $600 million synergy target is credibly on track.

The mid-2027 close deadline is a long horizon. Toms Capital's presence signals that at least one sophisticated investor has concluded that McCormick, at current prices, offers enough upside to make the wait worthwhile. The Ben & Jerry's saga, playing out in a different aisle, is a running reminder of what happens when the efficiency logic overwhelms the brands that justified the price in the first place.
