Barclays faces City questions over pre-collapse exits from scandal-hit MFS

Barclays' global headquarters at One Churchill Place, Canary Wharf — its structured lending arm was MFS's largest single creditor — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Barclays reduced its MFS exposure in November 2025 while detecting irregularities — raising City questions about what it knew and when, even as its £228m write-down now looks bounded.

Barclays began flagging concerns about Market Financial Solutions months before the specialist lender imploded — and the sequence of what the bank did next has drawn scrutiny from people across the City.

In November 2025, Barclays started restricting transactions linked to MFS after detecting irregularities in its accounts. Within weeks, a deal was structured in which a company tied to MFS borrowed approximately £143 million from Wells Fargo — with Barclays being repaid roughly that amount as a result. In early January 2026, the UK bank froze all of MFS's accounts outright. The administration filing followed on 20 February.

The question that has circulated among creditors and within the structured finance community since: if Barclays knew enough in November to restrict transactions and facilitate its own partial exit, what did it owe to the other lenders who remained in the trade?

Barclays has not been accused of wrongdoing. Its position, stated publicly, is that it "identified irregularities at MFS before its insolvency" and acted accordingly. Chief executive CS Venkatakrishnan, disclosing a £228 million impairment in the bank's first-quarter update, described the failure as "a fairly deep and sophisticated fraud." The bank said separately that funds appeared to have been "fraudulently circulated" around MFS's network of companies "on a massive scale."

But in a lending syndicate, early knowledge cuts both ways. The freeze on MFS's accounts in January — Barclays' decisive intervention — was the event that triggered the chain of events leading to administration. The November deal, in which Wells Fargo stepped in and Barclays was repaid, happened in the same window in which the UK bank says it was first detecting problems. Whether the timing of that exit meets the expectations of other creditors is the subject of ongoing, if informal, discussion.

A fraud built on borrowed property

MFS was founded in 2006 by Paresh Raja, a Mayfair-based operator who built the firm into a £2.3 billion institutional loan book by 2024, specialising in bridge loans and buy-to-let finance — the fast, flexible end of property lending that mainstream banks largely avoid. Turnover hit £71 million in 2024. Raja cultivated a public profile and won awards for innovation in specialist lending.

The administrators — AlixPartners — found a rather different picture inside the books. Against £1.2 billion owed to institutional creditors, actual collateral was estimated at around £230 million. The core mechanism was double-pledging: the same property assets used as security against multiple loans simultaneously, concealed behind what officials described as "systematic dishonest reporting." Court filings allege that eight companies presented as independent borrowers from the MFS group were in fact connected to individuals associated with Raja himself.

Mr Justice Briggs, overseeing the insolvency proceedings, noted that "the allegations of fraud are very serious." A worldwide asset freezing order — covering up to £1.3 billion — was imposed on Raja by courts in London and Dubai in March 2026. He is believed to be in the United Arab Emirates and denies all fraud allegations.

The total pool of creditors caught in the collapse is substantial: Barclays with up to £600 million in gross exposure, Apollo Global Management's Atlas SP unit with roughly £400 million, Jefferies at approximately £103 million, and further exposure at Santander, Wells Fargo, Elliott Investment Management, and Castlelake. Combined borrowings from the group exceeded £2.6 billion.

The auditors are now under their own scrutiny. The Financial Reporting Council has opened probes into Berkeley Finch, which issued an unqualified opinion on MFS's 2024 accounts, Silver Levene UK, which audited the connected Zircon Group, and Magus Chartered Accountants, along with several named individuals. A spokesperson for Silver Levene said the investigation "does not indicate that any findings have been made."

Questions that won't settle

For Barclays specifically, the MFS affair has produced a layered set of awkward questions beyond the headline write-down.

The Bank of England's Prudential Regulation Authority has expressed concern that insufficient risk assessment and due diligence was conducted by the warehouse lenders — including Barclays — on MFS and its affiliated entities. The FCA opened a formal enforcement investigation into MFS in March 2026.

A further thread: Rihan Dewan, a high-yield credit trader who worked at Barclays from 2022 until September 2025 — when he moved to Deutsche Bank — is understood to be the son-in-law of Paresh Raja. Dewan is not alleged to have any involvement in the MFS lending, and Barclays' structured lending functions are separate from its credit trading desk. But people inside the bank have noted the coincidence, and it adds to the texture of questions about oversight.

Barclays' response to the MFS fallout has been structural: the bank is pulling back from asset-based lending to smaller borrowers and, in Venkatakrishnan's words, will constrain lending to structured finance counterparties that "cannot convince us of the quality and independence of their financial controls." This is the lesson drawn — though critics will note the controls that mattered most were the ones that should have been applied before the exposure was built.

Despite the £228 million impairment, Barclays shares have recovered sharply from their post-collapse low. The stock currently trades at around 445p, roughly 14% below the analyst consensus target of 543p, and has delivered a one-year total return of more than 50%. The market's judgment appears to be that the MFS hit, though material, is bounded — the CEO's assurances that the total impairment would be "materially below" the £500 million gross exposure figure have been largely accepted.

What remains open is regulatory rather than financial. The FCA's investigation into MFS is at an early stage, and the PRA's scrutiny of warehouse lenders' due diligence practices has not been resolved. Whether the November 2025 sequence — the transaction restrictions, the Wells Fargo repayment, the eventual freeze — is seen as competent risk management or a duty of disclosure missed will be a question for those investigations to examine. Other creditors who were not repaid before the collapse may have views of their own.
