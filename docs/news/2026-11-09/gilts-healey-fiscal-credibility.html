Gilts test Healey's pledge to keep the Reeves fiscal rules after the Budget

In one sentence
Gilt yields eased to about 5.3% after Healey's 28 October Budget kept the Reeves rules, but thin headroom of roughly £15bn leaves the market's verdict open.

Gilts have been the part of the UK market that the new Chancellor cannot talk around. John Healey delivered his first Budget on 28 October with a pledge to keep the Reeves fiscal rules intact. Ten-year yields then eased to about 5.3%, which is still high by the standards of the past two decades. The question for investors is whether that relief reflects belief in the rules or only the absence of a bad surprise.

A market already on edge

The Budget landed after a rough autumn for government debt. The 10-year gilt yield reached about 5.44% in late September, the highest since July 2007, and touched roughly 5.46% around 14 October, according to Trading Economics. At the long end the 30-year yield rose above 6%, a level last seen in 1998, in what City AM described as a global bond sell-off that boxed Healey in.

Those moves cost the Treasury room to manoeuvre. One think-tank estimate put fiscal headroom against the rules at about £13bn before the Budget, because higher yields raise debt interest forecasts. The headroom is the margin by which the Chancellor meets his own borrowing test. With a margin that thin, small shifts in yields can decide whether tax rises are needed.

Bank Rate has added to the pressure. Markets were pricing a Bank of England hike on 5 November, with a further rise expected by February. A central bank tightening while the Treasury borrows heavily is a different backdrop from the one Rachel Reeves faced when she wrote the rules.

What the Budget did

The fiscal watchdog put headroom at about £15bn for 2029–30, which was in line with market expectations. Healey paired that with tax increases on banks, gambling, savings and landlords. The debt-sale plan was also broadly in line with forecasts. Portfolio Adviser called the Budget "broadly consistent with market expectations", and City AM labelled it a "placeholder Budget" that avoided a backlash.

Gilt yields fell afterwards. On 29 October the 10-year yield dropped to 5.29%, the lowest since 22 September.

A fall of about 15 to 20 basis points from the October peak is real but modest. It brings yields back to where they were a month earlier, not to anything resembling pre-rout levels. The reaction can be read two ways. The charitable reading is that the market accepted the rules as binding and found the Budget compliant. The sceptical reading is that headroom of £15bn is thin enough to be erased by a modest rise in yields or a weaker growth forecast, so the pledge is only as strong as the next data point.

The credibility question

The distinction matters because the fiscal rules are only useful if investors believe they will be kept when keeping them hurts. Healey has said he will keep them. His Budget kept them, with taxes raised to do so. That is the first piece of evidence, and it is a favourable one.

The test comes later. Yields are driven partly by factors a Chancellor does not control: Bank Rate, energy-led inflation and a global bond market that has pushed long-dated borrowing costs higher in many countries. If yields climb again, headroom shrinks, and the choice returns to further tax rises, spending cuts or a rewrite of the rules. Commentators have noted that a visible rewrite is what the market would punish most.

The stakes reach beyond trading desks. Gilt yields feed into the government's interest bill, set the base for fixed-rate mortgage pricing and determine the value of pension liabilities. Households are already absorbing a higher Bank Rate.

Figures here come from Trading Economics and press summaries. Dated November yield levels could not be confirmed, so the latest verified reading is from 29 October.

What to watch

The next fixed points are the Bank of England's decisions on Bank Rate, the Debt Management Office's gilt auctions and the next fiscal forecast from the OBR. A 10-year yield that holds below 5.3% would suggest the pledge is being accepted. A return towards 5.5%, or a 30-year yield back above 6%, would suggest that the market is still pricing a gap between what the Chancellor says and what the arithmetic allows.
