National Grid's near-£50bn debt pile raises questions over energy bills as H1 results land

In one sentence
National Grid's net debt rose to about £48.6bn as record capex pushed earnings up, but the size of any bill impact depends on Ofgem's allowed returns, which the results don't quantify.

National Grid is spending money on the network faster than at any point in its history, and borrowing to do it. Net debt reached about £48.6bn at the end of September, roughly £4.4bn higher than at the March year-end, according to secondary coverage of the half-year results published on 5 November. The Telegraph has framed that near-£50bn pile as a likely route to higher household energy bills. The mechanism is real. The size of the effect, though, is not something the company's results or the press framing settle.

What the half-year numbers show

The results are strong on earnings and heavy on spending. The company reported record first-half capital investment of £6.7bn, up 33% on £5.05bn a year earlier (about 32% at constant currency). Underlying earnings per share rose 22% to 36.5p, from 29.8p. Statutory EPS was 26.9p, against 12.6p. The interim dividend rose 4% to 16.97p.

Management lifted its guidance accordingly. Underlying EPS for 2026/27 is now expected to grow 15–16% from the 2025/26 baseline of 78.0p. The medium-term plan calls for at least £70bn of cumulative capital spending across 2026/27 to 2030/31, with around £13.5bn deployed this year. Group assets are expected to compound at about 10% a year.

The net debt figure comes from Interactive Investor's write-up, which said analysts had expected about £49.5bn. The £48.6bn is therefore slightly better than forecast, though still a sizeable rise. The figure has not been checked against the primary results statement, and the near-£50bn in the Telegraph's headline is a rounding of it. A further caution: the debt is group-wide, so it includes US and other non-UK operations. Not all of it sits behind UK bills.

How debt reaches bills

National Grid's UK networks are regulated monopolies. Ofgem sets price controls that allow the companies to recover efficient costs and earn a return on a regulated asset base. The return includes an allowance for the cost of debt. When the asset base grows, because more cables, pylons and substations are being built, the allowed revenue grows with it. Network charges are collected through supplier bills.

That is the logic behind the Telegraph's forecast that debt will feed through into higher bills. It is the paper's projection, not a statement from the company. The results themselves point to the same direction of travel, noting the "first-year impact of the new RIIO-T3 price control" as supporting higher allowed revenue. The company is also working on its ED3 business plan for electricity distribution.

The results give no Ofgem allowed-return figure in the material reviewed, so how much of the borrowing is covered by regulated returns, and at what rate, cannot be read from them. That is the number a bill estimate depends on. Without it, any per-household figure would be conjecture.

Leverage as a design feature

Rising debt is not a surprise in this business. The company says regulatory gearing, which is debt against regulated asset value, was 61% in 2025/26 and is expected to return to the high 60% range by 2030/31. In other words, the plan assumes more leverage as the asset base grows. Funding the build-out with debt rather than equity is cheaper for the company and, within the regulatory formula, usually for consumers too, provided Ofgem's allowed cost of debt tracks what the company pays.

The argument over bills is therefore less about whether National Grid borrows and more about who bears the risk if borrowing costs rise faster than the allowance, and whether the £70bn programme is sized correctly. Critics argue that consumers fund guaranteed returns on a growing asset base. The company's case is that grid reinforcement is a precondition for connecting new generation and demand. Both can be true, and the half-year statement does not adjudicate between them.

What to watch

The first test is the primary document: the full results statement should confirm the net debt figure and its split between UK and US operations. The second is Ofgem's allowed-return position under RIIO-T3 and the ED3 process, which will determine how much of the borrowing is recoverable and over what period. Until those figures are set against the £70bn plan, the Telegraph's forecast remains plausible but unquantified.
