CLIMATE · EV MANDATE · By S. Whitfield · 2026-11-09 · 3 min read

Government expected to water down EV sales targets this week

In one sentence
Ministers are reportedly set to relax the zero-emission vehicle mandate after carmaker lobbying, but no decision or draft rules have been published.

Ministers are expected to loosen the rules that force carmakers to sell electric vehicles, according to reports attributed to the BBC and Electrifying.com. No decision has been announced. Nothing in the material available for this article, such as a government statement or draft regulations, confirms what would change or when. If the reports are right, the policy that the car and charging industries have planned around would shift under pressure from the industry it regulates.

What the mandate requires

The Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandate has been in force since January 2024. It sets a rising share of each manufacturer's new car sales that must be zero-emission: 22% in 2024, 80% in 2030 and 100% in 2035, under the government's 2023 policy. Carmakers that miss their quota pay a fine on each car by which they fall short.

The fine has reportedly been cut from £15,000 to £12,000 per car, a change dated to 2025. That figure has not been checked against the official rules, and the current level should be confirmed before it is relied on. The trajectory, though, is the part that matters for planning. A target set years ahead lets manufacturers decide which models to build and where to allocate battery supply. It lets charging operators decide how many chargers to install and where.

The reported shift

The suggestion is that ministers will soften the mandate after lobbying by carmakers. The reports do not say which lever would move. It could be the 2030 milestone, the interim annual quotas, the penalty, or the flexibilities that let manufacturers trade credits or count hybrids. Each would have a different effect. A lower fine weakens the incentive without changing the headline target. A lower 2030 share changes the target itself. The two are easy to blur in political shorthand, and the distinction will matter once any text is published.

The case carmakers make is a familiar one. They argue that consumer demand for electric cars has not kept pace with the quotas, and that selling at a loss to hit the targets is not sustainable. Critics respond that the industry knew the schedule when it set its investment plans, and that diluting it rewards the slowest movers at the expense of those that complied. Both arguments are about who carries the cost of the transition. Neither is settled by the unconfirmed reports.

Who is exposed

A softer 2030 target would reduce demand certainty for electric vehicle makers and for public charging operators. Their business cases assume a growing fleet of plug-in cars. Slower growth in the fleet would mean lower charger utilisation and a longer wait for returns on sites already built. That could shift investment and share prices in those sectors, though the size of any move depends on what is actually changed.

There is also a wider test. The mandate is one of the few binding mechanisms in UK climate policy that acts on private sector behaviour rather than public spending. Whether it holds under industry pressure indicates how firmly other targets are likely to be held. It also bears on the electricity system the cars would draw on, a theme in the debate over National Grid's debt and energy bills.

What to watch

The first marker is an official statement or a consultation response from the government. The second is draft regulations, which would show whether the change touches the 2030 share, the interim quotas or only the penalty. Until one of those appears, the reported softening remains an expectation. The decision, if it comes this week as reported, will show whether a stated climate commitment can be adjusted for industry relief without the underlying target being formally abandoned.
