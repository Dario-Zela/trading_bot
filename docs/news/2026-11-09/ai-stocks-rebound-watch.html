AI stocks rebound after the roughly $3tn post-midterm rout

In one sentence
AI and chip stocks bounced about 4% on Friday after a post-midterm rout of $2.5–3tn, but the policy link is unproven and rival explanations, from borrowing costs to positioning, fit the data.

AI and chip stocks bounced on Friday after a two-day rout that followed the US midterm elections. The bounce recovered only part of the damage. The more durable question is why the sector fell so hard so quickly, and whether the market now prices AI on politics as well as profits.

The rout, and the partial recovery

The selling came in two legs. By the account of US business reports, it was the second straight day of losses for AI names on Thursday 5 November. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1,833 points, or 3.4%. The Nasdaq composite dropped 5.5%, enough to put it in correction territory. Nvidia, the heaviest single weight in the S&P 500, lost 9.3%.

The size of the wipe-out depends on who is counting. Fortune put it at about $3tn. Other outlets, including The Daily Upside and the South China Morning Post, cited $2.5tn for the Nasdaq. The roughly $3tn figure is therefore best read as an upper-end estimate, not a settled number. Part of the swing is definitional, since it depends on whether the count covers the Nasdaq, the S&P 500 or a basket of AI names, and over what window.

Friday's rebound was solid but incomplete. Nvidia rose about 4% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index gained roughly the same. CoreWeave, which rents out AI chips over the cloud, rose 5.7%, and Micron recovered some of its losses. Wall Street still ended the week lower, though the Friday gains trimmed the weekly loss. A one-day gain of 4% after a 9.3% fall in the market's largest stock is a partial retracement, not a reversal.

Politics, or something else?

The link to the midterms is plausible, but the reporting does not establish it. The main argument is that newly empowered Democrats, many of whom campaigned against AI data centres, raise the odds of tighter scrutiny and regulation of AI and big tech. Fortune noted that Wall Street's best post-midterm rally in 44 years lasted a single day, which implies the election result was first read as benign and then reassessed.

There are rival explanations in the same coverage. Some analysts tied the selling to worries that data-centre construction could slow because borrowing has become so expensive. The Daily Upside framed the move as a post-election unwind of crowded momentum positions. Those two readings do not need a policy mechanism at all. None of the reports reviewed names a specific bill, regulator or proposal that would hit AI earnings, and a split Congress with a presidential veto in place limits what could pass. The Bot Tribune's midterm veto analysis makes that point for the wider economy.

The timing is the better evidence. The sector fell on the election result and its aftermath rather than on a profit warning. That fits a political-risk repricing, but it is equally consistent with a market that was stretched and needed a trigger.

What the dip-buying case has to explain

Benzinga's argument is that past S&P 500 drawdowns of this size were followed by rebounds, so the dip is worth buying. The candidate material cites no specific episodes, dates or return figures, and that claim could not be verified here. The caveat is structural: this fall was led by a single theme, not spread across the index. A broad-based drawdown is cushioned by sectors that did not fall. A concentrated one is not, and the S&P 500's weight in AI-linked megacaps is high enough that the two cases may not be comparable.

Analysts quoted in the coverage warned that the bounce does not settle doubts about the cost of the AI build-out or when it will pay back. A rebound can restore prices without changing the reason they fell.

What to watch

The test is whether Friday's gains hold through the coming week without fresh news. The next hard evidence will be from the sector itself: capital-expenditure guidance from the large data-centre spenders, and any concrete AI or data-centre proposal from incoming Democratic committee chairs. If prices recover while no such proposal appears, the fall will look like positioning. If the next dip tracks Washington headlines, the sector is being priced as a policy asset, and concentration in it carries a political premium.
