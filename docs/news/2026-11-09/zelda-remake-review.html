Zelda remake: can it capture the magic of the original?

In one sentence
Nintendo's Ocarina of Time remake for Switch 2 wins praise as faithful and polished, but one reviewer finds it the 'safest of safe bets' that cannot replace the 1998 original.

Nintendo has gone back to its most revered game. The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, first released in 1998, returned this week as a remake for the Switch 2, and the early reviews agree on the main point: it is faithful, polished and cautious. The harder question is whether a cautious remake can bring back the feeling of the original. It is also a useful test of how far Nintendo can lean on its back catalogue.

A note on sourcing: the BBC Technology review that prompted this piece could not be located, so what follows draws on other published reviews and not on the BBC's own verdict.

Faithful, and then some

The remake launched on 5 November. Reviewing it for NPR, James Perkins Mastromarino called it one of the most faithful adaptations he had seen. He ranked it below the Demon's Souls remake but above the Final Fantasy VII remake trilogy and the Resident Evil 4 remake.

The changes are mostly about comfort. Dedicated jump and sprint buttons and a quick item menu make it play more like a modern game. A new "Threads of Time" quest log helps players who are stuck, and the Water Temple, long a byword for tedium, is "much more tolerable". Textures and animations are upgraded. Some character redesigns land in the uncanny valley, and Nabooru is the example Mastromarino gives. Enemy targeting is still clumsy, and the Biggoron Sword quest is still convoluted.

Some of the original's habits have also been softened. Mido is less abrasive, the owl Kaepora Gaebora no longer repeats himself, and Navi has stopped constantly shouting "Hey! Listen!" Video Games Chronicle gave the game five out of five, praising its respect for the source material, though it noted that the English voice cast may not suit everyone. TechRadar credited the controls with improving the pacing but did not count difficulty among the game's strengths. The Week describes the update as faithful and "pitch perfect".

The ghost of 1998

The dissent is quiet but pointed. Mastromarino admired the game and still came away feeling "oddly bereft". A remake can preserve a design, he suggests, but it cannot replace the memory of meeting that design for the first time, and the 1998 original was a generation-defining game. He calls the remake "the safest of safe bets", and means it as a description more than an insult.

That is the tension in Nintendo's approach. A remake that changes little protects the franchise and pleases the people who already love it. But a game this faithful is also asking to be judged against the original, and the original had novelty on its side. Nostalgia is a strong sales pitch and a weak creative one.

What comes next

The commercial side is easier to read than the emotional one. A remake of an acclaimed game carries little risk, and reviews so far suggest it delivers. Mastromarino expects it to keep the game relevant for decades and hopes it leads to a Majora's Mask remake. Whether Nintendo follows with that or another back-catalogue title is the thing to watch. Sales figures and any announcement of the next remake will show whether this is a one-off or the start of a pattern.
