Russell Brand case: Nandy says 'time is up' on abuse in media

In one sentence
Culture secretary Lisa Nandy says “time is up” for the media industry after Russell Brand’s convictions, but has so far offered only voluntary steps and a pledge to consider more.

Lisa Nandy has told the media industry that “time is up” on the abuse of women, three days after a jury convicted Russell Brand of rape and sexual assault. What the culture secretary has not yet done is attach a rule, a regulator or a deadline to the warning.

Brand was found guilty at Southwark Crown Court on Thursday 5 November of one count of rape and three of sexual assault. He was acquitted on the remaining counts, including two of rape and one of attempted indecent assault. He denied all the allegations. The convictions relate to incidents between 2004 and 2009: a rape at his home, a groping at a party, an incident at a London radio station, and an assault during an advertisement recording. Mr Justice Bennathan has told him to expect a “significant custodial term”, and he remains in prison pending sentence on Monday. The Metropolitan Police say a separate inquiry into rape allegations by two other women is continuing.

What Nandy said

Appearing on the BBC’s Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg and on Sky News, Nandy was blunt. “Time is up, it really is time for the media industry to take this seriously,” she said on the BBC. She told Sky that the sector needs “much stronger” protections for women who come forward, and that it is not doing enough.

She also doubted that anyone could claim the abuse “couldn’t happen today”, suggesting it is probably happening now. Asked what had changed in recent years in how institutions help people to speak up, she said she could not see much. Media and entertainment, like politics, carry particular responsibility, she argued: short-term, flexible contracts and steep power imbalances mean that complaining can cost a worker their reputation and their next job.

That last point echoes the testimony. Several complainants said they stayed silent because of Brand’s fame, fear of disbelief or worry for their careers. One told the court the industry “protects its own”. Another said that in 2009 two producers told her Brand would keep working only if she gave him a massage; she complained to her line manager, but said nothing came of it.

Rhetoric or rulebook?

The concrete ask was modest. Nandy urged media organisations to join the Creative Industries Independent Standards Authority, an industry body that protects whistleblowers. Joining is voluntary. On anything stronger, she said only that the government is “considering what further steps we can take”.

That leaves the real question open. Reports of her interviews carry no mention of legislation, a timetable, or new duties on broadcasters and producers around complaint handling, vetting or reporting, and Ofcom did not feature. Until ministers name one of those, the statement is a call for self-policing, aimed at an industry that has heard such calls before. Reviews at the BBC and at Banijay, the production group behind some of Brand’s work, followed the 2023 documentary that first aired the allegations. The BBC’s found that staff between 2006 and 2008 felt unable to raise concerns; Banijay’s found no formal complaints had been made. Broadcasters say they have since strengthened duty-of-care protections, though without public specifics.

The pressure may grow. Metropolitan Police chief Sir Mark Rowley said he “wouldn’t be surprised” if many more people came forward. A US civil suit over an alleged 2010 incident on the set of Arthur is also continuing, after a judge rejected Warner Bros’ bid to have it dismissed.

What to watch

Brand’s sentencing on Monday will set the tone, and a long term would sharpen the question of what the industry did, and did not, do at the time. After that, the test is simple: whether the government’s “further steps” arrive as a consultation, a statutory duty or nothing at all. Nandy, who this week also defended herself against claims of politicising Remembrance Sunday, has now put her name to a promise that can be checked.
