Government 'looking seriously' at banning under-16s from Vinted and eBay

In one sentence
Ministers say they are 'looking seriously' at under-16 limits on Vinted and eBay, but no mechanism, timetable or official text has been published.

Ministers are weighing whether children under 16 should be kept off Vinted and eBay. The signal is early and hedged. The only public detail is a BBC Business headline saying the government is "looking seriously" at the idea. A search for the underlying report, the minister's exact words or any official text found nothing, so the mechanism, the legal basis and the timetable are all unverified.

What is actually known

The headline names two platforms: Vinted, the secondhand fashion app, and eBay, the long-running auction and fixed-price marketplace. It implies age restrictions that would reach peer-to-peer selling and buying, which are not currently covered by the under-16 debate in the way social media is.

There is no confirmed draft, consultation document or announcement. Nothing public says whether the idea would be a ban on holding an account, a ban on selling, or a requirement for age checks. Nor is there any user data on how many under-16s use either site. Both platforms already set minimum ages in their terms, but those rules rely largely on self-declaration.

Why marketplaces are an odd fit

The UK's youth-safety debate has centred on social media. Ministers have weighed an outright under-16 ban against softer options such as app curfews and limits on use during school hours. Resale marketplaces were not the obvious next target. The pitch for covering them would presumably rest on the same grounds as other online-safety measures: contact between strangers, direct messaging, and payments involving minors. Those are the concerns to watch for if the idea firms up. This is inference from the wider debate, not something stated in the reporting.

The case against is practical. Teenagers selling outgrown clothes is a long-standing habit, and parents often run or supervise those accounts. A blanket ban would also force age verification on every user, adults included, which raises cost and privacy questions for the platforms.

There is a separate regulatory thread abroad. French authorities have questioned Vinted over minors' access to adult content, and a French minister has met marketplace operators over riot-looted goods. Neither is an age-limit measure, but both show resale platforms drawing more official attention in Europe.

What it could mean for the platforms

If limits did arrive, the costs would be verification technology, account clean-ups and the loss of some young sellers and buyers. How large that hit would be depends on figures nobody has published. For eBay, which is listed, the exposure is probably small next to its global business, though that is an assumption. For Vinted, which is privately held and built around fashion resale, a lot of its core activity involves younger users.

What to watch

The next real data point would be a formal consultation or a ministerial statement setting out what is proposed and which services would be covered. Until then, "looking seriously" describes an intention to examine the question, not a decision to act. Any rule would also have to be squared with the age-assurance duties that already apply to platforms under existing online-safety law.
