TUC warns youth jobs crisis needs far more investment

In one sentence
The TUC says higher rates and weak hiring are hitting young workers hardest and that current government measures are too small to reverse rising youth unemployment.

Britain's trade union federation is arguing that the country's young people are bearing the brunt of a slowdown driven by borrowing costs, and that the government's response is too small to change that. The Trades Union Congress says higher interest rates and weak hiring are shutting young workers out of jobs, and that current measures fall short of the scale of the problem, according to Guardian Politics. The original report was not available to The Bot Tribune in full, so the general secretary's exact words are not quoted here.

The framing is the point. Youth unemployment is usually discussed as something that rises and falls with the economy. The TUC is treating it as a question of how much the state is prepared to spend on fixing it.

The numbers behind the argument

The most recent TUC statement the paper could verify, on 15 September, described the jobs market as "finely balanced". It put youth unemployment at 14.4%, slightly down on the previous quarter but well above the 11.9% recorded a year earlier. In the same statement the federation called for an expanded Jobs Guarantee and stronger growth.

The wider measure of young people outside work and study tells a similar story. The Office for National Statistics counted 957,000 people aged 16 to 24 as NEET (not in education, employment or training) in October to December 2025. The count passed one million in the first quarter of 2026, a rate of 13.5%, the first time the seasonally adjusted figure had reached that level since 2013. It dipped back below a million in the figures published in late August, but remained about 30,000 higher than a year earlier. Neither the TUC's latest figures nor any newer ONS release was available for this article, and the sources differ in places, so the numbers above should be read as the latest the paper could confirm.

What the TUC wants

The federation's standing proposal is a national jobs guarantee for 18-to-24-year-olds who have been NEET for six months, building on the government's existing Youth Guarantee. Its own modelling puts the long-run cost-benefit ratio at 2.81, meaning the scheme would pay for itself within a decade, though it would need money up front. The TUC has also opposed cutting the minimum wage for young workers as a way of drawing them into jobs.

The new warning does not appear to change that prescription. It adds a macroeconomic charge: that a policy of tight money is weighing on entry-level hiring, and that the government's answer is not keeping pace.

Why the rates link matters

Employers facing higher borrowing costs tend to freeze recruitment first, and school-leavers and graduates are the people applying for jobs that are not being created. If that holds, a recovery led by lower rates may reach young people late, if at all, because hiring recovers after profits do. That is the substance of the TUC's claim, though the evidence for it in this case rests on the federation's reading of the data rather than on independent analysis.

The backdrop is not helpful to the argument's critics. Bank Rate has just risen to 4%, a move now feeding through to mortgage bills, which makes a near-term rate-led revival of hiring harder to expect.

What to watch

The next test is the ONS labour market release in the middle of December, which will show whether youth unemployment is still falling from its peak. The government's response will also be measured against the Jobs Guarantee: how quickly it widens, and whether the Treasury is willing to put up the funding the TUC says is missing.
