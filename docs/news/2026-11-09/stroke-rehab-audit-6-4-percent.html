Only 6.4% of stroke survivors in England receive enough rehabilitation therapy, audit finds

In one sentence
An audit finds only 6.4% of stroke patients in England, Wales and Northern Ireland get the recommended three hours of daily rehabilitation, down from 7.5% in late 2024.

A national audit of stroke care has found that fewer than one in 15 survivors receive the amount of rehabilitation therapy that clinical guidance says they should. The figure is 6.4%. It describes patients in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, not England alone, and it has fallen from 7.5% in the last quarter of 2024.

The number comes from the Sentinel Stroke National Audit Programme (SSNAP), whose latest annual report draws on hospital data from the three nations. The Guardian, which first reported the findings, says the 6.4% covers January to March 2026, the most recent quarter available. The Stroke Association called the figures "damning".

What the benchmark is

The yardstick is a clinical standard, not a satisfaction score. According to the Guardian's account, the recommended dose is three hours a day of rehabilitation, at least five days a week. It is built from physiotherapy, occupational therapy and speech and language therapy, and is based on guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). The standard applies whether a patient is on a ward or back at home.

Against that benchmark, the reported averages are low:

  • Patients received an average of 40 minutes of therapy a day in early 2026, six minutes less than at the end of 2024.
  • Across units, the daily average ranged from 15.2 minutes at the lowest to 57.3 minutes at the highest. Even the best-performing unit was therefore well under an hour a day.
  • Only 68% of patients went on to receive continuing rehabilitation support after leaving hospital.

These are secondary reports of the audit. The full SSNAP report was not available for this article, so the exact definition of "enough therapy" (for example, whether it is measured over a whole inpatient stay or a set period) has not been checked against the audit's methodology.

Why the gap matters

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term disability. Almost 100,000 people a year in the UK have one, and the Stroke Association projects that the number could reach 115,483 by 2030. The charity estimates that stroke costs the UK about £25.7bn a year, including £13.7bn in social care, £6.7bn in wider societal costs and £2.4bn in welfare payments and lost income tax. These are the charity's own estimates, not audit findings.

The reasoning behind the concern is straightforward. Rehabilitation is where much of the recovery of movement, speech and independence is won or lost. If it is under-delivered, the shortfall does not disappear. It shifts to families, to social care budgets and to later health-service demand. That is an inference from the cost estimates above, not something the audit itself measures.

Juliet Bouverie, the Stroke Association's chief executive, said services are "already overstretched to breaking point", and that the figures show many people lack necessary support in the first days after a stroke and in the months and years that follow.

Workforce or measurement?

The central unanswered question is why the figure is so low. Two explanations are plausible, and the available reporting does not separate them.

The first is capacity. If stroke units do not have enough therapists, patients will not get three hours a day. Earlier research on English stroke units, including the ReAcT study, linked therapy delivery to staffing levels and to specialist rehabilitation. The wide spread between units, from 15 to 57 minutes a day, is at least consistent with local differences in resources, though it does not prove it.

The second is measurement. A target of three hours a day, five days a week, is demanding, and a figure as low as 6.4% could partly reflect how therapy time is recorded or how the benchmark is defined. A fall from 7.5% to 6.4% is a modest change that could come from either real decline or recording variation. Nothing in the reporting reviewed here shows that recording is the cause. But the falling average minutes of therapy point in the same direction as the headline figure, which weakens a purely statistical explanation.

The official response

The Department of Health and Social Care did not address the rehabilitation figure directly, according to the Guardian. A spokesperson pointed to NHS work on prompt support and long-term rehabilitation, to efforts to increase rates of thrombolysis and thrombectomy (clot-busting drugs and clot removal), and to a plan to cut premature deaths from cardiovascular disease and stroke by a quarter over a decade. Those measures concern acute treatment and prevention, not the hours of therapy that follow.

The next data point will be SSNAP's quarterly results. They will show whether the 6.4% is a trough or part of a continuing decline, and whether the gap between the best and worst units narrows. The full annual report, once examined in detail, should also settle how the benchmark is defined. Until then, the audit establishes a large shortfall against guidance but does not by itself say whether the cause is too few therapists or how therapy is counted.
