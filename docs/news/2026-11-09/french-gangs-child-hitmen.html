French drug gangs are recruiting children as killers, BBC investigation finds

In one sentence
A BBC investigation finds French drug networks recruit children online as cheap, disposable labour, with over 10,000 minors involved in trafficking in 2025, per official figures.

Drug networks in France are recruiting teenagers to deal, to guard territory and, increasingly, to kill. A BBC investigation published on 9 November, reported by James Waterhouse in Paris and centred on the housing estates of northern Marseille, describes a system in which children are hired through social media, paid in cash and dropped when they are caught. Its main point is about the labour market rather than the killers. Minors are cheap, expendable and legally less costly to use, and the networks have turned them into a standing workforce.

A workforce that does not need a record

The BBC reports that adverts for drivers, couriers and hitmen now circulate on social media, often with AI-generated images. They reach well beyond Marseille, into Paris, Nantes and Lille. Recruits are now often people with no criminal record, the report says, but with vulnerabilities such as difficult home lives.

The pay shows the pull. "Joe", a 23-year-old who manages a deal point for a rival gang, told the BBC he earned €3,000 a month at 13. He now takes €5,000 to €10,000 a month. "Camil", 20, who says he has left the trade, rose to running security in an area controlled by the DZ Mafia. Gangs, he said, offer cars, apartments, drugs and sex, and exploit any weakness they find.

Thibaut Fontaine, who heads the French anti-organised-crime unit, describes recruits as sent into dangerous situations by "unscrupulous sponsors". Their inexperience makes them quick to arrest. That is the economics of disposability: the sponsor is insulated and the recruit is easily replaced.

One interviewee, "Adam" (not his real name), is a former hit-squad member awaiting trial for several murders. He spoke to the BBC over Snapchat. He described attacks in broad daylight and in traffic jams. He said he feels "in-part guilty" but has no regrets.

Coercion behind the wage

The pay is only part of the picture. The BBC revisits a 2022 case from the Frais-Vallon estate in Marseille. A woman who bought hashish from a 15-year-old dealer was handed a note: "We're being held captive and need help. We are 15-years-old."

The two boys had joined a gang looking for paid work. One handled money and the other worked the streets. Their bosses blamed them for shortfalls in takings. They were beaten and forced to dance naked on camera, which created material for blackmail. One was raped. Both tried to escape from a second-storey window and were hurt. One got away. The other was caught, beaten and stabbed, but survived. Six men have been sentenced.

The case shows how a wage relationship can become a trap. It also shows that the same children can be both offenders and victims, which is what makes the problem hard for a justice system built to separate the two.

Hugo Brouillard's story shows the family side. He first ran away at 15 from a village in the Nord department. Police found him three days later in Marseille, almost 1,000km away, carrying money and cannabis. In 2023 he went south again, telling his parents he had a restaurant job. Three weeks later a doctor told his mother, Karine, that he had been shot in the head. Police believe he was taken for medical help from Quartier Nord, a gang-controlled area, and suspect he was involved with a gang there. The restaurant confirmed it existed but said it had never recruited anyone named Hugo. "They destroy families," Karine Brouillard said of the gang leaders.

From policing problem to social-policy problem

The scale explains why this is moving from the crime pages to ministries. Dimitri Zoulas, head of the anti-drug trafficking unit, told the BBC that drug smuggling generates €7bn a year and involves 200,000 people. Official figures show more than 10,000 children were involved in trafficking in some way in 2025. Cocaine users are expected to reach 2 million, up from 600,000 in 2017. Zoulas links the trade to more than 700 overdoses a year, and warns of a "potential systemic breakdown" of the justice system.

Police say about 1,000 people linked to the DZ Mafia have been arrested since 2024, and 600 incarcerated. The group is Marseille's most powerful gang, and in 2023 it became the first French gang to release video statements. Its founder, Mehdi Laribi, was detained in Algeria earlier this year under a French arrest warrant. The arrests have not dried up the supply of recruits. If a sponsor can replace a jailed teenager with another through a social media post, enforcement aimed at the people on the ground treats a symptom.

This is why the story reaches beyond the police. A 14-year-old with no record who takes €500 for a night's work is, in law, treated differently from an adult. Networks know it. The questions that follow concern youth services that reach runaways before the gangs do, child-protection responses for children who are being exploited, the sentencing of minors who have killed, and online platforms that carry the adverts. The BBC piece does not say which of these the government will act on, and the report cites no new legislation.

France is not unique. The same model, with online recruitment, cash wages and young, deniable labour, is the template other European cities facing rising gang violence will recognise.

What to watch

The test is whether the arrests of 2024 and 2025 are matched by action upstream. That means how many of the 10,000-plus children counted in 2025 are offered protection rather than prosecution, and whether the justice system can handle the volume Zoulas warns about. Hugo Brouillard's parents, like the families of the Frais-Vallon boys, are left with the human cost. The ministries' next figures on minors in trafficking will show whether the pipeline is narrowing.
