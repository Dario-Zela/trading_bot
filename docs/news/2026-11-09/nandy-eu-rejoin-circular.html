Nandy wary of 'circular arguments' over rejoining the EU

In one sentence
Lisa Nandy has again warned against 'circular arguments' over rejoining the EU, echoing her May stance, while the government pursues closer trade ties.

Lisa Nandy has again cautioned against reopening the argument over Britain rejoining the European Union. According to the Guardian's politics coverage, she warned of 'circular arguments', even as the government pushes for closer trade ties with Brussels. The line is short and the reporting so far is thin. The question is whether it is a deliberate government position or an aside.

A phrase with a history

The wording is not new for Nandy. Reports from May 2026 attribute almost identical language to her in a Labour row over Europe. Wes Streeting had called for Britain to rejoin the EU. Nandy, then speaking as Culture Secretary, called his remarks 'odd'. She said the government was trying to repair the damage of Brexit without reopening the 'circular arguments' the country had ended up in.

That earlier exchange sets out her position. She agreed with Streeting that Brexit had been a mistake. Her objection was to the sudden focus on membership, and to what she saw as a pull back towards 2015, as if 'life was fine' then and the country simply needed to return to it. Streeting's allies replied that her reluctance reflected a wider unwillingness to take political risks.

The Guardian report, as summarised in triage, appears to cover the same ground. The date and full context of the new remark have not been independently confirmed, so the two episodes should not be assumed to be one.

Two tracks, one line

The government's approach has two parts. Ministers pursue closer trade and regulatory ties with the EU, and they decline to say that this leads to membership. Nandy's phrasing fits that division. Describing the membership debate as circular lets a minister argue for practical gains on trade without taking a side in a fight over the 2016 referendum.

The approach has a cost. Her May remarks drew a contrast with Andy Burnham, who has said he sees a 'long-term case' for rejoining but is not campaigning on it. The gap between Nandy's caution and Burnham's long-term openness suggests the party has not settled how to talk about the question. It does not show a formal split.

The remark would matter more if it were a coordinated line from the government than if it were a minister's personal view. Nothing in the available reporting establishes that. A single-source summary cannot show whether Downing Street has chosen this wording or whether Nandy is expressing her own preference.

What to watch

Two things would settle it. The first is whether other cabinet ministers repeat the 'circular arguments' framing or distance themselves from it. The second is whether the closer-trade agenda produces concrete steps on trade or regulation that test the claim that deeper ties can be pursued without a membership debate. Until then, the remark is better read as the continuation of a stance Nandy has held since at least May than as a new signal.
