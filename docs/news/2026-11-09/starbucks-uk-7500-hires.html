Starbucks plans to hire 7,500 staff in UK expansion

In one sentence
Starbucks plans 7,500 UK barista jobs and 500 new shops by 2031, a positive hospitality signal whose net effect depends on turnover the announcement does not disclose.

Starbucks says it will create 7,500 barista jobs in the UK as part of a plan to open 500 new coffee shops by 2031. The announcement is a rare piece of good news from a hospitality sector that has spent two years absorbing higher payroll costs. What it says about labour demand is limited, because the headline number does not separate new posts from replacements for staff who leave.

What was announced

The chain, which employs more than 18,000 people in the UK, said the roles will be created over the next five years. Recruitment will concentrate on the Midlands, the north of England, Wales and Scotland, as it targets growth outside London. Darren King, vice-president and managing director of Starbucks UK, said the company is "proud to be creating thousands of new jobs across the UK over the next five years." He added that entry-level roles are meant to be accessible: "You don't need previous industry experience and we'll give you the training and support you need from day one."

The plan follows a restructuring under chief executive Brian Niccol that included store closures and operational changes. The UK push therefore reads as a reallocation of growth toward markets the group considers worth expanding, not a general loosening of the purse strings. The reporting does not give the current UK store count, so the scale of 500 additional sites against the existing estate cannot be checked here.

Net new or backfill?

A five-year figure of 7,500 works out at about 1,500 roles a year. Against a UK workforce above 18,000, that is roughly 8% annually. The ratio is useful only if it is set beside turnover, and turnover in coffee-shop work is high. Starbucks' own commissioned research found that 68% of 18–35-year-olds see retail and hospitality jobs as temporary or stopgap work, and more than half of its UK company-store staff are under 24. A workforce that young churns.

If 500 new shops each employ a typical crew, the arithmetic implies about 15 roles per site. That is plausible for new openings, which suggests a meaningful share of the 7,500 is genuinely additional headcount tied to new premises. The announcement does not say so, however, and nothing in it gives a split between new posts and replacement hiring. Until the company breaks that out, the figure is best read as a ceiling on net job creation, not an estimate of it.

Costs in the background

The plan arrives as the national minimum wage is set to rise 3.9% in April. Employers in hospitality have cited wage and National Insurance increases as reasons for trimming hours and holding back hiring. A large chain committing to expansion in that environment indicates that unit economics at new sites still clear the bar, at least for Starbucks. It is not evidence that the wider sector's cost pressure has eased; smaller independents have less room to absorb it.

Helen Dickinson, chief executive of the British Retail Consortium, welcomed the investment. "At a time when too many young people are struggling to get a foot on the career ladder, this investment is hugely welcome," she said. The remark ties the announcement to a live argument about youth employment, which the TUC has pressed this week.

What to watch

The useful tests are in the data to come. Official labour-market figures and vacancy counts for accommodation and food services will show whether hiring in the sector is picking up or whether this is an isolated corporate decision. Starbucks' own planning applications and store openings through 2027 will show whether the 500-shop target is being met in the regions named. A first-year tally of roles filled, with leavers stated, would settle the net-versus-backfill question.
