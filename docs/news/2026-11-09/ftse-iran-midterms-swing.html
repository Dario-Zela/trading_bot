FTSE 100 fell on election day on Iran-related strikes, then rallied about 1.8%

In one sentence
The FTSE 100 fell on US midterm election day on Iran-related strikes, then rallied about 1.8%; the index levels and the size of the initial fall remain unconfirmed.

London's FTSE 100 fell on 3 November, the day of the US midterm elections, and then rallied by about 1.8% the following session. Market commentary attributes both moves to Iran-related military strikes rather than to the American vote. The index levels behind the moves could not be independently confirmed, so the percentage is the only hard number here.

What moved, and why

The sequence is a familiar one for a heavily international index. The FTSE 100 draws most of its earnings from outside the UK and carries large weightings in oil, defence and mining. It tends to react quickly to Middle East headlines, in either direction.

The midterms were the scheduled event of the day, but the market commentary points elsewhere. Reports of strikes involving Iran drove the sell-off, and the following day's rebound came as that pressure eased. That fits a pattern of geopolitical shocks in which the first reaction is a retreat from risk and the second is a partial or full recovery once the immediate escalation fears fade.

Two caveats apply. The 1.8% figure comes from a single summary of the move, and it is not clear whether it describes the close or an intraday swing. The size of the election-day fall is also not established. Without the closing levels, the net change over the two sessions cannot be calculated, and it is not known whether the rally recovered all of the loss.

What the midterms did not do

The US vote is a separate matter, and a quiet one for UK equities. Another piece in this edition, on midterm gains and the presidential veto, notes that on paper little changes while Mr Trump holds the veto. The wider market reaction to the election has centred on US technology shares and the dollar. AI stocks have since rebounded after a rout of roughly $3tn. A FTSE move driven by Iran headlines is therefore a sign that London traders were watching oil and security news more closely than Washington.

The diplomatic backdrop gives little reassurance. Marco Rubio has said talks with Iran may be unproductive, calling Tehran's demands "outrageous". If talks stall, headline risk persists, and a rebound of this size would be easy to reverse.

What to watch

Three things would turn a one-day bounce into a trend. The first is the direction of oil prices, which feed through to the index's energy heavyweights and to inflation expectations. The second is any confirmed escalation or de-escalation involving Iran. The third is domestic: the gilt market's test of Chancellor Healey's fiscal pledge after the Budget, and the Bank Rate rise to 4%, both bear on UK-focused shares in a way that Iran headlines do not. The FTSE 100's closing levels for 3 and 4 November, and where it sits relative to them now, would show whether the rally has held.
