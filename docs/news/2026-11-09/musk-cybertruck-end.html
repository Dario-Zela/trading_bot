Musk appears to signal the end of the road for the Cybertruck

In one sentence
Musk says Tesla will wind down Cybertruck production in Texas around mid-2027 to make room for the Cybercab; the post is firm but short on detail, and demand is the contested backdrop.

Tesla is to wind down production of the Cybertruck at its Texas factory around the middle of next year, according to Elon Musk. That is firmer than the early reading of his remarks, which suggested only that the pickup's future was in doubt.

Musk said it in a post on X on 6 November. He tied the move to making room for the Cybercab, Tesla's planned two-seat robotaxi. When a follower called it the "end of an era", he replied: "True, but the Cybertruck fleet will be around for a long time."

The brief note on which this piece was first assigned described the comments as hedged and unconfirmed. Later reporting by Fortune, Electrek, Motor1 and others quotes the post directly and gives the timing, so the story no longer rests on inference. It is still a social-media post, not a company filing or a formal Tesla statement.

What is known, and what is not

The post says Gigafactory Texas will taper Cybertruck output around mid-2027. It does not say whether production would continue at another plant. It gives no final build date. Tesla has not said how long it will keep selling leftover inventory or supplying replacement parts. Owners of the roughly three-year-old vehicle, which began customer deliveries in late November 2023, are told only that the fleet will stay on the road.

Why the Cybercab explanation is contested

Musk's stated reason is a matter of floor space. Critics point to demand instead. Electrek reports that the Cybertruck sells at roughly a tenth of the 250,000 units a year that Tesla originally built its line to produce. That would put annual sales in the region of 25,000, though the figure is the outlet's estimate rather than a Tesla disclosure. Fortune notes that Musk had once predicted eventual output of between 250,000 and 500,000 a year.

The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. A factory line is a scarce asset, and a vehicle that sells far below plan is an easy candidate to give up when a priority product needs the room. The evidence for the demand reading is the gap between planned and actual volumes. The evidence for the space reading is Musk's own wording. Neither can be confirmed without Tesla's production and sales figures, which it does not break out for the Cybertruck.

What it says about the line-up

The Cybertruck was Tesla's most distinctive product: a stainless-steel, angular pickup unlike anything else on the road. Ending it would leave the range concentrated on the more conventional models, with the company's emphasis moving to autonomy and the Cybercab. That is a bet on a vehicle that has not yet reached volume production, replacing one that has.

The next concrete markers are Tesla's quarterly results, which should show whether it gives any more detail on Texas capacity, and any formal notice on inventory, parts and warranty support for existing Cybertruck owners. A confirmed end date, or word of production elsewhere, would settle the questions the post leaves open.
