Burnham's pledge to 'get everyone in for Christmas' on homelessness is in jeopardy

In one sentence
The Guardian reports Andy Burnham's pledge to offer every rough sleeper a route off the streets by Christmas is at risk, with long-term rough sleeping rising and housing benefit frozen.

Andy Burnham's first instruction on entering Downing Street in July was to end rough sleeping. In August he gave the promise a date: everyone sleeping rough in England who wants it would be offered "a route off the streets by Christmas". The Guardian now reports that the commitment is at risk of missing that deadline. The evidence available publicly suggests why. The pledge is measured against a rough-sleeping count that, on the latest figures, is not falling fast enough.

What was promised

The government announced the drive on 19 August, with a £442m package. According to the government's announcement, £340m had been announced in July for settled accommodation for long-term rough sleepers. A further £102m was added for immediate routes off the street this winter. The money comes from uncommitted housing department budgets. It promises emergency accommodation across England this winter and more than 1,000 settled homes over three years. Areas with the greatest pressure get the largest share, with mayors leading local action.

The wording matters. The commitment is an offer, not a guarantee that nobody is outside. Burnham has said as much. Asked whether people sleeping rough on Boxing Day would count as failure, he said it "will have done to a degree", adding: "I can offer people a route off the streets, but not everybody will take it." He has also said government money alone may not be enough.

The delivery gap

The latest Chain figures for July to September, reported by the Big Issue on 30 October, show the scale of the task:

  • 4,678 people were seen sleeping rough in London, down 1% on a year earlier.
  • 2,168 were new to the streets, up 2%, and made up 46% of the total.
  • 819 were long-term rough sleepers, up 8% year on year and 20% higher than April to June.

The long-term group is the hardest to reach and the one the £340m settled-housing money is aimed at. Its growth means the programme is chasing a rising number, with the new homes not due to arrive in volume until later years. These numbers come from the Chain database, which records London only, so they are an indicator rather than a national tally. The government's announcement gave no national count against which to measure the Christmas promise.

Pressure from the Budget

Housing charities have pointed to a second problem. The Autumn Budget kept Local Housing Allowance frozen, so housing benefit will not cover the cheapest 30% of private rents as intended. Crisis chief executive Matt Downie has urged restoring it "so it reflects the real cost of private rents". The Big Issue headlined the argument as the freeze "puts rough sleeping promise at risk". The argument is that emergency beds can clear the streets while new people keep arriving, because tenants who cannot afford rent lose their homes.

The shadow housing secretary, Sir James Cleverly, has said Burnham is pledging to end rough sleeping nationally without a plan for delivery. Charities have been less hostile but similarly doubtful. St Mungo's welcomed the commitment while saying housing alone would not be enough without intensive, joined-up support.

This piece could not independently review the Guardian's own sourcing, so the specific evidence it cites for the slippage is not reproduced here.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the deadline holds. The first is the next Chain quarterly figures. The second is the national summit on ending rough sleeping, promised for the autumn. The third is how many councils have winter emergency places open by December. The government has framed the date as a test, and councils and charities will judge it by Christmas week.
