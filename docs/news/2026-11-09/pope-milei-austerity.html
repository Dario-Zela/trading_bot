Pope delivers veiled rebuke of Milei's austerity measures

In one sentence
Pope Leo XIV is reported to have criticised Argentine austerity without naming Milei during his visit, though the exact words are unconfirmed and rest on a single BBC report.

Pope Leo XIV is reported to have criticised Argentina's austerity during his visit to the country, without naming President Javier Milei. The report comes from a single outlet, BBC World. The Pope's exact words were not available to this newspaper when it went to press, so the characterisation of his remarks as a "veiled rebuke" rests on that report alone.

The setting is well documented. Leo arrived in Buenos Aires on Sunday 8 November for a stay that runs to 11 November, after a stop in Uruguay and before a final leg in Peru. His programme takes in Buenos Aires, Claypole, Luján and Córdoba. Argentine authorities expect around seven million people to follow him across several cities, and Security Minister Alejandra Monteoliva has called it "the largest operation in our history", according to MercoPress.

A visit the government wants kept pastoral

Milei's government has insisted the trip is pastoral. Karina Milei, the president's sister, who led the negotiations with the Church, said: "The Pope belongs to all Argentines, not to a political party or a political colour." Worship Secretary Agustín Caulo described it as an apostolic tour that "should not be politicised".

The programme makes that hard to enforce. MercoPress reports that two events are seen as politically awkward for the government. One is a meeting with trade unionists, business leaders and social movements at the Catholic University of Argentina. The other is a visit to a home for people with disabilities run by the Don Orione order. Cuts to disability support have been among the most contested parts of Milei's spending programme. The Vatican also prevailed over the government's wish to limit mass gatherings, so the Pope will celebrate open-air Masses.

A long-running quarrel with the bishops

The tension between Church and government predates the visit. Milei took office in December 2023 and has pursued sharp cuts to public spending. Argentina's bishops have been among the most persistent critics. A study by the Conicet sociologist Sol Prieto, cited by MercoPress, found that 42 of 44 Argentine Bishops' Conference documents on public policy between January 2024 and September 2026 took a critical position, about 95%.

The archbishop of Buenos Aires, Jorge García Cuerva, said in a sermon in May 2025 that Argentina was "bleeding" from inequality, with Milei in the front row. Before this visit he said the Pope "will find a wounded Argentina". Marcelo Colombo, president of the Bishops' Conference, answered Milei's doubts about widespread hardship by saying that people struggling to make ends meet "is a fact of reality, not something that needs to be proven".

The president, for his part, has called social justice "an aberration", while Leo has made it central to his papacy. Francis, Leo's predecessor, drew a sharp response in 2024 when he said an indebted government could not impose "unworthy deprivations" on its people. A cabinet chief suggested then that "Peronist sympathies" lay behind the criticism.

What is and is not established

The case for reading the Pope's remarks as a rebuke is circumstantial but plausible. The Church's record of criticism is extensive, and the itinerary puts him in front of unions, social movements and vulnerable people. A pope who talks about poverty, work and the common good in Argentina this week will be heard in the context of the cuts, whether or not he names anyone.

That is different from a confirmed rift. No direct quotation was available, and without the text it is not possible to say how pointed the language was, or whether it addressed Argentina's policies specifically. Relations also have a warmer side: Milei and Leo met at the Vatican in June 2025, when the Pope confirmed the trip, and the president greeted him ahead of the visit with a message that Argentina awaited him "with joy".

The next test comes before the Pope leaves on 11 November. The meeting at the Catholic University and the open-air Masses will give the clearest sign of how far Leo is willing to go, and the government's response will show whether it treats his words as pastoral or political.
