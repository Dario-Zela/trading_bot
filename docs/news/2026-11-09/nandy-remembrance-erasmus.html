Nandy rejects claims of politicising Remembrance Sunday after Erasmus row

In one sentence
Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy denies politicising Remembrance Sunday in a row tied to the Erasmus scheme dispute, though the specific claims remain unclear.

Remembrance Sunday is meant to sit above politics. Within a day of this year's ceremonies, on 8 November, it has become the subject of a political argument. Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy has rejected claims that the government politicised the commemorations, in a row that appears to have more to do with the Erasmus exchange scheme than with the act of remembrance itself.

What is known

The outline comes from BBC Politics, which reported Nandy's denial. The fuller detail is thinner than the headline suggests. The reporting available to this desk does not set out who made the accusation, what exactly was said or done at Remembrance events, or how Erasmus came to be attached to it. Those gaps matter, and they are not filled here with guesswork. The point of connection is stated only as a link between the commemoration complaint and the dispute over Erasmus.

That linkage is the unusual part. Remembrance rows in Britain usually concern the observance itself: poppies, wreaths, who was invited, who was left out, what was said from a podium. A row in which the real quarrel is about a student and youth exchange programme suggests the commemoration is being used as a stage for an argument that began elsewhere, which is how many culture-war skirmishes work.

Why Erasmus is a live issue

Erasmus, the EU's exchange scheme for students and trainees, is a long-running irritant in post-Brexit politics. Britain left it at the end of 2020 and replaced it with the domestic Turing Scheme. Reviving closer participation has been discussed as part of the government's wider effort to reset relations with Brussels. Any suggestion that it is being attached to a solemn national occasion is likely to provoke a reaction from both sides of that debate.

Nandy has been careful on the broader question. In a related exchange this week she was reported to be wary of "circular arguments" over rejoining the EU, a line consistent with a minister keen to keep the exchange-scheme discussion separate from the larger constitutional one. Her defence on Remembrance fits the same instinct: to treat the commemoration as non-partisan and to resist having it pulled into a dispute about Europe.

What to watch

The first test is whether the original complainants put specifics on the record. A general charge of politicisation is easy to make and easy to deny. A named event, a named remark or a documented decision would be harder to wave away. Nandy's department, which has responsibility for national ceremonial and commemoration, could be pressed to say what, if anything, was done differently this year.

The second is the Erasmus dispute that sits underneath. Whatever the government decides on exchange-scheme participation will be announced on its own terms, and the Remembrance argument will either fade or become a recurring reference point in it. With the Commons back and ministers facing questions, the next departmental question time is the likeliest place for the exchange to resume. Until the claims are set out in full, this remains a skirmish with a clear denial and an unclear charge.
