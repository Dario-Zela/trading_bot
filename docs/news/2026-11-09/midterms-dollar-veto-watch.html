STANDING WATCH · US MIDTERMS · By M. Halloway · 2026-11-09 · 3 min read

Midterm gains change little on paper while Trump holds the veto, economists say

In one sentence
Economists say a Democratic midterm gain changes little while Trump holds the veto, but HSBC flags near-term dollar pressure as markets price expected fiscal friction.

A Democratic gain in the US midterms alters the arithmetic in Congress but not, for now, the legal landscape. That is the reading from economists cited by Benzinga and InvestorsHub, who say little changes immediately because President Donald Trump retains the veto pen. For markets, the more useful question is not what Congress can pass but what traders now expect it to attempt, and whether that expectation is enough to move the dollar before any bill reaches the president's desk.

What a split Congress can and cannot do

The constraint is mechanical. A bill needs to clear both chambers and then the White House. A veto can be overridden only by two-thirds of each chamber, a threshold that a modest midterm swing does not come close to supplying. A Democratic-led chamber can therefore block, delay and investigate. It cannot, on its own, legislate over the president's objection.

That is the basis for the economists' caution. Tax policy, tariff authority and spending priorities set in the current term stay in place unless the president signs something that changes them. The practical effect of divided government is more often a stalemate than a reversal, and a stalemate leaves existing policy where it is.

The discovery summaries behind this piece did not carry vote margins, seat counts or the names of the economists quoted. None of those figures has been checked against a primary source, and they are not stated here.

Where the market sees pressure

Expected policy is priced before it is enacted, which is why a legislatively inert result can still move prices. HSBC, according to the same summaries, flags near-term pressure on the dollar following the midterm result. The bank's exact wording and any forecast levels were not confirmed, so the call is reported here only in outline.

The likely channel is fiscal. A Congress in which one chamber is controlled by the opposition makes large, unfunded tax or spending packages harder to pass, and it raises the odds of standoffs over funding deadlines and the debt limit. Each shifts the distribution of outcomes for Treasury issuance and for the currency that Treasuries are denominated in. None of this requires a law to change; it requires only that positioning change first.

There is a reasonable counter-argument. If the veto genuinely neutralises the result, then the gap between what the dollar might do and what policy can do is small, and any move could fade once desks conclude that nothing material has shifted. The two readings, a quiet legal outcome and a restless market one, are not contradictory. They describe different time horizons.

What to watch

The first tests are procedural rather than legislative. Leadership selection in the incoming House, the committee chairs who control oversight and the budget process, and the next government-funding deadline will show how much friction a divided Congress intends to create. Treasury auction demand and dollar moves around those dates will indicate whether the HSBC view is being adopted or has already been priced.

On the wider market backdrop, the same week brought a post-midterm rout in AI stocks and a swing in the FTSE 100 around election day, a reminder that the result is one input among several.
