Givaudan, Firmenich and IFF hit with about $630m in EU and UK cartel fines

In one sentence
The EU fined Givaudan, Firmenich and IFF €471.9m and the UK CMA added £85.2m on the same day for sharing sensitive pricing information, with the cartel's reported 'beer and curry' meetings the colour detail.

Three companies that between them supply a large share of the scents in the world's detergents, shampoos and deodorants have been fined by two regulators on the same day for the same conduct. The European Commission fined Givaudan, Firmenich and IFF a combined €471.9m on 25 November 2026. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) issued its own infringement decision that day, with fines totalling £85.156m. Investing.com puts the two penalties together at about $630m. FashionNetwork puts them at more than €570m.

The total is large, but the two decisions are separate. Brussels and London each reached a finding under their own law, with no overlap in the fines.

What the regulators found

The Commission's case concerns the European Economic Area. It says the infringement ran from March 2019 until March 2023, when its inspectors arrived unannounced. The central customer was Unilever. According to the Commission's account, all three firms exchanged commercially sensitive information about their dealings with the consumer-goods group. That included price increases, tactics for price negotiations and a possible energy surcharge. They also coordinated how to respond when Unilever asked for more transparency on fragrance composition and ingredient costs. And they agreed not to recruit each other's staff working on that account.

Givaudan and IFF went further, the Commission says. They shared information on their consumer and fine-fragrance businesses more broadly, including price-increase targets and the status of customer negotiations. Senior staff were aware of their subordinates' contacts with competitors. From November 2021, senior staff at Givaudan and IFF were directly involved.

Teresa Ribera, the Commission's executive vice-president for competition, said: "For years, they exchanged information on their prices, costs and other commercially sensitive information." She added that the conduct probably harmed customers and raised their costs.

The fines were reduced for all three because each applied for leniency and settled. The Commission's announcement did not give the size of the discounts in the material reviewed here.

The UK decision

The CMA's case page, updated on 25 November, records an infringement decision under the Competition Act 1998 against three groups. The addressees are Firmenich, Givaudan and IFF, each through its UK and parent entities. A Statement of Objections had been issued on 5 November. The fines total £85,156,000 and include a 20% settlement discount, granted because the companies admitted their role and agreed to cooperate. All three also received leniency discounts of undisclosed size. The page does not break the total down by company.

The UK dollar figure is a conversion of the CMA's sterling total. The £85.156m comes directly from the CMA's page. The combined $630m is Investing.com's number, and it moves with exchange rates.

Symrise, the fourth large supplier, is not among the addressees. The CMA closed its investigation of the German company in May 2025 on administrative priority grounds. The CMA's January 2024 extension of the case to cover reciprocal hiring arrangements is consistent with the no-poaching element the Commission has now described.

Beer, curry and the limits of the detail

The detail travelling furthest is the Telegraph's report that the cartel's participants met over "beer and curry". That is a press account. It is not a regulator finding, and it could not be confirmed against either decision in the material reviewed here. It should be read as colour, not as part of the legal case. The Commission's description of the conduct is drier: exchanges of information, coordination and a no-poach understanding.

The informal setting, if accurate, fits a pattern in the Commission's account. Contacts between subordinates, tolerated by their managers, can harden over time into a standing arrangement. The Commission says it found exactly that progression, with senior involvement from late 2021.

Firmenich's parent group has contested the characterisation, saying its involvement was limited to exchanging information about one customer in one business, and that the conduct did not involve a price-fixing agreement. The group did not dispute that the decisions were reached by settlement.

Why it reaches beyond perfume

Fragrance is a small line item in a bottle of fabric softener, but it is in thousands of fast-moving consumer products. The three firms, with Symrise, dominate supply. A buyer such as Unilever that wants to compare prices has few alternatives to approach, which is what makes information-sharing among the suppliers consequential. The Commission says the exchange likely raised customers' costs. It has not put a figure on the overcharge in the material reviewed here.

The decisions also show that, after Brexit, the UK and the EU can run parallel cases against the same conduct and arrive at the same destination on the same day. Both investigations opened in March 2023, and the CMA's page says the authorities were working alongside other regulators.

What comes next

The exposure is not finished. IFF was separately fined €15.9m in June 2024 for deleting WhatsApp messages during the 2023 inspection. Swiss competition authorities opened their own probe. US class actions have been allowed to proceed, and Reuters reported in August 2026 that Indian authorities had opened a second antitrust investigation. Settled findings of infringement in Brussels and London generally make it easier for customers to bring damages claims. The next data points are whether the Swiss and Indian cases end in findings, and whether Unilever or other customers file claims against the three firms.
