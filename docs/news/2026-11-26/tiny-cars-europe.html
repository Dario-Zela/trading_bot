Why tiny cars are taking off across Europe

In one sentence
The EU's proposed M1E category for small, EU-built electric cars up to 4.2 m offers emissions credits and stable rules, but cost savings on sub-€20,000 models remain unproven.

Europe's carmakers are being asked to build small, cheap electric cars at a price that has so far defeated most of them. The European Commission's answer, in its December 2025 automotive package, is not a subsidy. It is a new rulebook category. The open question is whether lighter paperwork can turn a sub-€20,000 car from a loss-leader into a product that earns its keep.

A category between two extremes

At the bottom of the market sits the quadricycle. Citroën's Ami, on sale in France since 2020, is about 2.4 metres long and tops out at 45 km/h. In France it can be driven from the age of 14 without a car licence. It is cheap because it is not really a car in regulatory terms: it escapes most of the crash and equipment requirements that apply to ordinary passenger vehicles.

Above it sits the conventional small car, which carries the full compliance load. Renault has pitched its electric Twingo at roughly €20,000, about 3.8 metres long. That price is the target the industry keeps naming, and it is hard to hit when batteries and safety systems cost the same whether the car is small or large.

The Commission's proposal creates a rung between the two. Reports on the package describe a sub-category of the M1 passenger-car class, labelled M1E. It would cover fully electric cars up to 4.2 metres long, assembled in the EU. By that measure the Renault 5, at about 3.9 metres, and Volkswagen's forthcoming ID Polo, at about 4.1 metres, would fit.

What the category offers

The benefits are mostly regulatory, not fiscal. Qualifying cars would earn "super credits" against the EU's fleet emissions targets: 1.3 credits for each zero-emission M1E car sold, against the standard 1. A manufacturer that sells enough of them can sell more of its higher-emitting models without breaching its target. The category would also give national governments a clear definition on which to hang incentives.

The Commission has also indicated that it intends to freeze the category's technical requirements for ten years, which would give planners a stable target. The detail is still to be written through amendments to type-approval Regulation 2018/858, so the final text may differ from the proposal.

Being a car still means being a safe car. Reporting on the proposal says M1E vehicles must meet existing EU safety standards, which limits how much cost the category can strip out. The European Transport Safety Council has objected to the decade-long freeze, arguing it would lock in requirements while safety technology moves on. Environmental groups have made a different point: microcars like the Ami already exist, and a bigger category is not what makes them cheap.

The margin problem

Carmakers' interest is plain. A €20,000 car leaves little room: the battery is the largest single cost, and the fixed costs of development, homologation and tooling must be spread over fewer euros of revenue. Simpler approval trims some of that cost. Emissions credits add a second, indirect contribution, because they let a maker offset the weak margin on a small EV against the stronger ones on larger models.

That is also the limit of the case. Credits help the group's accounts without making the individual car profitable, and the EU-assembly condition rules out the cheapest imported supply chains. Whether the category changes which small models are built in Europe will depend on the final length limit, the safety carve-outs and how much the freeze is allowed to hold.

What to watch

The next test is the legislative text. Parliament and member states have to agree the type-approval amendments, and the safety debate over the ten-year freeze is the likeliest place for the proposal to change. Carmakers' launch plans for models at or under €20,000 will show how much weight they put on it.
