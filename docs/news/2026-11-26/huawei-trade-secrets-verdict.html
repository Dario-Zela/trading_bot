Huawei found guilty of stealing US trade secrets but acquitted of racketeering

In one sentence
A Brooklyn jury convicted Huawei of conspiring to steal US trade secrets but cleared it of racketeering, a split that narrows the scope of the government's case.

A federal jury in Brooklyn has split its verdict on Huawei. It found the Chinese telecoms group guilty of conspiring to steal American trade secrets. It cleared the company of racketeering, the sweeping charge that framed the whole prosecution as a two-decade criminal enterprise.

The outcome is a partial win for the US Justice Department. The case was built to show that Huawei's rise rested on a pattern of crime. The jury accepted that specific crimes happened. It did not accept that they added up to a racketeering enterprise.

What the jury was asked to decide

The case was heard before US District Judge Ann Donnelly. Huawei Technologies and three affiliated companies faced 12 counts: racketeering conspiracy, bank fraud, wire fraud, conspiracy to steal trade secrets and obstruction of justice. The racketeering count alleged a conspiracy running for about two decades.

Prosecutors, led in court by Assistant US Attorney Meredith Arfa, argued that Huawei built its international business through crimes against American companies, banks and the government. They said the company encouraged staff to obtain competitors' confidential technology, including from Cisco and T-Mobile. One example was information about Tappy, T-Mobile's smartphone-testing robot. They also alleged that Huawei hid its control of Skycom, a company operating in Iran, and misled HSBC and other banks about it.

The defence, led by Brian Heberlig, said the government had distorted ordinary business practice. It credited Huawei's success to engineering and investment. It said any misconduct was the work of individual employees who were later disciplined or fired. It also argued that some of the allegedly stolen material was publicly available, and that the banks knew about Huawei's Iran activity.

The jury heard about two months of testimony, from more than 60 prosecution witnesses and about 15 for the defence. It began deliberating on 18 November.

Why the racketeering acquittal matters

Racketeering is the charge that turns a set of separate offences into one organised enterprise. To convict, a jury must find a pattern of criminal acts carried out through the company as a whole. That is a heavier claim than showing that employees took confidential material, and it is a harder one to prove.

The acquittal suggests the jury was willing to hold Huawei responsible for particular acts but not to accept the company-as-criminal-enterprise story. That fits the defence's central argument, that wrongdoing sat with individuals and not with the corporate structure. The jury's reasoning has not been published. This reading is an inference from the split, not a finding.

For prosecutors elsewhere, the practical lesson is about charging. Racketeering statutes give broad reach, including a long time horizon and a single narrative. A jury can reject that narrative and still convict on narrower counts. Cases against other China-linked technology firms may lean more on the specific counts.

What is not yet confirmed

The headline outcome, a conviction on trade-secret theft and an acquittal on racketeering, matches the BBC's technology coverage. A legal-commentary report on the trial goes further. It says the jury convicted Huawei on 10 of the 12 counts, including bank fraud, wire fraud and sanctions violations, and also acquitted it of conspiracy to obstruct justice. That report is only partly visible and has not been checked against court records. Treat the full count-by-count breakdown as provisional.

Sentencing dates, any fine and Huawei's response were not available at the time of writing. A corporation cannot be jailed, so penalties would come as fines, probation terms and possible compliance conditions. Huawei has long denied wrongdoing, and an appeal is the usual next step after a conviction.

What to watch

The next markers are the sentencing schedule set by Judge Donnelly, any post-trial motions from the defence seeking to overturn the convictions, and whether prosecutors say anything about the racketeering acquittal. Together they will show how much of the Justice Department's original case survives.
