US announces new tariffs on a host of countries, including China

In one sentence
Washington has proposed Section 301 tariffs of roughly 15-20% on China, the EU and 12 other economies over alleged overcapacity, but nothing takes effect for months.

The United States has proposed a fresh round of tariffs on China and 13 other trading partners, a measure that would reach well beyond Beijing. Announced on 25 November, the plan is a proposal rather than a law. Duties are unlikely to bite for some months. Even so, it is the clearest sign yet of how Washington intends to rebuild its tariff wall after the courts took apart the old one.

What has been proposed

The proposals rest on Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. The United States Trade Representative, Jamieson Greer, says an investigation opened in March 2026 found that the targeted economies sustain manufacturing capacity beyond what their own markets need. Of the 14 economies, CNBC names four: China, the European Union, Japan and India. Other coverage points to Brazil, Taiwan and Vietnam among the rest. The full list should be checked against the USTR's own notice.

Most proposed rates fall between 15% and 20%. China would face 20%, on top of existing Section 301 duties of roughly 25% imposed over forced technology transfer. That would take tariffs on many Chinese goods to about 45%. Some reports suggest a higher ceiling, up to 40% on the new tranche, if talks with Beijing fail. That figure is not consistently reported and should be treated cautiously.

Greer sought to soften the arithmetic. The rates, he said, are "not really additive" and generally will not stack on existing duties. "In effect, it'll look more like a top up," he said. China's foreign ministry rejected the premise: "The so-called 'overcapacity' does not really exist," it said.

Why Section 301, and why now

The choice of statute is the story's backbone. The Supreme Court struck down the earlier sweeping tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. A stopgap 10% duty under Section 122 then lapsed after its 150-day limit. Section 301 is slower and more procedural. It requires an investigation, findings, public comment and hearings. It is also sturdier in court, which is presumably the point. The administration has also used the same statute in separate forced-labour investigations covering 60 economies, which produced tariffs of 10% to 12.5%.

The timing is awkward for Beijing. The proposal arrives about a week after a Trump-Xi summit, and the South China Morning Post reports that it is unlikely on its own to upend the truce struck at Busan last year. A proposed tariff with months of process ahead is also a bargaining chip, and the summit's afterglow may be part of what is being tested.

The timetable

No effective date has been set. Greer said he did not think any of the duties would take effect "earlier than a couple months from now". Hearings and a comment period come first, and some outlets report January as the earliest realistic start. The precise dates for those hearings were not stated in the reporting reviewed, and reports differ on when the current China truce expires. Final rates may differ from those proposed.

What it means for UK exporters

The UK is not among the economies named in the coverage reviewed, and CNBC's report does not mention it. The exposure for FTSE-listed exporters is therefore indirect, and it runs through three channels.

The first is supply chains. UK-listed manufacturers that source components from China, Vietnam, India or the EU and sell finished goods into the American market could face higher costs if their inputs or their European plants fall within scope. The second is diversion. Chinese and other exporters hit by 20% duties may redirect goods to Europe and other markets, adding pricing pressure for British producers at home and abroad. The third is demand. A wider tariff list adds to uncertainty for American importers and could dampen orders.

Those effects cannot be sized until the product lists are published. The proposed rates are headline figures, and the damage depends on which tariff lines are covered. Whether any FTSE company is materially exposed will turn on that detail.

What to watch

The next markers are the USTR's formal notice and product lists, the dates of the public hearings and comment deadline, and any Chinese response before the truce deadline. A legal challenge is likely, given the way the previous tariffs ended. Until the lists appear, the plan is best read as an opening position, not a settled policy.
