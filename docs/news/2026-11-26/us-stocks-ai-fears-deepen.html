US stocks fall further on AI-boom fears as defensive names hold up

In one sentence
A deeper selloff in US stocks is being blamed on lingering doubts over the AI boom, with defensive names holding up, though verified figures for the moves were not available.

US equities extended their decline this week, and the explanation on offer is a familiar one: doubts about the artificial-intelligence boom have not gone away. A second, deeper leg down has put the market's most crowded trade back under scrutiny, and has sent some investors toward the least fashionable corners of the index.

The BBC's economics editor, Faisal Islam, has said that concerns over the AI boom persist. Index levels and percentage moves were not available from a verified source at the time of writing, so this article does not quote them. The direction of travel, and the reason being given for it, are better established than the size of the move.

The crowded trade

The logic of the worry is simple. A large share of recent US equity gains has been attributed to companies building, supplying or selling the infrastructure of AI: chips, data centres, cloud capacity and the software layered on top. When one theme carries that much weight, a loss of conviction in it does not stay contained. It drags on the headline indices, and it raises awkward questions about the spending plans that justify the valuations in the first place.

That is the exposure the selloff has exposed. If AI-linked names are leading the decline, the rest of the market is tied to the same shock through index weights, through earnings that depend on corporate AI budgets, and through the wider investment cycle in power, property and equipment. The brief behind this piece offers no verified detail on which stocks fell furthest, and that gap matters. A selloff concentrated in a handful of heavyweight names reads differently from one that is broad.

Where money went instead

One thread in the coverage points to a rotation. Barron's has highlighted what it calls anti-AI stocks, namely chocolate makers, cigarette companies and pest-control firms, as holding up while technology slumped. The appeal is easy to see. Demand for a chocolate bar or a pest-control visit owes nothing to data-centre build-outs, and earnings of that kind are the textbook refuge when growth stocks wobble.

Caution is warranted on how far this goes. The Barron's piece is the sole source, no prices or percentages have been confirmed, and relative resilience in a falling market is not the same as a rally. Nor is there support in the US-market source for any read-across to UK index composition; that would need separate checking before it is treated as a UK angle.

There is also a counter-voice. Barron's reports that HSBC's strategists describe equities as being in a "sweet spot", a constructive stance held despite the AI-linked volatility. The report's specifics were not available for this article, so the view is best treated as a marker of disagreement among strategists rather than a forecast to lean on. It does suggest that at least some houses see the selloff as a bump rather than a break.

A thin session, and a holiday

The timing complicates the picture. European American depositary receipts fell sharply in Wednesday's US session, according to MT Newswires, the day before the Thanksgiving closure of US exchanges on 26 November. Pre-holiday sessions tend to be lightly traded, and moves made on thin volume can exaggerate sentiment. MT Newswires' line framed the fall as a signal for the UK and European open, but the brief gives no cause for it, no named securities and no volumes. Whether it reflected the AI worries or simply holiday liquidity cannot be established from the material to hand.

That uncertainty is the honest conclusion on the day. The AI-doubt narrative is plausible and widely repeated, yet the numbers that would test it, such as the size of the falls, the breadth of the decline and the scale of the defensive bid, are missing from verified sources.

What to watch

With US markets shut on Thursday, the first real test comes when Wall Street reopens, likely in a shortened session, and volumes return to normal. Whether AI-linked names stabilise or extend their losses will show if the decline was conviction or thin trading. Beyond that, the next set of capital-spending commentary from the large technology groups will matter more than any single day's tape, since those budgets underpin the whole boom. If they hold, HSBC's "sweet spot" reading has a case. If they are trimmed, the defensive names may find they have company.
