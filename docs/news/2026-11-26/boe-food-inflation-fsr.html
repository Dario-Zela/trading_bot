Bank of England warns UK food inflation could reach 5% next year as Financial Stability Report lands

In one sentence
The Bank of England reportedly warned UK food inflation could near 5% next year, but it is an unverified forecast that may not break from its earlier projections.

The Bank of England has put food prices back into the rate-path argument. Reports say it warned that UK food inflation could reach 5% next year. That is a forecast, not a measured rise. The figures behind it could not be checked against the Bank's own text, and the numbers below are best read with that in mind.

What was said, and what was not verified

The headline claim comes from the Guardian and Retail Gazette. A Retail Gazette item dated September 2026, citing the Financial Times, said food inflation could approach 5% next summer. It put the cause down to higher energy prices and weaker agricultural output feeding through to shelves. That is the closest match to the claim found. It is a second-hand account and a conditional one.

The Bank's own documents give a muddier picture. The November 2026 Monetary Policy Report projects headline CPI rising to around 5% by the middle of next year before easing. That is a different measure from food inflation, and the two should not be conflated. An earlier Bank survey of firms, from April 2026, pointed to food inflation of 6–7% during 2026. On a no-intervention basis the Bank saw it pushing towards 9–10% by year-end. Those are higher than 5%, which suggests the new warning is not obviously a step up on the Bank's earlier thinking.

No Financial Stability Report text containing a food-inflation projection was found. The report is a scheduled publication on bank, household and sector risks. Nothing retrieved shows what it says on those, so there is no finding to report beyond its publication.

The date, vote split and policy rate of the Monetary Policy Committee's latest decision were also not confirmed, and are left out for that reason.

Why food matters to the rate path

Food is a small share of the CPI basket but a visible one. Shoppers notice it weekly, and it feeds into inflation expectations among households and firms. Those expectations are what the MPC watches when it weighs the risk of inflation proving persistent.

If food inflation does run near 5%, it does two things. It squeezes household budgets, which weakens consumption and adds to arrears risk, the kind of thing a stability report examines. It also gives rate-setters a reason to move cautiously on cuts. The two effects pull in different directions, which is the usual difficulty for a committee balancing weak demand against sticky prices.

The market channel is conventional. Slower cuts support gilt yields, keep mortgage pricing higher for longer, and weigh on rate-sensitive equities such as housebuilders and domestic banks. Retailers face the opposite pressure on the cost side. The recent Poundland administration shows how thin the margin for discounters has become.

A forecast, not an event

The caveat bears repeating. A Bank projection is conditional on energy prices, sterling, harvests and the policy path itself. It can be revised at the next Monetary Policy Report. Unless the new projection marks a clear break from the last one, it reads as a refinement of an inflation story already in the Bank's numbers rather than a new shock.

The evidence that would settle the matter is specific. The Bank's published food-inflation path in the Monetary Policy Report would show whether the 5% figure is new. The ONS CPI release gives the measured food rate to set against it. The minutes of the MPC's latest meeting would show whether food was cited by members who voted against cuts. Until those are checked, the 5% figure is a warning to track, not a number to price.
