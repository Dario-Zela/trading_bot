Treasury raises cost concerns over Milburn youth unemployment plan

In one sentence
The Treasury has pushed back on the cost of Alan Milburn's youth-unemployment review, notably its National Insurance cut, making it a test of fiscal headroom, though no figures were given.

The Treasury has pushed back on the cost of parts of Alan Milburn's review of youth unemployment. That turns a report about young people out of work into a test of how much room the government actually has to spend. The Guardian's report, which this article draws on, gives no figures for the Treasury's objection. Nor does it include a Treasury quote or say how large its concerns are.

What Milburn proposed

Milburn, a former health secretary, published his review in mid-November. It focuses on the roughly one million young people who are not in employment, education or training, the group known as NEETs. He argues the system needs a "complete system reset", according to The Week's summary.

The main recommendations are:

  • A legal guarantee. Young people unemployed for more than 12 months would be entitled to a six-month placement, training or employment support, delivered through a Youth Opportunity Service in England. Those who refuse could lose benefits.
  • Benefit changes. Under-25s would lose the health element of universal credit and personal independence payments. A new "youth allowance" at the standard universal credit rate would replace them. Milburn accepts that some young people could be worse off at first.
  • A hiring subsidy. Employers would receive a £3,000 Youth Jobs Grant for taking on someone who has been out of work for six months or more. The estimated cost is £5.8bn a year. Milburn offsets that with £3.6bn in welfare savings and £2.4bn in extra tax revenue and lower benefit payments. Critics say the net saving is nearer £200m a year.
  • Employer National Insurance. The review recommends reversing the employer contribution increases for workers aged 21 to 24. Retail and hospitality provide almost 40% of under-25 jobs.

Where the Treasury objects

The clearest reported resistance is on National Insurance. Retail Week reported on 19 November that the Treasury opposes this recommendation, which retailers widely welcomed. The article is paywalled, so the Treasury's reasoning is not visible. UnHerd describes the review as proposing to double the employer threshold for 21- to 24-year-olds from £5,000 to £10,000, but only when the Treasury judges the public finances allow it. That condition is already a concession to the Treasury's veto.

The wider spending ask is larger. Other coverage, including City AM, says the review tells the Chancellor to find billions more. Milburn has called for a "radical reprioritisation" of Whitehall spending. The Irish News put the package at £58bn a year, and the review attributes a £125bn annual cost to the current system. I could not confirm how those figures relate to one another. They should be read as claims, not as a settled Treasury costing.

Why the dispute matters

Milburn's case is that spending now pays back later. Reports say he projects higher employment adding to growth and tax receipts, and that welfare savings would not begin until 2029. A 10-year programme with costs up front and savings later is hard for a Treasury that is protecting thin headroom against its fiscal rules.

That is why the argument matters beyond one report. Which departmental proposals survive contact with the Treasury is a guide to how tight the headroom really is. It also decides whether young people out of work see the guarantee, the grant and the National Insurance cut, or only some of them.

What to watch

The test is how much of the package the government adopts and when. Ministers have been reported to be weighing a slower rise in the youth minimum wage. The next signal is a formal government response to the review, and whether it sets out costings, particularly for the National Insurance change.
