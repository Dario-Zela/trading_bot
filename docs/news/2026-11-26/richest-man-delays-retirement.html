Europe's richest man delays retirement until he is 95

In one sentence
Arnault's reported plan to stay at LVMH until 95 is governed by a bylaw the family-controlled board keeps raising; the filed limit is 85, and the Telegraph's 95 is unconfirmed.

Bernard Arnault, the 77-year-old chairman and chief executive of LVMH and Europe's richest man, is in no hurry to leave. The Telegraph reports that he intends to delay retirement until he is 95. The figure is eye-catching. The more useful question is how a listed company comes to let one man's birthday be a matter of bylaws.

A caution on sourcing: The Telegraph article itself could not be read for this piece. What follows rests on the public record of LVMH's age rules, and the 95 figure is attributed to the Telegraph rather than independently confirmed.

The mechanism: a rule that keeps moving

The lever is a clause in LVMH's articles of association capping the age of the chairman and chief executive. That cap has been raised twice in quick succession. It stood at 75 until 2022, when shareholders lifted it to 80. In April 2025 the annual meeting raised it again, to 85, with 99.18% of votes cast in favour.

A vote that lopsided says less about persuasion than about structure. The Arnault family controls the group through a holding company, giving it a commanding share of voting rights, so a proposal the family backs is in practice a proposal that passes. Retirement at LVMH is therefore set by the controlling shareholder, ratified by the others.

On the public record, the limit is 85. Reaching 95 would need another change to the bylaws, and another vote, before Arnault turns 85 in 2034. That is an inference from how the rule works, not a detail from the Telegraph.

What outside investors want

The repeated extensions have not gone unremarked. Arnault has said retirement is not an immediate priority, and investors have pressed for clarity on succession. Some read the move to 85 as a way of postponing a decision, not making one. In January, shareholders were reported to be asking for more transparency about the succession plan.

The family has no shortage of candidates. Arnault's five children hold senior roles across the group. Naming one as heir, or setting out how the choice will be made, is a different matter from tolerating an ever-higher age cap.

The argument for patience is not frivolous. Warren Buffett, then 93, was reported to have written to Arnault warning that an age limit of 80 was a mistake. Founders who built a group of this size tend to be hard to replace. The argument against is that continuity resting on one person's health is a risk, however well he looks.

What to watch

The next marker is LVMH's annual general meeting in spring, where any further amendment to the age clause would have to appear on the agenda. A resolution to lift the cap past 85 would confirm the Telegraph's account. Its absence would suggest that, for now, the working limit remains 85.
