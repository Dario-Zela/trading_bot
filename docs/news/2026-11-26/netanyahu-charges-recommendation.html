Israeli judges recommend dropping most serious charges against Netanyahu

In one sentence
Judges in Netanyahu's trial urged prosecutors to consider dropping the bribery case and questioned another, but the attorney general decides, and the president's pardon track is separate.

The judges hearing Benjamin Netanyahu's corruption trial said on Wednesday that prosecutors should consider dropping the most serious of the three cases against him. They also said another case may not describe a crime at all. The remarks were not a ruling. They went to the attorney general, who alone decides whether the prosecution goes ahead, and they did not go to the president's office. That differs from the framing in the triage notes for this piece, which suggested the matter would be routed to a political pardon process. The reporting reviewed here does not support that, and the pardon track is a separate matter that has been running for a year.

What the judges said

Netanyahu was indicted in 2019 on bribery, fraud and breach of trust in three linked cases. The trial began in 2020 and has been repeatedly delayed. He has denied all the charges and calls the prosecution a political witch hunt.

According to accounts of Wednesday's hearing, the panel's remarks fell into three parts:

  • Case 4000, the bribery case. Prosecutors allege Netanyahu gave regulatory favours to the telecoms group Bezeq, reportedly worth about 1.8bn shekels (about $500m). In return, they say, he received favourable coverage on a news site owned by the group's controlling shareholder. The judges said bribery and conflict of interest would be hard to prove and that the chances of conviction were low. They asked the attorney general to consider dropping the bribery charge.
  • Case 2000. This concerns an alleged deal with a newspaper owner that never materialised. The judges expressed serious doubt that any offence was committed.
  • Case 1000. Prosecutors allege Netanyahu accepted nearly $300,000 in gifts, including cigars and champagne, from the film producer Arnon Milchan and the billionaire James Packer between 2007 and 2016. In return, they say, he tried to extend a tax exemption for Milchan and help him renew a US visa. The judges said this case could be brought to a conclusion, which was read as pointing towards a plea agreement.

The suggestion is not new in kind. In 2023 the same bench proposed that prosecutors consider dropping the bribery charge, and prosecutors declined. In June, after Netanyahu finished testifying, the judges again indicated that bribery would be hard to establish. Wednesday's remarks go further because they cover all three cases and come at the end of the evidence.

Who decides

Israeli courts do not drop charges. Only the prosecution can withdraw them, and the attorney general, Gali Baharav-Miara, sits at the top of that chain. She told the court that she and her team would consider the judges' comments and remained open to all options. For Case 1000 she described the options as a verdict or a plea deal. If the cases continue, the same judges would issue the verdict unless a plea agreement is reached.

Netanyahu's response was immediate. In a televised statement he said "today, the trial has fallen" and that it "must end immediately." He called the judges' remarks a "ray of truth and justice." That reading goes beyond what the bench said. The panel questioned two cases, and it pointed the third towards a possible settlement, which usually involves some admission of wrongdoing. It did not suggest that the whole prosecution should end.

The pardon track

The president's office is still involved, though through a different route. A year ago Netanyahu formally asked President Isaac Herzog for a pardon, with public backing from Donald Trump. Herzog did not grant it. He offered to start a mediation process towards a plea deal instead. Herzog has said the attorney general was ready for talks and that Netanyahu's representatives had not responded.

A Justice Ministry pardons unit reportedly found the request did not meet the necessary conditions. It reportedly said a pardon would be appropriate only if he resigned, confessed or was convicted. Bloomberg reported in April that Herzog had put the request aside for now.

The judges' remarks could change that. If the attorney general drops Case 4000 and narrows the rest, the argument for a pardon weakens, because the pressure that prompted the request eases. If she does not, the judges' comments give Netanyahu's camp a public statement from the bench that it can use to press for a deal on better terms.

Political stakes

The timing matters. Netanyahu and his allies failed to win a parliamentary majority in last month's election. The centrist former military chief Gadi Eisenkot has been given the first chance to form a government. Most mainstream parties have refused to sit with a prime minister who is on trial. A material narrowing of the charges would weaken that objection, though it would not remove it. Case 1000 could still end in a conviction or a plea deal.

The longer-running conflict between the government and the judiciary is also in play. Netanyahu's allies have attacked the attorney general and the courts for years. A decision by Baharav-Miara to keep the bribery charge against the bench's advice would draw sharp criticism from them. A decision to drop it would draw criticism from opponents who say the prosecution was sound.

What to watch

The first marker is the attorney general's formal response to the court, which will show whether she drops Case 4000, narrows Case 2000 or keeps both. The second is whether Netanyahu's lawyers answer Herzog's mediation offer. The third is the coalition talks under Eisenkot, where the status of the trial will bear on who can sit in government.

A caveat on sourcing: the account here draws on reports of the hearing, including wire-agency copy carried by regional outlets, and the BBC original was not available for direct comparison. The judges' exact wording on each case should be checked against the court record.
