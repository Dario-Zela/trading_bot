NatWest targets £200bn in wealth assets as deal savings rise

In one sentence
NatWest aims to lift its wealth arm above £200bn of assets by 2030 and has raised its Evelyn Partners cost-savings target to £120m from about £100m.

NatWest has set itself a goal of taking its private banking and wealth arm above £200bn in assets under management and administration (AUMA) by 2030. It also lifted the cost savings it expects from integrating Evelyn Partners, the wealth manager it bought this year. The targets came in a strategy update on 25 November.

The angle that the savings are paying for the wealth push is an inference. The bank's own release does not say so. What it does show is a business that has bought scale and now has to make the numbers work.

The targets

The division held £132.4bn at the end of September, up from £130.6bn when the roughly £2.7bn Evelyn Partners acquisition completed. Reaching more than £200bn means adding about £68bn in four years. NatWest's plan rests on net new money of more than 5% of opening assets a year, plus market growth.

The other 2030 goals are:

  • a cost-to-income ratio in the mid-50s;
  • returns above 40%;
  • about £150m of revenue synergies, split roughly £50m from high-net-worth clients and £100m from affluent customers.

The cost saving is now put at £120m, against the roughly £100m flagged when the deal was announced in February. That is a rise of about a fifth. The February announcement put one-off integration costs at about £150m. NatWest's materials do not say whether that estimate has changed.

Where the growth is meant to come from

Most of the plan is about moving existing NatWest customers into wealth products. The bank counts 1.4 million affluent customers, of whom more than 40% could benefit from advice. It estimates that 23 million consumers in the UK are underserved by advice.

The stated aims are:

  • more than 50,000 affluent referrals a year;
  • more than 20,000 new financial-planning customers a year;
  • a quadrupling of retail customers investing through its digital platform;
  • a tripling of referrals from the commercial and institutional bank;
  • growth of more than 25% in ultra- and high-net-worth clients.

The bank also pointed to early momentum. New AUM inflows rose 33% in the first half of 2026, and 45,000 new investors joined, up 60% on the year. NatWest Invest's first-time investors rose 30% in the first nine months.

The 'targeted support' offering fits here. It is a regulated, lighter-touch form of guidance for people who do not want full advice. On the second-quarter call, NatWest said it had built an integrated financial-planning team to take advantage of opportunities such as targeted support, with referrals already running in both directions.

Emma Crystal, who runs the division, said that combining the two businesses "gives us the capabilities to meet more of our clients' needs."

How much it proves

The plan is ambitious but internally coherent. The cost synergies are modest next to the size of the division, and the larger prize is on the revenue side. That is also the harder part, because it depends on customers acting on referrals and on markets cooperating. A 40% return target is high for a business that has just absorbed an acquisition.

The release gives no total income figure for the division. It does not mention share buybacks. City AM reported that the Evelyn purchase was large enough for the bank to pause further buybacks. That report, rather than the release, is the basis for describing the deal as a draw on capital.

Market sites such as MarketBeat and Simply Wall St. repackaged the announcement, and the figures above come from NatWest's own release.

What to watch

The first test is the bank's full-year results in February. The thing to watch there is whether the £120m cost synergy and the net new money run-rate hold, and whether the 33% rise in inflows is sustained. Flows from the retail and commercial arms are the best early read on whether the cross-selling is working.
