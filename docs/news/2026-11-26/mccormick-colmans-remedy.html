McCormick offers to sell Colman's mustard to win UK clearance for Unilever deal

In one sentence
McCormick is reported to be offering Colman's to win CMA clearance for its $44.8bn Unilever deal, though the regulator's concerns centre on dry mixes and sauces, not mustard.

McCormick has put the sale of Colman's, the Norwich-born English mustard brand, on the table as it tries to clear its $44.8bn takeover of most of Unilever's food business with the UK competition authorities, according to Food Dive. It is an offer, not a sale. No buyer, price or response from the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) has been reported.

The offer sits awkwardly with the regulator's own findings, which are worth setting out.

What the CMA actually found

In its phase 1 decision, published on 11 November, the CMA said the deal raised a "realistic prospect" of a substantial lessening of competition in the UK. The problem areas were dry ingredient mixes and dry sauces sold to retailers, where the combined group would be the largest supplier "by far". Nestlé's Maggi in dry mixes and Premier Foods' Bisto in dry sauces would, it said, offer only limited competitive pressure, and it did not expect new entrants to fill the gap.

Mustard was not on that list. The CMA found no competition concerns there, reasoning that shoppers do not treat English, French and American-style mustards as substitutes for one another. McCormick owns French's; Unilever owns Colman's.

The companies had until 18 November to offer remedies, formally undertakings in lieu of reference. If the CMA judges them insufficient, the case goes to an in-depth phase 2 investigation, which is slower and carries a real risk of blocking or reshaping the deal.

Why offer Colman's at all

The brand's disposal pre-dates the CMA's decision. In August the companies said they would sell Colman's to address potential competition concerns, and Unilever has said it has already decided to market the brand and its assets, with talks with potential buyers "progressing well". Press reports at the time described the brand's value as not material to the group.

That makes the framing of Colman's as the price of clearance something to hold loosely. On the CMA's published reasoning, the mustard overlap was not the problem. A plausible reading is that the sale is a pre-emptive concession, cheap for a group of this size and politically legible in Britain, offered alongside whatever else is needed for dry mixes and sauces. The reports reviewed here do not say whether the package also includes brands in those categories. The CMA's response will show whether Colman's is doing any work in the remedy at all.

What to watch

The CMA must decide whether to accept the offered undertakings, or consult on them, or refer the deal to phase 2. Any named buyer for Colman's, and the terms of the remedy on dry mixes and sauces, will say more about the cost of clearance than the mustard does. Completion of the deal depends on clearance from the CMA among other authorities. For shoppers, the practical question is who ends up owning the yellow tin; for the deal's economics, it is the dry-goods aisle.
