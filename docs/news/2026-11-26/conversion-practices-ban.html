Labour 'committed' to conversion practices ban despite calls to scrap it

In one sentence
Labour says it remains committed to banning conversion practices despite calls to scrap the proposed law, leaving scope and timing as the real fault lines.

Labour is standing by its pledge to outlaw conversion practices in England and Wales, even as critics press ministers to abandon the proposed law. The government has described itself as "committed" to a ban, according to reporting by the Guardian. The argument is now less about whether the measure is wanted than about whether a government with a large majority and a crowded legislative timetable will keep a promise that has already outlasted several prime ministers.

A promise with a long history

Conversion practices are attempts to change or suppress a person's sexual orientation or gender identity. They range from organised "therapy" to pressure exerted within religious or family settings. Banning them was first promised by Theresa May's government in 2018, after a national LGBT survey found that a notable minority of respondents had been offered or had undergone such treatment.

The pledge then stalled. Boris Johnson's government confirmed a ban in 2021, retreated in 2022 to a version covering sexual orientation only, and never put a bill before Parliament. The reversals left the subject politically charged. For campaigners, the delay was evidence of reluctance. For opponents, the shifting scope showed how hard the underlying questions are.

Labour's 2024 manifesto promised to deliver a ban that was inclusive of trans people. That wording matters, because the inclusion of gender identity is where most of the dispute lies.

Where the objections come from

Critics of the proposed law tend to raise three kinds of concern. The first is definitional: that a statute broad enough to catch coercive practices could also reach ordinary conversations between clergy, counsellors, parents and young people. The second is clinical: that clinicians treating children and teenagers who are questioning their gender could fear prosecution for cautious, exploratory approaches. The third is that existing criminal law on assault, coercion and harassment already covers the worst abuses.

Supporters answer that the worst practices are well documented, that existing offences are hard to apply when the harm is psychological and the setting is religious or domestic, and that carve-outs for consensual, non-coercive support can be written into the bill. Drafting is therefore the real battleground. The detail of how a ban defines "practice", how it treats consent and what protections it gives to counselling and pastoral care will decide whether the legislation is workable.

What the government is signalling

Holding the line is a choice with a price. A contested bill takes up parliamentary time, and ministers have other business to protect. Dropping it would break a manifesto commitment and would be read as a concession on the question of trans inclusion. The decision to restate the pledge publicly suggests ministers have judged that backing away would cost more than pressing on.

The pledge says nothing about timing, however. A commitment to a ban is different from a date for a bill, and this one has so far been expressed as the former. Devolved administrations are also moving at different speeds on their own approaches, which adds pressure for a consistent position across the UK.

What to watch

The test will be whether a draft bill is published, and what it says about scope. Publication of draft text would allow scrutiny by a parliamentary committee, which is where the definitional arguments would be fought out in detail. Until then, "committed" remains a statement of intent that campaigners will measure against the legislative calendar.
