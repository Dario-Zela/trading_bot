US 7-year Treasury auction shows softer demand

In one sentence
A $44bn 7-year Treasury sale cleared at 4.708%, a basis point wide of expectations, a small softness that feeds the term-premium debate without yet showing a trend.

The US Treasury sold $44 billion of seven-year notes on Wednesday, and the market took them with a little less enthusiasm than it likes to show. The sale cleared at a high yield of 4.708%, about one basis point above where the notes were trading in the when-issued market just before the auction, which was 4.698%. That gap, known as a tail, means the Treasury paid slightly more than the market expected to get the deal done.

One basis point is small. The Wall Street Journal has tied the result to the debate over term premium and how much US debt investors must absorb, a link this piece could not check against the article itself. The numbers support a modest reading: a routine sale that was soft, not a failed one.

The numbers

The bid-to-cover ratio, which compares total bids with the amount offered, was 2.43. Indirect bidders, a category that includes foreign central banks and large asset managers, took 58.2% of the sale. Direct bidders, mostly domestic institutions bidding on their own account, took 33.2%. Primary dealers, the banks obliged to bid at every auction, were left with 8.6%.

A low dealer share is usually a good sign, because dealers take what real-money buyers leave behind. These figures do not say whether this was a weak sale on that measure. Comparisons with previous auctions' averages for bid-to-cover, tail and bidder shares were not available in the sources checked, and the Treasury's own results page was not reachable for this article. Descriptions of the sale as having "disappointed" rest on secondary reports, and some of the history quoted in them does not reconcile with the November figures.

Why term premium comes into it

Term premium is the extra yield investors demand for holding a long bond instead of rolling short-dated ones. It cannot be observed directly. Estimates vary by model, and it tends to rise when investors worry about inflation, or about the volume of debt arriving on the market.

An auction is one of the few places where that demand is tested in public. A wide tail, or a thin cover ratio, suggests buyers want to be paid more to take the supply. A one-basis-point tail is within the ordinary noise of the process. One Australian fixed-income commentary linked the sale to a strong inflation reading that kept a December Federal Reserve rate rise in play, and said the global term premium "remains sensitive to supply even as growth risks rise". That is an interpretation of the result, not a finding in it.

What to watch

The case for a trend needs more than one auction. The next tests are the Treasury's upcoming coupon sales, where the tail and the share taken by indirect bidders can be compared with this week's 2.43 cover and 58.2% indirect share. If tails widen and foreign demand fades over several sales, the argument that long-end buyers want a higher yield to hold US debt gains weight. For now, the result is one soft data point.
