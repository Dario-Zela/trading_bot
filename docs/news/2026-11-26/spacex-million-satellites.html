US approves SpaceX plan to send a million data satellites into orbit

In one sentence
The FCC has licensed SpaceX to launch up to one million orbital computing satellites by 2040, overruling objections from Amazon, Viasat and astronomers, though none has yet flown.

The US Federal Communications Commission has cleared SpaceX to launch up to one million satellites, each a small orbital computer, in what would be the first data-centre constellation in space. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr announced the approval on Wednesday 25 November. Elon Musk replied on X: "Much appreciated. This will be a great asset to America and the world." The system, called Starmind, is a licence to try rather than a working network. No Starmind satellite has flown, and SpaceX has not said how much of the plan it expects to build.

What the FCC actually approved

SpaceX filed its application on 30 January, soon after it acquired the AI company xAI. The Space Bureau accepted it for comment in early February. The authorisation covers up to one million satellites at altitudes of 500 km to 1,002 km. Each satellite would carry a computing payload averaging 175 kW, peaking at 250 kW, powered by solar arrays based on Starlink's design and cooled by deployable liquid radiators, according to trade outlet Via Satellite. Data would move mainly over optical links between satellites. Ka-band radio, under Starlink's existing authorisation, is limited to backup and emergency use. Some bands carry waivers and must run on an "unprotected" and non-interference basis.

The licence runs for 15 years from the first Starmind launch and sets milestones, per Via Satellite:

  • one satellite by 25 November 2033
  • 10% of the fleet by 25 November 2035
  • 50% by 25 November 2038
  • the full million by 25 November 2040

SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell has said launches should begin in 2027.

The approval itself says little about the number of satellites SpaceX must actually build. Milestones of this kind are common in satellite licences, and operators have missed them before. Amazon is already behind on its own deployment targets, a point Carr made when answering its objections.

The objections that were overruled

Amazon's Kuiper unit (now Amazon Leo), Viasat and the American Astronomical Society all petitioned to deny the application. Amazon called Starmind a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" and said SpaceX would need "centuries" to build it. The FCC replied that questions of feasibility and business viability fall outside its mandate and declined to substitute its judgment for the applicant's.

The agency also dismissed concerns about atmospheric effects from satellites burning up on re-entry as "speculative and not supported in the record". It required no further National Environmental Policy Act review and brushed aside worries about optical astronomy. It said it does not cap the number of stations it can authorise in similar orbits, so congestion did not block the approval. Earthjustice's Jan Hasselman criticised both the skipped environmental review and the timing, in a holiday week. Astrophysicist Adam Becker warned of "a cascading chain reaction of space junk collisions". The Secure World Foundation had urged a phased, demonstration-based approach, calling constellations of this size a "qualitative shift".

For scale, roughly 15,000 satellites currently operate in orbit, according to figures cited around the filing. One million is about 65 times that.

Why compute people are watching

The pitch is that space offers near-constant sunlight and no land or grid constraints. SpaceX argued in its filing that orbital data centres are "the most efficient way to meet the accelerating demand for AI computing power". Analysts quoted by Via Satellite say the concept remains technically and economically unproven. Heat rejection, radiation damage to chips and the cost of getting hardware up and replacing it are the open questions. Nothing in the approval tests those.

The approval does matter for competitors. Google's Project Suncatcher put its first AI chips into orbit in October, on a SpaceX rocket and a Planet Labs satellite. Blue Origin has asked the FCC for up to 51,600 satellites under Project Sunrise, and Starcloud has applied for 88,000. The FCC says it will decide those "expeditiously". If the agency treats Starmind as precedent, the first such approval was also the largest.

The spectrum side is narrower than the headline suggests. Because the links are mostly optical, Starmind leans less on scarce radio bands than a conventional broadband constellation does. That may help explain why the radio-spectrum objections did not carry the day.

What to watch

The first test is the 2027 launch schedule, which will show whether a 175 kW satellite exists as hardware. The second is whether the FCC's pending decisions on Blue Origin and Starcloud adopt the same light-touch approach to debris and environmental review. The third is whether Amazon, Viasat or the astronomers seek reconsideration or take the matter to court. None has said so.
