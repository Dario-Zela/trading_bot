No 10 won't rule out re-inviting influencer at centre of 'vile video' row as social media unit spend set to double

In one sentence
Burnham won't apologise for a 'vile video' and No 10 hasn't ruled out re-inviting the influencer involved, as social media spending is reported set to double to £25m.

Downing Street has declined to rule out inviting back the online influencer at the centre of a row over a video that has been described as 'vile'. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has refused to apologise for the video. That has turned a short-lived controversy into a question about how far No 10 is prepared to court the creators it has cultivated, and whether it will keep doing so.

The detail available so far is thin. The Guardian and BBC political desks report that Burnham will not apologise and that No 10 has not closed the door on a return invitation. Neither the triage notes nor the searches run for this piece turned up a verbatim Downing Street line or a full account of the video's contents. This article therefore sticks to what is reported and does not characterise the video itself.

A government that courts creators

The row sits within a wider strategy. Burnham's team has leaned heavily on TikTok, podcasts and informal formats. Commentators have called him Britain's first 'TikTok prime minister', and one report says No 10 has told him to tone down his 'blokey' pub antics. Inviting influencers into Downing Street is part of the same approach: it reaches audiences that conventional briefings do not.

The cost of that approach is that the government shares a platform with people over whom it has little control. Refusing to rule out a repeat invitation keeps the channel open. It also means the government is not distancing itself from someone it has already hosted. Burnham's refusal to apologise points the same way, though a spokesperson's non-answer is not the same as a decision to invite the person again.

The money behind the megaphone

Separately, the BBC reports that spending on the government's social media operation is set to double to £25m, at a time when ministers' communications are under scrutiny. The report does not give the baseline or the period covered. Doubling to £25m implies a current budget of about £12.5m. That is close to the £13.2m annual messaging budget that Civil Service World reported for the New Media Unit, which has about 20 staff. A parliamentary answer gave a lower planned spend of £10.9m. The figures may not be like for like, and the unit named in the BBC report has not been confirmed as the same one.

Government departments that disclosed their figures have also reported spending about £2.1m on influencers since the 2024-25 financial year began, according to LBC. The true total is probably higher, because some departments declined to disclose.

Informing or promoting

The two threads meet on one question. Public money spent on the government's own channels can be justified as public information: campaigns on health, benefits or safety delivered where people actually spend their time. Critics will say the larger the budget, the more it looks like promotion of ministers. Paid or favoured relationships with influencers sharpen that concern, because audiences may not distinguish a public-information message from a political one.

The row has limited reach beyond Westminster, and it is not a market story. Its significance is as a test of how a government that has built its presentation around online personalities handles one of them when the association becomes awkward.

What to watch: whether No 10 gives a clearer answer on future invitations, and whether the £25m figure is confirmed with a baseline and a time period. A published breakdown of how much of it goes to influencers would show whether the spending is mostly information or mostly promotion.
