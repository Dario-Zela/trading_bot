Images of asteroid hit in planetary-defence test reveal stadium-sized crater

In one sentence
ESA's Hera spacecraft has imaged the crater NASA's DART left on Dimorphos in 2022, a stadium-sized scar that gives planetary-defence scientists their first measurements of the impact's aftermath.

The crater that NASA's DART spacecraft gouged into the asteroid Dimorphos in 2022 is as big as a stadium, and it is wide enough to have flattened most of the face it hit. That is the first clear view of the scar, taken by Europe's Hera spacecraft, and it was released on Wednesday. It also sits awkwardly against the forecasts made before Hera arrived.

What Hera saw

DART struck Dimorphos, a small moonlet orbiting the larger asteroid Didymos, on 26 September 2022. The collision shortened Dimorphos's orbit by about 32 minutes. It was the first time humans had measurably changed the motion of a natural body in space. DART's own camera died on impact, so nobody has seen the aftermath from close range until now.

Hera, launched by the European Space Agency (ESA) on 7 October 2024, reached the Didymos system in November. Its first pictures, released around 11 November, showed the side of Didymos that DART never imaged. Dimorphos was barely visible, sitting mostly in its parent's shadow. Patrick Michel, Hera's principal investigator, said of that first view: "The first thing we said was wow!"

The later close-ups are more informative. Coverage of the release describes a crater roughly the size of a stadium, spanning much of the asteroid's width. Sabina Raducan, a member of the Hera team, called it "a big crater that is almost as large as the body itself." Away from the crater, ESA says the boulder fields look much as they did in DART's final frames, with no evidence of major changes elsewhere on Dimorphos. Long-exposure images also show hundreds of boulders drifting around the system.

Michael Kueppers, an ESA scientist on the mission, said the impact left Dimorphos "tumbling wildly in its orbit." Some of the boulders near Didymos may be debris thrown across the roughly 1 km gap between the two bodies.

Why the crater matters

Before Hera arrived, ESA had reported a simulation study suggesting no tidy crater would be found. Dimorphos is thought to be a loosely bound pile of rubble, and the model implied the impact might have reshaped the whole body. The new images fit part of that picture: the crater is huge relative to the asteroid, and the body is clearly disturbed. They do not fit the idea of a wholesale remodelling with no identifiable crater. How the two reconcile is one of the early questions for the team. Only reported coverage was available for this piece; the full ESA release and the underlying images were not checked directly.

The size of the crater matters because of how kinetic impactors work. Hitting an asteroid does not only transfer the spacecraft's momentum. Rock blasted out of the crater leaves in the opposite direction and acts like a thruster, so the total push can exceed what the spacecraft alone delivered. Planners call the multiplier "beta". Estimating it requires knowing how much material left the crater, which depends on the crater's volume and the asteroid's internal strength and mass. Hera can measure both. DART could not.

What comes next

Hera's close-approach phase is the part that will yield numbers. ESA says the spacecraft is due to come within about 13 km of the asteroid in December, taking higher-resolution images and measuring Dimorphos's mass. Over the following months it will run a dedicated crash-site investigation with its full instrument set.

Those measurements, a mass and a crater volume, are what will let scientists check the roughly 32-minute orbital change against a physical account of where the momentum came from. The answer determines how confidently the same technique could be scaled to a real threat on a different kind of asteroid.
