Ministers to create single stalking offence, scrapping need to prove fear or distress

In one sentence
UK ministers plan to merge stalking offences into one and drop the need to prove victim fear or distress, though details such as sentencing and timetable remain unconfirmed.

The UK government intends to fold its separate stalking offences into one, and to drop the requirement that prosecutors prove the victim felt fear or distress. That is the substance of reports from BBC UK and the Guardian UK. The detail below comes largely from secondary summaries, because the primary announcement text was not available to this writer. Specifics beyond the headline should be read with that in mind.

The law as it stands

Stalking was made a specific crime in England and Wales in 2012, when the Protection of Freedoms Act added offences to the Protection from Harassment Act 1997. The structure has two tiers. A basic offence covers a course of conduct that amounts to stalking. A more serious offence covers stalking that causes the victim to fear violence, or that causes serious alarm or distress with a substantial effect on their day-to-day life.

The serious tier carries the heavier penalty, and it is also the one where the victim's reaction becomes part of what must be proved. Campaigners have long argued that this puts the wrong person under examination. A determined stalker who leaves a victim frightened may be easier to convict than one whose target has learnt to hide the effect, or whose conduct is controlling rather than overtly menacing.

What is reported

The reports describe a single standalone offence in place of the current split, with the fear-or-distress test removed. Aggregated coverage links the plan to a review led by the barrister Richard Wright, and to the case of Alice Ruggles, the young woman killed by a former partner in 2016 after he had stalked her. Her family is reported to have welcomed the change.

Some points remain unsettled. Accounts conflict on the maximum sentence: one says up to 10 years, another says the government has decided not to raise the existing maximum. This article does not state a figure. No legislative timetable has been given; the Home Office is reported to have said a bill will follow when parliamentary time allows. The Victims' Commissioner is reported to have welcomed the single offence while arguing that the government had missed a chance to deal with stalking that continues after a conviction.

What to watch

The test will be the drafting. Removing a fear-or-distress requirement shifts the weight onto what the defendant did and what they knew or ought to have known. Other jurisdictions have wrestled with the same question: New Zealand's reform debate has turned on whether prosecutors should have to prove what a stalker knew, and Ireland has kept a fear-based test in its own standalone offence. How narrowly or broadly Westminster defines the conduct, and what defences it allows for lawful or reasonable behaviour, will decide whether the change helps victims in practice or invites challenge. The first marker is the publication of a bill, or of the Home Office's own statement, which should settle the sentencing question.
