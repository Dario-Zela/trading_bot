Sri Lankan asylum seeker killed himself after UK sent him to Eswatini

In one sentence
Kishanth Peramalya, a Sri Lankan man with documented suicide risk, died in Eswatini on 13 September after a UK-arranged transfer, raising duty-of-care and transparency questions.

A Sri Lankan man who was moved by the British government to Eswatini, a small landlocked kingdom in southern Africa, has died by suicide there. Kishanth Peramalya died on 13 September, according to reporting by the BBC and the Guardian that emerged this week. The case is not a conventional asylum removal. It is, however, a documented death after a UK-arranged transfer to a country with no connection to the person sent. That makes it a test of what the government owes people it relocates, and of how far third-country arrangements can be run without parliamentary scrutiny.

From a stranded boat to Diego Garcia

Peramalya was among a group of Sri Lankans who left India by boat in 2021 hoping to reach Canada. The vessel got into difficulty in October 2021. The Royal Navy rescued those on board and took them to Diego Garcia, the British Indian Ocean Territory island that hosts a UK-US military base. The group stayed there for years in a camp that a critical UN report later described as unsuitable.

In November 2024 the UK allowed 61 of the migrants to travel to Britain. Peramalya was not among them. The then Home Secretary, Yvette Cooper, refused him entry on the basis of convictions recorded on the island: four counts of sexual assault, one of assault causing actual bodily harm, and one of arson. UK courts upheld that decision.

He could not simply be sent home. He had been protected from return to Sri Lanka because, as a teenager, he was tortured and sexually abused by members of the Sri Lankan military. A High Court judgment in February 2025 recorded that he had post-traumatic stress disorder and had made several suicide attempts on Diego Garcia. The government therefore faced a man it did not want to admit, could not return, and knew to be at serious risk of self-harm.

The move to Eswatini

According to the BBC, the Maldives agreed in late March to host him for 30 days. He was then moved to Eswatini, which borders South Africa and Mozambique. The BBC reported a draft arrangement under which the UK would pay for his resettlement for three years, and that Britain was working on plans for him to stay permanently. The Guardian said it had not seen the draft.

Eswatini's government gives a different account. Its spokesperson, Thabile Mdluli, said the original arrangement was temporary, meant to protect him while the UK sought a lasting solution, and that the two governments never concluded a formal agreement on long-term residency. She declined to discuss his stay or death, and said Eswatini had received no other comparable request from the UK.

His family told the BBC that he was first housed alone in a secluded guesthouse and struggled with isolation, phoning his sister up to four times a day. His psychiatric appointments became daily as his condition worsened. His relatives said they had not known of his convictions, or that the move might become permanent.

A UK government spokesperson said public safety remained the government's foremost concern, including preventing people convicted of serious offences abroad from entering Britain, and that the UK continues to work with Eswatini on the case.

Why it reaches beyond one case

Eswatini is not new to this kind of arrangement. Since July 2025 it has taken dozens of people deported from the United States who are not its nationals. In November 2025 its government confirmed it had received $5.1m to accept as many as 160 migrants from Washington. The pattern is one of small, dependent states paid to host people that larger ones will not.

Two caveats matter. Peramalya's case is unusual: his convictions, his protected status and his stay on a British overseas territory set it apart from ordinary asylum claims, and it does not by itself show how any wider UK removals scheme operates. And the reporting so far has not established what support was in place at the time of his death, or whether the draft terms were ever finalised.

What the case does raise is a duty-of-care question. The government's own courts had recorded his suicide risk. Whether a transfer to an isolated placement in an unfamiliar country, with a dispute between two governments over who was responsible for him, met that duty is likely to be tested.

What to watch

Three things will decide how far this travels. Ministers may face questions in Parliament about the unpublished arrangements, which the BBC described as never having been disclosed to MPs. An inquest or investigation in Eswatini or the UK, if opened, would set out what monitoring and psychiatric care existed. And lawyers acting for the family could use the case to challenge future transfers on grounds of known risk to health. The government has so far said only that it is working with Eswatini.
