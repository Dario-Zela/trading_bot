UK-EU reset treaties to carry a 10-year break clause as Brussels tables 'Made in Europe' terms

In one sentence
The UK-EU reset treaties will reportedly carry a 10-year break clause, and Brussels has tabled 'Made in Europe' terms, but neither is confirmed in published text.

The reset between Britain and the European Union is reported to have a built-in expiry date. BBC UK says, citing its understanding of the text and not an on-record confirmation, that the new treaties will carry a 10-year termination clause. Either side could walk away after a decade. Neither government has published the wording, and the reporting gives no notice period, no mechanics, and no list of the provisions the clause would cover. Until the text is out, the claim rests on a single outlet and should be read that way.

The report arrives alongside a second development, also from BBC Politics: Brussels is said to have tabled new terms that could let UK manufacturers into preferential procurement under its "Made in Europe" scheme. That offer has no figures on scope, eligibility or timing attached to it. Together the two stories describe a relationship being rebuilt with an exit door and a conditional entry ticket.

A clause with a history

A termination provision is not a surprise in itself. Earlier in the reset talks, reports said EU diplomats wanted a draft veterinary agreement to include a termination clause that would oblige Britain to pay a penalty if it left. It was nicknamed the "Farage clause", after the Reform UK leader. UK officials replied that termination clauses are standard in international agreements and apply equally to both parties. Farage's answer was that no Parliament can bind its successor and that Reform would not honour any such clause. Reform confirmed to the Financial Times that it would scrap the veterinary agreement if it took power.

The EU's reported motive is practical. Rebuilding border food-safety infrastructure is costly, and Brussels does not want to pay for it only to see a future British government reverse course. A fixed review point after ten years, or compensation for early exit, is one way of pricing that risk. A 10-year break clause is a different instrument from a penalty: it sets a date for a decision, not a charge for leaving. Which of the two the treaties contain, or whether they contain both, is exactly what the unpublished text would settle.

The politics of the reset have been unsettled too. Reports from recent months refer to the prime minister, Andy Burnham, pressing von der Leyen on "Made in Europe", and a separate piece in this edition covers the opening of fronts on the reset by Badenoch and Farage. The treaties are being negotiated against a domestic backdrop in which a change of government could revisit them.

What a decade means for planning

For companies, a break clause changes the arithmetic more than the headline. Food exporters are the clearest case. A veterinary agreement removes much of the border paperwork that has weighed on British agrifood sales since 2021. Investment in the plant, logistics and certification that goes with it normally pays back over a period close to ten years. If the legal basis can lapse at year ten, a firm must either accept that risk or demand a faster return.

Energy and finance face a version of the same problem. Cross-border energy arrangements involve assets with lives of twenty to forty years, far longer than any break point. Financial firms depend on market-access terms that regulators on both sides can revisit. The triage notes name all three sectors as exposed to the clause's durability. They do not say which treaty provisions in each sector are tied to it. Assumptions about coverage would be premature.

Such clauses tend to matter less in practice than on paper. Break rights are seldom exercised when the economic ties they govern have become deep. The EU's own agreements with other partners often include termination mechanisms that have sat unused. But the credibility the clause lends or removes depends on its detail. A long notice period and an orderly wind-down would give businesses time to adapt. A short one would leave long-lived investment exposed, and contracts would probably start to include their own exit terms.

'Made in Europe' and the conditional offer

The procurement offer is the other half of the story. The EU's "Made in Europe" proposals, part of its industrial accelerator plans, would favour European-made content in publicly funded purchases. Reporting from earlier this year said the proposals include minimum local-content thresholds, with 70% of components cited for electric vehicles. London's fear was that British firms would be shut out of a market in which public contracts are worth trillions of euros. The UK lobbied Germany, Italy, the Netherlands and Poland to resist stricter French versions. The manufacturers' body Make UK has argued for a "Made with Europe" approach that recognises trusted partners.

The new terms are therefore a reversal of the earlier worry, but the report supplies no detail on how far they go. The summary says the terms "could" give UK manufacturers access. No Commission document or official quote has been cited. It is also unclear whether access would be tied to the same treaty framework, and so to the same ten-year horizon, or negotiated separately. Carmakers have their own complication. Reports say the UK government has been wary of measures that could provoke Chinese retaliation against British exporters such as Jaguar Land Rover.

What to watch

The decisive evidence is the treaty text. Three things in it would show how time-limited the reset really is: the length of the notice period before a break takes effect, whether the clause covers every agreement or only some, and whether it is paired with compensation or wind-down obligations. A confirmation or correction from the government or the Commission would also settle whether the BBC's understanding is accurate. On procurement, the test is whether the offer is published with eligibility rules attached. Until both appear, firms in food, energy and finance are planning against a deal whose lifespan is reported but not confirmed.
