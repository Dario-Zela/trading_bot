Russia presses on with Moscow-St Petersburg high-speed line despite railway debt crisis

In one sentence
Russia reportedly keeps building its Moscow–St Petersburg high-speed line while the state railway carries heavy debt, though the single-source report gives no figures.

Russia is pressing ahead with a high-speed railway between Moscow and St Petersburg even as the state-owned railway operator carries heavy debt. The report, from the business outlet IntelliNews, offers a picture of priorities more than of finances. The line is a flagship of the state, and the balance sheet of the company that must help pay for it has not stopped it.

The report is thin on detail. It gives no total for the operator's borrowings, no cost for the project and no statement from the government or Russian Railways. Those gaps matter, and the account below is limited to what can be said with confidence.

A project with a long history

A fast link between Russia's two largest cities has been discussed, in one form or another, for well over a decade. The two cities lie roughly 650 kilometres apart, and the existing corridor already carries some of the busiest passenger services in the country. A purpose-built line would cut journey times sharply, and officials have long presented it as the centrepiece of a wider high-speed network.

The idea has also repeatedly run into the question of who pays. Projects of this kind are expensive almost everywhere. They depend on a mix of state budget money, borrowing by the operator and, in some models, private or sovereign-fund capital. How Russia intends to combine these is exactly what the IntelliNews report does not set out.

Decision or drift?

The report supports two readings, and the evidence does not separate them.

The first is that this is a deliberate choice. A state that regards a project as strategic will fund it regardless of the operator's leverage, using the budget or the National Wealth Fund to cover what the railway cannot borrow. Debt at a state company is, on this view, a secondary concern.

The second is drift. Large projects acquire momentum: contracts are signed, land is cleared, and stopping becomes politically and administratively harder than continuing. A railway with heavy debt can find itself committed to spending without a settled funding plan.

Telling them apart would require the numbers: the size of the debt, its maturity and cost, the project's budget and the share that the state has committed. Without them, the report shows only that work continues.

The wider squeeze

The backdrop is a Russian budget under strain. Defence spending is high, and borrowing costs have been elevated. In that setting, any flagship civil project becomes a test of whether the state will protect long-term infrastructure while it is short of money elsewhere. A line that proceeds despite its sponsor's debt would suggest that it will, at least for projects with symbolic weight. It would also suggest that the cost is being pushed onto the state's balance sheet, where it is harder to see.

That inference is a hypothesis, not a finding. The claims rest on a single outlet, and the figures that would confirm them have not been published.

What to watch

The useful evidence will come from Russian Railways' next financial disclosures, which should show debt and investment spending, and from the federal budget documents, which would reveal any state funding committed to the line. A published cost estimate and financing plan would settle whether this is a decision or drift. Until those appear, the story remains unverified.
