Airbus to sell its US space business to Cerberus

In one sentence
Airbus is reported to be selling its US space business to Cerberus; the buyer, price and scope remain unconfirmed beyond an Axios report and an earlier strategic review.

Airbus is reported to be selling its US space business to Cerberus Capital Management, the New York private equity firm. The report comes from Axios, which could not be independently confirmed. Two searches turned up no corroboration of the buyer, no deal value and no closing date. What is on the record is narrower: Airbus has been weighing a sale of this operation since late summer.

What is established

On 29 August the Financial Times reported that Airbus had opened a strategic review of its US satellite manufacturing business, with a sale among the options. No buyers were named then. The asset at the centre of that review is the automated satellite production plant at Merritt Island, Florida, beside NASA's Kennedy Space Center. Airbus took full ownership of the plant in early 2024, when Eutelsat OneWeb sold its 50% stake in the joint venture that had built OneWeb's constellation there.

The unit employs a little over 200 people at the site. People familiar with the division put annual sales in the hundreds of millions of dollars. That is a small slice of a group of Airbus's size. The figures are estimates from press reports, not company disclosures.

Airbus has said the review does not cover the wider Airbus U.S. Space & Defense business, which spans military aircraft, helicopters, space and government services. If the Axios account is accurate, the deal involves the Florida satellite operation, not the whole American defence presence.

Why it is a portfolio move

The timing points to a routine pruning, not a retreat. Airbus is working towards a combination of its space activities with those of Leonardo and Thales, a project reported under the name Bromo. Airbus has stressed that the US review is separate from that deal. One French report says a sale could raise several hundred million dollars, which would help fund the European consolidation. That is one outlet's reading, and Airbus has not framed it that way.

The logic is plain enough. A factory built to mass-produce small satellites for a single constellation customer is a hard business to run at scale without a steady order book. A buyer with patient capital and a taste for restructuring is a natural home for it. Cerberus, which has long had a defence and government-services bent, fits that profile, if the report is right.

What would change the reading

Three details would decide whether this is a trim or a shift. The first is price, which would show whether Airbus is exiting at a discount or realising value. The second is what comes with the plant: contracts, intellectual property and any US government work. The third is whether US export-control or foreign-ownership rules shape the terms, since the unit sits close to defence customers. A sale from a European champion to US private equity would feed the sovereignty argument in European space policy only if capabilities Europe wants to keep leave with it. A commercial small-satellite line in Florida is not obviously one of them.

Next to watch: a statement from Airbus or Cerberus confirming the buyer and terms, and any regulatory filing. Progress on the Airbus-Leonardo-Thales combination will show whether the proceeds are meant for it.
