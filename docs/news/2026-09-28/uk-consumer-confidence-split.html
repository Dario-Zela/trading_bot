UK consumer confidence splits as retail and personal-finance gauges slide

Shoppers on a UK high street ahead of the retail sector's crucial fourth-quarter trading period. — traveljunction / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
GfK's headline UK confidence index rose in September, but BRC-Opinium data shows retail-spending and personal-finance expectations falling sharply, worst among women.

British households told two different stories about their own finances this September, and the gap between them is the story.

The headline GfK/NielsenIQ Consumer Confidence Barometer rose one point to -13, its third consecutive monthly increase and the strongest reading since August 2024. But a parallel survey, run by Opinium for the British Retail Consortium among 2,000 UK adults between 8 and 11 September, told a starker tale: expected personal retail spending fell to +5, down from +8 in August, while the outlook for personal finances dropped to -15 from -9. Expectations for the wider economy over the next three months slid to -34 from -28, ending four straight months of improvement.

The decline in the BRC-Opinium reading, notably, was "much sharper among women," according to the retail body — a detail that outpaces the aggregate numbers and points to where the pressure is concentrating.

Two surveys, one household

It is tempting to treat the GfK uptick and the BRC-Opinium slide as contradictory, but they are measuring different things. GfK's headline score is built from perceptions of the general economy and personal finances over the past year, both of which improved — up four points and three points respectively — as memories of the acute cost-of-living squeeze in 2024 and 2025 continue to fade. The forward-looking, spending-specific numbers are a different matter. GfK's own Major Purchase Index fell a point even as its Savings Index jumped five, a combination that reads as households banking gains rather than spending them. The BRC-Opinium figures sharpen that picture further: shoppers say they intend to spend less on retail specifically, not just feel more cautious in the abstract.

Category by category, the retreat is uneven. Consumers remain willing to spend on food and groceries — that net balance held at +28, only a point off August — but appetite for fashion and clothing weakened to -13 from -11, and large electronics slipped to -12 from -11. That is the discretionary end of the household budget contracting first, which is the pattern retailers dread heading into the fourth quarter.

What households are blaming

Both surveys converge on the same explanation: costs are rising again, and they are rising on multiple fronts at once. GfK's Neil Bellamy flagged "inflation, energy and fuel prices moving higher" as the risk to the recovery. The BRC's Helen Dickinson was blunter, pointing to a "triple blow" of escalating employment costs, business rates and energy bills squeezing retailers themselves — costs that filter through to shelf prices — while households separately brace for a steep rise in winter energy bills that are set to rise faster than shop prices. Dickinson said sentiment around the economy had "stumbled after four months of improvement, with a similar trend for sentiment around personal finances," and called on the Chancellor to freeze the business rates multiplier ahead of the Budget rather than impose "yet another burdensome tax increase."

Why the divergence matters

Headline confidence indices are the number that makes the news, but retailers plan Q4 stock and staffing against the spending-intention numbers, not the general-sentiment ones. A BRC-Opinium reading of +5 for expected personal retail spending, down a third from August's +8, arriving just as the "Golden Quarter" gets under way, is the more operationally relevant figure — and it is moving in the wrong direction. The sharper drop among women, who disproportionately manage household budgets and Christmas spending decisions, adds a specific signal worth tracking rather than dismissing as noise.

The next test comes with October's readings and, more pointedly, the Autumn Budget, which both the BRC and GfK cited as the near-term wildcard for consumer sentiment — energy bills, business rates and any tax changes will land in these same surveys within weeks.
