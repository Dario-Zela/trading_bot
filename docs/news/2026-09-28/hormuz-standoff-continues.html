Hormuz standoff drags on: Trump rejects Iran's ceasefire terms, oil stays bid

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint between Iran and the Arabian peninsula through which a fifth of global oil trade normally passes. — NASA / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Hormuz transits stay near zero seven months into the Iran war after Trump rejected Tehran's ceasefire terms, though he told Axios talks resume this week.

Seven months into the conflict that shut the world's most important oil chokepoint, the pattern is numbingly familiar: a proposal, a rejection, a promise of more talks. President Trump on Saturday rejected Iran's latest offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within a week in exchange for an end to the US naval blockade, a lifting of oil-sanctions and a regional ceasefire. By Sunday he was telling Axios he expects American negotiators — Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, who met Iranian foreign minister Abbas Araghchi on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly last week — to re-engage "this week." He also said he had not ruled out further strikes before the November midterms.

The strait itself remains close to sealed. PortWatch tracking data put transits at six on September 6 and seven on September 9 — against a pre-war baseline of roughly 85 to 138 vessels a day, depending on the measure used. Al Jazeera has documented traffic down as much as 95% since the shooting started in February. Trump disputes the scale of the shutdown, telling Axios the US military is "facilitating" more than 20 million barrels a day out of the strait — a figure a defense official partially corroborated for a single night's tally — but independent shipping data does not show anything close to normal flow resuming.

A floor under crude that central banks can't touch

Brent settled near $104.50 a barrel on Friday, well off the roughly $145 peak reached in the conflict's worst stretch but still elevated by the standards of the past decade. The mechanism is simple: Hormuz normally carries close to a fifth of global oil and gas trade, and a chokepoint running at 5–10% of capacity keeps a risk premium baked into every barrel regardless of what demand is doing elsewhere.

That matters for monetary policy in a way rate-setters have said explicitly. The ECB has flagged the shock as a driver of second-round inflation effects — headline eurozone inflation rose to 3% in the spring and staff scenarios put it as high as 4.4% if disruption worsens — while the Fed has cited the same dynamic domestically. It is, in effect, an inflation problem neither central bank can hike its way out of: raising rates cools demand, but it does nothing to reopen a strait held shut by naval blockade and mines.

Traders and analysts are pricing the war in, not around it

The volatility has been extremely good business for the firms built to trade it. Glencore's marketing division booked roughly $3.3 billion in adjusted profit in the first half of 2026 alone — already ahead of its full $2.9 billion take for all of 2025 — putting the unit on track to blow through the top of its long-run annual guidance range. Shell's trading arm has told a similar story, with oil and gas trading results the company itself attributed to "market turmoil caused by the Iran war,” helping push first-quarter adjusted earnings up 24% year-on-year to $6.9 billion.

Equity analysts have followed the traders' lead. HSBC lifted its Chevron price target to $250 from $218, explicitly citing the company's oil-price sensitivity and comparatively low direct exposure to Middle East operating risk — part of what the bank called a broader repricing of major energy names around the conflict. The catch, as HSBC's own logic makes clear, is that the thesis runs in both directions: a real reopening of Hormuz would unwind the war premium in oil prices, and with it a meaningful share of the cash-flow assumptions now underpinning those upgraded targets.

That is the shape of the story seven months in. A blockade that has proven durable through multiple rounds of failed diplomacy is now a structural feature of the oil market — priced into inflation forecasts, trading desks' P&Ls and analyst spreadsheets alike — even as this week's renewed talks carry at least the possibility of unwinding it overnight. Watch for any confirmation that Witkoff and Kushner have resumed direct or indirect contact with Araghchi's team, and for PortWatch's daily transit count, which remains the most reliable real-time gauge of whether the blockade is actually loosening.
