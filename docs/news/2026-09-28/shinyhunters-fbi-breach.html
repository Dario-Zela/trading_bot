Hacking group claims theft of FBI agents' medical records

The J. Edgar Hoover Building, FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C. — Rob Young / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
ShinyHunters says it stole FBI agents' medical and personnel data via an Oracle flaw, unconfirmed but consistent with its pattern of real breaches paired with exaggerated claims.

The FBI's job applicants fill out a fitness-for-duty medical form before they're hired: blood work, urine tests, a doctor's notes on old allergies, sometimes a line about depression symptoms from high school. It's the kind of paperwork nobody thinks about again once they've got the badge. This week, a cybercrime group called ShinyHunters says it has thousands of those files — plus the same kind of records for serving special agents and their families — and it is showing reporters samples to prove it.

Reuters reviewed documents the group provided and reported that they include blood and urine test results, doctors' notes on conditions from shellfish allergies to high cholesterol, and the names, addresses, phone numbers and dates of birth of FBI employees and their spouses. The bureau has not confirmed a breach. It says only that it is "actively and aggressively investigating this matter" and trying to determine whether its own systems were compromised directly or whether the point of entry was a third-party contractor.

What ShinyHunters says it took

ShinyHunters claims it pulled between two and three terabytes of data by exploiting a previously unknown flaw in Oracle's PeopleSoft software — the enterprise system many large organizations, including federal agencies, use to run HR and recruiting. From there, the group says it moved into four internal FBI platforms: FBIJobs, the public recruiting portal; a background-check system some reporting has labeled FBI BEAST; FBI MedLink, which stores the medical files; and a fourth system tied to criminal-justice and investigative records.

The PeopleSoft angle fits a pattern security researchers have been tracking since the summer. Google's Threat Intelligence Group said last week that ShinyHunters had renewed "mass exploitation" of a PeopleSoft vulnerability after earlier attacks prompted a round of patching. The specific flaw cited in some technical write-ups of the FBI incident is tracked as CVE-2026-35273. None of this has been independently verified by Reuters or other outlets covering the story — the claims currently rest on the data samples ShinyHunters has released and its own account of how it got in.

That caveat matters, because ShinyHunters has a documented history of overstating its reach. An FBI public-service advisory issued in May, well before this incident, warned that people using the ShinyHunters name have in the past exaggerated the scope of their access, made false claims of holding compromising photos or video, and harassed victims and relatives with threats and swatting calls to extract payment. The group disputes that characterization of itself, and its retaliation against the bureau this week is, by its own account, largely about that advisory.

An unusual motive

Most ShinyHunters operations — including a wave of Salesforce-linked extortion campaigns last year that hit an estimated 300 to 400 organizations — have followed a straightforward financial script: steal data, threaten to leak it, demand payment. This one is different. "This is NOT financially motivated," a spokesperson for the group told The Register, framing the hack as retaliation for what it called "substantial false allegations" in the FBI's May advisory and demanding the bureau retract it.

Whether or not that framing is sincere, it changes the risk calculus. A purely financial extortion attempt has an obvious off-ramp: pay or don't. A grievance-driven leak against active law enforcement personnel doesn't have the same logic, and there's no guarantee the data — if genuine — stays contained regardless of what the FBI does next. Security researchers have pointed out that fitness-for-duty files, background-check data and family contact details together are close to a blueprint for targeting agents: blackmail material drawn from medical history, plus enough personal detail about spouses and addresses to support impersonation or intimidation.

The bigger gap this exposes

Even with the FBI's role unconfirmed, the incident lands in the middle of a broader story about PeopleSoft as a soft target. Oracle's enterprise software sits underneath HR and recruiting operations at universities, hospital systems, and government agencies that never expected it to be a front line. ShinyHunters' campaign against the platform this year has already produced breaches attributed to the group at multiple organizations well outside its usual retail and e-commerce hunting ground, and Google's researchers flagged the renewed exploitation specifically because earlier fixes hadn't held.

For an agency whose entire value proposition rests on operational security, a confirmed breach of personnel and medical data would be more than embarrassing — it would be evidence that the systems protecting the people who protect against foreign intelligence services, cartels and domestic threats are running on the same commercial software stack, with the same vulnerabilities, as everyone else. That's the argument likely to gain traction on Capitol Hill regardless of how this particular claim resolves: it strengthens the case for mandatory, faster patching cycles and stricter oversight of how sensitive personnel data is segmented from general enterprise systems in federal agencies.

The next concrete marker is whether the FBI confirms a breach occurred at all, and if so, its scope. Congressional oversight committees and the Department of Justice's inspector general are the likely venues if that happens. Until then, the story sits in the gap that has become ShinyHunters' signature: real technical capability, a track record of exaggeration, and victims who often can't say publicly which parts are true without validating the extortion.
