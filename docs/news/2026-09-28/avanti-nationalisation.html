Avanti West Coast to be nationalised from March 2027

An Avanti West Coast Class 390 Pendolino at London Euston, the southern terminus of the West Coast Main Line. — David Wheatley (davwheat) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Avanti West Coast will be nationalised from March 2027, with £840m earmarked for line upgrades, as the UK's rail renationalisation reaches its eighth operator.

Avanti West Coast, the operator that runs trains between London, Birmingham, Manchester, Liverpool, Glasgow and Edinburgh, will be taken into public ownership on 7 March 2027, Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander confirmed this week — ending a contract that has become a byword for delay and cancellation on Britain's railways.

"For years, we've heard stories of Avanti underperforming, with passengers left paying the price," Alexander said. "Enough is enough." The government will exercise a termination right built into Avanti's existing contract rather than wait for it to run its full course, which would otherwise have stretched to 2032.

In one sentence: Avanti West Coast will be nationalised from March 2027, with £840m earmarked for West Coast Main Line upgrades, as Labour's rail renationalisation programme reaches its eighth operator.

A contract run out of road

Avanti, jointly owned by FirstGroup (70%) and Italy's state-owned Trenitalia (30%), took over the West Coast Partnership in December 2019. It quickly became one of the most complained-about operators in the country. Cancellation rates peaked at 18.9% in early 2023 — nearly five times the national average at the time — as the company blamed driver shortages and a lack of rest-day working agreements for gutted timetables. Performance has since recovered, with Avanti-caused cancellations falling below 3% by mid-2023, but the reputational damage, and pressure from northern mayors including Greater Manchester's Andy Burnham, proved hard to shake.

The termination follows the pattern set by seven other operators already folded into public ownership since Labour took office: South Western Railway, c2c, Greater Anglia, West Midlands Trains, Thameslink, Chiltern Railways and, most recently, Great Western Railway. Only CrossCountry and East Midlands Railway will remain in private hands once Avanti transfers, with the full public-ownership programme due to complete by the end of 2027 under the incoming Great British Railways body.

Money alongside the takeover

Alexander's announcement was paired with a spending commitment rather than a bare change of ownership. The government has pledged £840m for West Coast Main Line infrastructure, on top of an additional £10m earmarked specifically for power and signalling maintenance. London Euston — the line's southern terminus and long a source of passenger complaints over cramped concourses and platform-allocation chaos — is set for a £50m facilities upgrade, while other stations along the route will share a further £10m by the end of 2027. Ministers say timetables will also be revised to speed up journeys, particularly on the Manchester corridor.

What it signals, and what it doesn't

The decision extends a policy reversal that began under the last government's own flirtation with rail nationalisation and accelerated sharply under Labour: the franchising model introduced in the 1990s privatisation is being wound down route by route, replaced by direct public operation under the Great British Railways banner Parliament is still legislating into existence. For FirstGroup and Trenitalia, the loss of Avanti removes a troubled but sizeable contract from the balance sheet; neither company issued a formal reaction to the announcement, and the transfer is not expected to trigger compensation disputes given the termination clause was contractual rather than punitive.

For markets, the read-through is thin. Avanti's nationalisation confirms a direction of travel already priced into how investors view UK rail operators as a sector — largely as regulated, low-margin contract work rather than a growth story — and the £840m infrastructure spend is incremental within a Department for Transport capital programme already running into the tens of billions. It is, more than anything, a domestic political story: a Labour government demonstrating it can act on a specific, visible source of public anger, ahead of Great British Railways formally taking shape.

The next marker is the CrossCountry and East Midlands Railway contracts, both still with private operators — whether either faces an early termination clause similar to Avanti's, or is left to run its course toward the 2027 completion deadline, will show whether this was a one-off response to Avanti's specific record or the template for finishing the job.
