Labour conference: Burnham's tax hints, Healey's defence pledge and Miliband's Palestine mea culpa

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose cost-of-living measures have driven the improved mood at Labour's Liverpool conference. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street
In one sentence
Burnham's popular bill cuts and care-service pledge have lifted Labour's mood in Liverpool, but October's budget and looming EU and North Sea decisions will test whether the optimism survives contact with the arithmetic.

Ten weeks ago Andy Burnham was the mayor of Greater Manchester. Now he is Britain's seventh prime minister in a decade, and this week he stood in front of his party in Liverpool trying to convince it — and the country — that Labour's long run of turmoil is over. Judging by the mood in the hall, he has at least won the argument for now. Judging by the decisions stacking up on his desk, the harder test starts in a few weeks' time.

Burnham took over in July after Keir Starmer resigned amid a slow-burning leadership crisis, dismissing Rachel Reeves as chancellor in the process and installing John Healey, previously his defence secretary, at the Treasury instead. The conference that opened beside the Mersey on Sunday was Burnham's first as leader, and by most accounts a markedly happier affair than Labour's gathering a year ago, when Starmer's government was sinking in the polls. Delegates have taken to calling it the "Burnham bounce": a mix of cheap, tangible cost-of-living measures now, and much bigger unresolved arguments — over tax, oil and Europe — still to come.

The bounce: bills, buses and a care service Burnham won't yet pay for

The measures driving the improved mood are modest by design. Burnham has cut tax on electricity bills and introduced a bus fare cap, cost-of-living relief aimed squarely at voters who feel squeezed by energy prices that have risen sharply amid the ongoing disruption to oil tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. He has paired that with a government-backed loan scheme to help first-time buyers onto the property ladder — a retail offer with immediate, visible beneficiaries, in contrast to the more abstract fiscal debates Westminster usually has in the autumn.

The bigger and more consequential pledge, though, is a promised National Care Service — a free-at-the-point-of-use system for England modelled on the NHS, spreading the cost of social care across the population rather than leaving individual families to absorb catastrophic bills. Burnham told the BBC the issue has personal resonance: his father died in a care home earlier this month. He has been candid that a dedicated new tax will be needed to fund it, though he has declined to say which tax or how large, and has explicitly ruled out setting out the details before the next election, due by mid-2029. "Trust is really important to me here," he said, arguing that a reform this large needs a mandate rather than a rushed announcement. It is a strikingly patient position for a prime minister two months into the job — but it also means Labour is now on record promising a new tax without saying who pays it, an IOU that will follow Burnham through two more conference seasons before he has to cash it.

Healey's industrial pitch, and the budget that follows

If Burnham supplied the optimism, Healey supplied the substance — or tried to. In his first conference speech as chancellor, delivered after his move from the Ministry of Defence, Healey pledged what he called a "new age of industrialisation," built around defence manufacturing and shipbuilding rather than the coal industry of Labour's past. "Our coal mines are not coming back," he told delegates, framing defence spending as a growth strategy as much as a security one. The concrete element was £115 million for a new Royal Navy support vessel due in service in the early 2030s, alongside orders for floating docks and a maritime research ship — real money, but a fraction of what a genuine reindustrialisation drive would eventually require.

The reception was correspondingly mixed. The GMB union called it a "massive boost" for shipbuilding; the Conservatives' Andrew Griffith dismissed it as "reheated announcements" with "no clarity on where the money is coming from" — a jab that lands because Healey is the one who has to answer it. He delivers Labour's first budget as chancellor on 28 October, exactly a month from now, against a backdrop of rising energy prices squeezing households and a Treasury balancing defence and industrial commitments against a care-service tax that hasn't been designed yet. Healey has openly said tax rises, spending cuts, or both are on the table. The industrialisation speech was the vision; the budget is where the arithmetic has to work.

Miliband's reckoning, and the trade-offs still to come

The conference's most emotionally charged moment came from the foreign secretary, not the chancellor. Ed Miliband told delegates that Labour's past caution on Israel-Palestine had not been enough: "I want to say to the millions of people in this country and indeed elsewhere who have been moved and outraged by the plight of the Palestinian people: we hear you, you were right." He said the government would "call out ethnic cleansing by the settler terrorists in the West Bank" and "call out Israel's illegal occupation of Palestine," building on sanctions his department imposed earlier this month on individuals and companies financing settlement expansion, alongside an expanded arms embargo.

It was a genuine break in tone from a party leadership that had, under Starmer, resisted exactly this kind of language for two years — and it came the same week police arrested more than 50 Palestine Action protesters outside the conference venue, a reminder of how much pressure built up on the issue while Labour held its previous line.

Burnham's government still faces two decisions that will matter more to markets than anything said from the conference stage. One is whether to approve new North Sea oil drilling, opposed by environmental groups and backed by trade unions worried about jobs — a decision that bears directly on UK energy investment and, at the margin, domestic gas supply at a moment when Hormuz-related disruption is already keeping prices elevated. The other is Europe: Burnham has pushed for closer EU ties, but a reset summit originally planned for July has slipped to November at the earliest, snagged on the bloc's "made in Europe" content rules, which Britain argues would disadvantage its manufacturers — the same manufacturers Healey is now trying to build a growth strategy around. Burnham is also still bound by Starmer's 2024 pledge not to rejoin the EU single market, which limits how far any November reset can actually go.

None of the week's set-piece announcements — the bill cuts, the care service, the shipyards, the settlements sanctions — required Burnham to resolve either question. That is precisely what makes the conference mood fragile rather than solid: the popular measures were the easy calls, funded from headroom or announced without a funding mechanism at all, while drilling, EU alignment and the shape of a new social-care tax are the decisions that actually reveal what kind of government this is. The budget on 28 October is the first of those moments that cannot be deferred to the next election.

What's next: John Healey's budget on 28 October will be the first hard test of whether the conference's fiscal promises — the care service, the industrial pledges, continued bill relief — can be paid for without the tax rises or cuts he has already flagged. A rescheduled UK-EU summit, no earlier than November, will show whether the "made in Europe" dispute can be resolved without reopening Labour's single-market red line. The North Sea drilling decision remains undated.
