Fed raises rates to 4%, signals extended tightening as inflation hits 40-year high

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, who took over from Jerome Powell in May, has pushed the committee toward a harder line on inflation. — Federal Reserve / Wikipedia (official portrait)
In one sentence
The Fed hiked to 3.75%-4% and 16 of 18 officials penciled in more increases, abandoning hopes of a 2026 pivot to cuts.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate a quarter point on September 16th, pushing the federal funds target to 3.75%-4% and, more importantly, telling markets to stop expecting relief any time soon. Sixteen of the 18 officials who sit on the Federal Open Market Committee projected at least one more increase before the end of the year. Four of those sixteen see room for two.

The vote among the twelve committee members who actually cast one was unanimous, 12-0. That unanimity matters as much as the rate itself: this was not a fight between hawks and doves resolved by a narrow majority. It was a committee that has, for now, stopped arguing about direction and started arguing only about pace.

From pause to hike in seven weeks

As recently as July, the committee held rates steady, and did so despite three regional bank presidents — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas — dissenting in favor of an immediate hike. At the time, that trio looked like the hawkish edge of the committee. Two months later, their position is the consensus.

What changed was the data, not the doctrine. August's consumer price report showed headline inflation running at 3.4%, and core PCE — the Fed's preferred gauge — has now printed above 3% in every month of 2026. Layer that onto a labor market that produced a stronger-than-expected August payrolls report, rising oil prices and climbing long-term Treasury yields, and the case for holding fire weakened by the week. By the September meeting, waiting was no longer the low-risk option.

The rhetorical groundwork was laid at Jackson Hole in late August, where Fed Chair Kevin Warsh — five months into the job after succeeding Jerome Powell in May — used his keynote to flag inflation as "concerning" without committing to action. Analysts read it as hawkish throat-clearing at the time; the September vote confirmed it.

Warsh draws the line

"Inflation has been too high, for too long. We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed."

That was Warsh's framing at the post-meeting press conference, and it is a notably harder line than the Fed struck for most of the past two years, when officials leaned on language about inflation being "on a path" toward target. There is no such hedging now. Asked directly whether the committee still saw a case for cuts in 2026, Warsh declined to rule it in or out — but the dot plot did the talking. The median projection now points toward a fed funds rate of roughly 4.00%-4.25% by year-end, implying one more quarter-point move is the committee's working assumption, not a tail risk.

This is a sharp reversal from where market pricing sat at the start of the summer. Futures markets had, at various points earlier in the year, priced in the possibility of a pause giving way to cuts as growth cooled. Immediately after Wednesday's press conference, the probability of an October hike implied by futures markets jumped to 49%, and the odds of at least one more increase before year-end rose to 87%, up from 77% just hours earlier. The market did not merely accept the Fed's guidance — it moved further in the Fed's direction than the Fed itself had signalled.

What moved, and what it means for positioning

Treasury yields initially eased on the news — the two-year note fell roughly 3.6 basis points to 4.625%, the ten-year dipped about 3.1 basis points to 4.965% — as investors read the hike as confirmation the Fed is willing to act rather than jawbone. That is a familiar pattern: a hike removes uncertainty even as it raises the cost of money, and bond markets often reward clarity over comfort in the short run.

The more durable consequence is for anything priced off a discount rate. An extended tightening cycle, rather than a one-and-done hike followed by cuts, means the risk-free rate used across equity valuation models, options pricing and leveraged strategies stays elevated for longer than the year's earlier consensus assumed. Algorithmic and systematic strategies that had begun pricing in a 2026 easing path — duration bets, rate-sensitive equity baskets, carry trades built around an anticipated Fed pivot — now face a recalibration. The forward guidance embedded in the dot plot is the operative signal here: sixteen of eighteen officials expecting further hikes is a committee, not a chair, speaking with one voice, and single-official dissent risk that traders might have leaned on to fade the move is largely absent.

The dissent that wasn't, and what comes next

Only two of the eighteen dot-plot participants see the Fed stopping at this hike. That is a thin base of opposition inside an institution that, seven weeks earlier, needed three dissents just to register hawkish discomfort with a hold. Whether that reflects growing confidence that inflation is genuinely entrenched, or a committee falling in line behind a new chair still establishing his authority after a bruising confirmation fight, will shape how much further this cycle runs.

The next test arrives at the October 27-28 meeting, where futures markets already price better-than-even odds of another quarter-point move. Between now and then, the September jobs report and the next CPI print will carry outsized weight — either data set could reinforce Wednesday's hawkish turn or reopen the argument the July dissenters lost and then won.
