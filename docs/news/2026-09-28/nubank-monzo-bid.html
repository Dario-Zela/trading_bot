Nubank in early talks to buy Monzo for up to £10bn

David Vélez, founder and chief executive of Nubank, at Web Summit Rio in 2023. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nubank is in early talks to buy Monzo for up to £10bn — double its last valuation — while Monzo's board also weighs a funding round to expand into Europe alone.

Nubank, the Brazilian digital bank that has spent thirteen years turning overdraft fees and bank branch queues into a punchline across Latin America, is in early talks to buy Monzo, Britain's best-known fintech upstart, for as much as £10 billion. If it happens, it would be one of the largest fintech acquisitions on record and would hand a São Paulo-headquartered company an instant, fully licensed retail bank in the United Kingdom.

The talks, first reported by Sky News and confirmed by Bloomberg, are preliminary — there is no agreed price, no signed term sheet, and people close to the discussions caution a deal may not happen at all. But the range under discussion, £8 billion to £10 billion, is striking on its own terms: roughly double the £4.5 billion valuation Monzo achieved in an October 2024 employee share sale, and, as Forbes calculated, about 115 times the £87.3 million pre-tax profit Monzo reported for the year to March.

What Nubank is actually buying

Strip away the headline number and the appeal is straightforward. Nu Holdings — Nubank's NYSE-listed parent, worth roughly $66 billion — has no UK or European banking licence of its own. It runs an engineering hub in Berlin and a cross-border product called Nu Global, but neither confers the right to take deposits in London. Monzo does: more than 12 million personal customers, 600,000 business accounts, and — since December — a second licence from the European Central Bank and the Central Bank of Ireland that has let it open outposts in Dublin and start hiring in Barcelona and Madrid.

For David Vélez, Nubank's founder and chief executive, that is the whole point. Nubank now serves 138.9 million customers across Brazil, Mexico and Colombia and has just pushed into the United States with a stablecoin-linked international account. "We are excited to start executing our thesis beyond our core region," Vélez said of the broader expansion strategy. Buying Monzo outright would let Nubank skip years of licensing applications and compete for continental European deposits from day one, rather than building a bank from scratch the way it did in Mexico City.

Monzo's fork in the road

Monzo's board, chaired by Gary Hoffman until his planned departure, is treating the Nubank approach as one of two live options rather than a fait accompli. The alternative, being weighed alongside it, is a fresh funding round that would value Monzo above £8 billion and fund the European push on its own terms — Spain and Ireland first, with more markets to follow now that the US plan has been shelved. Monzo has hired Morgan Stanley and Qatalyst as advisers, the same bankers who had been preparing the bank for a London Stock Exchange listing targeting roughly £6-7 billion. That IPO, long pitched as a rare win for a City desperate for fintech listings, is now effectively on hold. Private equity has also circled: Advent International has reportedly discussed a deal at a similar valuation to Nubank's.

The backdrop is not entirely tidy. Last year Hoffman oversaw the replacement of chief executive TS Anil with Diana Layfield, a former Google and Standard Chartered executive, only for a shareholder rebellion to reinstate Anil to the board as vice-chair. A sale to a well-capitalised outsider would settle that instability at a stroke, which may be part of its appeal to some investors — even if it means ceding independence.

The Revolut comparison

The contrast with Revolut, Monzo's larger domestic rival, sharpens the stakes. Revolut secured a full UK banking licence in March, sold shares at a $75 billion valuation, and is now discussing IPO valuations of $150 billion to $200 billion with investors — fifteen to twenty times what Nubank is offering for Monzo. One challenger bank is being talked about as a candidate for one of the largest tech listings in history; the other is weighing whether to sell itself outright or raise money to keep trying. Both banks were founded within a year of each other in the mid-2010s on nearly identical premises: better apps, no branches, faster onboarding. A decade on, scale — not the app — appears to be what separates a seller from an acquirer.

What comes next

Nothing here is close to finished. Monzo's board and major shareholders — a mix of venture funds and, since 2019, a chunk of the UK public via crowdfunding rounds — still have to decide whether £8-10 billion in cash and stock beats the uncertainty of a standalone European build-out or a London listing that markets have already priced conservatively. Any Nubank transaction would also need sign-off from the Bank of England and the Financial Conduct Authority, which have not waved through a foreign takeover of a UK retail bank of this size before and will want assurances on deposit protection and governance. Watch for whether Monzo's board narrows to a single path in the coming weeks — and whether the Prudential Regulation Authority signals early how closely it intends to scrutinise a Brazilian-owned buyer of a UK-insured deposit book.
