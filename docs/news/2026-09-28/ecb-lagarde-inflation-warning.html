Lagarde warns eurozone inflation shock will be 'longer-lasting'

ECB president Christine Lagarde, who says the eurozone's current inflation shock will be "longer-lasting" than previously expected. — European Union 2023 / European Parliament (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Lagarde says the eurozone's energy-driven inflation shock will outlast earlier forecasts, and Nagel says rates may need to turn mildly restrictive.

The European Central Bank has stopped talking about a quick return to normal. In an interview with the French regional paper Ouest-France published earlier this month, ECB president Christine Lagarde said plainly that the inflation shock rattling the eurozone "is longer-lasting" than policymakers had assumed — a rare admission that the bank's own models undersold the durability of the problem it is fighting.

Euro-area inflation ran at 3.3% in August, well above the ECB's 2% target, with energy inflation alone jumping to 14.3% from 10.3% the previous month. Lagarde pointed to two compounding forces: the ongoing Middle East conflict, which has kept crude flows through the Strait of Hormuz constrained, and what she described as the destruction of refining capacity "especially in Russia," which has throttled the supply of finished fuel products even where crude itself is available. Together, she argued, those two shocks are pushing energy costs through the rest of the price chain in a way that a temporary supply blip would not.

A shift in the ECB's own arithmetic

The comments matter because they mark a change in the bank's internal narrative, not just its public messaging. The ECB has already raised its deposit rate twice since the outbreak of the Iran war — first to 2.25% in June, then to 2.5% this month — and staff forecasts published alongside the September decision revised inflation expectations for 2027 and 2028 higher, with 2028 price growth now seen sitting slightly above target rather than safely back at it. Lagarde called the September move a "no brainer" given the data, but the more telling signal was the forward revision: the ECB is no longer pricing this as a shock that fades on its own timetable.

Bundesbank president Joachim Nagel sharpened that point a day after the rate decision, saying the ECB's key rate is now at the "upper bound of the neutral territory" and that he would "not exclude" the need to move into "mild restrictive territory" if energy prices keep climbing. That is a meaningful shift in language from a central banker who has generally counselled patience — Nagel framed further energy-price moves, not labour-market conditions, as the decisive variable for where policy goes next.

Why the market should care

For a market that had been pricing the Iran war and Russian refinery damage as transitory supply noise, the combination of Lagarde's and Nagel's remarks argues for a longer hold at restrictive-to-mildly-restrictive settings than futures curves reflected as recently as midsummer. That keeps euro-area borrowing costs elevated for longer, extends the drag on rate-sensitive sectors of eurozone growth, and widens the case for holding euro rate exposure over eurozone equities, which carry more direct exposure to a slower-growth, higher-cost environment than fixed income does.

The next test comes with the ECB's October meeting and the flash September inflation print due in the first days of that month — both will show whether the acceleration in energy costs that drove the September hike is still building or has begun to plateau.
