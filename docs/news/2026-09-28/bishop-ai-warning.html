Bishop warns UK politicians have failed to keep pace with AI

Steven Croft, former Bishop of Oxford, in his official 2019 portrait — Roger Harris / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Bishop Steven Croft says politicians have failed to keep pace with AI and that both church and state have been reaching for the wrong language to discuss it.

The Rt Revd Dr Steven Croft spent nine years in the House of Lords watching artificial intelligence arrive faster than Parliament could discuss it. Now retired as Bishop of Oxford, he has a theory about why: the politicians tasked with governing AI have been reaching for the wrong vocabulary — or none at all.

Speaking at a Religion Media Centre briefing on "Artificial Intelligence and the Human Future" on 24 September, Croft argued that AI's deployment in warfare, health and education had "continually outstripped public debate," and that MPs in particular had proved hard to engage, cycling through changes of government and prime minister while the underlying technology kept moving. It's a familiar complaint about Westminster and fast-moving tech — but Croft's specific claim is more pointed: that political resistance to grappling with AI is entangled with a kind of secular squeamishness about apocalyptic language, even as AI boosters and critics alike keep reaching for exactly that language anyway.

Rapture, rupture, regulation

The briefing, chaired by the broadcaster Roger Bolton, put Croft alongside the Oxford internet researcher Professor Beth Singler, Dr Sam Tranter and the tech ethicist Benjamin Crockett. Singler's contribution supplied the intellectual spine of the session: that contemporary AI discourse is saturated with borrowed theology, from Silicon Valley talk of the "singularity" as a moment of rupture, to "mind uploading" as a technological Rapture, to dueling visions of AI-enabled abundance or extinction that map neatly onto heaven and hell.

Croft's contribution was to turn that observation back on the church rather than leave it as a critique of tech culture. He suggested Christianity "may need to rediscover some of its own apocalyptic vocabulary" — the language of judgment, death and ultimate things — precisely because that vocabulary, however unfashionable, is suited to a technology that genuinely poses questions about human dignity and the future of the species. It's a notable reversal: rather than dismissing apocalyptic framing as hype to be debunked, a senior Anglican cleric is arguing the church largely abandoned ground that secular AI discourse has since occupied badly.

Faith at the table, not just at the pulpit

The more concrete ask, made in a BBC One interview on 20 September, was institutional rather than rhetorical: Croft said faith leaders should have "a significant role in government oversight of AI," on the grounds that leaving the big judgment calls to technologists alone misses questions — about what it means to be human, about relationships, about meaning — that religious traditions have spent millennia sitting with. He made a similar case in his valedictory Lords speech in July, warning that AI's risks extend to mental health, social cohesion, truth and the distribution of power, and that "the speed of change will itself undermine the very benefits that change brings."

None of this amounts to a policy proposal. There is no bill, no amendment, no named regulator Croft wants strengthened. What there is, is a fairly unusual public argument from within the Church of England that its own reticence — treating AI anxiety as either science fiction or someone else's department — has left a vacuum that politicians have failed to fill and that accelerationist voices have filled instead, often by recasting caution and democratic oversight as obstacles to progress rather than as due diligence.

What to watch

Croft is no longer a legislator, so his platform now is moral suasion rather than a vote in committee. The test of whether this amounts to more than one bishop's post-retirement reflection is whether the Church of England's synod or House of Bishops picks up the theme formally, and whether other faith bodies — the briefing's line-up suggests interest well beyond Anglicanism — start showing up in AI-governance consultations rather than commentary pieces. For now, it's a data point in a debate that has plenty of technologists and few theologians, not a shift in UK AI policy itself.
