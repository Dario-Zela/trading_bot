China's growth outlook cools to 4.5%, piling pressure on stimulus decision

Shanghai's financial district, the visible face of an economy where factories are outperforming households. — Kestreltail / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A Reuters poll puts China's GDP growth at 4.5% for 2026 and 2027 as weak consumption piles pressure on Beijing over year-end stimulus.

China's economy is on course for its slowest sustained stretch of growth in decades, with economists in a Reuters poll putting GDP expansion at 4.5% both this year and next — down from 4.9% in 2025 and sitting at the floor of Beijing's own 4.5%-to-5% target band. The median estimate, drawn from 73 economists, sharpens a question policymakers can no longer defer: whether to deploy fresh stimulus before year-end or accept a permanently lower cruising speed for the world's second-largest economy.

The forecast reflects a widening split between China's factories, which keep outperforming, and its households, which have not shaken off years of caution. August data laid the divide bare. Industrial production rose 5.2% year-on-year, beating forecasts and accelerating from July's 4.4%, helped by exports of AI-related hardware such as chips and servers. Retail sales, by contrast, grew just 0.4% — the slowest pace since May and well short of the 0.8% economists had expected. Fixed-asset investment fell 7.2%, a steeper contraction than forecast, as the property slump ground into its 32nd consecutive month of price declines; new-home prices across 70 cities slipped again in August even after officials rolled out fresh support measures at the end of July.

China's National Bureau of Statistics put the imbalance plainly, saying "the domestic imbalance between strong supply and weak demand remains pronounced." Zhiwei Zhang of Pinpoint Asset Management struck a similarly cautious note, observing that fiscal support "takes time to be implemented" — a reminder that even a decisive move by Beijing now would take months to show up in consumer spending data.

A consumption gap that won't close on its own

Household spending accounts for roughly 40% of China's economy, some 20 percentage points below the global average, according to policy advisers who have set a target of lifting that share to 45% by 2030. Closing the gap means persuading households battered by falling property values and a patchy labour market to spend rather than save — precisely the behaviour that three years of piecemeal support has failed to shift. Exports have papered over the shortfall so far, with 2025's trade surplus hitting a record near $1.2 trillion, but Goldman Sachs economists warned that "domestic demand has softened notably" even as headline activity holds up.

The People's Bank of China has pledged to keep monetary policy "moderately loose" and to cut the reserve requirement ratio and interest rates further, and poll respondents expect another trim to the seven-day reverse repo rate. The fourth quarter is traditionally when Beijing signals its policy stance for the year ahead, and the question now is whether that signal extends beyond monetary tinkering to the kind of fiscal push — larger bond issuance, consumer-goods subsidies, direct property-sector support — that economists say is needed to shift the growth mix. October's Politburo economic meeting is the next point where any change in language toward a more "proactive" fiscal stance would tell markets, and metals traders in particular, which way policymakers are leaning.
