Venezuela frees 39 political prisoners after Rodríguez-Trump UN talks

Interim President Delcy Rodríguez, who has led Venezuela since Nicolás Maduro's capture in January 2026. — Zurimar Campos / Prensa Presidencial (Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Venezuela freed 39 political prisoners after Rodríguez met Trump at the UN, part of a broader US bet on her post-Maduro government that includes oil-sanctions waivers.

Thirty-nine political prisoners walked out of Venezuelan jails between Friday and Saturday, the latest and largest batch freed since interim leader Delcy Rodríguez met President Trump on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York this week. The releases, confirmed by rights groups tracking detentions inside the country, push the total freed since January above 1,000 — a marker of how far Caracas has moved from the Maduro era in barely eight months.

Rodríguez, Nicolás Maduro's former vice president and oil minister, has run Venezuela since a US military operation captured Maduro in Caracas on January 3 and flew him to the United States, where he and his wife, Cilia Flores, await trial on drug-trafficking and weapons charges. Rodríguez was sworn in two days later, becoming the first woman to hold presidential power in Venezuelan history, and has since traded cooperation with Washington for the recognition and sanctions relief her government needs to function.

Who came out, and who didn't

Among those freed this weekend were eight women who had been detained since 2021 on "narco-terrorism" charges, and six people accused of working with the United States in a 2020 plot to oust Maduro. The rights group Justicia, Encuentro y Perdón said 29 of the 39 were on its list of recognised political prisoners; a separate release from a prison outside Caracas on Saturday included Wilder Vásquez, a university student serving five years for an alleged 2018 assassination plot against Maduro.

The group said it "will remain vigilant and continue to demand full freedom for all those who are still waiting." Foro Penal, which has tracked detentions for over a decade, counted 344 political prisoners still held as of September 18 — down from 391 a month earlier, but a reminder that the pace of releases, while real, is incremental rather than sweeping. Families continued to protest outside the US embassy in Caracas and El Rodeo I prison, which rights groups have long described as a site of torture.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio called the latest releases "a crucial step" toward reconciliation. In her UN address, Rodríguez told delegates Venezuela had "begun a process of democratic transformation," pointing to the releases and pledges on future elections as evidence her government intends to keep pace with Washington's demands rather than stall, as Maduro so often did during a decade of on-again, off-again negotiations.

The oil calculus behind the diplomacy

The releases are the visible output of a much larger negotiation. Since Rodríguez took over, the Trump administration has lifted personal sanctions on her and issued waivers letting US firms resume work in Venezuela's oil, mining and financial sectors — authorisations that can be revoked as easily as they were granted, but that have already drawn Chevron back into expanding its position in a country sitting on the world's largest proven crude reserves. A second round of talks between Rodríguez's government and the opposition, held under US oversight, followed an August round focused on unlocking frozen Venezuelan funds for earthquake recovery and on judiciary reform.

Analysts remain doubtful the arrangement will quickly translate into meaningfully higher crude output — Venezuela's fields and refineries need investment that takes years to pay off, not months. But even the direction of travel matters for markets already jumpy over the Hormuz standoff and elevated oil prices: a Venezuela credibly reopening to Western capital is a supply-side release valve that didn't exist under Maduro, and every incremental sanctions waiver chips at the risk premium built into Atlantic Basin crude.

What's next

Rodríguez has promised further releases and, eventually, elections — the terms and timing of which remain the central unresolved question in Washington's bet on her government. Foro Penal's next tally, due in mid-October, will show whether this weekend's releases mark an acceleration or another modest instalment. Markets, meanwhile, will be watching whether the general licenses granted to Chevron and other operators survive contact with Congress, where skepticism about legitimising a government that came to power via a foreign military raid has not gone away.
