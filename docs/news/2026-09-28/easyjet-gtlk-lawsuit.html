easyJet sued for $72m over jets abandoned in Russia sanctions fallout

An easyJet Airbus A319 at Madrid-Barajas, where three of the six disputed jets remain stranded — Abxbay / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
GTLK's Irish liquidator STLC is suing easyJet for $72m, alleging six Russian-owned jets were left to rot in Madrid and Cyprus after 2022 sanctions lease terminations.

Four and a half years after Russia's invasion of Ukraine triggered a scramble to unwind aviation ties with Moscow, easyJet is back in court over how that unwinding was handled. STLC Europe Eight — the Irish-registered leasing arm of Russia's state-owned GTLK, now under Irish court-appointed liquidation — is suing the budget carrier in London's High Court for at least $72 million, alleging it dumped six Airbus A319s in Madrid and Cyprus without maintaining them once their leases ended.

In one sentence: GTLK's Irish liquidators are suing easyJet for $72m, alleging six Russian-owned jets were left to rot in Madrid and Cyprus after 2022 sanctions-driven lease terminations.

A 2022 decision, a 2026 bill

The aircraft themselves had an odd history even before the war. GTLK Europe bought the six A319s in March 2018 and leased them to easyJet, but the jets were grounded in March 2020 — a pandemic-era casualty rather than a sanctions one. They were still sitting idle when the EU adopted aviation sanctions against Russia on 25 February 2022, and when GTLK itself was added to the EU sanctions list that April.

EasyJet notified STLC on 8 April 2022 that it was terminating the six leases, telling the lessor that EU sanctions barred it from maintaining or repairing the aircraft. The airline confirmed as much in its 2022 annual report, describing the move as a considered response to the sanctions regime rather than a unilateral walkout.

STLC's liquidators — appointed after Ireland's High Court ordered the wind-up of GTLK's Irish entities in May 2023, with an Irish court confirming their control of the assets that July — see it differently. Their claim, filed on 19 August 2026 as STLC Europe Eight Leasing Limited v. easyJet Airline Company Limited, argues the sanctions logic doesn't hold: the aircraft never went near Russia, and STLC itself is an Irish company, not a Russian one. On that reading, nothing stopped easyJet keeping the jets airworthy while the lease dispute was sorted out.

Where the $72m comes from

The claim is itemised rather than a round number plucked from the air. STLC wants $36.7 million in unpaid rent and interest, $32.5 million for the diminished value of the aircraft, and €2.8 million in accumulated airport charges. Three of the six jets were stored at Larnaca in Cyprus and have since been recovered and sold — but, the lessor says, for tens of millions less than they would have fetched in airworthy condition. The remaining three are still parked at Madrid-Barajas, unsold.

EasyJet has not conceded the allegations. A spokesperson said the airline intends to defend the case in full, and the $72 million figure remains STLC's claim rather than any established liability. The market shrugged: shares dipped a fraction of a percent on the news, filed away by investors against a stock still up more than 30% for the year.

What's next

The case, filed in the Commercial Court, has yet to reach a substantive hearing, and the core factual dispute — whether sanctions genuinely constrained easyJet's ability to service Irish-registered aircraft outside Russia — will likely turn on internal compliance documentation from spring 2022 that neither side has yet made public. It is a reminder that the legal tail of the 2022 sanctions shock is still being litigated asset by asset, lessor by lessor, years after the headlines about grounded fleets moved on.
