BoE's 6-3 hold and gilt-sale pause still setting the tone in rates markets

The Bank of England's Threadneedle Street headquarters, where the MPC split 6-3 to hold rates in September. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
BoE split 6-3 and eased QT to £46bn a year, but gilt yields near 1998 highs and swaps pricing show markets betting on a November hike anyway.

The Bank of England spent September doing two things that don't usually sit together: holding rates while making its policy stance look, on paper, more dovish — and doing both while three of its own rate-setters said it was making a mistake.

On 17 September the Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, the sixth straight hold since the last cut in December 2025. The dissenters — Catherine Mann, Megan Greene and Huw Pill — wanted an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%, pointing to the Bank's own forecast that CPI inflation could push above 4% in early 2027. Headline inflation was already running at 3.1% in August, up from 2.9% the month before, with core CPI stuck at 2.6% and services inflation at 3.4%. Mann's argument was blunt: raising rates now was cheaper risk management than waiting to be proved wrong later.

The same meeting also, unanimously, softened the Bank's balance-sheet plumbing. Quantitative tightening — the run-off of the gilts the Bank hoovered up during QE — will average about £46bn a year going forward, down from £70bn, with the stock now expected to reach zero only by September 2034. On top of that, active gilt sales are paused entirely while Threadneedle Street works out a new arrangement for offloading holdings to the Debt Management Office, with details promised by April 2027.

A hold that doesn't feel like one

Taken together, those look like the actions of a central bank leaning dovish. But markets are not reading it that way. Swaps pricing shortly after the decision implied roughly a two-thirds chance of a hike at the next meeting, on 5 November — a striking number for a Bank that has just voted 6-3 to sit still. Barclays, UBS and J.P. Morgan have all pencilled in a November move. Governor Andrew Bailey, in a pooled broadcast interview on decision day, was candid about the source of the pressure: a run-up in energy prices since July, with the Gulf tensions around the Strait of Hormuz doing much of the damage, though he maintained the pass-through into broader inflation had so far been "quite subdued."

The long end isn't waiting to find out

Gilt investors have made their own judgment in the meantime. The 30-year yield touched 5.90% at the start of September, its highest since 1998, before easing modestly on the QT news to around 5.74-5.90% depending on the session — still near multi-decade highs. The 10-year has been comparatively becalmed, but even there the relief has been shallow: yields eased only slightly even as the Bank signalled a friendlier approach to its balance sheet. That gap between a soothed front end and a stubborn long end is the market's way of saying it doesn't think the inflation problem is solved, whatever the MPC's majority believes about second-round effects.

In one sentence: The BoE split 6-3 and eased its bond-market footprint, but gilt yields and rate swaps say traders still expect the committee to be hiking again within two months.

The next hard data point is UK CPI for September, due in October, followed by the 5 November MPC decision — the meeting markets are already treating as live. If energy prices keep climbing, the argument Mann, Greene and Pill lost in September gets harder to lose twice.
