US backs Musk's X in bid to overturn €120m EU fine

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which imposed the €120 million fine on X. — Euro Pictures / Sarra Benyaich, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
The US Justice Department asked to back X's court challenge to the EU's €120m DSA fine, and Brussels says it will defend the penalty regardless.

The United States government has waded directly into Elon Musk's fight with Brussels, asking a European court for permission to help X overturn a €120 million fine — and turning what had been a company's legal defence into a dispute between two governments.

The fine dates to December 5, 2025, when the European Commission found X Internet, X Holdings and Musk personally jointly liable for breaching the Digital Services Act, the EU's rulebook for policing large online platforms. The Commission broke the penalty into three pieces: €45 million for what it called the deceptive design of X's paid blue-checkmark verification system, €40 million for denying independent researchers access to platform data, and €35 million for failing to run a transparent advertising repository. X appealed to the EU's General Court in Luxembourg in February, arguing the investigation was "incomplete and superficial" and marred by procedural errors.

Washington steps in

On Thursday, the US Department of Justice filed to intervene in that appeal on X's side — an unusual step for one government to take in another's regulatory litigation. Brett Shumate, the assistant attorney general who heads the DOJ's Civil Division, said the Commission had "inappropriately attempted to expand its regulatory authority to reach American companies not present or operating within its jurisdiction." The department went further, warning that if the fine stands, it "may have significant implications" for other US platforms doing business in Europe.

That framing matters: the DOJ isn't just defending Musk's math on ad transparency or checkmark design. It's arguing the EU has no business fining a US company for conduct the department considers outside Brussels' reach — a jurisdictional objection that, if it persuaded the General Court, could blunt the DSA's ability to touch any American platform.

It is also not a one-off. The same DOJ intervened three months ago to back xAI, Musk's AI company, in a separate dispute over its Mississippi data centre — a pattern that suggests the department is treating Musk-linked companies as test cases for pushing back on regulation it views as extraterritorial overreach.

Brussels won't blink

The Commission's response, delivered the following day, was blunt. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier said it was the EU's "sovereign right" to legislate to protect its citizens, adding: "We are enforcing our legislation objectively, transparently and with a solid case." He also pushed back on the suggestion that the DSA singles out American firms, saying the Commission is "not targeting anyone, not targeting any company, not targeting any jurisdiction based on their colour or their country of origin."

That denial matters because the politics are hard to ignore. The DSA has been a running irritant between Brussels and the Trump administration, which has repeatedly characterised the law as a vehicle for censoring speech rather than a consumer-protection statute. X's own public posture — Musk has called for the law's abolition — has made the case a natural flashpoint for that broader argument.

Why a legal footnote isn't one

Procedurally, the DOJ's move is modest: it is asking only for permission to submit arguments as an interested party, not to overturn the fine itself. But the substance is bigger than the case file. A US government brief arguing that EU fines on American platforms amount to jurisdictional overreach sets a marker for how Washington intends to respond every time Brussels enforces the DSA against a US company — Meta, Google and Apple have all faced or face similar scrutiny. For platforms operating in both markets, the fight raises an uncomfortable prospect: complying with EU transparency rules could now draw them into a geopolitical argument they didn't start.

The General Court in Luxembourg has not set a date for ruling on the US intervention request, let alone the underlying appeal — DSA fine cases of this type typically take one to two years to resolve. The case is being watched closely as the first real test of how the DSA's non-compliance fines hold up once challenged, and now, of how far Washington is willing to go to shape that outcome.
