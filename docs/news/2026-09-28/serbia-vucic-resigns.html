Vučić quits presidency to run for PM as Serbian protests grind on

Aleksandar Vučić, who resigned Serbia's presidency to seek a return as prime minister ahead of the October 25 snap election. — duma.gov.ru / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Vučić resigned the presidency to seek the premiership instead, sidestepping term limits as student-led protests push toward an October 25 snap election.

Aleksandar Vučić has run Serbia in one top job or another since 2014 — first as prime minister, then, since 2017, as president. On Sunday he gave up the presidency seven months before his term was due to end, with a plan to come back as prime minister instead. Serbia's constitution bars him from a third presidential term. It says nothing about how many times he can be prime minister.

Ana Brnabić, the outgoing parliament speaker and a longtime Vučić ally, has stepped in as acting president. A presidential election must now follow within 90 days. But the vote that matters is the snap parliamentary election Vučić has called for October 25 — the one he intends to contest as the lead candidate for his Serbian Progressive Party, seeking the premiership he first held two decades' worth of political life ago.

A resignation two years in the making

The backdrop is a wave of unrest with a specific origin. On November 1, 2024, a concrete canopy collapsed at the railway station in Novi Sad, killing 16 people. Investigators and protesters alike pointed to corner-cutting on a recently renovated, politically showcased infrastructure project. The deaths detonated a protest movement that has not really stopped since — student-led marches, road blockades, and city-to-city walks that drew participants from across the country, including one contingent that took 16 days to reach Novi Sad for the collapse's first anniversary, a day for each victim.

What began as a demand for accountability over the collapse hardened into something broader: allegations of entrenched corruption, opaque public spending, and a governing party that has absorbed the courts, police and much of the media into its orbit. Vučić had signalled as early as June that he would eventually resign to seek the premiership instead; he waited three months to do it, timing the move to the moment the protest movement had converted itself into an electoral threat.

An election with a challenger and no settled outcome

That threat has a name: Students Win, a movement that grew directly out of the protest campaign and is now fielding roughly 250 candidates, backed by opposition parties including the Democratic Party and grouped alongside a pro-Western alliance called European Serbia. Its platform is built around the same grievances that filled the streets — investigations into unexplained wealth among officials, and transparency in how public money is spent. Some polling suggests it could deny the Progressive Party the majority it has enjoyed uninterrupted since 2012.

"Vucic is not the only actor in this country," the columnist Dejan Ilic said this week. "People have said that Serbia is in a bad state, they demanded elections and they forced elections." Not everyone reads the moment as vindication for the protest movement, though. Aida Corovic, a former parliamentarian, put it more starkly: "I fear that the crisis in Serbia could only begin after the October 25 elections."

That captures the uncomfortable logic of the manoeuvre. Vučić is technically obeying a term limit while arranging to keep hold of executive power through a different office — one that, unlike the presidency, carries no limit on renewal. For a candidate country still notionally working toward EU membership, it is the kind of institutional sleight-of-hand that invites exactly the rule-of-law scrutiny Brussels has already been levelling at Belgrade over the government's handling of the protests. Serbia's economic footprint is too small to move regional markets on this alone, but sovereign risk pricing and dinar stability over the next month will track something less tangible: whether voters, and Serbia's institutions, treat a change of title as a change of power.

The next marker is October 25 itself, and the presidential contest that must follow within 90 days of Sunday's resignation — a race Vučić, for the first time in over a decade, will not be in.
