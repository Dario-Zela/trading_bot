BEYOND THE TAPE · YOUTH · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-11-17 · 3 min read

Where are the jobs for 16-24 year-olds, and what is it like to buy a home with strangers?

In one sentence
BBC Business offers a tool mapping entry-level jobs for 16-24s and a feature on buying homes with strangers, two answers to one squeeze: work and housing in the wrong places.

Two questions that look unrelated share a root: how does anyone under 25 get started? One is where the work is. The other is how a first home gets paid for when a salary and a deposit won't stretch far enough. BBC Business has put both to its readers, one as a data tool and one as a human-interest feature.

Finding the work

The first piece is a look-up feature that lets 16-24 year-olds see which parts of the country have the most entry-level jobs. The idea is simple and overdue. Youth unemployment is usually reported as one national number, while a school-leaver's real choice is local: a bus route, a rent, a town. A tool that sets vacancies beside place lets a job seeker ask whether the move is worth it before making it.

The tool's underlying figures, its date and its source could not be checked for this article, so none are quoted here. Anyone using such a tool should look for two things: whether it counts vacancies or filled jobs, and how recent the data is. Vacancy counts shift month to month, and an area with many openings may also have many applicants.

The policy backdrop is not in doubt. Elsewhere in today's edition, the Milburn review calls for a "complete system reset" to get 440,000 young people into work. A tool that maps where the entry-level jobs are is a small, practical companion to that argument.

Buying with strangers

The companion piece looks at the other end of the same problem: young people who pool resources to buy a home with people they did not previously know. Co-ownership between friends is well documented in lender and estate-agent surveys, and a few have also asked about strangers. Those polls measure willingness, not behaviour, and they come from firms with something to sell, so they are better read as a mood than a market size.

The practical questions are the unglamorous ones. Who is on the mortgage, and is each buyer liable for the whole debt if another stops paying? How are shares set, and what happens when one person wants out? Lawyers generally advise a written co-ownership agreement covering exactly those points before anyone collects keys. The BBC's own earlier experiment in this area involved a participant who treated the arrangement as a temporary step, not a forever home. That is a useful framing: a shared purchase is as much an exit-planning exercise as an entry one.

What connects them

Both pieces answer the same squeeze. Jobs cluster in places where housing is dear, and housing is cheaper in places where the jobs are thinner. A young worker can trade one against the other, or share the cost of the first with strangers.

There is no direct market hook, and the claims above go no further than the evidence supports. The thing to watch is the next official labour-market release, which will show whether the local gaps the BBC tool maps are narrowing, and how far the government's youth-employment push gets in turning the Milburn review's numbers into jobs.
