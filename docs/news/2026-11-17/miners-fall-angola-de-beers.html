Miners lead London fallers on weak China data, as Angola moves to buy 10% of De Beers

In one sentence
London miners fell on weak China data, though the move is unverified against the release; Angola's 10% De Beers stake is a request, not a signed deal.

London's miners have been selling off on weak Chinese data, and Anglo American is also in the news over De Beers. The two stories are linked by one company, but they are separate in cause. One is a demand signal from the world's largest metals consumer. The other is a transaction that has not yet been signed.

This piece sets out what is established and what is not. The triage material names Anglo American, Glencore and Antofagasta as leading the FTSE 100 lower. It points to weak Chinese activity data and softer metals prices as the cause. Searches for today's session turned up no sourced share-price moves, no named data release and no consensus forecast. Percentage falls and index points are therefore left out below rather than estimated.

The China trade

The mechanism is familiar. China consumes roughly half of the world's refined copper and a majority of its iron ore, steel and several other industrial metals. A soft reading on industrial output, fixed-asset investment or property tends to be read straight across to demand for those metals. London's diversified miners and Chile-focused copper producers are among the most liquid ways to trade that view.

The pattern has repeated many times. Earlier episodes in the archive show Anglo American, Glencore, Antofagasta and Fresnillo sitting among the FTSE 100's biggest fallers after disappointing Chinese manufacturing, services or industrial-production figures. On several of those days the falls were in the low single digits. A day of this kind is not by itself evidence of a change in the demand trend.

The test is whether the data itself marks a change. Three things would settle it:

  • The release: the National Bureau of Statistics monthly activity data (industrial output, retail sales, fixed-asset investment, property investment) or an official or private PMI, with the actual figure set against the consensus forecast.
  • The metals: copper, iron ore and the other relevant prices, and whether they moved with the shares or merely lagged them.
  • The breadth: whether the sell-off extended beyond London to Australian and Hong Kong-listed miners, which would point to a sector-wide repricing rather than a UK-specific move.

Until those are in hand, the defensible reading is that this is a China-led move of the usual kind. The data does not yet show whether it is a turn in sector earnings expectations. The index weight matters here. Miners are a large enough share of the FTSE 100 that a China-led fall can pull the headline index lower even when domestic stocks are steady.

Angola and De Beers

Anglo American's own news cuts across the day's trading. Reuters reported on 13 November that Angola is seeking a 10% stake in De Beers as part of Anglo's proposed sale of its majority holding. A mines ministry spokesperson confirmed the figure to the agency. The request is a step down from last year, when Angola first sought a minority stake and then, in October 2025, bid for Anglo's entire majority holding.

The field around it is crowded. Anglo still owns 85% of De Beers. Botswana holds a right of first refusal and has said it will double its holding to 30%. Namibia was reported by Novo Jornal to be seeking a 10% stake too, though Namibian officials had not responded to requests for comment. The Global Diamond Consortium has said it is working with Oman towards co-ownership. Anglo has said it will keep working with the African states and the consortium towards binding documents.

The price, the terms and the timetable are not public in the sources reviewed, and no binding agreement has been reported. Angola is therefore an applicant for a stake, not yet a buyer. A headline saying it will buy one runs ahead of the record.

What ties them together

The two threads pull in different directions for Anglo American. Weak Chinese demand lowers the value of what the company keeps: copper, iron ore and the rest of its portfolio. The De Beers sale, if it completes, shrinks the company to those same assets. A smaller, more concentrated Anglo is more exposed to the Chinese cycle, not less. Diamond demand is cyclical too, but it follows consumer spending in the US and China, not construction and infrastructure.

The next checkpoints are concrete. The first is any statement from Anglo American or the Angolan government on binding documents for the De Beers stake. The second is the next Chinese monthly activity release, which will show whether this week's weakness persists. Two weak readings would be a trend. One would be a bad month.
