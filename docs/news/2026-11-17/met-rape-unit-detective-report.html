Met failed to stop rape-unit detective who abused women, report finds

In one sentence
A report says the Met missed repeated warnings about Warren Arter, a rape-unit detective who abused women, renewing scrutiny of police vetting, supervision and leadership.

A detective who worked in the Metropolitan Police's rape and serious sexual offences unit abused women, and the force repeatedly missed or failed to act on signs that he was a danger. That is the account in a report covered on 17 November by BBC London and picked up by other outlets. It concerns Warren Arter, a detective inspector who died in custody in July 2024. The case adds to the long list of episodes that have damaged the Met's standing with women who report sexual crime.

A caution on sourcing. The core finding comes from BBC UK reporting, which we have not been able to read directly. The detail below comes from secondary coverage, from earlier press reports and from published inquest material. Those sources differ on some dates and figures, and where they do the article says so.

A detective inside the unit

Arter was a Met officer of about two decades' service. He worked with the force's Sapphire unit, which handles rape and sexual-assault investigations. In 2017, four women reported that he had sexual contact with them after meeting them through that work. Reporting describes the women as vulnerable; some had histories of addiction.

The pattern the report is said to describe is the one at the centre of the Met's credibility problem: a man trusted with the worst moments in other people's lives, using that position to exploit them. London Crime News, summarising the BBC's reporting, says the Independent Office for Police Conduct (IOPC) identified 21 potential victims. The Met's own current count of women involved in the misconduct allegations has been reported as eight, including his wife, Rebekah. The gap between those numbers is unexplained in the sources available.

What the force knew

The sequence of missed chances is the heart of the criticism. The IOPC arrested Arter in October 2018 and found cocaine traces at his home. It concluded in 2020 that he had a case to answer for gross misconduct. The Crown Prosecution Service decided there was insufficient evidence to charge him with drug offences.

He remained suspended on full pay for about seven years, at a reported cost of roughly £600,000. When he was finally dismissed, in May 2023, it was for drug-related conduct, not for the allegations from the women he had met through his work. No criminal charge over those allegations followed for years.

London Crime News reports further warnings: medical staff finding him alone with a woman hours after she had reported a rape in 2010, and several corroborated intelligence reports between 2014 and 2018. Those items rest on a single secondary summary, and the other sources we read do not repeat them. They should be treated as unconfirmed here.

The last missed opportunity is better documented, because it came out at an inquest. In March 2024, an anonymous woman reported Arter for grooming, voyeurism and sending unsolicited upskirting images. She was later confirmed to be his wife, Rebekah Arter. A fault in the Met's CONNECT records system meant officers did not act on the report. Assistant Coroner Andrew Harris said that had the Met acted, Arter would likely have been arrested and bailed with conditions barring contact with her.

Rebekah Arter died in Barbados on 28 June 2024. Her cause of death could not be determined. The coroner said failings in the handling of her body raised the prospect of concealment or obstruction. Arter gave conflicting accounts of her death. He was returned to the UK, charged with misconduct in public office, and was found dead in his cell at HMP Wandsworth on 12 July 2024.

The coroner did not find that the Met knew of a real and immediate risk to her life. Commander Simon Messinger cited that finding in the force's response. Her son, Elliot Usher, had asked for a wider inquiry into institutional failure, saying: "My Mum was let down in life, please do not let her be let down in death."

Vetting, supervision and trust

The case lands on ground the Met has been over many times. The Casey Review in 2023 described the force as institutionally misogynistic, after the convictions of Wayne Couzens and David Carrick. Carrick's case turned on the same theme as this one: complaints that went nowhere and vetting that did not stop him. Commissioner Sir Mark Rowley has pledged to address the underlying problems.

The Arter case differs in one respect that makes it harder to dismiss as an aberration. He was not in a general patrol role but in a unit whose purpose is to win the trust of people at their most exposed. A failure of supervision there damages the willingness of victims to come forward, which in turn undermines prosecutions.

The weakness the sources describe is less a failure of rules than of follow-through. A suspension that ran for seven years, a records fault that swallowed a report, and misconduct findings that addressed drugs but not the women. A 2022 database check found 161 serving officers with previous convictions, three of them for sex offences, which suggests the checking systems have long had gaps.

What to watch

The Met says it has apologised to Arter's victims, is examining his electronic devices and is urging other women to come forward. It has not yet contacted all the women whose cases he handled, according to the coverage. The test is whether that outreach is completed and whether any recommendations from the IOPC are published and accepted. The CONNECT fix was reported as not due until April 2026, so whether it has been delivered is a fair question for the force. Pressure on Rowley's leadership is likely to turn on those answers.
