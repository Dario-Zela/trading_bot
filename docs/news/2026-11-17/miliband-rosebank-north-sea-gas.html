Miliband says he won't resign if Rosebank is approved, as new North Sea gas comes online

In one sentence
Ed Miliband reportedly says he would stay in post if Rosebank is approved, making the field a personal and political test for the government, Equinor and Ithaca.

Energy Secretary Ed Miliband has said he would not resign if the government approves the Rosebank oil field, according to BBC Politics. The remark ties one minister's job to one North Sea field, and it turns a planning decision into a test of how far the government's climate commitments extend.

The wording has not been independently checked. A search for Miliband's remark returned no matching report, so it rests on the BBC item cited in triage. The closest sourced precedent concerns Heathrow: reports said he advised cabinet that expansion would probably breach carbon targets, while saying he would not resign over it.

A ministerial decision, not a regulator's

Rosebank, west of Shetland, is operated by Equinor with Ithaca Energy as partner. The ownership split should be confirmed against company filings before it is relied on. It is the largest undeveloped field in UK waters.

On who decides, the evidence is clear. The Institute for Government has said Miliband must rule on Rosebank and the Jackdaw gas field. The prime minister resisted Conservative calls to intervene on the grounds that the decision legally sat with the energy secretary.

The resignation pledge therefore matters beyond Miliband. It suggests ministers regard an approval as something they will own, rather than a technical consent handed down by a regulator. Approval would not be a surprise concession. The government's line has been that existing licences can be honoured while new ones are not issued.

The consent decision has been pending since an earlier approval was set aside by the courts. The current status of that decision, and whether it is imminent, should be confirmed with the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero.

The pressure inside Labour

The political risk is on the government's own benches. Labour MPs have warned that approval would provoke a serious backlash. One was quoted as saying that many of them, from every wing of the party, would "go absolutely nuts". Against that, industry has pressed for timely approval, citing winter energy demand and the investment at stake.

Miliband has form on resignation threats. One report recalls him threatening to quit over a third runway at Heathrow when he sat in Gordon Brown's cabinet in 2009. A statement that he will stay in post, whatever the outcome, sets the opposite precedent. It tells colleagues and campaigners that approval would not be treated as a breach of his own brief.

New gas, unsettled argument

The backdrop is a report from Oilprice.com that production has started at a new North Sea gas field, amid continuing argument over licensing and windfall tax. The triage material did not include the field's name, operator or output, and searches did not find them. Those details are not stated here.

What can be said is that new supply does not settle the argument. The Energy Profits Levy, introduced in 2022 as a windfall tax on oil and gas profits, remains part of the dispute. Its current rate and end date should be checked against present policy. Producers argue that it deters investment. Critics argue that new fields add emissions without lowering household bills.

Rosebank sharpens that dispute because it is oil as well as gas, and because it is long-lived. Any new field also raises the question of how emissions from the oil it produces are counted, an issue central to the earlier legal challenge.

What to watch

The next data point is the decision itself. Approval would test Miliband's stated intention to stay, the patience of Labour backbenchers, and the investment plans of Equinor and Ithaca. Refusal would test the government's relationship with the industry and with the Conservatives, who have pressed it to approve. Either outcome is Miliband's to sign.
