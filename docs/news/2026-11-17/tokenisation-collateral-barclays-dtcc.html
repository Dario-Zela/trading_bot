Barclays and DTCC say tokenisation's real prize is unlocking idle collateral

In one sentence
DTCC and Barclays Research both argue tokenisation's nearest-term value is moving idle collateral faster, with DTCC projecting intraday funding costs could fall about 50%.

Two of the institutions that would run a tokenised market have, in separate publications this year, made much the same argument: the early money in tokenisation is in moving collateral that is currently stuck, not in issuing new digital securities. DTCC, the US post-trade utility, published its case in May. Barclays Research followed in September. Decrypt's report pairing the two was not retrieved for this article, so the account below rests on the primary documents.

What DTCC says

On 13 May, DTCC released a paper with the consultancy Finadium, Collateral Infrastructure for Tokenized Capital Markets. It estimates that intraday funding expenses could fall by about 50% at large dealer banks if collateral could move in near real time. Tokenised bonds, money market funds and cash, it says, offer the most immediate balance-sheet benefits.

The mechanism is intraday repo settled on a digital ledger in minute-level increments. Faster movement of collateral would shrink end-of-day exposures and might ease liquidity coverage and counterparty credit requirements. It could also reduce forced asset sales when markets turn volatile. Nadine Chakar, DTCC's global head of digital assets, called digital assets "the next phase of capital and liquidity optimization in global markets."

The paper is tied to a product. DTCC's Collateral AppChain, a shared collateral mobility platform, is expected to go live in the fourth quarter of 2026. DTCC has also said it processed live production trades with more than 30 firms in tokenised form, and that its Tokenisation Service is due to launch in October.

What Barclays says

The bank's note, Tokenisation: From digital assets to market infrastructure, was published on 14 September by Zornitsa Todorova, head of thematic FICC research. It argues that tokenised repo improves "collateral mobility, reconciliation and operational efficiency". Tokenising the repo record would let collateral be "reallocated, substituted or pledged more quickly" without changing the underlying intermediation model.

The diagnosis is fragmentation. Collateral sits across custodians, central counterparties, brokers and jurisdictions. A common transferable network "could allow collateral to move more quickly to where it is needed". Combined with near-real-time settlement, that could cut the liquidity buffers institutions hold for operational reasons.

The note's figures point the same way. Tokenised real-world assets are about 1% of a near-$3 trillion digital-asset ecosystem, above $30 billion, with nearly half in US Treasuries. Tokenised money market funds grew from under $100 million in early 2023 to almost $14 billion by April 2026. In tokenised infrastructure, a market approaching $400 billion, repo is more than 90%. Barclays flags that the repo number is the face value of securities used as collateral on a given day, not a volume or market-value measure. Broadridge's DLR platform averaged about $360 billion a day in 2026, against about $250 billion in 2025.

Why the framing matters

The thesis moves the business case away from crypto-native issuance and towards back-office plumbing, where banks, custodians and utilities already sit. A halving of intraday funding costs would be a benefit that accrues mainly to dealer balance sheets, and the infrastructure that delivers it would be run by incumbents.

Caution is warranted. The 50% figure is a projection from a research paper commissioned by a firm that is selling the platform. Barclays' note is research, not a commitment to a product. Stablecoins and tokenised money funds would need to be accepted as margin by clearing houses and counterparties before the larger gains appear, and that depends on regulators and clearing members rather than on technology.

What to watch

The first test is the Collateral AppChain's fourth-quarter go-live: whether it launches on schedule, and how many dealers post collateral on it. The second is whether clearing houses accept tokenised money funds and stablecoins as margin. Until both happen, the collateral thesis remains a well-argued projection.
