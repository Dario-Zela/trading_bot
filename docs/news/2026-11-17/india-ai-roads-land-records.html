Indian start-ups use AI on broken roads and messy land records

In one sentence
Indian start-ups are applying AI to pothole detection and land-record reading, but the evidence so far is company claims and government trials, not verified large-scale deployments.

Two of India's oldest public failings, broken roads and unreliable land records, are drawing a new kind of fix. A set of start-ups is pointing machine learning at both. The BBC's technology desk has reported on the trend. That article could not be checked here, so what follows rests on the companies' own accounts and on Indian trade and news coverage. The open question is whether any of this runs at scale inside government, or whether it is still mostly demonstrations.

Roads: cameras instead of inspectors

The road tools are the more developed half. The idea is simple: a camera, GPS and motion sensors on a vehicle record the surface as it drives, and software labels the damage.

Bengaluru's RoadMetrics says its system can identify 10 types of road defect and has analysed more than 50,000 km of roads, according to coverage in The Better India and a government-backed tech portal. Those are company figures, and the sources do not say how much of that distance was under a government contract. Delhi's HanuAI sells a platform that flags potholes, cracks and surface damage, and tries to predict when maintenance will be due.

One project is narrower and arguably more pointed. Bengaluru engineer Gaurav Sen built an app that detects potholes and cross-references government contract data to identify which contractor is responsible for a stretch of road. That moves the tool from detection to accountability, and it works only where contract records are public.

Official bodies are also experimenting. The Centre is reported to be testing AI that analyses phone video of rural roads, and Delhi's public works department is building a system called IRMMS to monitor about 1,000 km of roads from video feeds. These are tests and development projects, not completed rollouts.

The stakes are physical. Road transport ministry data cited in Indian coverage attribute 2,161 deaths to potholes in 2023.

Land records: reading the paper

The land-record work is harder to assess. India has spent years on the Digital India Land Records Modernisation Programme. Ministers say about 95 per cent of village land records are digitised, but only 11 states and Union territories have finished digitising cadastral maps, according to ThePrint. Madhya Pradesh has launched a drive to digitise nearly 150 million old revenue documents.

The start-ups work on top of that. Landeed has launched Terra, which it describes as an AI layer that reads and connects land records, registry data and legal documents. A Kolkata company, Vixplor Analytics, offers DocXplor, which reads faded handwritten records. In Bhubaneswar, The Land Bankers summarises official records from portals such as Bhulekh Odisha. All of this comes from company announcements. None of it has been independently checked.

The motivation is the volume of disputes. A widely repeated estimate holds that about 66 per cent of civil suits in India concern land or property. That figure comes from older official and think-tank estimates, so it should be read as indicative.

What would make it news

The risks differ between the two. A wrongly flagged pothole costs a wasted repair visit. A wrongly read land record can cloud someone's title to their home or farm. Handwritten records are error-prone, and a model that reads them confidently but wrongly could cause real harm. Nothing in the available coverage describes error rates, or says whether any state accepts AI-read output as legally authoritative rather than as a research aid.

What to watch is procurement. A signed state contract with stated coverage and accuracy terms would show that this is infrastructure. The results of the Delhi IRMMS build and the Centre's rural-road trial are the nearest tests.
