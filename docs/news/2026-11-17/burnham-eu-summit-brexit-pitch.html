Burnham says the UK lost more than it gained from Brexit as the 20 November EU summit nears

In one sentence
Burnham says Brexit cost the UK more than it gained and wants food, carbon-market and youth-mobility deals at the 20 November EU summit, while ruling out rejoining this Parliament.

Britain goes into its 20 November summit with the European Union wanting to renegotiate parts of the Brexit settlement without reopening the question of membership. Andy Burnham, the prime minister, has said the UK lost more than it gained from leaving. He has also ruled out rejoining in this Parliament. The summit is the first real test of whether those two positions can be held together.

A reset with a ceiling

The outline of Burnham's pitch comes from his stated aims, reported by ITV on 12 October, when he said he would use the summit to seek new post-Brexit deals. Three areas are named: food and drink, emissions trading and youth mobility.

Each is a piece of the 2020 trade deal that has caused friction since it took effect. Food and drink covers the checks on agricultural and food products crossing the Channel, which exporters have long said add cost and delay. Emissions trading concerns whether the UK's carbon market could be linked to the EU's, which would spare British exporters some of the EU's carbon border charges. Youth mobility is a scheme under which young people could live and work across the border for a fixed period.

What unites the three is that none requires a vote on membership. Any movement on single-market rules for food, on linking carbon markets or on youth mobility would change trade and immigration policy through negotiated agreements instead. That is the attraction for a prime minister who says Brexit cost more than it delivered but who has closed off the most obvious remedy. It is also the vulnerability. Critics on one side will say a reset that stops short of rejoining leaves the UK accepting EU rules without a say in them. Critics on the other will say any alignment betrays the referendum.

Business groups, Brussels and the opposition will all read the outcome as a signal of how far a reset can go. This article has not seen the detail of what either side will table, and the summit may produce only a statement of intent rather than signed texts.

Rail enters the frame

Campaigners are using the moment to press a narrower case. The Guardian reports they are urging Burnham to back investment in international rail as part of the reset, with cross-Channel services included in the summit package. The pitch casts rail as a visible, practical benefit of closer ties, in contrast with the technical argument over food standards.

There is no sign in the material reviewed that the government has committed to this. The Guardian's report was only partly examined for this article, and no funding figures have been verified. For now it is best read as lobbying that shows how many interests are trying to attach themselves to the summit.

The cost argument returns

The politics of the reset is also being fought over numbers. The Guardian has fact-checked a claim that rejoining the EU would cost £750m a week. The figure invites comparison with the £350m-a-week claim on the Vote Leave campaign bus in 2016. The UK Statistics Authority ruled that the 2016 figure was misleading because it used gross payments and ignored the UK rebate.

The same test applies now. A weekly figure means little unless it says whether it is gross or net of the rebate and of EU spending that returns to the UK. The £750m figure and its methodology could not be verified in any source available for this article, so it should be treated with caution until the basis is clear. The fact-check's existence is itself informative, though. Rejoining is off the table for this Parliament, yet it is already being argued over in the language of 2016.

What to watch

The summit on 20 November will show whether the three named areas yield agreements or only commitments to keep talking. Watch for any text on the EU's single-market rules for food, for a timetable on carbon-market linkage, and for whether youth mobility is described as a scheme with numbers attached. A thin outcome would give opposition parties an argument that the reset is more rhetoric than substance. A substantial one would raise the harder question Burnham has so far avoided, which is how much of the single market the UK can accept without seeking membership.
