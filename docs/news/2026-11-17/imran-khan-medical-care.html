Imran Khan seeks urgent medical care, fearing he 'may not return alive'

In one sentence
Jailed former Pakistani PM Imran Khan says he fears he 'may not return alive'; officials say his care is adequate, and no independent medical assessment has been made public.

Imran Khan, Pakistan's jailed former prime minister, is seeking urgent medical care and has said he fears he "may not return alive", according to a BBC report. Khan, 74, has been in custody since August 2023, most of it at Adiala jail in Rawalpindi. The warning comes from Khan himself and has not been independently verified. This account does not reproduce the full BBC text. Other reporting shows that his health, and who gets to assess it, has been contested for months.

A recurring dispute

This is not the first alarm. In August the Supreme Court ordered that Khan be examined in hospital. He was taken from Adiala to a hospital in the Islamabad–Rawalpindi area. Doctors reportedly judged him medically fit and he was returned to jail within hours. Pakistani outlets reported that his sister alleged ill-treatment in custody, and that his sons spoke publicly of their fears for him. BBC World Affairs Editor John Simpson also urged the authorities to ensure he received proper care.

In early November, Pakistani media reported fresh concern from PTI figures, including party chairman Gohar Khan, about his health. Khan was reportedly taken to hospital overnight for an eye examination and then back to jail. The government's line is consistent. Parliamentary Affairs Minister Tariq Fazal Chaudhry has said Khan is receiving the care he needs and that his treatment is not being neglected. Jail authorities have previously called rumours about his condition baseless.

The question of who examines him

The dispute is less about whether Khan sees doctors than about which ones. State-appointed physicians have examined him in jail and in hospital. PTI and his family have pressed for tests at a private hospital and for physicians of his own choosing, arguing that official assessments cannot be trusted when the state is also his jailer. The government has hinted at flexibility, with one minister saying private treatment might be considered, but this has not translated into a settled arrangement.

That is why his warning matters beyond its drama. A claim that a prisoner may die is, on its own, a statement of fear. What would change the story is evidence: an independent clinician's findings, a court order for a specific assessment, or a detailed official medical report that can be tested against his family's account. None has been publicly established in the reporting available.

What the stakes are

Khan remains the central figure of Pakistan's opposition, and PTI has built much of its case against the government around his treatment. A genuine medical emergency would put pressure on the courts, which have already intervened once, and on a prison system that controls access to him. It could also energise PTI supporters at a time when the party has struggled to turn his popularity into political leverage.

The authorities, for their part, face a reputational risk if a prominent prisoner's health deteriorates without outside verification. Allowing a credible independent examination would cost them little if their account of his health is accurate.

The next markers are practical: whether the Supreme Court or the Islamabad High Court acts on the family's pending petitions, whether the government allows an examination by doctors Khan's side trusts, and whether any official medical findings are published.
