Milburn review calls for a 'complete system reset' to get 440,000 young people into work

In one sentence
Alan Milburn's review urges a 'system reset' to bring 440,000 more young people into work by 2036, including a phased employer National Insurance cut for under-25s that would cost the Treasury.

A government-commissioned review is asking ministers to treat the number of young people outside work, education and training as a problem needing a rebuild, not a patch. The final report of Alan Milburn's review of young people and work, due out on Tuesday, models a decade of reform that would bring about 440,000 more young people into sustained work. Its most pointed proposal for the Treasury is a gradual cut in the employer National Insurance paid on workers under 25.

The report is a set of recommendations, not government policy. The coverage reviewed for this article gave no overall price for the reforms, so the size of the bill remains an open question.

The scale of the problem

Milburn, a former Labour health secretary, delivered an interim report in May. It put the number of young people not in education, employment or training (Neets) at nearly a million, roughly one in eight. The review estimates that this costs the UK about £125bn a year in lost tax revenue and higher health and welfare spending. It says 60% of Neets have never had a job.

The interim findings also pointed to an imbalance in spending. In 2024-25 about £25 went on benefits for young people for every £1 spent on helping them into work. Without action, the interim report warned, the Neet rate could rise from one in eight to one in six within five years.

Milburn spoke in stark terms at that stage. "We are at risk of a lost generation," he said. "That is a moral crisis. It has economic consequences." He told the BBC that a "complete system reset" was needed, and said there was "neither a system nor a plan to increase youth participation."

What the review proposes

The final report sets a national ambition to cut the Neet rate from 13% to around 5% by 2036, which would put the UK among Europe's better performers. Its modelling suggests about 600,000 more young people in education, employment or training each year by then, with around 440,000 of them in sustained work.

The main recommendations, as reported ahead of publication, are:

  • A legal guarantee of work, training or employment support for young people, with those who refuse potentially losing benefits.
  • A youth opportunity service across England by 2029, giving each young person a personalised participation plan and a named adviser.
  • A new "youth allowance" for under-25s, replacing Personal Independence Payment and the health element of universal credit.
  • A doubling of the number of young people completing apprenticeships over the decade.
  • Faster access to mental health support for Neet young people, without waiting for a diagnosis.

The review's own phrase for the approach is that youth participation has to run through public policy "like a stick of rock."

The Treasury question

The employer National Insurance proposal is where the report meets the public finances. The review recommends gradually cutting the contribution businesses pay on under-25 staff, starting in retail and hospitality, two sectors that employ many young people. Any such cut is a direct loss of revenue to the Exchequer.

It also reopens an argument about the rise in employer National Insurance that took effect in 2025. Employers argued that the increase, together with higher minimum wages, made entry-level jobs more expensive to create. In the run-up to the final report, Milburn said that scrapping the 1.2 percentage point increase would help, because it was making hiring "more expensive and more difficult for employers." The final report's proposal is narrower, targeting the under-25 age group, but the reasoning is the same: make young workers cheaper to take on.

The youth allowance is the other fraught element. Replacing disability-related support for under-25s is likely to draw opposition from charities and some Labour MPs, since earlier proposals could leave some young claimants with less money at the start of a claim. Milburn conceded as much, saying some people "will get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim than under the current system." He has said he expects new investment in support, training and jobs to come before any change to benefits.

Reaction and what comes next

Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the "status quo clearly isn't working" and that the government was "ready to grasp the nettle here and make a better system for young people." That is a commitment to engage with the report, not to adopt each recommendation.

Outside government, responses were broadly warm but guarded. Enver Solomon of the crime-reduction charity Nacro called the youth opportunity service "a once in a generation moment of change." Dame Clare Moriarty of Citizens Advice said change "must be done carefully." Lisa Russell of Manchester Metropolitan University argued that decisions should rest on individual circumstances rather than age alone, a challenge to the age-based youth allowance.

The test is what the government commits to in its formal response, and whether it will put a figure on the cost. It will have to decide whether to fund a National Insurance cut for young hires while it is under fiscal pressure, and whether to sequence investment ahead of benefit changes as Milburn urges.
