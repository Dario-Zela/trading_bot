European-made pesticides named in 1,605 poisoning reports in Brazil

In one sentence
Brazilian health reports name European-sold pesticides 1,605 times, a third involving chemicals banned on EU farms but still exportable, though companies dispute the data.

Brazil's health ministry received 1,605 medical reports last year that named pesticide brands sold by European companies. A third of the cases involved chemicals that cannot be used on European farms. The figures come from an analysis by Unearthed and Public Eye, reported by the Guardian on 17 November, and they bear on a live question: whether EU chemical rules protect only European users.

What the data show

Brazil logged more than 6,700 medical reports on agricultural pesticides last year. Of these, 2,857 named an identifiable product, and 1,605 of those named brands sold by European firms. In the 2,857 reports, 1,420 were classed as confirmed poisonings and 1,108 as exposures. The rest were unknown or had other diagnoses.

Across the European-linked reports there were 32 deaths. Of these, 31 were confirmed poisonings and 26 were recorded as suicides. Syngenta products were cited 425 times and BASF products 179 times. Those counts overlap, because some reports name products from more than one manufacturer.

The caveat is one the analysts themselves note: a brand's appearance in a report does not prove it caused the poisoning or death.

The export gap

The story turns on which chemicals are involved. Diquat, the active ingredient in Syngenta's Reglone, was banned on EU farms in 2019 and in the UK in 2020, after experts found a high risk to workers, nearby residents and birds. Reglone is made in Huddersfield. It was named in 44 Brazilian reports, including four deaths. Brazil recorded 75 diquat cases last year, against 36 across the whole of 2018–2022.

Other examples follow the same pattern. Cyanamide, in Dormex (made by Alzchem in Germany and distributed in Brazil by BASF), was withdrawn in the EU in 2008 after an assessment found operator exposure could exceed safe levels. Glufosinate, in BASF's Finale, was banned in the EU in 2018 and is classified there as toxic to reproduction. Bayer's Roundup, a glyphosate product, appears in many reports, including 12 deaths. The report's list of banned chemicals does not include glyphosate.

EU rules do not stop a company manufacturing such chemicals for export to countries where they remain legal. That is the loophole. Brussels has said it intends to phase out such exports under its chemicals strategy. As of the Guardian's 2023 reporting, no timetable had been set. I did not find evidence of one since.

Companies dispute the reading

The firms contest the analysis. Syngenta said nearly half the Brazilian cases attributed to it were not classified as confirmed poisonings, and accused the investigation of ignoring final classifications. It added that Brazil's regulator assesses products independently and that it trained 95,000 people in Brazil in 2025.

Bayer said its products are safe when used as labelled. It questioned the data's reliability and noted that "Roundup" is often used generically and may refer to other brands or counterfeits. BASF said it was deeply concerned, suggested some cases may involve deliberate ingestion, which it called misuse, and said it is confident in its products when properly used.

The suicide figure cuts both ways. It supports the companies' point that some cases are intentional. It also supports campaigners, because a 2020 WHO-linked study found national bans on highly hazardous pesticides could be a cost-effective way to reduce pesticide-related suicides. Nina Schrank of Greenpeace UK called the findings a "deadly double standard." Ivan Castoldi, whose father Nelson died after ingesting Reglone, said: "That poison, Reglone, should be banned in Brazil."

The data have limits of their own. Vanda Garibotti, a public health biologist in Rio Grande do Sul, said the "insufficient training of healthcare professionals to recognise and manage these cases is a major limiting factor," which suggests the true count may be higher.

What to watch

The test is whether the EU sets a date for ending exports of chemicals it has banned at home. Until it does, each such data set will draw the same argument: companies point to Brazil's own approvals, and critics point to Europe's. Note that the figures here rest on one investigation, read through syndicated copies of the Guardian's report, and the companies' challenges to the data have not been independently resolved.
