Cass report author does not back draft conversion practices bill

In one sentence
Baroness Cass told the joint committee she does not support the draft conversion practices bill as written, though she still opposes abusive practices.

The woman whose review reshaped how England treats gender-questioning children has told MPs and peers she cannot back the government's draft ban on conversion practices as it stands. Baroness Cass, the paediatrician who led the independent review of gender identity services for children and young people, gave evidence on 16 November to the joint committee scrutinising the draft Conversion Practices Bill. Asked directly whether she supported it as drafted, she said no.

The answer matters because her name had been attached to the bill's launch. When the government published the draft for England and Wales in late June, she welcomed it. Her position now is more guarded, and it removes a piece of expert reassurance from a policy that remains contested.

From welcome to withholding

The draft bill is aimed at abusive attempts to change a person's sexual orientation or gender identity. Reports on its publication said offenders could face up to five years in prison, and the draft also reaches practices carried out overseas against British citizens or residents. It includes an exemption for healthcare. Cass's June comments praised the clearer definition and that exemption.

To the committee, she explained the gap between that welcome and her present view. According to accounts of the session posted by BBC journalist Hannah Barnes, she said she saw the bill only about 15 minutes before the government wanted her press statement signed off. She had backed the principle but had not read the detail. After, in her words, a long time spent thinking about it, she could not support the text.

The BBC report is the source of record here. This article draws on that headline and on contemporaneous accounts of the hearing and press coverage, not on the full BBC text, so the wording above should be read as reported rather than as a verbatim transcript.

The concerns

Cass's objection is to drafting, not to the aim. She said she still believes no one should be subjected to conversion practices. Her worry is that the wording is not tight enough to prevent serious unintended consequences.

Two strands stand out in the reporting:

  • Misuse in a polarised climate. She suggested that the heat around gender could lead people to use a new law in ways it was never intended for.
  • Process as punishment. She said clinicians in the new NHS gender dysphoria clinics fear that a complaint could be damaging even if it never ended in prosecution.

She also gave the committee an example from her review: a mother in an abusive relationship, whose daughter, dealing with anorexia and unexplained physical symptoms, began identifying as trans after the father left the family home. The point was that cases of this kind are complicated, and that a poorly bounded offence could catch ordinary clinical or parental conduct.

This echoes what she said in 2024, shortly after her review was published, when she warned that a ban could frighten therapists away from working with distressed young people.

What the government says

Ministers did not dismiss her. The government welcomed her continued support for ending abusive conversion practices and her comments on avoiding adverse consequences. That framing suggests officials will treat her evidence as a drafting problem to be fixed, not a rejection of the bill.

Critics have come at the draft from both sides. Campaigners such as QueerAF have called its loopholes an "instruction manual for abuse", while Sex Matters and commentators including Sonia Sodha argue it could expose parents who do not endorse a child's social transition to prosecution. Cass's intervention lends weight to the second group without settling the first.

What to watch

The joint committee's pre-legislative scrutiny is the next step. Its report will show whether it recommends tightening the definitions Cass criticised, and how the government responds before any formal bill is introduced. The test will be whether the healthcare exemption and the treatment of parental conduct are rewritten, since those are the points on which her support now appears to depend.
