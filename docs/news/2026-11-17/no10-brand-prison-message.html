No 10 investigating Russell Brand prison message as 'appalling'

In one sentence
Downing Street calls a prison message from Russell Brand, read out by his wife, 'appalling' and says it is being urgently investigated, with no outcome yet reported.

Downing Street has called a message from Russell Brand, written in prison and read out publicly by his wife, "appalling". It says the message is being "urgently investigated". The government's reaction is the story so far. No sanction has been reported, and the investigating body has not been named in the coverage seen.

The message

Brand, 51, is held at Littlehey Prison in Cambridgeshire, which houses men convicted of sexual offences. Earlier this month he was found guilty of one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault. He was sentenced to 10 years and six months. He denied the allegations throughout.

On Sunday 15 November his wife, Laura, read a statement from a notepad in a YouTube video streamed to his "Sunday Service" online community. In it he said he was "not guilty for the offences I have been jailed for". She also asked viewers to "support my treasured wife and family".

An assertion of innocence from a convicted man is not unusual in itself. What drew the government's attention was that it came from inside a prison and was published through a third party.

What No 10 said

On Monday a Downing Street spokesperson described the message as "appalling" and said it was "being urgently investigated". The spokesperson said action would follow if Brand were found to have broken the rules.

The spokesperson also set out the principle in play:

  • Prisoners are banned from making statements that distress victims.
  • Speaking to the media is permitted only in exceptional circumstances.
  • Prisoners cannot get around those rules by having someone else publish their words.

The last point matters most here. Brand did not post anything himself. His wife read his words aloud. The question for the authorities is whether that counts as the prisoner communicating with the public, and whether the content amounted to the kind of statement the rules are meant to prevent.

What is not yet clear

The coverage seen does not say which body is carrying out the review. It is probably the prison service or the Ministry of Justice, but that is an inference. It also does not say what sanction, if any, is available or likely. Prison rules on contact with the media and on distressing victims exist, but how they apply to a message passed to a family member and then broadcast is untested in this case. There is no reported response from Brand's representatives or from his wife beyond the broadcast itself.

The case's wider significance is limited for now. It becomes a stronger story only if the authorities confirm a finding or take a step against Brand. Until then, the main thing on the record is a government statement about what the rules say.

What to watch

The next marker is whether the review reports and in what form. A confirmed breach, with a named consequence such as lost privileges, would show how far the ban on proxy publication can be enforced. A quiet outcome would suggest the "urgent" language was mostly for show.
