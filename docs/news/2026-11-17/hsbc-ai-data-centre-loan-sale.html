HSBC sells $300m troubled AI data centre loan at a steep discount

In one sentence
HSBC reportedly sold a $300m slice of an Oracle-linked New Mexico data-centre loan at a mid-80s price, a rare public marker for AI infrastructure credit.

HSBC has sold about $300m of a construction loan for an AI data centre campus in New Mexico at a price in the mid-80s, which is roughly 85 cents on the dollar. The report comes from Bloomberg, published on 16 November and relayed by Investing.com, and it rests on people familiar with the matter. Neither HSBC nor Oracle was quoted in the coverage reviewed. A sale about 15 points below par is a rare public price for debt in a sector that has mostly been described through valuations rather than loan marks.

What was sold

The $300m is a slice of a much larger package: an $18bn construction financing for a campus in Doña Ana County, New Mexico, that is tied to Oracle. Coverage describes the project as part of the Stargate build-out. One outlet says it is being built by a developer unit of Blue Owl Capital. That attribution comes from secondary coverage and was not confirmed against the primary report.

The discount is reported as "mid-80s". One analysis puts the shortfall at about $45m against face value on $300m. That is the outlet's own arithmetic, not a reported figure, and the exact price, the buyer and HSBC's reasons for selling have not been disclosed.

The timing matters. According to the reports, the sale took place several weeks ago, shortly after Oracle sent the project's developer a notice citing force majeure. That clause can excuse a party from contractual obligations when events outside its control intervene. The loan was therefore sold while the project's status was in question, and the reports call it "troubled". The sequence suggests the price reflected project risk rather than a general market move, though the coverage does not say so directly.

Why a single trade carries weight

Loan sales of this kind are usually private and rarely produce a visible price. A trade at a stated level gives other holders a reference point. One commentator argues that a visible discounted trade can set a level investors use to value similar AI-infrastructure loans. If that holds, the mid-80s mark matters beyond one bank's balance sheet.

Construction loans carry a distinct risk. Repayment depends on the project being finished, powered and leased to a tenant that pays. A dispute between tenant and developer goes to the heart of that chain. A lender holding the debt in that position is exposed to delay and to the possibility that the cash flows it was underwriting arrive late or on different terms. A price of 85 is consistent with that, though a single trade cannot establish how widespread such problems are.

HSBC's wider positioning

The sale is not HSBC's first sign of caution on the sector. Bloomberg reported earlier, in a separate story, that the bank was in early talks with investors on a significant risk transfer (SRT) covering more than $1bn of data-centre-linked loans. An SRT moves part of a loan portfolio's credit risk to outside investors or insurers, usually while the bank keeps the loans on its books. Those talks were described as preliminary and might not lead to any deal. An HSBC representative said the plans were part of normal portfolio management and that its data-centre exposure was small.

Other lenders have been doing similar things. Barclays is reported to be seeking insurance on more than $10bn of AI-infrastructure credit, and Deutsche Bank has been working on an SRT tied to about $2bn of project finance debt. Separately, HSBC has also been a lender to the sector: it provided $550m in facilities to Core42, a G42 company, earlier this year. That was new financing and is unrelated to the New Mexico sale.

Taken together, these moves point to banks that remain active in AI infrastructure while seeking to lay off part of the risk. That is different from a retreat. Selling one slice at a loss and hedging a separate portfolio are also different actions with different motives, and the reports do not link them.

What to watch

Three things would sharpen the picture. The first is whether Oracle or the developer addresses the force majeure notice and what it means for the project's schedule. The second is whether other participants in the $18bn package trade their holdings and at what levels. The third is whether the SRT discussions at HSBC, Barclays and Deutsche Bank reach pricing, since the cost of insuring this credit is a second read on how investors rate it. The Bloomberg report and any bank or Oracle statement are the primary sources to check; until one appears, the price and the Oracle link rest on anonymous sourcing.
