UK's approach to China is 'incoherent' and lacks expertise, report finds

In one sentence
A report calls Whitehall's China policy incoherent and short on expertise while trade and security policy are under review; its author and detailed evidence could not be checked.

Britain's approach to China is "incoherent" and short on expertise, according to a new report that criticises the government's capability in Whitehall. It lands while ministers are reviewing both trade and security policy towards Beijing, which gives the finding more weight than a critique of this kind would usually carry.

The account of the report that has reached this newsroom is thin. The headline finding and the target of the criticism, Whitehall's China capability, are clear. The author, the evidence base and the specific recommendations are not, and readers should weigh the conclusions with that in mind.

What the criticism says

The two charges, incoherence and a lack of expertise, are separate problems and call for different remedies.

Incoherence is a problem of structure. China policy touches the Treasury, the Foreign Office, the business and trade departments, the security services and the Home Office. If each pursues its own priorities without a settled overall position, the result is the pattern critics usually describe: a welcome for investment in one department and a security warning from another, with no one owning the trade-off.

A lack of expertise is a problem of people. It usually means too few officials with Mandarin, deep regional knowledge or long postings. It can also mean that those with such knowledge rotate out of the relevant jobs before their experience pays off. Both can be fixed, but only slowly and at some cost.

Why the timing matters

The review gives the report its relevance. A policy under active review is a policy that can still be changed. That cuts two ways.

One reading is that the review is the best chance in years to set up a single coordinating structure and build a deeper pool of specialists. The other is that a review conducted without that expertise could simply lock in the existing muddle, producing a fresh document that papers over the divisions beneath it.

The report's own evidence will decide which reading is more persuasive. Without the underlying text, it is not yet possible to say whether it identifies specific gaps or offers a general complaint. Nor is it clear whether the government has responded.

Who is affected

The stakes are practical. Companies exporting to or sourcing from China, universities with Chinese partnerships and those responsible for critical infrastructure all plan around what Britain's position will be. An inconsistent policy raises their uncertainty, and a policy made without deep knowledge of how Chinese firms and institutions actually operate risks misjudging both the opportunities and the threats.

The security side of the ledger is the harder one to read from outside. Decisions about which technologies and investments are acceptable depend on informed assessment, and the report's central complaint is that this is where Whitehall is weakest.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the criticism changes anything: whether the government publishes a formal response, whether the outcome of the trade and security reviews includes a named lead or unit responsible for China policy, and whether any commitment to recruit or retain regional specialists appears. The absence of all three would suggest the review is the moment the problem gets locked in rather than fixed.
