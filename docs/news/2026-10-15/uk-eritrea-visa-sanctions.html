UK threatens visa sanctions on Eritrea over refusal to take back migrants

In one sentence
The UK government is weighing visa sanctions on Eritrea for refusing to take back its nationals, extending returns-linked pressure to a major asylum-origin state.

The British government is considering visa sanctions against Eritrea because Asmara will not accept the return of its own nationals, according to a BBC Politics report. No penalty has been announced. The reported step would take a tool ministers have used to press other governments on returns and apply it to one of the countries that sends asylum seekers to Britain.

The principle is simple. A state that will not take back citizens who have no right to stay abroad leaves the removing country with few options. Visa penalties are one of them: Britain can slow down, restrict or suspend visas for a country's officials, businesspeople or other applicants until cooperation improves. The aim is to give that government a reason to issue travel documents and accept flights.

An unusual target

Eritrea is a harder case than most. The countries Britain has pressed on returns have generally been ones with large numbers of visa applicants, such as students, workers and visitors, so a restriction costs them something. Eritrea is a closed, tightly controlled state with little legitimate travel to Britain. It is also at odds with much of the West, which limits how much a visa lever can bite.

Eritrean asylum claims have also been treated differently from most. Indefinite national service, and the repression that UN human rights investigators have long documented, have made Eritreans among the nationalities most likely to be recognised as refugees in Britain. Many Eritreans in the asylum system are therefore not candidates for removal at all. The sanction would bear on a narrower group: those whose claims fail, or who have no right to remain for other reasons, and whom Eritrea will not receive.

The report's details, including how many Eritrean nationals are awaiting removal and what the Home Office has said to Asmara, could not be independently checked. Home Office and ONS figures on Eritrean claims and removals would be needed to judge how large the practical problem is. The legal basis for any penalty should be confirmed against the statute rather than the summary circulated so far.

What it would signal

The significance is partly diplomatic. If ministers go ahead, it would show returns leverage being extended from places with substantial visa traffic to a major source of asylum seekers, where the sanction mostly works as a statement of intent. That has consequences for Britain's wider migration diplomacy in Africa, where governments weigh cooperation on returns against domestic politics and against what London offers in aid, trade and visas.

There is also a humanitarian objection. Because Eritrean refugee status has so often been upheld, critics are likely to argue that pressing Asmara to accept returns sits uneasily with Britain's own assessment of conditions there. Ministers would likely answer that returns policy applies to people found not to need protection, and that a government's refusal to cooperate should not be rewarded.

What to watch

The test is whether the Home Office confirms the plan and sets out the form of any penalty, which would show whether this is a formal sanction or a warning aimed at securing talks. A statement from Asmara, or evidence that it has started issuing documents, would indicate whether the threat has had any effect.
