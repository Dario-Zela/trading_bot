Kelly Clarkson in row with Brooklyn novelty shop over a mechanical bird

In one sentence
Kelly Clarkson and Brooklyn shop Art of Play are trading competing accounts over a mechanical bird the store says was never for sale, and fan reviews have swamped the shop.

A Brooklyn shop that sells games, puzzles and gifts is at the centre of an online row with the singer and talk-show host Kelly Clarkson. The subject is a mechanical bird. Each side has told the story differently, and the shop has said it is trying to put things right.

What each side says

The shop is Art of Play. According to US local-news reports of the dispute, Clarkson went in wanting to buy a mechanical bird. She then complained on X that she had felt judged on arrival and had been told a comparable item could be found for her. She also told her followers to stay away from the store.

The shop's account is more specific. It says she asked for "Molly", a singing and dancing bird automaton that features in its magic shows. Molly, the store says, is fixed to the wall and is not for sale. Staff offered an alternative instead: a different singing-bird automaton from a German clockmaking workshop, priced at around $2,000. The store says Clarkson left before she saw it.

Some headlines put a $15,000 price tag on the affair. The reporting reviewed here does not say clearly which object that figure refers to, so it is left out of this account.

The pile-on

The consequences came quickly. Clarkson's fans left a wave of negative reviews on the shop's pages, which the store described as a "surreal experience". For a small independent retailer, a flood of one-star ratings from people who have never set foot in the building can matter more than a single unhappy customer, because those ratings are what a passing shopper sees first.

The store says it has since contacted Clarkson to resolve what it calls a misunderstanding. At the time of the latest report she had not responded publicly.

Why it travelled

The dispute has the ingredients of a good viral story: a famous name, a whimsical object, a small business, and two accounts that cannot both be complete. Nothing here touches a market, a policy or an industry. The one real question is whether a celebrity's post, aimed at a single shop, is enough to swamp its public reputation.

The next development is Clarkson's own. A reply, or a visit to see the German automaton, would settle the matter. Silence leaves the shop with the reviews.

This account rests on secondary reports of the exchange. The original posts were not reviewed, and neither party's statements have been independently confirmed.
