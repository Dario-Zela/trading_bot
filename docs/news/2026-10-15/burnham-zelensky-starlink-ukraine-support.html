Burnham promises Kyiv UK support 'will not change' as Zelensky voices frustration with Washington over Starlink

In one sentence
Burnham told Zelensky in London that UK backing 'will not change', as Kyiv accused Washington-linked Starlink limits of hobbling its drones and Zelensky said he felt betrayed by Trump.

Britain's new prime minister told Volodymyr Zelensky on Wednesday that London's backing for Ukraine "will not change", a public assurance that arrived at a moment when Kyiv's relationship with Washington has visibly frayed. Zelensky was in London for talks with Andy Burnham and an audience with King Charles. He arrived after a night of heavy Russian attacks and after Ukraine's accusation, hours earlier, that Elon Musk's SpaceX had crippled its drones by limiting Starlink services.

The reassurance was explicit. "We are behind you and that will not change," Burnham said, according to the reporting from the visit. He added that he would try to "repair bridges" between Donald Trump and Zelensky, which is an unusual job for a British prime minister to announce in public.

A visit staged as a signal

The timing explains the tone. Trump said at the weekend that Zelensky should be replaced as Ukraine's leader, according to Reuters. That came days after Trump struck a deal with Moscow to lift sanctions on Russian diesel exports, and after he announced an "energy ceasefire" that, by one account, blindsided both sides in the war. Zelensky has said he felt betrayed by Trump over the energy ceasefire and the diesel deal. He has also said Ukraine still counts on "partnership with the United States".

Britain does not support the diesel arrangement and has urged allies to keep economic pressure on Moscow. In a statement Burnham said Britain was with Ukraine "for as long as it takes" and would "go further and faster in our support". He said backing Kyiv was vital to keeping people in Britain safe, and he asked officials to draw up further sanctions, military aid and fiscal support. His foreign and finance ministers are lobbying allies to follow Britain in banning maritime services that support Russian liquefied natural gas exports.

The concrete offer was modest. Burnham announced a planned delivery of £45m of surface-to-air missiles, used against jet-powered Russian drones, with £10m of it fast-tracked to meet an urgent need. That is useful against the drone-and-missile raids Ukraine is absorbing nightly, but it is small beside what the United States has provided. The value of the visit lay less in the money than in the public commitment, and in the fact that it was made in front of cameras with the king alongside.

This is a familiar pattern. London hosted Zelensky after his Oval Office clash with Trump and JD Vance in February 2025, and European capitals have since been pushed repeatedly into reassuring Kyiv in public. What is new is the instruction implicit in the setting. When Washington's position is unclear, European governments are expected to state theirs plainly, so that Ukraine, its enemy and the other allies hear it.

Starlink as a lever

The Starlink dispute shows how far Ukraine's war effort depends on decisions made elsewhere. Ukraine says recent changes to the system slowed the battlefield communications used to strike Russian-occupied territory and to operate interceptor drones. Zelensky called the decision "a big mistake" and said "of course people will die". He said he hoped it was a technical fault and asked for service to be switched back on. He did not name who was responsible.

The account of who is responsible is contested. A senior US official said the administration had nothing to do with the limits, and US officials blamed a technical malfunction. Ukrainian officials suspect a warning shot from the Trump administration, and defence officials suggested Washington was behind slower speeds on some terminals. Service was partially restored on Wednesday, but Kyiv was still seeking answers. A senior Kyiv official also said Washington had threatened to stop sharing intelligence on incoming Russian missile launches, which Trump denied.

Musk has added to the confusion. Under a headline in the Washington Times, he said Trump has the final word on Starlink restrictions in Ukraine. The triage material for this piece also attributed to him a claim that the limits on Starshield, SpaceX's military-oriented product, including drone-speed limits, are set by the US military and ultimately the president. That formulation could not be confirmed in the reporting reviewed here, so it is best read as Musk's own account of where authority lies. Separately, he wrote on X that "Zelensky has gone too far" over strikes on Russian fuel facilities.

The argument over responsibility matters in itself. If a private company controls the terminals, the dispute is commercial. If a presidential decision sits behind them, a satellite service that Ukraine's drones rely on has become a diplomatic lever. Kyiv has been here before. Reuters reported in 2025 that the US had threatened to cut off Starlink unless Ukraine signed a minerals deal. In August this year, Zelensky was reported to have asked Trump to press Musk to allow Starlink guidance for drone strikes inside Russia, with Starshield floated as a costlier and harder-to-integrate fallback.

What the pledge guarantees

Burnham's assurance is a statement of continuity, not a change of policy. Britain's line on Ukraine has not shifted. What has shifted is the audience. A pledge once aimed at Moscow is now also addressed to Kyiv, as insurance against a United States that may not deliver.

It has limits. Britain cannot replace American intelligence sharing, and it does not control Starlink. It can sanction, supply missiles and lean on partners, and Burnham has committed to all three. Whether European governments will follow London on the LNG shipping-services ban is the next measurable test. Another is whether Starlink service is fully restored and whether Washington explains the throttling. A third is whether Burnham's offer to repair bridges with Trump produces any contact between the two presidents. Until it does, the guarantees on offer to Ukraine come from Europe.
