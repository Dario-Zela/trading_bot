ASML lifts its sales floor and says it is sold out to 2028, but the shares fall

In one sentence
ASML reported strong Q3 results, said it is effectively sold out for 2028 and asked suppliers for 30% more output a year, yet its shares fell on fears the AI cycle is peaking.

ASML, the Dutch company that makes the machines used to print the most advanced computer chips, reported strong third-quarter results this week. It also said it is effectively sold out for 2028 and is asking its suppliers to raise output by about 30% a year. Its shares fell anyway.

The reaction is the story. A company with no spare capacity three years out, and with enough pricing power to be mentioned in the same breath as its results, would ordinarily be rewarded. That the market sold instead suggests investors are asking how long the order book can stay this full, not whether it is full.

The figures below come from Benzinga's coverage and from analyst commentary attributed to Cantor. They have not been independently verified against ASML's own filings, and the size of the share move is not given here for the same reason.

Why a lithography maker sets the pace

ASML sits at an unusual point in the chip supply chain. It builds the lithography systems that etch circuit patterns onto silicon wafers, and for the most advanced chips, including the processors that train and run large AI models, its extreme-ultraviolet machines have no commercial substitute. Chipmakers can design as many AI accelerators as they like, but they can only manufacture as many as their tools allow.

That makes ASML's order book a useful proxy for the AI capital-spending cycle. Its customers are the foundries and memory makers that turn out AI hardware, and they commit to tools years before the chips ship. A book that is effectively full through 2028 means those customers are planning capacity well beyond the current budget year.

It also makes the company's own constraint worth noticing. ASML does not make everything it sells. It depends on a network of specialist suppliers for optics, light sources, precision mechanics and other components. A request for roughly 30% more output a year from that network is a demanding ask for firms whose products are built to tolerances measured in atoms. It indicates that the shortage has moved upstream, from chips to the tools that make chips, and now to the parts that go into the tools.

What the numbers say, and what they do not

Three points from the reporting carry the argument:

  • Strong Q3 results and pricing power. Benzinga and Cantor both cite pricing power, meaning ASML can hold or raise prices because buyers have few alternatives.
  • Effectively sold out for 2028. The qualifier matters. "Effectively" suggests that remaining capacity is spoken for or too small to matter, not that every slot has a signed purchase order.
  • A 30% annual supplier ask. This is a request, not a delivered result. Whether suppliers can meet it is untested.

The sold-out claim is the more bullish of the two for revenue visibility. The supplier ask is more ambiguous. It signals confidence in demand, but it also shows that ASML's growth is limited by what its supply chain can produce. In a market already nervous about timing, both facts can be read as reasons the good news is already in the price.

Why the shares fell

The market's reasoning is not stated by ASML, so any explanation is inference. The evidence points toward a peak-cycle reading. When a supplier of critical equipment says demand is covered several years out, investors may conclude that the steep part of the growth curve is behind it. A fully booked 2028 can look like a ceiling as much as a floor, because a company with no spare capacity cannot grow faster than its own output allows.

There is also the matter of who pays for the machines. ASML's customers are spending heavily on AI infrastructure, and that spending is being questioned elsewhere in this edition: a Senate inquiry into Meta's data-centre tax breaks, and Michael Dell's warning that the memory shortage could last four years. A long order book is only as sound as the budgets behind it. If the AI buildout slows, orders placed for 2028 are the kind that could be pushed back, not cancelled outright, and that is the risk a falling share price may be pricing.

The broader backdrop is also unhelpful. A hot September inflation print has pushed markets toward an October Fed rate rise, and the Nasdaq 100 dropped 1% on the day. Long-duration growth stocks tend to suffer when rate expectations climb, whatever their order books say.

What to watch

Three things will test the sold-out narrative. The first is ASML's full-year guidance and its next quarterly order intake, which will show whether bookings keep pace with the 2028 claim. The second is whether its suppliers publicly commit to the 30% annual increase or push back on it. The third is the capital-spending guidance from ASML's largest customers in their own earnings reports, since those budgets ultimately fund the machines. If those hold up, the share fall will look like a pause in a long cycle. If they soften, ASML's order book will be the clearest place to see it.
