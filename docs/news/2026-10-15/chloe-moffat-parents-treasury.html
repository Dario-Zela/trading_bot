Parents of Chloe Moffat want Treasury staff held to account over her death

In one sentence
A coroner found the Treasury materially contributed to Chloe Moffat's death by not following its disciplinary policy; her parents now want staff held to account.

The parents of Chloe Moffat, a 26-year-old Treasury employee who died the day after a disciplinary-style meeting at work, are calling for staff at the department to be held to account. The demand, reported by the Guardian, follows an inquest in which a coroner found that HM Treasury "materially contributed" to her death. The record on the inquest is clear. The detail of what the family now wants from individual officials is thinner, and this article does not claim more than the sources support.

What the inquest found

Moffat was a personal assistant to a director at the Treasury. She died by suicide on 20 May 2025. The day before, she attended what she believed was a routine one-to-one with her line manager. A more senior manager was also in the room. She was told of allegations that she had shared sensitive personal information about colleagues, which she denied.

She asked whether she would lose her job. The senior manager replied that the outcome could not be predicted. Her access to the director's email inbox was temporarily removed. She was allowed to go home, and she said she feared being fired.

Assistant coroner Anna Crawford, sitting at Surrey Coroner's Court, recorded a conclusion of suicide. She found that the Treasury contributed materially to the death because it did not apply its own written disciplinary policy. That policy required Moffat to receive a letter setting out the allegations. It would also have told her that, if they were proven, the likely penalty was a written warning rather than dismissal. The allegations had first been raised on 8 May. Everyone agreed Moffat enjoyed her job and had a good record, with no earlier disciplinary history.

This is the documented failing that the headline alone does not supply. It is a departure from a written procedure, found by a coroner, and it is specific.

Process or people?

The finding is about a process that was not followed. It does not name any individual as culpable. The Treasury's account is that the meeting was an early fact-finding exercise, not a formal disciplinary stage. Moffat's line manager told the inquest: "No, I think we did all we could at the time with the information and advice we were given." The senior manager described the written policy as "high level."

The coroner also said such informal fact-finding meetings are standard practice across the Treasury and other departments, and called that a matter of concern. That sits awkwardly with a demand to single out staff. If the practice is common, the failing is arguably institutional. It would then be a question for the department's leadership and the wider civil service, not only for the two managers in the room.

In statements around the inquest, the family concentrated on reform. They said the Treasury failed in its duty of care and left Moffat "frightened and isolated" when she needed support. They said they would "seek the changes needed to ensure that no other family suffers a similar loss." One account has Moffat's father saying the Treasury's description of the meeting "beggars belief." I could not verify the newer call for accountability beyond the Guardian's headline. I found no detail on what form it would take, such as disciplinary action, a review or legal proceedings. One lower-quality aggregator reported the parents were weighing a lawsuit. I found no confirmation from a primary source.

The Treasury's response

A spokesperson said the department was "considering the findings carefully" and had already improved its guidance, support and wellbeing arrangements. Reports say it is piloting new advice for staff handling disciplinary matters and giving managers extra training.

What to watch

Crawford will send a prevention of future deaths report to the Treasury, the head of the civil service and Acas. Recipients of such reports are normally obliged to respond in writing, within 56 days. That reply will show whether the Treasury treats this as a flaw in procedure or as a question about individual conduct. It will also show whether the changes extend beyond one department, given the coroner's remark that the practice is widespread. The family's next step, and whether any named official faces a review, will decide if this remains a case about institutional failings or becomes a dispute over personal responsibility.
