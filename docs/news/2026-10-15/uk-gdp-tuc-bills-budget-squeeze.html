Thin August growth and a TUC bills tally leave Healey little room before the Budget

In one sentence
The UK grew just 0.1% in August after two strong months, so Healey's room for manoeuvre will depend on the OBR forecast on 28 October, not this one print.

The UK economy grew by 0.1% in August, according to the Office for National Statistics. That is a modest figure in itself, but it lands 13 days before John Healey's first Budget as Chancellor, due on 28 October. Healey took the job on 20 July, when Andy Burnham succeeded Keir Starmer and Rachel Reeves left the Treasury.

The print is less alarming than the headline suggests. It follows two strong months, and a pause after a run of gains is not a recession signal. Its significance is political and fiscal, and it arrives alongside a union campaign that frames the Budget around household bills.

What the ONS reported

The ONS's August bulletin, as retrieved for this article, shows the following:

  • Monthly growth: 0.1% in August, after July's 0.4%. June was revised up to 0.4%.
  • Production: up 0.8% in the month, the main driver of growth.
  • Services: flat. Services are the largest part of the economy.
  • Construction: down 0.2%, the fourth monthly fall in a row.
  • Three-month view: GDP was 0.7% higher in the three months to August than in the three months to May. That is the eighth consecutive three-month rise. Output was 1.8% higher than a year earlier.

So the soft August number sits on top of a firm run. The ONS also attributed revisions to the July detail, including a lower construction figure, to "updated source data". Monthly GDP is a noisy series, and the revisions show it. A single 0.1% reading rarely changes a fiscal forecast by itself.

Healey has been able to point to the earlier strength. After the April–June quarterly estimate in August, he said the UK had seen the "fastest growth in the G7 this year". The August print gives that line less support, though the three-month and annual figures still back it.

Where the pressure comes from

The constraint on Healey is the margin between the government's spending and tax plans and its fiscal rules, not last month's growth rate. Headroom is set by the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecast, which depends on the medium-term growth outlook, interest rates and borrowing costs. A flat month for services does not move any of these on its own.

What matters is whether the OBR, preparing its pre-Budget forecast, reads the August data as a pause or as a sign of weaker momentum. Neither the OBR nor the Treasury has commented publicly on the August figures in anything retrieved for this article. Any claim about headroom shrinking or growing is therefore inference for now, not reporting.

The bills argument

The second thread is political. The TUC has published a cost-of-living analysis, reported by the Guardian, saying UK households have paid an extra £13,961 on bills since 2021. The basis of the figure, whether a per-household average or a cumulative total, and the bills it covers could not be confirmed. A search of the TUC's recent published material turned up a much smaller figure: a May 2026 analysis found energy payments up £437 a year on May 2021, about £2,500 in total. The two numbers may measure different things, and the larger one should be treated as the TUC's claim until its method is checked.

The campaign matters for the Budget because it pushes the debate toward cost-of-living relief, which costs money. It also turns weak growth into an argument for support as well as caution. That is the squeeze: slack growth reduces the room to pay for relief, while pressure on bills increases demand for it.

The link to markets runs through inflation and rates. Hot US inflation has pushed investors toward expecting a Federal Reserve hike (see our report on the September CPI). Higher global yields would raise UK borrowing costs and cut into headroom regardless of what the UK data show.

What to watch

Three things will settle how tight Healey's position is. The first is the OBR's forecast, published alongside the Budget on 28 October, where any downgrade to medium-term growth would cut headroom directly. The second is gilt yields in the run-up, which show how much room markets think the new Chancellor has. The third is the September GDP release, which will show whether services stayed flat or the August print was a one-month pause. Until then the August number is a caution flag, not a verdict.
