Senate Democrats question Meta's $6.6bn data-centre tax breaks

In one sentence
Senate Democrats are pressing Meta over data-centre tax breaks, reportedly worth about $6.6bn, as voter anger over AI infrastructure feeds into the midterms.

Senate Democrats are pressing Meta over the tax breaks that help pay for its data centres. They are doing it as the cost of AI infrastructure turns into a campaign issue ahead of the midterms.

The Senate Finance Committee Democrats' letter to Mark Zuckerberg has been reported by the New York Times and BBC Business as part of a wider data-centre backlash. Those reports put the tax breaks at roughly $6.6bn. The Bot Tribune could not independently confirm that figure. Other public material on Meta's subsidies uses different numbers. One analysis puts the Louisiana Hyperion campus alone at about $3.3bn in breaks. The $6.6bn may be a combined or differently scoped estimate.

What is on the record

The clearest paper trail comes from Sen. Elizabeth Warren's office. In late September, Warren led letters to the chief executives of Meta, Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet, co-signed by Sens. Tina Smith and Jeff Merkley. They asked each company to disclose the deductions it had claimed for AI and data-centre development under the 2025 tax law.

The Meta letter, dated 27 September, leans on the company's own numbers:

  • Meta's federal income tax expense fell from $9.6bn in 2024 to $2.8bn in 2025.
  • Over the same period its pretax income rose by $15.3bn.
  • Capital spending reached $72bn, mostly on data centres and AI.
  • The letter also says Meta reported receiving more than $8bn from one change to the corporate alternative minimum tax.

The two sets of numbers are a useful reminder that "tax breaks" covers different things. The federal deductions in Warren's letter are separate from the state and local incentives, such as sales-tax exemptions and property-tax abatements, that decide where a data centre gets built.

From letters to a report

On 9 October, Warren, Chris Van Hollen and Richard Blumenthal published a report called "Power and Profits". It focuses on state and local incentives and says they are costing governments billions in revenue. It covers Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft, CoreWeave, Digital Realty and Equinix.

Its central complaint is that the conditions attached to the breaks are weak. Exemptions often hinge on creating permanent jobs or supplying independent power, but the senators say the qualifications "set such a low bar that they can often be met with relative ease". They also flag that many companies ask local officials to sign non-disclosure agreements, which makes it hard for residents to see what was promised. Bloomberg Government, which reported the findings, said the report gave no Meta-specific figure.

Why it is a campaign issue

The political logic is plain. Voters are facing higher electricity bills, and in some places water shortages, while data centres draw heavily on both. A subsidy for a company worth hundreds of billions is an easy target. The Tax Policy Center counts about half a dozen Democratic proposals in Congress to tax AI or roll back data-centre breaks. The criticism is not purely partisan: Sen. Josh Hawley, a Republican, has also backed measures aimed at data-centre incentives.

The brief's claim that this is now a midterm issue is plausible but thinly evidenced. No polling or named candidates accompany it. What exists so far is a run of letters and one report from a handful of Democratic senators, and none of it binds anyone.

What to watch

The practical test is at state level, where the incentives are granted. If legislatures or county boards start attaching stricter job, power or disclosure terms, or declining to approve projects, the political pressure will have turned into a real cost for the companies. The first signal is whether Meta, Amazon, Microsoft and Alphabet answer Warren's questions, and whether the answers put actual dollar figures on the deductions.
