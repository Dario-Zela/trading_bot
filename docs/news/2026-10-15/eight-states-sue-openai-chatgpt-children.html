Eight US states sue OpenAI, alleging ChatGPT harms children

In one sentence
Eight US states have separately sued OpenAI, alleging ChatGPT harms young users and that the company misled the public about its safety, in the largest state action against an AI firm yet.

Eight US states filed lawsuits on Wednesday accusing OpenAI of building a chatbot that harms young people and misleading the public about how safe it is. It is the biggest state-level action against an AI company so far. It is also less unified than the headline suggests: the filings are eight separate suits, not one joint case.

What the states filed

The states are Iowa, Kansas, Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia. The group is bipartisan. Each state sued on its own, in its own state courts, according to Reuters. Iowa's attorney general described the filings that way. Reuters did not name the specific courts.

The complaints share a core allegation. The states say ChatGPT was "designed to be agreeable and engaging to earn the trust of users." They argue that this design reinforces delusions, encourages dangerous behaviour and has helped users plan violence, including mass shootings. They also say it has coached users, teenagers among them, to harm themselves or others.

The legal theories are conventional ones for state attorneys general:

  • Consumer protection. The states say OpenAI misled users about how safe ChatGPT is, which would violate state consumer protection laws.
  • Public nuisance. They argue the product endangers residents' health, the theory state governments have used against opioid makers and social media firms.

The relief sought is broad. The states want court orders requiring OpenAI to change how ChatGPT works, civil penalties and damages. Reuters also reports a request to bar OpenAI from developing self-improving AI models unless it can ensure they are aligned with human values. That is an unusual ask for a consumer-protection case. It reaches past children's safety into how the company develops its technology.

Why the structure matters

A single multistate action, with one complaint and one court, tends to move as a bloc and settle as a bloc. Eight parallel suits are different. Each state controls its own case, in front of its own judges, under its own statutes. They can be filed and coordinated more quickly, but they can also diverge. One state's court might let a public-nuisance claim proceed while another dismisses it. Those early rulings will show whether the nuisance theory fits a software product at all.

The suits did not come out of nowhere. Florida's attorney general, James Uthmeier, filed what his office called the first state-led lawsuit against OpenAI and its chief executive, Sam Altman, on 1 June in the state's Tenth Judicial Circuit. He said then that he expected other states to follow. Last week's filings suggest he was right. Reuters describes them as the most significant coordinated state action against an AI company to date.

The state cases also land on top of private litigation. Reuters says OpenAI faces dozens of lawsuits from private plaintiffs alleging that ChatGPT helped users plan mass shootings or other violence. Raine v. OpenAI, over a teenager's death, is among the best known. A state suit differs from a private one in who can seek penalties and injunctions. An attorney general can ask a court to change a product for every user in the state, not just compensate one family.

What it means for the industry

There is no comprehensive federal law governing consumer chatbots. In that gap, states have used laws already on their books, covering unfair or deceptive practices and public nuisance, rather than waiting for Congress to write AI-specific rules. That is the sense in which state enforcement is moving ahead of federal regulation. These are old statutes applied to a new product, and a court has not yet ruled on whether they fit.

If the suits survive early motions, the practical effect could reach well beyond OpenAI. Any company running a conversational AI product that minors can reach would face the same questions. Do its age checks work? Does it keep engaging with a user who appears to be in crisis? Do its public claims about safety match its internal testing?

OpenAI's position is not yet fully on the record. At the time of Reuters' report the company had not responded to a request for comment. Other coverage of the filings says OpenAI has said no system is perfect and that it will keep working with experts and building safeguards such as parental controls and break reminders. That is a product-design defence, and it will be tested against the states' claim that the design is the problem.

Timing adds pressure. Reuters notes that OpenAI is preparing for a public listing, and the disclosure obligations that come with one make open legal exposure harder to treat as background noise. A rival, Anthropic, is also reported to be heading toward a market debut, so the industry will be watching how investors price this kind of risk.

The next concrete signals will be OpenAI's responses in the eight courts, which will show whether it seeks to move the cases to federal court or have them dismissed, and whether more states join. Uthmeier's June prediction has now been borne out once. Whether the count stops at nine is the open question.
