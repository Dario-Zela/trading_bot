Phase Eight and Hobbs owner hit by cyber-attack as H&M probes a separate hack claim

In one sentence
The owner of Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles confirmed a cyber-attack, while H&M probes a hacker's unverified claim it says it cannot substantiate; no link is established.

Two UK fashion groups are dealing with data-security problems at once. The owner of Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles has confirmed a cyber-attack. H&M is looking into a separate claim by someone who says they have hacked it. The two cases differ a lot in how well they are established, and nothing public yet ties them together.

What has been confirmed

The confirmed case is TFG London, the UK arm of South Africa's The Foschini Group, which owns Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles. It said it identified a cybersecurity incident affecting some of its IT systems on Sunday 11 October. Two days later several systems were still unavailable, and the company was still working out whether any personal or sensitive information had been compromised.

The practical effects so far are on orders. Customers who bought before Sunday were told their purchases would be processed as normal. Orders placed after that could be delayed. The company said it has "no reason to believe payment information is at risk" and advised customers to watch for phishing messages. It has not confirmed that any customer data was exposed.

What is only a claim

The H&M story is weaker. Reports say someone claiming to belong to the ShinyHunters group has said they hold stolen data and threatened to leak it. That is an attacker's assertion, and such claims are sometimes exaggerated or false.

H&M's position is that it has "not found any indications that our data environment has been compromised." It says it is still investigating, and it describes the claim as separate from the Phase Eight and Hobbs incidents. The BBC's technology coverage, which prompted this piece, reports the same.

Is there a pattern?

The sector has been hit repeatedly, and coverage of the latest incidents has lumped the brands together. Lumping is not the same as linking. Retailers hold large customer databases and rely on online ordering, so they are natural targets, and several unrelated attacks could land in the same week without a shared cause.

A shared cause would matter more. A common supplier, cloud platform or software provider would mean other retailers could be exposed too. For now no such link has been confirmed. TFG London has not described how the attackers got in, and H&M has found nothing to examine. Reports of other brands being caught up in the same run have also cited a platform provider, but that provider has denied that a flaw in its own product was responsible.

What to watch

The first question is whether TFG London confirms that customer data was taken, and which kinds. The second is whether H&M's investigation backs up the hacker's claim or the company's denial. If either company names a common technology provider, the story would stop being about individual retailers and become about shared infrastructure. Any such statement, or a data leak that can be checked against company records, would settle it.
