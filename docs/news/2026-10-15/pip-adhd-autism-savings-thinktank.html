Think tank says stopping PIP for ADHD or autism would not bring big welfare savings

In one sentence
The Resolution Foundation says barring people with ADHD, autism or anxiety from PIP would save little, and argues that targeting by reported disability costs could save about £4.4bn a year.

A think tank has warned the government that barring people with ADHD, autism or anxiety from Personal Independence Payment (PIP) would not bring the large savings its advocates expect. The Resolution Foundation, in analysis reported by the Guardian on 14 October, says condition-based exclusions are "crude changes" aimed at unrealistic savings targets. It adds that they would probably make it harder for those affected to work and take part in society.

The intervention comes as ministers weigh how to slow the growth of the disability benefits bill. It also lands in a political climate where the idea of excluding certain conditions has been aired openly.

The numbers behind the argument

PIP is a non-means-tested payment towards the extra costs of long-term illness or disability. Spending in England and Wales has risen 183% in real terms since the benefit was introduced in 2014. Official forecasts put the cost at £28bn this year and £34bn by the end of the decade. Much of the recent growth in claims is linked to mental health conditions, ADHD and autism, particularly among young people.

That pattern explains why those conditions have become the focus of proposals for restriction. The Resolution Foundation's case is that the pattern does not translate into easy savings. Its research director, Lindsay Judge, said that claimants "don't trust" the system and that awards "don't fully reflect the nature of disability today."

A central objection is practical. Most claimants have several conditions at once, often a mix of physical, mental and behavioural ones. Tying eligibility to a diagnostic label is therefore, in the think tank's words, simplistic and impractical: removing a single condition from the list would not remove the person, or their other needs, from the caseload.

An alternative route to savings

The Foundation does not argue that the status quo should stand. It describes PIP as failing on several fronts and calls for a major overhaul, saying spending growth of this kind looks unsustainable and that public trust in the system is low.

Its own estimate is that about £4.4bn a year could be saved through better targeting. The main lever is cash support, which could be removed from an estimated one in six working-age claimants, around 670,000 people, who report no extra costs arising from their disability. A reformed system, it says, should keep cash awards for ongoing costs while also helping disabled people to participate in society.

The distinction matters politically. Targeting by reported costs is a different test from targeting by diagnosis, and the think tank is arguing for the former. It also stresses that any change should be made carefully, since PIP helps many disabled and ill people to work, care for others, study or volunteer.

A political backdrop

Welfare reform has been costly for governments recently. The previous Labour government under Keir Starmer tried to push through £5bn of PIP savings, but the plan collapsed last year after a rebellion by backbench MPs. The current Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, has said renewed reforms would not involve "crude cuts."

Officials are reported to be waiting for the review of PIP led by the welfare minister Stephen Timms, and for the Milburn review of the youth jobs crisis. Excluding under-25s from PIP is reportedly among the options under consideration. The Conservative leader, Kemi Badenoch, has argued that PIP should not be paid to claimants with "mild" conditions that are not disabling.

A Department for Work and Pensions spokesperson said PIP is awarded on the basis of a functional assessment of how a condition affects day-to-day needs. The spokesperson said the benefit is "no longer fit for purpose" and that the Timms Review's final recommendations would guide sustainable reform.

What to watch

The reports do not give a date for the Budget or confirm that a diagnosis-based exclusion is formally on the Treasury's table, so the link between the analysis and the fiscal event is still an inference. The next concrete markers are the publication of the Timms Review's final recommendations and the Milburn review. Whether either leans on conditions, age or reported costs will show which of these competing approaches ministers favour.
