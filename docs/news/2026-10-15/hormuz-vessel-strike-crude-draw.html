STANDING WATCH · HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-10-15 · 3 min read

Hormuz: a vessel is struck near the strait as API reports an 11.1m-barrel crude draw

In one sentence
A large API-reported US crude draw and a projectile strike that sank a container ship near Hormuz landed together, with Brent near $106 and the market weighing both.

A large US crude inventory draw and a projectile strike on a commercial ship near the Strait of Hormuz arrived within a day of each other this week. Oil traders had to price a tighter American stockpile and a more dangerous shipping lane at the same time.

The strike

The GFS Pride, a Singapore-flagged container ship, sank in the Strait of Hormuz on 14 October after being hit by a projectile, according to Reuters. Singapore's maritime authority said the vessel took damage and caught fire before going down. All 21 crew were evacuated without injury. Reports said the vessel was hit by a projectile and sank, but they did not name a responsible party in the material reviewed.

It was not the only loss. The dry bulk carrier Cape Dao also sank near the strait, after a projectile hit it on 23 September. One crew member died. Regional coverage links the attacks to Iran's response to a US blockade of its ports and says they have become more frequent over the past ten days. That attribution comes from secondary reporting and is not an official finding.

The pattern predates this week. UKMTO, the Royal Navy-run maritime security body, reported an unidentified projectile striking a ship leaving the strait on 18 August. Marine Insight reported four projectiles hitting two vessels off Khasab, Oman, in September. Traffic is reported to be in single digits a day, against more than 130 before the war began.

The inventory figure

The American Petroleum Institute reported an 11.1m-barrel draw in US commercial crude stocks for the week ended 9 October. That compares with a draw of about 2m the week before, and analysts had expected a build of roughly 1.9m barrels. Reports described it as the largest draw since late January. Gasoline stocks rose by about 1.1m barrels, distillates fell by about 1.5m, and stocks at Cushing, Oklahoma, rose by 1.82m. No barrels left the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, which stands at 283m.

The API number is an industry estimate, not the official one. The Energy Information Administration's weekly report is the government figure, and it often differs from the API's in size and sometimes in direction. A draw this large is a signal until the EIA confirms it.

Pricing the two together

Prices on Wednesday's settlement moved modestly: December Brent rose 0.35% to $106.06 a barrel, and November WTI gained 1% to $95.09. Those are small moves for two pieces of bullish news. That suggests the market had already built much of the Hormuz risk into the price. A surprise draw adds to a supply story that is already tight.

The two items work through different channels. The inventory figure bears on the balance of barrels in the US, and it fades if the EIA data disagree. The ship sinkings bear on freight rates, war-risk insurance premiums and the willingness of owners to send vessels through a lane that carries a large share of seaborne crude. A confirmed loss of a commercial ship pushes all three up, whatever the weekly stock data say.

The next checks are the EIA's official inventory numbers, which show whether the draw holds up, and the next UKMTO notices, which show whether the attacks continue at their recent pace. Any formal attribution of the GFS Pride strike would also matter for how insurers rate the route.
