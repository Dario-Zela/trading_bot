Burnham rules out a coalition with the Greens after 'Zionism is racism' motion

In one sentence
Burnham told the Commons Labour will not partner with the Greens while their 'Zionism is racism' motion stands, narrowing Labour's options after a hung parliament.

Andy Burnham has told the Commons that Labour will not enter a coalition or partnership with the Green Party while it keeps a conference motion describing Zionism as racism. The prime minister turned a row inside one party into a statement about how the next hung parliament might be settled.

What was said

At Prime Minister's Questions on 14 October, Burnham said he condemned the motion "unequivocally" and called it antisemitic. He said he would not back a partnership with a party that holds that position, according to the BBC's report, which I could only check through reposted copies, and the Shropshire Star. The condition matters. The rule-out is tied to the motion staying in place, not to the Greens as a party for all time.

The condition also marks a shift. Days earlier, speaking in Berlin, Burnham had declined to say what he would do in future. He called the vote a serious error of judgment, said it undermined the two-state solution and urged the Greens to reverse it. Critics read the refusal to rule out a deal as leaving the door open.

The motion and the pressure

The motion passed at the Greens' conference in Brighton by 1,022 votes to 813. It described Zionism as an ideology advocating "an ethnonationalist Jewish state". Leader Zack Polanski has called the wording "imperfect" and said he wanted it amended, but he defends its core claim about racist ideology underpinning the treatment of Palestinians. Some Green figures have already quit over it, including the party's deputy leader in Wales, and former co-leader Adrian Ramsay has not ruled out leaving.

Burnham had been pushed from several directions. Tony Blair urged Labour to reject a coalition with the Greens, saying the vote should serve as a warning. Labour MP Luke Akehurst called for a "firewall". Joshua Gardfield, a Jewish member of Labour's National Executive Committee, said Labour should draw the same line against the Greens that Kemi Badenoch has drawn against Reform. Badenoch described the motion as "the disgraceful antisemitism we saw at the Green Party conference".

Why the arithmetic changes

The practical stakes lie in what a Labour government could do without a majority. Labour's natural partners on the left are few, and the Greens were the most obvious. Polanski has said it would be "silly" to rule out a deal with Labour at the next general election. Burnham has now said he will not make one on current terms. If the motion survives, the routes to a Labour-led minority or coalition narrow to the Liberal Democrats and the nationalist parties, each with their own price.

The position is also untidy at local level. Labour runs more than ten other councils in coalition with the Greens, and a Downing Street spokesman would not say whether the stance applies to them. Without an answer, the line could be tested in town halls long before it is tested at Westminster.

What to watch

The first question is whether the Greens amend or rescind the motion. Polanski has signalled he wants changes, which would hand Burnham a way to soften his position. The second is whether Downing Street says what the rule-out means for the shared councils. A reversal at the Greens' next conference would bring the matter back to Burnham, and so would a hung parliament.
