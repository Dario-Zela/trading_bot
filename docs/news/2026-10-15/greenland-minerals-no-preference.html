Denmark says the Greenland minerals deal gives US investors no preferential treatment

In one sentence
Copenhagen restated that the 22 September US-Denmark-Greenland agreement gives American investors no preferential access and that US exploration must follow Greenlandic law.

Denmark has restated that American companies get no special access to Greenland's minerals and that any US exploration on the island must follow Greenlandic law. The Danish Foreign Ministry made the statement on 14 October. It followed a Truth Social post in which Donald Trump said "Massive Oil, Gas, and Mineral Exploration is getting ready to begin," without giving details.

The ministry's point is that the agreement signed on 22 September does not say what Mr Trump's post implies. The agreement, it said, "does not grant investors from the US any preferential treatment" compared with investors from other NATO countries, EU countries and NATO partners when it comes to access to natural resources in Greenland. The ministry's wording is, in effect, a clarification of terms that already existed. It carries more weight as a statement about who holds regulatory authority than as news about the deal.

What was signed

The 10-page agreement was signed in New York on the margins of the UN General Assembly by Mr Trump, the Danish prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, and Greenland's premier, Jens-Frederik Nielsen. Its substance is military. According to Reuters reporting carried by Al Jazeera, it lets the US expand its activities at Pituffik Space Base and paves the way for two new bases and the "Golden Dome" missile-defence system. It also reaffirms Danish sovereignty and the right of Greenlanders to self-determination.

It grants no mining or drilling rights. That absence is the basis of Copenhagen's position. If the text confers nothing, Washington has nothing to cite when claiming privileged access. The investment provisions that do exist work as a screening mechanism, run by Greenland in close cooperation with Denmark and in consultation with the US. Analysts who have read the text describe it as a way to restrict certain investments rather than a concession to American miners.

The gap between the post and the paper

Mr Trump's post gave no company, site or timetable. Greenland's own rules are also tighter than the post suggests. The territory banned uranium mining and all future oil exploration in 2021. The one oil project in the news is a partial exception. Greenland Energy, a Texas company, has said it plans to drill in 2027 at an untested basin on the east coast. It is seeking to rely on permits that predate the ban, and the Greenlandic government is reviewing whether it can.

Danish analysts urged caution about the US account. Mikkel Olesen of the Danish Institute for International Studies said information from the American side should be taken "with a grain of salt." Richard Weitz of the NATO Defense College judged that Mr Trump had "backed down and settled for less" than he originally sought. Jacob Kaarsbo, a Danish security-policy adviser, was blunter in warning that it would be "extremely dangerous" to assume the agreement had changed Mr Trump's intentions.

A familiar position

None of this is new in substance. Greenland's minerals minister, Naaja Nathanielsen, said in January that the territory would not accept its mineral future being "decided outside Greenland." The 14 October statement applies that line to a signed agreement. Part of its purpose is to stop an ambiguous presidential post from setting expectations among investors.

The statement's weight depends on the agreement text, and on this point the public record favours Copenhagen. The text reportedly contains no resource allocation, and the ministry's reading matches that account. I have not seen the full document independently, so this rests on the ministry's description and on press accounts of the text.

What to watch

The nearest test is the Greenlandic government's review of the Greenland Energy permits. A ruling would show whether pre-2021 licences survive the oil ban, and so whether a US-linked drilling project can proceed under Greenlandic law. Any further licence applications from American firms will also show how far the screening mechanism is used in practice, and whether Washington accepts that it must apply in the same way as it does for other NATO and EU investors.
