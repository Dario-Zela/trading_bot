Russell Brand trial opens with the prosecution calling him a 'sexual predator'

In one sentence
Russell Brand's rape trial opened with prosecutors calling him a ruthless sexual predator, while he denies all seven counts and no evidence has yet been heard.

A jury at Southwark Crown Court in London has heard the prosecution case against Russell Brand, the comedian, actor and former broadcaster who reinvented himself in recent years as a wellness and anti-establishment commentator. Opening the trial on Wednesday, the prosecutor, Riel Karmy-Jones KC, described him as a "ruthless" sexual predator. Brand, who has pleaded not guilty, denies all the allegations. Nothing has yet been tested in evidence. What the jury heard on Wednesday was a barrister's account of what she expects to prove, and the defence has yet to present its case.

The charges

Brand faces seven counts relating to six women: three of rape, three of sexual assault and one of indecent assault. The alleged offences span 1999 to 2009. Press reports of the opening say the prosecution placed them in a hotel room, workplaces, a bar toilet and Brand's home.

The jury was sworn in on Tuesday, 13 October: eight men and four women, with one reserve. The judge, Joel Bennathan, told them not to research the defendant. Six members of the jury panel were dismissed after saying they remembered the media coverage. That caution matters because Brand's public profile is unusually large and unusually polarised. The trial is expected to last about six weeks.

The case traces back to 2023, when a joint investigation by The Sunday Times, The Times and Channel 4's Dispatches published allegations from several women. Brand rejected them from the outset. He has consistently denied wrongdoing and said his relationships in that period were consensual. He was later charged, and the allegations now go before a jury.

The prosecution's framing

According to reports of the opening, Karmy-Jones described conduct over a decade that was "ruthless", "calculating" and "manipulative", and portrayed Brand as a man with a strong appetite for sexual contact. One structural point stood out: the prosecution told jurors the six women did not know one another and came forward independently. That is the standard way a prosecutor addresses the risk of collusion, the suggestion that accounts have been shaped by one another or by publicity.

The prosecutor also addressed why none of the women reported the alleged assaults at the time, citing guilt and shame. Delayed reporting is common in historical sexual offence cases, and juries are routinely given directions about it. The women's own evidence has not yet been heard, and it will be the real test of the prosecution case.

What the jury will weigh

Historical cases of this kind tend to turn on credibility, consistency and the weight of independent accounts, not on documents. The defence is expected to challenge the accounts, and the jury will have to decide each count on its own evidence.

Brand's status complicates this in two directions. His fame and the years of coverage mean jurors may arrive with strong impressions. His later persona, a self-styled critic of elites and of the media, has been part of the public argument around the case. A jury is directed to put all of that aside and judge only what is said in court. Whether it can is the question the trial puts to the system, and the judge's warnings in the first days show the court is alert to it.

What happens next

The complainants are due to give evidence in the coming days and weeks, and each will be cross-examined by the defence. Brand has not yet said whether he will give evidence himself. Until the jury has heard from the women and the defence, the prosecution's characterisation remains an allegation, and a verdict is not expected until the six-week trial reaches its end.
