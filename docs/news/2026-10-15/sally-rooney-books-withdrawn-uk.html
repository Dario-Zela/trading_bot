Sally Rooney books withdrawn in UK over Palestine Action support

In one sentence
Faber has stopped supplying Sally Rooney's books in the UK, citing the Palestine Action ban, in a test of whether proscription chills literary expression.

A novelist who says she supports a banned organisation can no longer be paid for her books in Britain, and her publisher has stopped selling them. Faber, the London house that publishes Sally Rooney, said this week that it will no longer supply her work in the UK in any format. The withdrawal covers Conversations with Friends, Normal People, Beautiful World, Where Are You and Intermezzo. Between them they have sold millions of copies, and the BBC adapted the first two for television.

The decision was not imposed on Rooney. Both sides say it was made with her consent. Rooney said it was taken "with my full agreement". That makes it less a case of a publisher disowning an author than of a legal trap closing on both of them.

The mechanism

The Labour government proscribed Palestine Action under the Terrorism Act 2000 in July 2025, after activists broke into an RAF base in June of that year to protest British military support for Israel's offensive in Gaza. Membership of, or support for, a proscribed group can carry up to 14 years in prison.

In August 2025 Rooney wrote in the Irish Times: "I too support Palestine Action. If this makes me a 'supporter of terror' under U.K. law, so be it." She said she would use proceeds from her novels and television fees to go on backing the group.

That sentence is where the trouble starts. Faber says the ban has created circumstances in which it cannot pass UK earnings on to her, a position it calls "unconscionable" for a publisher. The concern is not the content of the books. It is the money. If royalties flow to an author who has said she will fund a proscribed group, the publisher worries it could be handling funds connected to terrorism offences.

Rooney had flagged the problem before. In a High Court witness statement reported in late 2025, she said it was "almost certain" she could no longer publish or produce new work within the UK while the proscription stood. She has now said she is "wholly prepared to accept the consequences" of her beliefs.

Where the ban stands in court

The legal footing has shifted more than once. In February 2026 the High Court's Divisional Court quashed the proscription, finding flaws in how the government had applied its own policy and a disproportionate interference with expression and assembly. On 15 June the Court of Appeal reversed that, unanimously holding the decision lawful. The five-judge panel was presided over by the Lady Chief Justice. It rejected the description of the group as a non-violent direct-action organisation.

Palestine Action's co-founder Huda Ammori has said she will go to the Supreme Court. NBC reports that lawyers will ask the court to overturn the ban next month. Prosecutions of people charged under the ban are on hold until it rules.

The scale of enforcement gives the literary dispute its context. More than 3,000 people have been arrested since the ban, some of them holding placards supporting the group. More than 1,200 have been charged under the Terrorism Act, and none has yet been convicted. Civil liberties groups argue the ban unjustifiably restricts protest and free speech.

What the case tests

The government's position is that proscription targets an organisation, not opinions. The Rooney episode shows how that line blurs in practice. Nobody has charged her, and no minister has ordered a book off the shelves. Yet a compliance question at a publisher has had the same practical effect as a ban on her work. Her books are gone from the UK market, and the author who says she supports the group is the one who lost out.

Two caveats apply. First, Faber's reading of the law has not been tested in court, and the reports I found carry no government comment on whether supplying her books would in fact be an offence. Second, the strongest version of the chilling-effect argument needs more than one case. It would need other publishers or booksellers to follow, and none has been reported to do so.

The Supreme Court hearing next month is the next catalyst. A ruling that quashes the proscription would dissolve the problem at its root. A ruling that upholds it would leave publishers, and any author who has voiced similar support, working out for themselves where the line sits.
