White House explores adding to the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve via existing fund inflows

In one sentence
Trade-press reports, unconfirmed by any primary source, say the White House is weighing adding to the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve using existing fund inflows, bypassing appropriations.

The White House is reportedly looking at a way to add bitcoin to the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve without asking Congress for money. The reports come from trade press, including Binance's news feed and Benzinga, and rest on unnamed sourcing. No primary government document, named official or on-record quote has surfaced. The Bot Tribune could not independently confirm the claim, and it should be read as a report of deliberation, not a decision.

What is being reported

As relayed by those outlets, the administration is weighing whether existing fund inflows could be routed into bitcoin purchases for the reserve. That mechanism would avoid new appropriations. No dollar figure, funding source or timetable accompanies the reports.

The idea is not new. The reserve was set up by Executive Order 14233 on 6 March 2025 and was capitalised with bitcoin already held through criminal and civil forfeiture, not with purchases. The order barred sales of those coins. It also told Treasury and Commerce to look for "budget-neutral" ways to acquire more, provided they carried no incremental cost to taxpayers.

Why the mechanism matters

On the available evidence, that budget-neutral route had not been used. A May 2026 status review found that the reserve had not bought a single coin, and that no acquisition channel had been put into operation. Analysts have floated several candidates: surpluses in the Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund, repurposed forfeiture-fund balances, and gains from revaluing the government's gold certificates. Senator Cynthia Lummis, the reserve's leading congressional advocate, has said funding could begin without new appropriations, while also describing the legislative effort as a slog.

The distinction is more than accounting. If money that Congress has already assigned to one purpose can be redirected into a new asset by executive choice, the reserve could grow without the budget vote that would normally govern it. Supporters would call that a use of existing authority. Critics would call it a way around the power of the purse. Which description holds depends on the specific fund and its governing statute, and the reports name neither.

There are also unsettled basics. Public estimates of the reserve's holdings range from roughly 200,000 BTC to about 328,000 BTC, and Treasury has not published a consolidated figure. Any purchase programme would be measured against a base that outsiders cannot pin down.

What would confirm it

The story needs a primary source before it carries weight: a Treasury or White House statement, an executive order or memorandum, or a named official on the record. Congressional reaction, particularly from appropriators and from Lummis, would also indicate whether a funding route exists. Until one of those appears, the report is a signal of interest, not evidence of policy.
