Burnham faces questions over two dinners with the Man City chairman and deleted records

In one sentence
Deleted GMCA records reportedly show Burnham had two dinners with Man City's chairman, prompting questions over whether his contacts were fully declared and kept.

Andy Burnham, while Greater Manchester mayor, twice attended hospitality dinners with Khaldoon Al Mubarak, the chairman of Manchester City. The entries recording them have reportedly disappeared from the website of the Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA). That is the substance of a BBC report on 14 October. It has left the prime minister answering questions less about the meals than about whether a public record of them was kept, and for how long.

The account rests on a few points that the reports agree on. The dinners are said to have taken place at matches in 2017 and 2018. The register that listed them is no longer online. A GMCA statement issued on Monday about Burnham's dealings with the club did not mention them. The reports do not give the venues or the cost of the hospitality, and they do not establish why the entries were taken down.

What was disclosed, and what was not

The Monday statement listed an October 2021 formal meeting with Al Mubarak at the government's Global Investment Summit. It also listed an informal conversation in the stands at Vincent Kompany's testimonial in September 2019, and said Burnham had been a guest at home matches "on a small number of occasions". The two dinners were absent.

Burnham's press secretary said his attendance and hospitality had been declared "in the usual way". Asked about the deleted entries, the press secretary said the authority's website was a matter for the GMCA. The reports also say the authority keeps declarations online only for the length of a mayoral term. If that is the policy, the omission and the disappearance may have an administrative explanation. But a statement meant to set out the mayor's dealings with a club would be expected to draw on the full record, not on whatever remains on a live website. The GMCA had not answered the BBC's question about the omission when the reports were published.

The distinction matters. If the dinners were properly declared at the time and later removed under a routine retention rule, the failure is one of transparency in how the record was rebuilt. If they were not properly declared, the problem is different and more serious. Nothing published so far settles which it is.

Why the club's position raises the stakes

The timing sharpens the questions. City have been found guilty on all counts of breaching the Premier League's financial rules, and on most of the charges relating to failure to co-operate with the league's investigation. The club is expected to appeal. Burnham told the Commons on Wednesday that City's owners and directors did not raise the charges when he met them.

That answer addresses what was said in the meetings. It does not address the opposition's wider point: that a serving prime minister has a relationship with the leadership of an Abu Dhabi-owned group, and that the public is entitled to see its full extent. Burnham has said that "all of my dealings" with the owners would be published.

The political response

Louie French, the shadow sports minister, said Burnham "cannot hide behind the GMCA" and should "come clean". Kemi Badenoch has demanded that No 10 release all details of meetings between the prime minister and senior figures at the club.

The Conservatives have an obvious partisan interest, and the dinners are years old. Hospitality at football matches is common among regional political leaders, and nothing in the reporting suggests that favours were sought or given. The sharper issue is procedural. Registers of interests work only if they are complete when published and kept long enough to be checked afterwards. A mayor whose declarations lapse when a term ends, and who then becomes prime minister, exposes a gap between local rules and national scrutiny.

What to watch

The first test is whether Burnham publishes the promised account of his dealings with the owners. The test of that account is whether it includes the 2017 and 2018 dinners, with dates, venues and values, and whether it comes from the original declarations or from a reconstruction. The second is the GMCA's reply on why the entries were removed and when. Further requests for the full record from the opposition benches, and from the Commons, are likely until both are answered.
