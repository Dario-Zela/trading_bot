Government advises every household to stockpile water and food

In one sentence
New resilience guidance asks households to keep bottled water and long-life food at home, as ministers cite cyber attacks, flooding, wildfires and conflict as disruption risks.

Britons are being asked to hold a few days of bottled water and long-life food at home. The request is modest in its detail and large in its implication. Ministers are no longer treating a prolonged loss of power, water or supplies as a remote contingency to be handled by the authorities. They now expect households to cope on their own through the first stretch of one.

The message is being carried by Wes Streeting, the cabinet minister fronting the campaign. Reports differ on his portfolio: the brief for this piece calls him health secretary, while at least one trade outlet describes him as defence secretary. His own phrase is the clearest guide to the tone. Households, he said, should be "alert not alarmed", with "a supply of bottled water indoors" and "some tinned goods". The stated reasons are power cuts, flooding, wildfires and cyber attacks, along with the possibility of conflict.

What is actually being asked

The detail is thin. Coverage of the advice gives no fixed quantities and no official storage period. Press reports describe an aim of getting households through roughly 72 hours without help, but outlets disagree on the length of the checklist, with 11-item and 17-item versions both circulating. The official gov.uk text should be treated as the authority on the number of days and the list, and this article has not been able to confirm either from it.

The items are mundane: bottled water, food that needs no cooking, a manual tin opener, a torch, spare batteries, a phone power bank. Streeting is also reported to want people to keep some cash at home, on the reasoning that online banking could fail along with everything else. The government says no large purchases are needed and that there is no warning of an immediate emergency.

That last point matters. A Cabinet Office announcement on 14 July confirmed that a national resilience campaign would launch later this year. Press reports in late August, citing the Financial Times, said it would encourage households to cope independently in the early stages of a crisis so that emergency services can concentrate on vulnerable communities. In other words, the request is partly about triage. If most homes can manage for three days, responders can focus on the minority who cannot.

Why the state is saying it now

The risks named are a mixed list, and the mix is the story. Floods and wildfires belong to the climate column; the same week's coverage includes a plan to draft military aircraft against wildfires and a study attributing about 50,000 summer deaths in European cities to heat. Cyber attacks and conflict belong to the security column, where reports link the campaign to concern over Russian threats to critical infrastructure and to an Iran-linked attack on a small UK energy facility. Those links come from press accounts, not from a published government assessment, and should be read with that caution.

Other items in the news fit the same pattern. Ministers are weighing emergency powers to protect undersea cables, and a flaw in PHP sent public-service teams scrambling to patch systems. Meanwhile gas supply remains tight and Britain is bracing for high winter costs. None of this proves a crisis is close. It does show why a planner would want a population that can sit out a few bad days.

There is precedent for the approach. A 2024 campaign urged households to keep three days of supplies, and Cold War civil defence leaflets asked far more of the public. What has changed is the pairing of weather and hostile action in one message, and the fact that a senior minister is delivering it.

The questions the guidance leaves open

Two are obvious. The first is what the state guarantees. A request to self-supply implies a threshold below which the government does not promise to act quickly. Officials have not said where it sits, or what households without storage space, spare income or the ability to carry heavy water can expect.

The second is cost. A modest kit is cheap for many families and out of reach for some, and the campaign mentions no help for low-income households. Retail analysts note that suppliers of longer-life products could see higher demand if small reserves become habit, and the government stresses it does not want panic buying.

The next marker is the formal launch of the Cabinet Office campaign, which should publish the official checklist and the number of days. Whether the text says 72 hours, and whether it comes with any commitment from the state in return, will show how seriously Whitehall expects the next shock to arrive.
