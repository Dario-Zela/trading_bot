Billionaire Reform donor was in business with a foundation linked to the Iranian regime

In one sentence
A Guardian investigation says Reform UK donor Sasan Ghandehari was the main investor in a 2015 Iranian telecoms venture with a foundation the US says answers to the supreme leader.

A British-Iranian billionaire who has given millions of pounds to Reform UK was once the main investor in a telecoms venture with a foundation that the United States describes as answerable to Iran's supreme leader, according to a Guardian investigation. The dealings ended a decade ago. Whether they matter for Reform is a different question, and the evidence so far does not say.

The donor is Hossein Ghandehari, who is known as Sasan. He is a 61-year-old Iranian-born businessman with British citizenship. He says he oversees a family trust worth $10bn (£7bn), the HP Trust, with assets in Europe and North America. He has not disclosed where the family's wealth came from. The reporting available to The Bot Tribune comes mainly from secondary accounts of the Guardian's work, and the original article has not been independently reviewed. The details below are attributed accordingly.

The venture

The Guardian's findings rest on a French arbitration ruling covering Ghandehari's business with Iranian state-backed institutions between 2013 and 2016. According to that account, he became the main investor in a project to build a fibre-optic communications network across Iran.

The vehicle was a joint venture called Omran Razavi International Co. In 2015, Ori Group, whose ultimate owner is the HP Trust, acquired 49% of it. The other 51% belonged to Astan Quds Razavi (AQR), a religious foundation based in Mashhad that controls billions of pounds in assets.

AQR was then led by Ayatollah Abbas Vaez Tabasi, who was appointed by Ruhollah Khomeini and ran the foundation from 1979 until his death in 2016. He is described as having had good relations with Ali Khamenei. The venture reportedly collapsed that year, when a hardliner succeeded Vaez Tabasi and Ghandehari's companies were pushed out.

The US Treasury sanctioned AQR in 2021. It called the body a bonyad, a semi-state organisation reporting to the supreme leader, and said it held significant parts of the Iranian economy, including assets taken from activists and religious minorities. The sanctions came five years after the venture ended, so they do not show that the 2015 deal broke any rules at the time.

The donations

Ghandehari came to public attention in the UK in summer 2026, when he gave Reform millions of pounds. He has also been reported as giving Nigel Farage £50,000 for two passes to the World Economic Forum in Davos. Wikipedia separately records that in January 2026 the family trust paid for Farage to attend as a representative.

Ghandehari has said he is moving his tax residence from Switzerland to Britain, and that this will let him contribute "one or two million" to Reform. Donations to UK parties must come from permissible sources, usually people on the UK electoral register or UK-registered entities. The Electoral Commission checks that on reported gifts.

The Iran link adds a separate question. Parties are expected to be satisfied that a donor is who they say they are, but there is no rule against taking money from someone who once did business abroad with a now-sanctioned body. The allegation concerns a past commercial relationship, not money from Iran flowing to Reform. Nothing in the reporting suggests the donations themselves came from Iranian sources.

The response

Ghandehari's lawyers say that the phrase "working with Iran", without context, is misleading. They say Iran's Supreme National Security Council singled him out after 2016, on false charges of working with hostile Western states. They also say the telecoms project was meant to bring political change to Iran through information, and that US and French authorities approved it.

The account of the Guardian's findings carries no response from Reform UK or Farage on the donations or the Iran links. The same account notes that the disclosures do not necessarily show Ghandehari backed the Iranian government's policies.

The political awkwardness is real nonetheless. Farage said earlier this year that Iran was "more of a threat to the UK than Russian President Vladimir Putin". The party's leader has built part of his foreign policy pitch on hostility to Tehran, and the revelation arrives while Western governments are weighing their own options on Iran.

What to watch

Three things will decide how far this goes. The first is whether Reform sets out what vetting it carried out on Ghandehari and when. The second is whether the Electoral Commission, which regulates donations, has anything to say about the gifts. The third is whether further documents emerge about the venture beyond the arbitration ruling, in particular about its approval by US and French authorities, which his lawyers cite but which has not been independently confirmed. Without those, the story remains a question about judgement and due diligence rather than an established case of improper foreign influence.
