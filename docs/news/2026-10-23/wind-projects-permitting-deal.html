Pentagon advances two wind projects as White House courts Democrats on permitting reform

In one sentence
The Pentagon has reportedly advanced wind projects in Maine and South Dakota as the White House courts Democrats on permitting reform, but the report is unconfirmed by a primary source.

The Pentagon has reportedly moved forward on two wind projects, one in Maine and one in South Dakota. The reports come as the White House tries to win Democratic votes for a bipartisan Senate permitting bill. The details rest on thin sourcing. The claim traces to Punchbowl News and was relayed by the aggregator Benzinga. We could not locate the Punchbowl report itself, and no primary source, whether Pentagon, White House or named developer, has confirmed which projects cleared or what, if anything, was promised in return.

Why wind approvals matter to the talks

The Defense Department reviews onshore wind projects for possible interference with military radar and airspace. In May, 55 House Democrats led by Rep. Seth Magaziner said those reviews were stalling "at all stages," leaving nearly 200 projects in limbo. They called it a de facto moratorium.

The backlog has become a bargaining issue in the permitting talks. Democrats walked away from negotiations in December 2025 after the Interior Department paused offshore wind leases. Sens. Sheldon Whitehouse and Martin Heinrich said then that the "illegal attacks on fully permitted renewable energy projects must be reversed" before talks could resume. In June, Whitehouse said the Pentagon's blockade "has to stop" if negotiations were to succeed.

Republicans have also been pressed from their own side. The Senate Armed Services Committee's fiscal 2027 defense authorisation bill carries a provision, pushed by Maine independent Sen. Angus King, to curb delays in the Pentagon's wind reviews. Committee chair Roger Wicker, a Mississippi Republican, said the dispute "needs to be resolved."

What is and is not established

The sequence is plausible. Democrats have made unblocking wind a condition of engagement, and clearing a couple of projects would be a low-cost signal. The Maine and South Dakota choices would also map onto the politics: King is a leading voice on the Senate wind provision, and Majority Leader John Thune is from South Dakota. In August, Thune called permitting reform a rare opportunity for bipartisan action, citing shorter federal reviews and time limits on legal challenges.

But that is inference. Reporting reviewed for this article does not tie the Pentagon's reviews to any South Dakota project, and nothing public shows approvals being formally linked to votes. The headline framing that the administration "eased a blockade" is also unverified. Two clearances against a backlog of roughly 200 would be a gesture at most, not a lifting of the freeze.

What to watch

The first test is whether the Pentagon or the developers name the projects and the dates they were cleared. The second is whether further approvals follow, which would suggest a policy shift rather than a one-off. The third is whether Whitehouse, Heinrich and other Democratic negotiators say the clearances are enough to return to the table. The defence authorisation bill's wind provision gives Congress a second route to the same end, and its fate in conference will show how far either side is willing to go.
