UK to draft in military aircraft to fight wildfires

In one sentence
The PM says the military will supply aircraft for wildfire response, but no fleet, numbers or funding are public, and the MoD ruled out converting its A400Ms in September.

The UK government says the armed forces will supply aircraft to help fight wildfires. The detail behind the announcement is thin, and it sits against a record that points the other way. In September the Ministry of Defence said it had no plans to adapt its A400M Atlas transport fleet for aerial firefighting.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has framed wildfire as a national-capacity problem since August. On 14 August he visited Stourbridge in the West Midlands, where a blaze had destroyed homes. He described Britain as a "tinderbox" and said the country must "face up" to a changing climate. He announced a package of measures. It included a ban on disposable barbecues and plans for emergency financial support for fire services. He also said he had "called in the military" and would commission firefighting aircraft of the kind used in Europe and the US. Reports from the same period said about 100 military personnel were sent to a blaze in South Wales.

The latest line, relayed by BBC Politics, is that the military will provide aircraft. The reporting available does not say which aircraft, how many, from when, or whether this is a new deployment or a standing arrangement. Those gaps matter, because the pieces so far do not fit together neatly.

What the record shows

Two things are being conflated in the public debate. One is commissioning dedicated firefighting aircraft, which would be a procurement decision. The other is lending military aircraft that already exist, which would be a call on the defence budget. Burnham's August language pointed to the first. The new wording points to the second.

The military's most obvious firefighting asset is the Chinook helicopter, which can carry water buckets. Reports in August said the RAF Chinooks and crews equipped for this work were deployed at RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus, not at home. It is understood the deployment could be reviewed if the aircraft were needed elsewhere. One report also said the Scottish government had turned down an offer of Chinooks for a Cairngorms fire. The reports differ on which minister made that offer, so the detail needs checking against a primary source.

The Ministry of Defence set out its position in a parliamentary answer dated 8 September. Responsibility for wildfires rests with civil authorities and emergency services. Military help is available on request under the Military Aid to the Civil Authorities (MACA) arrangements. The ministry also said it had no plans to convert the A400M fleet for firefighting. If the new announcement changes that posture, it has not yet been spelled out.

Why the detail matters

The distinction between MACA support and a standing capability is the substance of the story. MACA is ad hoc. A department is asked, weighs the request against its other commitments, and may or may not have the aircraft free. A standing arrangement would mean fixed numbers, a funding line and crews trained and held ready, probably through the fire season. That would turn wildfire from a fire-service problem into a defence-readiness question. It would also raise a funding question, since a Chinook flying a water-bucket sortie is not flying something else.

There is a second tension. The aircraft best suited to the job are also in demand overseas. Pulling them back from Cyprus, or buying or leasing dedicated firefighting aircraft, would have to be paid for.

What to watch

Three things would settle it. One is a written statement or Ministry of Defence announcement naming the aircraft and numbers. Another is whether the money comes from the defence budget or from the emergency support promised in August. The third is whether the arrangement is in place before the next fire season. Until then, the announcement stands as an intention.
