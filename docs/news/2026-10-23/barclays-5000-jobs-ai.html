Barclays to cut about 5,000 jobs as it leans harder on AI

In one sentence
Barclays says it will cut about 5,000 jobs, around 5% of staff, as it leans on AI and process simplification, though its chief executive credits earlier technology investment for most gains so far.

Barclays said on 22 October that it will cut about 5,000 jobs, roughly 5% of its workforce. It announced the figure alongside third-quarter results and tied it to a push to use artificial intelligence and simpler processes to lower costs. The bank is not in distress. Reports of the results put quarterly profit up 12% at about £2.3bn. Cutting jobs while profits rise is what gives the announcement its weight.

What has been reported

The details below come from secondary accounts of the announcement, chiefly CNBC, Bloomberg and TechRadar. The full texts could not be retrieved in full, so figures should be read as reported rather than confirmed against Barclays' own statement.

  • Size and mix. About 60% of the cuts are said to fall in support functions, including developer roles.
  • Phasing. Bloomberg reports that the 5,000 includes about 1,000 positions already removed in the first half. TechRadar says the remaining roughly 4,000 are expected by the end of the year.
  • Rationale. The bank describes the reductions as part of lowering its cost base to fund investment.

The brief for this story flagged an open question: are the cuts compulsory, phased or achieved through attrition? The reporting so far does not settle it. The 1,000 already gone suggests at least part of the programme has been running quietly through normal turnover and unfilled vacancies. That was also how Barclays described its roughly 5,000 reductions in 2023, when some roles were cut through redundancy and some by not refilling vacancies.

Is it really about AI?

The framing is contested, including by the bank's own chief executive. CS Venkatakrishnan is reported to have credited "deep investments" in technology and process simplification for most of the gains so far. He said AI "may have a more important role to play in the future." TechRadar's headline quotes him calling AI the "icing on the cake", though the article body could not be retrieved to check the context.

Taken together, those remarks point to a more modest claim than "AI replaces 5,000 workers". On this account, the cuts come mainly from years of automation, platform consolidation and process redesign. AI is an accelerant, and increasingly a convenient label.

That matters because the label does work. A bank that attributes job losses to AI signals to investors that the technology is already producing savings. It also invites scrutiny of whether it is, since the dominant figure in the reporting is a headcount number, not a stated saving. No cost target or payback timeline has been confirmed in the sources reviewed.

The role mix is the more telling detail. Support functions and developer roles are where banks have most openly claimed AI productivity gains, through code generation, document handling and operations tooling. If developers are among those going, that is a sign that firms expect the tools to change how many engineers a given amount of work needs.

What the rest of the sector will be asked

Barclays is a FTSE 100 lender with a large UK workforce, so its announcement sets a reference point for peers. The other big UK banks report their own results in the coming weeks and months. Analysts and unions are likely to ask each of them whether similar reductions are planned, and whether AI is being cited as the reason.

The broader market has been testing the same question from the other direction. This week's selloff in Meta on AI spending fears showed investors demanding evidence that heavy AI investment pays back. Banks cutting costs are one place where a return could be shown. Barclays' announcement offers a cost-side answer to the question, though without published savings it is an assertion, not a proof.

Three things would sharpen the picture:

  1. Barclays' detail on the programme. The split between redundancy and attrition, the geographic spread, and any stated savings figure.
  2. Peer disclosures. Whether other UK lenders adopt the same AI language when they report.
  3. Union and regulatory response. Staff representatives are likely to press on consultation and on how many UK roles are affected, which the sources reviewed do not specify.

Until the first of those arrives, the safest reading is that Barclays has announced a sizeable restructuring in which AI is one stated driver among several, and that the sector will now be watched for whether it follows.
