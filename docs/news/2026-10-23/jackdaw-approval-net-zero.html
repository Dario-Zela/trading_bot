Burnham vows UK will still hit net zero despite Jackdaw gasfield approval

In one sentence
The UK has approved the Jackdaw North Sea gasfield while Burnham insists net zero stands, a position that depends on treating an existing licence as different from new drilling.

The government has signed off production at Jackdaw, a long-delayed gasfield in the North Sea, and the prime minister, Andy Burnham, says the decision does not put the UK's net zero target at risk. The two positions can be held together, but only if the line between existing and new North Sea projects holds. Critics say the approval has already blurred it.

What was approved

Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh agreed that the regulator can grant consent for production at Jackdaw, according to reports of the decision. The field lies about 150 miles off the Aberdeenshire coast and is operated by Adura, the joint venture of Shell and Equinor. Gas is expected to start flowing from the end of October.

At peak, the field is expected to supply roughly 6% of UK domestic gas production. Adura puts the project's value at about $2.8bn and says peak output could heat the equivalent of 1.4 million homes. Those figures come from the company and from reports of the government's position. They have not been independently checked.

Jackdaw was first consented in 2022 under the Conservatives. That approval was overturned after a legal challenge. A 2024 Supreme Court ruling held that the emissions from burning the extracted fuel, not just those from drilling, must be assessed before consent is granted. The field went back through the process, and it is the first new North Sea project approved since the Iran war pushed up energy prices. Reports in April suggested Ed Miliband was poised to approve it. His department described that speculation as unfounded.

Burnham's case

Burnham has said he remains committed to net zero by 2050 and to the Labour pledge to issue no new licences for North Sea exploration. In a Guardian article he argued that protecting jobs and strengthening energy security in the short term "does not mean wavering" on the party's commitment to a cleaner future. The wording here is taken from secondary reports and should be checked against the original piece.

The argument rests on a distinction. Jackdaw is an existing licence, awarded years ago, so developing it is not a new licence. The manifesto promise concerned future exploration. The government also points to domestic gas displacing imports, which carry their own emissions, notably from liquefied natural gas. That matters in a tight market. The edition's report on Qatar's search for LNG supply describes a Britain bracing for high winter costs.

Where the tension lies

Critics say the distinction is a technicality. They argue the decision breaks the spirit of the pledge, and that the 2024 ruling means the government must now weigh the emissions from burning the gas it approves. Campaigners, including Greenpeace, opposed the project in 2022 and have continued to do so. The approval will also be read as a signal for Rosebank, the larger undeveloped field whose consent has also been tied up by the same legal test.

The arithmetic is the real test. Net zero is governed by carbon budgets, and the UK's emissions are counted where gas is burned. Domestic gas that replaces imported gas leaves the UK's burn-related total unchanged, so the budgets are met or missed by how fast demand falls. That is a matter of heat pumps, power-sector decarbonisation and industrial demand, not of where the molecules come from. Jackdaw's supporters can say, correctly, that it does not alter that trajectory by itself. Opponents can say, equally correctly, that it adds supply to a system that is meant to be shrinking, and that investors read each such decision as a hint about how firmly the target will be enforced.

There is also a political cost. The same edition carries reports of a summer in which heat killed about 50,000 people in European cities and of the UK drafting in military aircraft to fight wildfires, which keep the climate case in public view as the government leans on the security one.

What to watch

Three things will decide whether the reconciliation holds. First is the emissions assessment published with the consent, and whether it is challenged in court as the 2022 approval was. Second is the fate of Rosebank, which would show whether Jackdaw is an exception or a template. Third is the next progress report on the carbon budgets from the Climate Change Committee, the statutory adviser, which will show whether demand is falling fast enough for the government's claim to stand.
