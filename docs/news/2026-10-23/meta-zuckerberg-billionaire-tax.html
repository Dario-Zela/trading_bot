Meta agrees arrangement to help Zuckerberg cover a possible California billionaire tax

In one sentence
Meta will issue non-voting shares Zuckerberg can sell to fund a possible California wealth tax, in exchange for an eight-year residency pledge; the tax vote is 3 November.

Meta has agreed to a share arrangement that would let Mark Zuckerberg raise cash to pay a California wealth tax, if voters approve one, without giving up voting control of the company. The terms were reported by the BBC and Reuters and appear in a Meta filing. They are more specific than the headline suggests. The company is not writing a cheque. It is creating a new class of stock that Zuckerberg can sell.

What Meta has agreed

Meta plans to issue a new class of non-voting Class C common stock in January 2027. Zuckerberg would receive a block of it and could sell 20% of that block immediately. According to Reuters, that equals about 10% of his overall economic stake. Selling non-voting shares avoids the usual cost of raising cash: Meta's dual-class structure keeps Zuckerberg's control in the high-vote shares, and selling those would dilute it.

In return, Zuckerberg commits to remain a resident of California, or of whichever state hosts Meta's headquarters, for eight years. The filing reportedly carves out exceptions for "material unforeseen circumstances".

A special committee of independent directors recommended the decision unanimously. Per the filing, it said Zuckerberg's presence in California supports close coordination with management and proximity to Meta's Superintelligence Labs. Susan Li, the chief financial officer, described the stock on the third-quarter earnings call as a way of "preserving the voting power of existing Class A and B shareholders". Meta also lists general purposes for the class: raising capital, paying employees and funding acquisitions.

The tax behind it

The trigger is Proposition 40, a ballot initiative that would impose a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of billionaires who were California residents on 1 January 2026. Californians vote on 3 November. If it passes, the tax would fall due in 2027, with payments spread over five years at additional cost. Real estate, pensions and retirement accounts would generally be excluded.

The measure divides Democrats. The state party endorses it; Governor Gavin Newsom opposes it. The Legislative Analyst's Office says some billionaires may leave and puts the lost state income-tax revenue at under $1bn a year, against a wealth tax that could raise tens of billions over several years.

Zuckerberg's net worth is put at close to $250bn by Forbes, other outlets cite figures above $220bn, and his bill has been estimated at more than $11bn. That is not a fixed cash figure, since the value of the shares depends on Meta's price when he sells. He had told Meta he was weighing a move out of the state, and earlier reports linked him to a Florida home purchase.

What is still open

The BBC framing, a company helping its chief executive meet a tax cost, is fair as shorthand but overstates the mechanics. Zuckerberg bears the liability. Meta supplies a more liquid way to fund it and receives a residency pledge. The cost to other shareholders is mostly dilution and governance: a new class, created for one holder's benefit, with a board committee attesting that his address is a corporate asset. Whether that reasoning satisfies investors, and whether the 20% initial sale limit is adequate against a bill of this size, are matters the filing does not settle.

The timetable is short. The vote on 3 November decides whether the arrangement is ever used; the Class C issuance follows in January 2027.
