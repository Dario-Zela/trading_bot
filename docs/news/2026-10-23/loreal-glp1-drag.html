L'Oréal says weight-loss drugs are denting beauty and skincare sales

In one sentence
L'Oréal's CEO says GLP-1 drugs are affecting beauty and skincare spending, yet the group's Q3 sales rose 7.7% adjusted and no figure was put on the effect.

L'Oréal, the world's largest beauty group, has said the spread of GLP-1 weight-loss drugs is affecting beauty and skincare consumption. It made the remark in the same week it reported a third quarter that beat forecasts. Chief executive Nicolas Hieronimus said on the investor call that the rise in use of the drugs is having an impact on beauty consumption, and on skincare in particular. The reported numbers do not show the effect.

A drag that does not show in the accounts

The quarter was strong. The Paris-based group reported third-quarter sales of €11.56bn, up 11.9% on a reported basis. Like-for-like growth, which strips out currency and acquisitions, was 7.2%, or 7.7% adjusted for the effect of an IT systems change. Hieronimus called it "an exceptional third quarter".

The skincare-heavy parts of the group did well. Dermatological Beauty, the division that includes La Roche-Posay and CeraVe, grew 12.2% like-for-like to €1.87bn. L'Oréal Luxe, the premium brands, grew 6.4% to €4.18bn. Consumer Products, the mass-market division that would be most exposed to a broad shift in household spending, grew 4.3%.

By region, Europe grew 9.9% like-for-like. North America, where GLP-1 use is highest, grew only 2.2% (5.6% adjusted), and Latin America fell 1.0%. Those weaker readings are consistent with the CEO's remark. They are not evidence for it, because the release does not attribute any shortfall to the drugs. L'Oréal has not published a figure for the effect, and the sources reviewed contain none.

What the company says it is doing

The company's response is a product one. Reuters-based coverage says L'Oréal is testing products on GLP-1 users in separate studies. It is also promoting products aimed at secondary effects associated with rapid weight loss, such as sagging facial skin. That framing treats the drugs as a new customer segment as much as a threat.

The drugs in question are the GLP-1 class, including semaglutide (sold as Ozempic and Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro and Zepbound). Their appetite-suppressing effect has already led food and beverage groups to talk about changed basket sizes. A beauty group naming them as a factor extends the argument into discretionary categories. The mechanism is unclear. Users may spend less, or they may redirect spending toward different products.

Reading across

The claim is better treated as a management observation than as a measured trend. A company growing 7.7% adjusted can say the drugs are an influence without showing a loss. The test is whether the North American softness persists, and whether L'Oréal ever quantifies the effect. Retail volumes and the fourth-quarter figures, due with full-year results early next year, are the next points where it could appear. Peers in European consumer stocks reporting this season will show whether the same language spreads.

The market's own verdict was muted: the shares were flat after the release, according to coverage of the earnings call.
