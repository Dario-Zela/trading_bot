Houthi strikes reported on Riyadh and Dammam as oil holds near $111

In one sentence
The Houthis claim a strike on Saudi Arabia's defence ministry in Riyadh, unconfirmed, while a deadly attack on Dammam's airport is confirmed and oil holds near $111.

The Houthis say they have struck Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Defense in the capital, Riyadh. Saudi Arabia has not confirmed that claim. It has confirmed a deadly attack on King Fahd International Airport in Dammam, in the kingdom's Eastern Province. The group has widened its campaign against the kingdom at a time when the wider regional war with Iran has already pushed crude to about $111 a barrel, according to Benzinga's market coverage.

What is claimed, and what is confirmed

Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree said the group carried out four operations over 48 hours using ballistic missiles and drones. He said a precision strike hit the Defense Ministry building in Riyadh. He also claimed hits on Aramco facilities in Yanbu and on airports in Dammam and Abha. Several outlets, including Türkiye Today and UA News, relay the claim. The group says it is the first time it has named the ministry as a target since the current escalation began in July.

So far there is no independent confirmation of damage or casualties at the ministry. A Saudi-led coalition spokesman, Turki al-Maliki, said air defences had intercepted and destroyed four drones over Riyadh and Madinah. He did not name the targets and neither confirmed nor denied a strike on the ministry, Yanbu or Abha.

The Dammam attack is better established. Saudi authorities confirmed two deaths and eight injuries at King Fahd International Airport, and Al Jazeera reported the attack on 22 October. One account adds that two Aramco workers died when a drone was intercepted near the Shaybah oil field, which would put the confirmed toll at four. Sources differ on the figures, and the Saudi and Houthi accounts have not been reconciled.

The original triage report rested on a single Benzinga item. Corroboration since then has come mostly from regional and aggregator outlets relaying the Houthi statement, and from the Saudi acknowledgement on Dammam. The ministry strike should still be read as a claim, not an established fact.

Not the first strike on the capital

The Riyadh claim is not the first time this month that the capital has been hit. A strike on 10 October on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport killed at least 13 people and wounded more than 300, and the Houthis claimed responsibility. A second attack the next day, which the coalition said targeted Terminal 4, caused a fire and injuries. The group has said the kingdom's airports and airspace are "legitimate targets", excepting Mecca and Medina.

The ministry claim therefore reads as a change in the type of target, from civilian aviation infrastructure to a core state institution. It is not a change in geography. The Eastern Province, roughly 400 km from Riyadh and home to much of Saudi oil production, was already in range. The attacks have prompted Washington to order non-essential personnel to leave the kingdom, and Donald Trump has said the US is considering joining Saudi strikes on the Houthis.

The Houthis have long fired at Saudi territory, but the current phase is tied to the war that began on 28 February with US-Israeli strikes on Iran. Houthi-Saudi hostilities have become one of the supply risks on top of the unresolved question of the Strait of Hormuz.

Oil and the politics of the price

Benzinga's coverage puts oil near $111. The reports do not say whether that is Brent or WTI. Brent reached as high as $105.88 on 8 October, so the market has moved higher in the two weeks since, which fits the escalation.

Trump has offered a different explanation. In a Truth Social post, he said Iran was faking attacks and mines to lift prices, and that more crude was moving through Hormuz than before the conflict. That claim can be tested against tanker-tracking and shipping data, and no such data was available for this report. Houthi strikes on Saudi facilities also sit awkwardly with the idea that the disruption is staged. Aramco has reportedly evacuated staff from Dhahran, and the dead at Dammam and Shaybah are on the Saudi side's account.

The politics are plain. The US midterm elections are on 3 November, and polls show voters strongly dissatisfied with fuel prices. On 8 October Trump said the US would not strike Iran before the vote, citing "productive discussions" with Tehran, while keeping the blockade in place. A separate report in this edition says planning for strikes is reportedly timed for after the election.

Markets and what comes next

US index futures for the S&P 500, Dow and Nasdaq 100 fell after a sell-off in Asia, and Benzinga attributed the move to Middle East tensions. Netflix and Super Micro Computer were among stocks in focus. A futures dip on a single morning is a thin basis for reading the market's view of Saudi risk. Oil is the cleaner gauge.

Several things will show whether the escalation holds. The first is whether Riyadh confirms or denies damage at the ministry, and whether satellite imagery or wire reporting settles it. The second is whether Saudi Arabia retaliates directly, and whether Washington joins in. The third is whether strikes reach Eastern Province oil infrastructure beyond Dammam's airport, because Shaybah and the Aramco sites are what would turn a security story into a supply shortfall. The fourth is the Iranian reply to the US response on its seven-day plan to reopen Hormuz. The midterm vote on 3 November sets the political deadline against which Washington will judge all of it.
