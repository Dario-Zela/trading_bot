Committee warns UK's Arctic defence gaps leave it open to Russian attack

In one sentence
MPs on the Commons Defence Committee say shortages of ships and submarines leave UK northern infrastructure exposed to Russia, while the MoD says it is investing and working with allies.

A cross-party group of MPs has concluded that Britain's defences in the far north are not good enough to deter or withstand Russia. The Commons Defence Committee, reported by the BBC, describes "glaring gaps" in the UK's Arctic and High North posture. Coverage of the report is so far available mainly in syndicated copies, so the detail below rests on those accounts and not on the full text.

What the committee found

The central worry is hardware. According to coverage of the report, a shortage of Royal Navy warships and submarines leaves critical High North infrastructure exposed. That includes the undersea cables and energy supplies that run through the northern waters between Britain, Norway and the Atlantic.

The most pointed claim concerns submarines. The Telegraph reported that MPs warned the Astute-class attack boats are not yet certified to operate under Arctic ice, and that the capability was allowed to lapse. If so, the Navy's most capable hunters would be unable to follow Russian vessels into part of the theatre where they matter most.

The committee's chair, Tan Dhesi, said work to close the gaps "cannot start soon enough". He described the High North as a demanding, hostile environment that Russia seeks to dominate.

Funding or strategy?

The findings as reported do not settle the question of whether the shortfall is mainly one of money or of planning. Ship and submarine numbers point to funding and procurement: hulls cannot be conjured quickly, and those in service are tied to other tasks. The lapsed under-ice certification looks more like a strategic choice. Skills fade when a capability is not exercised, and the Navy has spent years focused elsewhere.

There is a precedent. In 2018 the same committee published On Thin Ice: UK Defence in the Arctic. It warned that the UK's ability to defend against threats from the region was at risk from cost savings, lost skills and weak intellectual investment. Eight years on, the diagnosis sounds much the same, which suggests the problem is not new and that neither funding nor strategy has been fixed.

The threat itself has grown. The Ministry of Defence said earlier this year that Russian submarine activity in the North Atlantic is back at Cold War levels.

The government's answer

The MoD said it is "clear-eyed" about the Russian threat and is investing heavily in protecting undersea cables. It added that the Joint Expeditionary Force, the UK-led grouping of northern European states, is already strengthening NATO's northern flank.

That reply points to alliance burden-sharing as part of the answer. It does not directly address the committee's specific complaints about ships, submarines and ice certification.

The report also lands as ministers weigh other protective measures. The government has said it plans emergency powers to protect undersea cables, a sign that seabed infrastructure is already a live concern in Whitehall.

What to watch

The government is expected to respond formally to the committee in due course. Committee reports usually get a written reply within a set period. The test will be whether that reply commits to a timetable for under-ice certification and to extra northern-capable vessels, or restates existing programmes. Without dates and numbers, the gap the MPs describe will stay open.
