Heat killed about 50,000 people in Europe's cities this summer, scientists say

In one sentence
Two Nature Health studies estimate about 50,000 heat deaths in 854 European cities this summer, the highest in a 26-year record and two-thirds linked to climate change.

Heat killed an estimated 50,391 people in 854 European cities between June and August this year, according to two studies published in Nature Health. That would make it the deadliest summer in the 26-year record the researchers built. The figure is a modelled estimate of deaths attributable to heat. It is not a count of death certificates, and almost none of those deaths will have "heat" written on them.

What the study counted

The work was led by researchers at Imperial College London and the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine. It covers 1 June to 31 August 2026. The method is statistical. Researchers take the relationship between daily temperature and deaths in each city, then estimate how many more people died on hot days than would have been expected on days of ordinary temperature. A second step models the same summer in a world without human-driven warming. On that comparison, about two-thirds of the deaths, more than 32,000, are attributed to climate change.

The record claim needs care. Reported comparisons put the previous worst summer in these cities, 2022, at about 29,000 deaths, and 2003 at roughly 18,000. Those are like-for-like figures for the same set of cities.

They should not be set against the better-known 61,600 deaths in 2022 from a 2023 study of 35 countries. That work covered whole countries, not selected cities. The new figure is lower in absolute terms but covers far less ground. The authors say 854 cities account for about 40% of the population of the countries studied.

Who died, and when

Nearly a third of the deaths fell in a single late-June heatwave. Italy, France, Spain, Germany and the UK recorded the most, according to the Science Media Centre.

Death rates were highest among people aged 85 and over. But more than 800 people aged 20 to 44 also died, a reminder that heat risk is not confined to the very old. Heat does its damage through heart, lung and kidney failure, often in people already ill, which is why it rarely appears as a cause of death.

Why the number is probably low

The authors describe their total as likely an underestimate. It leaves out a May heatwave, which falls outside the study window. It also leaves out everyone living outside the 854 cities. Rural and small-town populations tend to be older and have fewer health services, so the omission may matter.

The uncertainty also runs the other way. Models of this kind rest on temperature-mortality curves estimated from past years. Cooling, housing and health systems have changed, so those curves may not hold for every city. The headline figure is best read as an order of magnitude with a margin of error, not a precise tally.

What it means for policy

Because heat deaths are missing from routine statistics, estimates like this one are often the only evidence available to city health departments. They show where cooling centres, heat-action plans, tree cover and building standards would do the most good. The attribution finding adds a point about cost. If two-thirds of the toll is tied to warming, then emissions policy and adaptation spending are, in effect, the same budget line.

The issue is already reaching British policy. The government has said it will draft in military aircraft against wildfires, and the Prime Minister has faced questions about net zero after the Jackdaw gasfield approval.

The next test is whether national statistics offices begin publishing their own heat-mortality figures alongside such models. Until then, estimates like this one are the main evidence of a toll that official counts largely miss.
