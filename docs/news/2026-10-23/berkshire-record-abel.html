Berkshire Hathaway hits record highs under Greg Abel

In one sentence
Berkshire's Class B shares hit a record near $543 on 21 October, nine months into Greg Abel's tenure, as buybacks, deals and earnings growth ease key-person worries.

Berkshire Hathaway's shares set fresh all-time highs this week, roughly nine months into Greg Abel's tenure as chief executive. The stock now has to answer a question that was easy to postpone while Warren Buffett held the title: whether investors were paying for the man or for the machine.

On Wednesday, 21 October, the Class B shares touched an intraday high of about $543, and the Class A shares reached about $814,028. Sources differ on the exact Class B print: one report gives $543.34, another $543.06. The previous Class B record close was $539.80, set on 2 May 2025, the trading session after Buffett told shareholders he would step down. In other words, the stock has spent about 17 months going nowhere on a closing basis, then pushed through the old peak during the successor's first year.

The handover

Buffett announced in May 2025 that he would leave the CEO role at the end of that year. Abel, who had run Berkshire's non-insurance operations, took over on 1 January 2026. Buffett stayed on as chairman. The market's initial reaction was a sharp fall, consistent with a discount for key-person risk. The stock then traded below that level for much of the interval, and a recovery to an eight-month high came in early August, around the second-quarter results. Class B rose 2.3% to about $534 after those results, which showed operating earnings of $13 per share-equivalent, 16% higher than a year earlier, according to coverage of the report.

What is driving it

Accounts of the rally point to capital deployment as much as to the underlying businesses. Abel is reported to have put more than $21.3bn to work in under a year, through the acquisition of Taylor Morrison Home, an investment in Alphabet (a position Buffett's team had started) and share repurchases. Berkshire bought back $4.5bn of its own stock in the second quarter alone. For a company that sat on a record cash pile for years, spending it is itself news, and buybacks at these prices signal that management thinks the shares still carry value.

That mix makes the test less clean than the headline suggests. The Alphabet stake and much of the equity portfolio were assembled under Buffett, and the operating earnings growth comes from businesses he built. What is Abel's own is the willingness to deploy cash at a pace Buffett often declined to match. Whether the price reflects approval of that, or simply a strong equity market lifting a large stock portfolio, cannot be separated from one day's price action. Berkshire's record also arrived while some other large-cap names were under pressure, which fits a defensive bid for a diversified, cash-rich holder, as in the rotation toward defensives seen this week.

What to watch

The cleaner evidence comes on 7 November, when Berkshire is expected to report third-quarter earnings. Three lines matter: operating earnings from the insurance and rail-and-utilities units, the size of the cash pile after the year's deals, and the pace of buybacks. If operating earnings keep rising at double digits while the share price holds above the old peak, the case that investors are valuing the business rather than the figurehead will have survived its first real audit.
