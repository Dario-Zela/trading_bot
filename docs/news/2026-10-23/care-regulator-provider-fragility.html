Care regulator warns rapid market change risks the National Care Service

In one sentence
The CQC's State of Care report says England's adult social care market is fragile and shaped 'by accident', raising questions for reform; the National Care Service link rests on BBC's headline.

England's care regulator has described the adult social care market as one shaped "largely by accident rather than by design". It has warned that the fragility this produces needs addressing as the government works towards a National Care Service. The Care Quality Commission (CQC) made the warning in its annual State of Care report for 2025-26, published on 23 October.

Two caveats apply. The BBC's headline links the warning to the National Care Service. The fuller report coverage retrieved for this article does not mention that body. The link below is therefore taken from the headline and from the government's reform programme, not from quoted regulator language. The service itself is also not a Scottish matter here. The regulator is the CQC, which covers England, not Scotland's Care Inspectorate.

What the regulator said

Chris Badger, the CQC's chief inspector of adult social care, said the market is being reshaped by economic pressure and by uneven planning among councils, not by a deliberate strategy. "A clearer vision is needed", he said. The CQC's chair, Baroness Julia Neuberger, said: "To repair a fracturing system, there must be a collective response."

The report points to several specific strains:

  • Self-funders. Providers are increasingly seeking people who pay for their own care in order to stay financially viable. The CQC warns that this could reduce choice for those whose care a council funds.
  • Fragmentation and consolidation. Home care is splitting into more small providers. Care homes are consolidating, and institutional investors are buying homes.
  • Funding limits. The means-test threshold has stayed at £23,250 for 15 years. The CQC says many people "must either try to fund care themselves, count on support from unpaid friends and family, or go without".

The figures show how the state-funded side of the system has changed. Council spending on adult social care reached £31.6bn in 2025-26, up roughly a third from £23.7bn in 2022-23. The CQC estimates that 223,000 fewer adults were receiving long-term council-funded care at the end of 2024-25 than 20 years earlier. For 70% of new requests for council-funded support, nothing beyond information is provided. The average hourly cost of externally provided home care rose 5% to £24.72, though the source's wording on that figure is slightly unclear.

Why fragility matters for reform

The CQC's earlier work gives some indication of why small providers worry it. A previous State of Care report found that services supporting four or fewer people had grown by seven percentage points, to more than 28% of home care providers over two years. The regulator noted that such providers "may be less financially resilient". The CQC has also reported that providers in its market oversight scheme are handing back contracts to councils because fees do not cover rising costs.

The implication is a reasonable one, though it remains an inference. A national service built on top of a provider base that is shrinking, consolidating or drifting towards self-funders would inherit those weaknesses. Reform of commissioning or funding would then depend on providers that remain willing and able to deliver. The CQC did not, in the coverage available, set out that argument in those terms.

The picture is not uniformly bleak. Of the first CQC assessments of councils' duties under the Care Act 2014, 59% were rated good and 3% outstanding. 37% were rated requires improvement and 2% inadequate. Wigan Council, rated outstanding, was cited for collaborative commissioning that cut out-of-borough placements. The regulator's point is that quality and access increasingly depend on how well local systems work together.

Government response and what comes next

No ministerial statement was found in the coverage retrieved. Badger welcomed the government's "renewed focus" on social care as an opportunity. He cautioned that "none of this can be considered in isolation". The government published its latest local authority spending figures in the same week.

The next test is whether ministers answer the "clearer vision" call. That would mean setting out how the market is meant to look under any national service, and who carries the financial risk if providers fail. The Casey Commission, set up in April 2025 to plan adult social care reform, is the body to watch for that detail.
