UK plans emergency powers to protect undersea cables

In one sentence
The UK plans new emergency powers to compel action against sabotage of undersea cables, but scope and timetable remain unpublished pending a promised white paper.

The UK government is preparing new emergency powers to defend the subsea cables that carry its internet and financial traffic, according to BBC Politics coverage. The reported plan would let the state do more than watch the seabed. It would give ministers legal authority to compel action when infrastructure is threatened. The BBC report gives no detail on the scope of the powers or a timetable, and this article does not fill that gap.

What is already on the table

The idea is not new. In late May, telecoms minister Liz Lloyd set out proposals to replace a 140-year-old law on cable damage. ISPreview and Ocean News reported that the package included:

  • fines and prison terms for vessel operators who intentionally or recklessly damage cables;
  • new security duties on cable owners and operators to prevent, detect and respond to compromises;
  • emergency powers to direct businesses to protect the infrastructure, which would help the government respond to major incidents.

A fuller white paper and consultation were promised later in 2026. The latest BBC-sourced report may reflect that process moving forward, but the triage material does not say so. It is also unclear whether the emergency powers are the same ones floated in May or a firmer version of them.

Why the legal shift matters

Until now, protection has rested largely on monitoring, naval patrols and the threat of prosecution after the fact. Emergency powers would add a lever that acts before or during an incident. In principle the state could instruct an operator to reroute traffic, harden a landing station or accept government direction in a crisis.

That raises questions for the industry that the government has not yet answered:

  • Who pays for any ordered measures?
  • What compensation or liability protection operators would get.
  • How the powers would apply to cables owned by overseas firms.

Critics have already noted that the May proposals leaned on penalties rather than direct defensive capability, as The Register observed.

A hard problem to prove

Most cable breaks are accidents, mainly from fishing gear and anchors. Ministers have acknowledged a "grey zone" in which malicious intent is hard to establish. Any emergency power would need a trigger that works in that ambiguity without sweeping in routine faults.

The security backdrop is plain enough. In April, Defence Secretary John Healey said British forces had exposed a covert Russian submarine operation near undersea infrastructure. Moscow rejected the claim. A parliamentary committee has separately warned of gaps in UK Arctic defence, in the same northern waters that cables cross.

What to watch

The test is the white paper and the consultation: the definition of an emergency, who can invoke the powers, and whether Parliament gets oversight. Until those are published, this is an announcement of intent rather than a settled regime.
