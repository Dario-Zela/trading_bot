Ebola patients from Nigeria and Kenya may have crossed paths at Uganda airport

In one sentence
Africa CDC is investigating whether Ebola patients in Nigeria and Kenya met at Entebbe airport on 3 October; the theory is unconfirmed and one of several.

Health officials in Africa are checking whether the first Ebola patients identified in Nigeria and Kenya were in the same part of Uganda's Entebbe airport on the same day. That would be one possible explanation for how a Nigerian case arose with no known source. The theory is unconfirmed, and the officials running the investigation describe it as one of several.

What is known

Dr Wessam Mankoula, head of health emergencies at the Africa CDC, said both patients are known to have passed through Entebbe International Airport on 3 October. He framed the airport theory as a question rather than a finding: "Has there been any touch or contact between the two cases at the airport at the same time?" He also said "there are a lot of possibilities" and asked that the investigation not be pre-empted (Health Policy Watch).

The Kenyan patient is a man who had spent several weeks in the Democratic Republic of Congo. Mankoula said he crossed into Uganda illegally. He flew from Entebbe to Nairobi's Jomo Kenyatta International Airport, where a relative collected him and drove him to Nairobi Hospital. He died of Ebola two days later. Eight of his contacts have since been infected: four health workers and four relatives. Kenya has traced 135 contacts and has 118 of them in quarantine.

The Nigerian patient is the Ugandan-born wife of a Swiss diplomat based in Nigeria. She spent two weeks in Uganda from mid-September and then flew to Abuja. Her symptoms began on 7 October and she was admitted on 11 October. She was first diagnosed with malaria. Ebola was suspected on 19 October and a positive test that day was confirmed by a second test the next day. Her virus was identified as the Bundibugyo species. Nigeria is monitoring 44 high-risk contacts in a designated quarantine facility, and its health minister has said the case remains contained, according to the Associated Press report carried by ABC News.

Why the airport theory is plausible, and why it is not settled

The theory is attractive because the Nigerian case lacks an explanation. Uganda has been declared Ebola-free since August. As reported by the AP, Uganda's government disputes that she was exposed in the country and has said she travelled to Congo. Mankoula said there is no indication that she did. That leaves a gap, and a shared airport is a candidate for filling it.

The timeline is not inconsistent with it. Ebola's incubation period is generally put at 2 to 21 days, and a 3 October exposure followed by symptoms on 7 October falls inside that range. That fit does not show causation, though. The Kenyan man's illness stage on 3 October is not documented in the sources reviewed, and Ebola spreads through direct contact with the bodily fluids of a symptomatic person. Sharing a terminal does not by itself carry meaningful risk. Whether the two came close enough to touch, or whether either showed symptoms, is the very thing being asked.

The other possibilities are not detailed publicly. The Nigerian patient could have had exposure elsewhere in her two weeks in Uganda, or the dispute over a Congo trip could resolve in either direction. Nothing reviewed identifies flight numbers, airline records or the results of any interview with the patient. Her condition and her ability to give an account were not described.

The wider outbreak

The backdrop is serious. The DRC is dealing with what Health Policy Watch calls the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record: 9,704 confirmed cases and 4,687 deaths as of 20 October, with Ituri and North Kivu provinces accounting for 94% of cases. Reported cases have dipped slightly since epidemiological week 37, but the report says it is too early to call a sustained decline, given reporting lags, community resistance and insecurity. More than 80% of cases arise outside known contact lists, and over half of deaths occur in the community.

Those figures matter for the airport question. When most cases are not linked to known contacts, travellers can carry the virus without anyone knowing, and the Kenyan man's illegal crossing shows that the DRC–Uganda border, open officially only to goods trucks and humanitarian traffic, is not sealed. Mankoula named South Sudan, the Central African Republic, Burundi and Rwanda as the countries most at risk, and urged neighbours to strengthen screening and surveillance at entry points to catch travellers who are not yet showing symptoms.

What to watch

The first test is whether investigators can establish the Nigerian patient's movements at Entebbe on 3 October, through airport records and her own account. Confirmation would make the case for coordinated screening at transit hubs. A finding of no overlap would send the search for the source back to Uganda and possibly to Congo, and would widen the dispute between Uganda's government and the Africa CDC. In the nearer term, the result of Nigeria's 44-contact follow-up will show whether the case stays contained, and Kenya's quarantined contacts will be watched through the end of the 21-day window.
