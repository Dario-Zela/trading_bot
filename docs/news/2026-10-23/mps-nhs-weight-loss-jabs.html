MPs call for wider NHS access to weight-loss jabs

In one sentence
A Commons committee says the NHS weight-loss jab rollout, covering about 6% of eligible people, is too slow and that access now depends on ability to pay.

The Commons Health and Social Care Committee has called the NHS rollout of weight-loss jabs "unambitious" and urged ministers to widen access. Its report, published on 22 October, says the current plan reaches a "woefully small number of people" while the private market fills the gap.

What the committee found

The report, from the committee chaired by Layla Moran, centres on Mounjaro (tirzepatide). NICE recommended the drug for managing overweight and obesity in June 2024. At NHS England's request, NICE agreed that the rollout should be gradual. The plan now covers about 220,000 people over three years. The Health Foundation puts that at roughly 6% of those eligible under NICE's recommendation, and NHS estimates suggest about 2.8 million patients qualify.

Outside the NHS, demand is much larger. The committee cites more than 2.37 million packs of GLP-1 drugs prescribed every month in the UK, "with the vast majority" obtained privately. Moran's summary of the consequence was blunt:

"Access to GLP-1 medication appears increasingly to be determined by ability to pay rather than clinical need."

The report also describes a postcode lottery in NHS access. It calls obesity "a chronic, relapsing condition that leads to further serious illnesses" and wants it managed long term, like diabetes, with a national care pathway.

What MPs want

The recommendations are procedural more than numerical. The committee says the government "must urgently reassess the pace of rollout of Mounjaro". It also asks for:

  • a summit on GLP-1 access with providers and clinicians;
  • a public awareness campaign on the risks of the drugs;
  • a review of how online advertising is regulated;
  • a review of whether the medicines regulator, the MHRA, has the capacity to tackle the illegal trade in these drugs.

The report does not set a target number of patients. Wegovy (semaglutide) is also approved for NHS use, but, as with Mounjaro, many patients buy it privately.

The cost question

The constraint is money. The figure quoted in coverage of the report is £15.2bn over five years if every eligible patient presented in the first year. That is a ceiling, not a forecast. It shows why NHS England has staged the rollout, and why "wider access" is a budgeting decision as much as a clinical one. I could not locate the report's own costing in the sources available, so the figure is attributed to press coverage and not independently checked.

The commercial side is thinner. Coverage of the report does not name the manufacturers, and nothing I found indicates the committee proposed price negotiations. A faster rollout would raise NHS volumes for the companies that make these drugs, but any pricing arrangement would be negotiated separately and is not described in the report as summarised. The same caution applies to the claim that demand would rise "sharply": the report's own evidence is that demand already exists, mostly privately, so a faster rollout would move some of it onto the public purse.

Reaction and what comes next

Henry Gregg of the National Pharmacy Association said community pharmacy was "ready to support a wider rollout". An NHS spokesperson said NHS England recognises the need to accelerate access and is testing new routes for starting the medicines, with priority going to those with the most severe and complex needs. The coverage reviewed contains no response from the Department of Health and Social Care.

The next test is that response. Government replies to select committee reports are normally due within two months, so ministers' answer on the pace of rollout, and whether any funding accompanies it, should be on the record by around the end of December. The proposed summit is the other thing to watch.
