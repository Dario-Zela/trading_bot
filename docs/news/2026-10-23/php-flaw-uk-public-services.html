PHP flaw sparks scramble to patch UK public services

In one sentence
Two critical PHP flaws forced 32 UK government services offline as the state scrambled to patch; a claimed '500 vulnerabilities' figure could not be verified.

Thirty-two UK government services were unavailable on Thursday, and four more were partly down. The cause was not an attack that had succeeded. It was a defensive reflex: two newly disclosed flaws in PHP, the programming language behind a large share of the web, and a decision to pull sites offline until they could be patched.

Tech minister Baroness Lloyd of Effra told the House of Lords that "32 government services are unavailable and four are partially unavailable", because of "precautionary measures taken to protect systems while security updates and mitigations are applied". She added: "We are not currently aware of any confirmed compromise of central government systems resulting from these vulnerabilities."

What the flaw is

The two bugs are tracked as CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619. According to The Register, either could let an unauthenticated attacker run code on a vulnerable server. That means no password and no prior foothold. The bugs have been nicknamed PHP2Shell.

Both were found with AI tools. The security firm Calif found one using OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol model. Google's Wiz independently found both. OpenAI then worked with vendors and government agencies on disclosure. PHP's maintainers have released fixes and urged all users to update immediately. Coverage citing the BBC says the fix is available only for up-to-date versions of the software. That detail matters for the argument below. The sources reviewed do not give a full list of affected versions.

PHP runs on roughly 70% of websites whose server-side language can be identified, according to the tracker W3Techs. Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 said it had identified more than 22.6 million internet-exposed instances that were potentially vulnerable.

Why the state took sites down

The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) told organisations to patch at once or consider taking affected systems offline until they could. It sent about 50,000 warnings to UK organisations. It said OpenAI had briefed it the week before, and the government stood up a cross-departmental incident response before the public announcement.

The scale of the shutdown shows how hard it is to patch quickly. The Office for Budget Responsibility took its website down. Peers raised disruption at the DVLA and in police services. Baroness Ludford named Edinburgh, Oxford, Bristol, Lambeth and Barnet councils as reportedly offline. No full list of affected services has been published.

The pattern was international. Reports cite government sites taken down in Germany, France, Belgium, Finland and the Netherlands, and in Canada. There, AI minister Evan Solomon said some federal sites had been removed temporarily on the advice of the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security.

Pulling a site is the fastest control an administrator has. It also suggests that, for those sites, the fix could not be applied fast enough, or that nobody was sure where PHP was running. A council site built years ago by a contractor, or a department's old application on a hosted platform, may be running a version with no simple upgrade path. Taking it offline costs a service but removes the risk.

The timing adds pressure. AWS says attackers were exploiting the flaws four days before public disclosure, and Cloudflare has recorded millions of attempts to weaponise them. Other agencies are less alarmed: the US cyber agency CISA said it had not observed exploitation when it published its guidance. The sources conflict, and the picture may differ by the day and the sensor.

The "500 vulnerabilities" figure

The story was pitched with a claim that an AI minister said 500 new vulnerabilities had been found across government systems. That figure could not be verified. It does not appear in The Register's account of the Lords exchange. The Lords statement attributed to the UK tech minister contains only the counts of 32, four and 50,000. The only minister described as an "AI minister" in the reporting reviewed was Canada's Solomon, and no source found ties a figure of 500 to him or to the UK.

Until the BBC's original wording is checked, the number should not be read as a measure of the UK's PHP exposure, nor as evidence of a wider patching backlog. It may refer to something else entirely, such as a different sweep or a different country. The shutdowns are the better evidence for the backlog argument, since they show how many services could not be patched in the time available.

What to watch

Three things will show how serious this is. The first is whether the government publishes a full list of the affected services and a timetable for restoring them. The second is whether the "no confirmed compromise" statement survives forensic review, given the exploitation attempts that began before disclosure. The third is whether the NCSC and the government say how many of the 50,000 warned organisations are still unpatched. Those figures would show whether the offline services are a short-term precaution or the first sign of a longer problem.
