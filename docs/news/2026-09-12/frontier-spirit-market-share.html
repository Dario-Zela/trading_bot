Frontier Airlines races to capture market share left behind by Spirit's collapse

A Frontier Airlines Airbus A321neo, the workhorse of the carrier's expansion into former Spirit Airlines markets. — Acroterion / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Frontier is grabbing Spirit's old routes and slots and raising fares, not cutting them — for now.

Spirit Airlines flew its last flight on May 2, 2026, ending a 33-year run as the pioneer of the American no-frills fare after two bankruptcies in as many years finally caught up with it. The question left hanging over the budget end of US aviation was who would absorb the seats, the routes and the price-sensitive passengers Spirit left behind. Frontier Airlines has answered with the loudest voice.

The overlap made Frontier the obvious claimant: it competed with Spirit on more than 100 routes, more than any other US carrier, and Raymond James analyst Savanthi Syth calculated that Spirit's seat capacity overlapped with Frontier's by 35% in the markets both served. Frontier has pushed hard into former Spirit strongholds — Orlando, Fort Lauderdale, Dallas-Fort Worth, Detroit, Las Vegas — lifting overall capacity 23% year-over-year and adding more than 415,000 seats in September 2026 versus a year earlier, well ahead of JetBlue's roughly 228,000-seat gain, the next-largest beneficiary. Analysts reckon Frontier could realistically push its share of the budget segment to 5-6% within two years if it keeps moving at this pace.

Picking through the wreckage, not buying it whole

Rather than attempt a full acquisition, Frontier — under Jimmy Dempsey, who became permanent chief executive in January after Barry Biffle's December departure — has cherry-picked from Spirit's bankruptcy estate: used A320-family aircraft and airport slots at LaGuardia, Detroit and Chicago O'Hare, the scarce assets that actually constrain growth. The payoff showed up fast. Second-quarter revenue hit a record $1.3 billion, up 38% year-over-year, with revenue per available seat mile up 28% as fares on routes freed by Spirit's exit rose more than 50% in places. Frontier is guiding to RASM growth above 20% again in the third quarter, on capacity up 17-18%. It still lost $90 million in the quarter and more than $362 million over the first half, but management now expects a return to profitability in the second half of the year.

The discipline question

The more telling number may be one that shows restraint rather than ambition. Even as it chases market share, Frontier has deferred 69 A320neo-family deliveries from 2027-2030 out to 2031-2033 and sold 11 A321neos to lessor Avolon, trimming a backlog that stood at 160 aircraft in February. That is not the behavior of a carrier trying to flood the market with seats — it is one matching near-term capacity to a vacuum left by a dead competitor while keeping its cost base, already the lowest in the industry at roughly 9.5 cents per available seat mile, from ballooning. Executives have described the environment as a "favorable competitive capacity" backdrop rather than a fare war, and so far the data backs them: fares are rising, not falling, industry-wide.

That balance is fragile. JetBlue is growing into the same gap, and Allegiant and Southwest both compete for overlapping leisure demand. If any of them decide to chase Frontier's share with capacity of their own, the pricing discipline that has driven Frontier's rebound could unwind quickly. The next test comes when Frontier reports third-quarter results and reveals whether that 20%-plus RASM growth held up through the peak of its route additions, or whether rivals have started undercutting the gains.
