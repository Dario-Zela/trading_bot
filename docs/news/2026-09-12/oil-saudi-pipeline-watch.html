Oil holds above $100 as Saudi pipeline stays shut, Red Sea risk builds

The Bab-el-Mandeb strait, now largely encircled by Houthi-held territory after this week's capture of Mocha and Perim Island. — Archer90 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0), data via OpenStreetMap
In one sentence
Brent crude topped $100 a barrel after Saudi Arabia's Hormuz-bypass pipeline was hit and Houthi forces completed their seizure of Yemen's Red Sea coast.

Brent crude pushed above $100 a barrel this week and held there, touching $105 before easing back toward $104 on Friday — a level oil hasn't traded at consistently since the spring. The immediate trigger was Saudi Arabia shutting down its East-West pipeline, the kingdom's main artery for moving crude away from the Persian Gulf, after drone strikes launched from Iraq set sections of it ablaze near Medina on Thursday. But the pipeline shutdown landed on top of a second, arguably more consequential development: Houthi forces have finished seizing Yemen's entire Red Sea coastline, capturing the port of Mocha and Perim Island this week and tightening their hold on the Bab-el-Mandeb strait, the narrow gateway at the southern end of the Red Sea through which roughly a tenth of the world's seaborne oil passes.

A bypass with no bypass

The East-West pipeline, also called the Petroline, was built for exactly this kind of moment. It runs 1,200 kilometres from Abqaiq in the east to the Red Sea terminal at Yanbu, carrying up to 5 million barrels a day — designed explicitly to let Saudi Arabia get oil to tankers without sending it through the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint Iran has threatened and periodically shut down since the US and Israel struck Iranian targets in a war that began in February. Riyadh leaned on the Petroline harder than usual this year for precisely that reason. Now the workaround has become a target itself, and the extent of the damage — and how long repairs will take — is not yet clear from Saudi state disclosures.

That leaves Saudi Arabia squeezed from both directions. Hormuz, to the east, remains contested after Iran's near-closure of the strait in the spring and continuing skirmishes — American forces reportedly struck several Iranian-linked tankers this week alone. Bab-el-Mandeb, to the west, is now effectively under the control of an Iran-aligned militia that has spent the past week demonstrating it can also strike overland at the pipeline meant to avoid the strait entirely. Houthi missiles and drones hit Saudi energy sites and other targets on Wednesday, wounding more than 70 people, before the drone strikes on the Petroline followed a day later.

Repricing risk as permanent

Oil's move past $100 is a different kind of signal than the spikes traders got used to filtering out over the past two years, when Red Sea attacks caused brief detours and quick recoveries. This time the market is responding to the closing of options rather than a single incident: both of Saudi Arabia's export routes are now compromised at once, with no third route available. Brent has risen more than 18% in September alone. That kind of move tends to feed quickly into inflation expectations, and from there into bets on how long the Federal Reserve can keep cutting rates — a linkage traders were already watching closely ahead of the September 16 decision.

The practical question now is repair speed on the pipeline and whether the Houthi advance stalls at the coast or pushes further to threaten shipping lanes directly. Saudi Arabia has not given a timeline for restoring East-West flows, and Aramco has not detailed the scale of the fire damage. Both will move the price faster than anything traders can do in the meantime.
