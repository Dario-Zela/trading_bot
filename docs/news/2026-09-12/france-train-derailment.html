Passenger train derails between Rouen and Caen, injuring 44

A TER Caen–Rouen train at Lisieux station, on the line affected by Friday's derailment near Cléon — Mouliric / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A Rouen–Caen regional train carrying 186 people derailed after hitting an unidentified object, injuring 44 and leaving one passenger critical.

The 7:12pm regional train from Rouen to Caen never made it past the small industrial town of Cléon on Friday evening. Somewhere along the single track through the Seine valley, the four-carriage TER struck an object investigators have yet to identify, derailing one carriage entirely and knocking four others off the rails. Of the 186 passengers on board, 44 were injured. One — an 18-year-old woman — was airlifted to Rouen University Hospital in critical condition.

The scale of the emergency response reflected the uncertainty in the first hours: 130 firefighters, five medical teams and 80 vehicles were dispatched, and officials activated a mass-casualty protocol more commonly reserved for incidents with a much higher toll. Overhead power to the line was cut, and rail traffic between Rouen and Caen — a corridor that also carries commuters toward Lisieux and Serquigny — was suspended.

Witnesses described a sudden, escalating violence rather than a single jolt. "Suddenly there were a few jolts, and then after about three seconds it got stronger and stronger," one 24-year-old passenger, identified only as Louis, told reporters at the scene. Transport Minister Philippe Tabarot confirmed the derailment on social media within the hour; Rouen prosecutor Sébastien Gallois later told reporters that drug and alcohol tests on the driver had come back negative, ruling out the most immediate cause investigators would have checked first.

An object, or something older

What the train actually struck remains the open question, and the answer will decide whether this becomes a footnote or a genuine flashpoint for French rail policy. Deputy prosecutor Marie-Valérie Albert cautioned that "the investigation will take a long time," and SNCF Réseau, which manages the infrastructure, has not yet said whether the object was foreign debris — a fallen branch, dumped material, deliberate sabotage — or a piece of the track itself, such as a dislodged fastening or rail component that failed under the train's weight.

The distinction matters well beyond Cléon. France's rail network averages 29 years of age against roughly 15 in Switzerland, and the Cour des Comptes and rail unions have warned for years that a maintenance backlog — estimated at some €15bn — is being managed rather than closed. Tabarot has pushed a transport bill promising SNCF Réseau an extra €1.5bn a year from 2028, an implicit acknowledgment that current spending of roughly €3bn annually is not enough to arrest a gradual decline in track condition. None of that proves this derailment stemmed from deferred maintenance. But it is the backdrop against which investigators, and the public, will read whatever they find.

For now, the story is a local one: a packed evening train, a frightening few seconds, and a hospital in Rouen treating the injured. Whether it becomes something larger depends on a report that prosecutors say will take weeks, not days, to produce — and on whether the object at Cléon turns out to be a stray piece of debris or a symptom of track wearing out faster than it is being replaced.
