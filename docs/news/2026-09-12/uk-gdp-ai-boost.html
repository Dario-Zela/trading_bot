AI investment helps drive surprise UK GDP growth in July

Canary Wharf's financial district, home to much of the business-services activity the ONS credits for July's growth surprise — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK GDP grew 0.4% in July, beating forecasts on AI-linked tech and consultancy spending, but the gain was narrowly concentrated outside consumer-facing sectors.

The UK economy grew 0.4% in July, the Office for National Statistics said this week, comfortably ahead of the flat reading most economists had pencilled in. It is the third straight month of expansion — 0.3% in June, none at all in May — and it pushed annual growth to 1.6%. The detail behind the number matters more than the number itself: a meaningful slice of it came from businesses spending on artificial intelligence.

In one sentence: UK GDP beat forecasts in July on a jump in AI-linked computer services, but the gain was narrowly concentrated in business-to-business tech and professional services.

Where the growth actually came from

Services did the heavy lifting, up 0.4% on the month and 0.6% over the three months to July, while production and construction each fell 0.5% over the same three-month window. Within services, computer programming and consultancy activities grew 3.5% in July alone — on the ONS's own rounded arithmetic, that one sub-sector accounts for roughly 30% of the month's entire GDP growth, contributing around 0.12 percentage points.

The statisticians were careful not to overclaim. Many of the firms reporting the largest turnover gains in July, the ONS said, are "involved in activities related to artificial intelligence and cloud computing" — but it added a caveat worth sitting with: "because of the nature of our data collection, it is difficult for us to quantify the exact impact of these types of activities on turnover." In other words, the AI story is visible in the sector classification, not cleanly isolated in the accounting. ONS director of economic statistics Liz McKeown also noted that warm weather and the football World Cup had distorted activity in some industries, cutting both ways depending on the sector.

The wider professional-services complex backs up the picture: professional, scientific and technical activities rose 2.1% over three months, driven by a 7.0% jump in scientific R&D, 3.4% growth in advertising and market research, and 3.1% in legal services. Susannah Streeter, chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, called it evidence that "the AI spending boom is starting to feed through into the wider economy, as businesses invest in the computing power, software and expertise needed to put the technology to work." Sanjay Raja, Deutsche Bank's chief UK economist, was blunter still: "Investment in AI is helping drive growth in sectors such as telecoms and information services. The UK growth story is becoming harder to ignore."

A recovery with a narrow base

The caveats start as soon as you look past the headline sectors. Wholesale trade fell 3.0% over three months — the single largest negative contribution within services — while retail and hospitality gave back some of their earlier summer gains. Matt Swannell of the EY Item Club described the strength as "narrowly focused on a handful of business-to-business sub-sectors," chiefly IT and administrative services, even as consumer-facing industries went the other way. Yael Selfin at KPMG put it more starkly: "Despite strong activity in July, the headline growth figure masks a weaker picture for households."

That split matters for how the number should be read. A GDP beat driven by data-centre spend and consultancy fees is real activity — it shows up in wages paid, taxes collected, and equity earnings for the firms selling picks and shovels for the AI build-out. But it is not the same as a consumer recovery, and it leaves the UK economy dependent on a comparatively small set of firms and sub-sectors continuing to spend at the current pace.

The rates angle

For traders, the timing is awkward. The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, but only by a 6-3 vote — Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann all pushed for a rise to 4%, citing energy-driven inflation risk. A stronger-than-expected GDP print doesn't ease that pressure; it adds to the case that the economy can absorb tighter policy. Gilt yields, already sitting near multi-decade highs elsewhere in markets this week, have limited room to fall on growth data that also strengthens the hawks' hand. Sterling ticked up modestly against the dollar on the release, a sign markets read the number as growth-supportive first and inflation-relevant second — but that reading could flip quickly if next week's inflation data comes in hot.

The next test lands fast: August inflation figures are due on 16 September, the last significant data point before the Monetary Policy Committee's 17 September decision. Two more hawkish switches would tip the vote toward a hike. Until then, this week's GDP figure stands as the clearest evidence yet that AI capital spending is showing up in national accounts — just not yet broadly enough to call it a recovery.
