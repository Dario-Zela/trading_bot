Reform UK receives record £36m donation from crypto billionaire Ben Delo

Nigel Farage, whose Reform UK party has drawn record donations from crypto-wealth backers including Ben Delo — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Pardoned BitMEX co-founder Ben Delo gave Reform UK £36m — Britain's largest-ever political donation — timed ahead of expected curbs on big gifts.

The largest single donation in British political history was not made by a hedge-fund grandee or a supermarket heir. It came from a Bank Secrecy Act felon turned crypto billionaire who received a presidential pardon from Donald Trump eighteen months ago, and who spent much of the last decade running his business from Hong Kong.

Ben Delo, co-founder of the crypto derivatives exchange BitMEX, has pledged £36 million to Nigel Farage's Reform UK — structured as £1 million a month running through August 2029, the outer limit of the current parliamentary term. He opted to hand over the full sum upfront rather than staggering it, according to people familiar with the arrangement, a choice made in anticipation of tighter caps on large donations that ministers are already drafting. The gift eclipses the previous record, a £10 million bequest from the late Sainsbury's grandee Lord Sainsbury, and dwarfs the £9 million that fellow crypto investor Christopher Harborne gave Reform last year.

"Reform are making serious plans for government, which is more than Labour did before they won in 2024," Delo wrote in the Telegraph. "It takes time to develop thought-through policies and draft legislation, and it takes real money."

Farage called himself "honoured and humbled" by the gesture. The scale of it is not subtle: Reform now says it has outraised Labour and the Conservatives combined this year, largely on the back of two men — Delo and Harborne — whose fortunes both trace back to cryptocurrency trading.

From Bank Secrecy Act felon to top donor

Delo's path to Reform's largest backer runs through a US courtroom. As BitMEX's co-founder and the executive most associated with inventing the perpetual swap — the leveraged derivatives contract that became a staple of crypto trading — he pleaded guilty in February 2022 to a single Bank Secrecy Act violation for failing to build an anti-money-laundering programme at the exchange. He was sentenced to 30 months' probation and paid a $10 million civil penalty; he was never extradited, having voluntarily surrendered to US authorities in New York in 2021.

On 29 March 2025, Trump issued a full and unconditional pardon to Delo and his two BitMEX co-founders, Arthur Hayes and Samuel Reed, calling the prosecutions the product of "an obscure, antiquated law." Delo has since relocated his personal residence from Hong Kong back to the UK — a move that also happens to resolve an awkward regulatory question. Ministers introduced a £100,000 annual cap this year on donations from anyone who has not held UK residence for a full twelve months, aimed squarely at donors with an overseas base. Reform has insisted Delo's move clears that bar; the party has not published the paperwork that would let anyone outside it check.

A donation timed against the rulebook

The money itself is sterling, not crypto — which matters, because Britain banned cryptoasset donations to political parties outright in March, on the recommendation of a review led by the former senior civil servant Philip Rycroft, who warned that untraceable digital transfers could be "used as the vehicle to channel foreign money into the political system." Delo's fortune originates in crypto; the vehicle for giving it away does not.

That the sum landed as a lump payment, ahead of expected legislation to cap large individual gifts, is itself a data point. Labour's Pat McFadden has pushed to make the crypto-donation moratorium permanent, and backbenchers led by Liam Byrne have tabled amendments to the Representation of the People Bill aimed at donor transparency more broadly. Reform, for its part, is not merely accepting crypto wealth quietly — Farage announced at the Bitcoin 2025 conference in Las Vegas that Reform would be the first European party to accept crypto donations directly, before the subsequent ban intervened. The party's draft Cryptoassets and Digital Finance Bill proposes cutting capital-gains tax on crypto to 10%, establishing a Bank of England bitcoin reserve, and restricting banks' ability to close accounts linked to lawful digital-asset activity.

Why traders should care

None of Reform's crypto platform is law, and the party trails Labour narrowly in most September polling — Andy Burnham's premiership has produced what pollsters are calling a modest "Burnham bounce," with Ipsos putting Labour three points ahead. But donation patterns are a leading indicator of policy expectation, not a lagging one: money tends to arrive before the votes that would justify it, priced against the probability of influence rather than certainty of it. A concentrated, ideologically aligned £36 million bet from a man whose entire net worth sits in crypto is a wager that a Farage-influenced Treasury would mean lighter capital-gains treatment, a friendlier stance on bank account closures for crypto firms, and a slower, looser version of the stablecoin and custody regime the Financial Conduct Authority is currently building.

That regime is arriving regardless of who ends up governing. The FCA's authorisation gateway for crypto firms — trading platforms, custodians, stablecoin issuers — opens for applications on 30 September, with the full mandatory regime taking effect in October 2027. Delo's donation does not change that timetable. What it does is signal, in unusually concrete terms, which side of the regulatory debate Britain's crypto wealth is backing, and how much it is willing to spend to be heard before the rules are finished.

The next marker is procedural rather than dramatic: whether the government's promised cap on large individual donations — the one Delo appears to have paid ahead of — actually reaches the statute book, and whether it would have applied to gifts already banked. Until then, Reform's war chest, and the industry funding a slice of it, keeps growing.
