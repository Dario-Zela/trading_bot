Fed rate-hike odds firm even as CPI cools, ahead of Sept 16 decision

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC meets September 15-16. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Markets now price an 85% chance the Fed hikes rates Sept 16 despite cooling core inflation, betting Iran-driven oil shocks — not demand — justify tightening.

The Federal Reserve is on track to raise interest rates on September 16 for a straightforward-sounding reason that turns out to be anything but: oil, not demand, is doing the inflating.

CME FedWatch pricing put the odds of a quarter-point hike at roughly 85% after Friday's CPI release, up from about 56% in late August and barely above a coin flip a month before that. The move looks odd on its face. Core inflation — the Fed's preferred gauge of underlying price pressure — has cooled for two straight months on a year-over-year basis, from 2.6% in June to 2.5% in July to 2.4% in August, its lowest reading since March 2021. That is the kind of trend that would normally argue for the Fed to sit still, not tighten.

Two inflations in one report

The August CPI report, released September 11, split the difference and left both hawks and doves something to point to. Headline CPI rose 0.4% on the month, holding the annual rate at 3.4%. Core CPI rose 0.3% monthly — a touch hotter than the 0.2% economists expected — even as its annual rate kept falling. Gasoline prices, up 3.9% in August alone, accounted for more than a third of the headline gain. That is the split that matters here: the slow-moving, broad-based price pressure the Fed is mandated to fight keeps easing, while a fast-moving, narrow, energy-driven spike keeps showing up in the monthly prints.

The energy spike has an address. Since the U.S. and Israel began striking Iran in February, and Tehran responded by squeezing tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the channel that carries roughly a fifth of the world's oil and a third of its LNG — crude has been on a wild ride, touching nearly $120 a barrel at the worst of it before settling back above $100. Iran-backed Houthi forces seizing a port on Yemen's Red Sea coast this month added a second chokepoint to worry about, squeezing the alternative routes Gulf exporters had been leaning on.

Warsh's wager

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, five months into the job after succeeding Jerome Powell in May, used his Jackson Hole speech in late August to signal he'd rather tighten into an energy shock than risk it feeding into expectations. That single speech is largely what moved hike odds from a coin flip to a near-certainty; Friday's CPI simply confirmed traders' bet. The S&P 500 rose 0.8% on the day, part of a pattern all week in which stocks caught a bid whenever data suggested the CPI shock wouldn't derail the disinflation story, and sold off when oil headlines suggested otherwise.

The wager Warsh is making is that a geopolitical oil shock, left unanswered, is more dangerous to the inflation-fighting mandate than a hike into a cooling core print. Critics — including at least one prominent investor flagged in recent coverage — argue the market has this backwards: raise rates to fight a supply shock and the FOMC risks tightening financial conditions into what is, underneath the oil noise, a genuinely disinflating economy. A hike now would push the federal funds range to 3.75%–4.00%, the first increase since the tightening cycle that began under Powell.

The FOMC meets September 15–16, with a rate decision and Warsh press conference on the second day. Traders will be listening for how explicitly he ties the move to Hormuz and the Red Sea rather than to underlying demand — the distinction that will determine whether this is a one-off insurance hike or the start of a longer, oil-driven cycle.
