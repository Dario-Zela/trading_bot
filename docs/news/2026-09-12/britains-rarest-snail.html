Battle to save Britain's rarest snail, found in only one lake in the UK

Llyn Tegid (Bala Lake) in north Wales, the only place in Britain where the glutinous snail survives in the wild — Geograph.org.uk
In one sentence
Britain's rarest snail survives only in one Welsh lake, and zoos are now breeding insurance colonies in case that single population fails.

It is the size of a fingernail, wears a coat of clear slime thick enough to give it a glassy sheen, and for decades was presumed to have vanished from Britain entirely. The glutinous snail — Myxas glutinosa — turns out to have one address left: Llyn Tegid, a glacial lake near Bala in north Wales, also known as Bala Lake. Nowhere else in the country, and almost nowhere else in Europe, does it survive in the wild.

The species was once common enough to turn up in ponds and gravel pits across Kent, Surrey, East Anglia and Cumbria. Agricultural run-off and industrial pollution did for most of those populations over the twentieth century; the last confirmed English sighting was at a gravel pit near Oxford in 1993. Llyn Tegid held on. The snail was first logged there in 1852 and was described as abundant until the 1950s, after which it seemed to disappear too — divers and survey teams using glass-bottomed buckets found nothing for four decades, until a 1998 survey for the Countryside Council for Wales turned up a surviving colony.

One lake, one shot

Myxas glutinosa is a fussy animal. It grazes on the thin algal film that forms on stones in clean, cold, unpolluted water, and unlike its more robust pond-snail relatives it cannot go dormant when food runs short — a trait that leaves it exposed to anything that disrupts the lake's water quality or temperature even briefly. Llyn Tegid's size, its protected Ramsar-wetland status and its sparsely populated upland catchment have so far kept it clean enough. But a species with a single population has, by definition, no fallback if a pollution spike, an algal bloom or an invasive competitor takes hold there. Conservationists have spent nearly a decade warning that one bad year at one lake is the entire gap between "rare" and "extinct."

That risk is what has driven the response. Ecologist Ian Hughes began breeding the snails in garden ponds in Ceredigion in 2017 after taking a small rescue population from Llyn Tegid, and has since built what he calls a thriving ex-situ colony. This year that effort has scaled up considerably: the British and Irish Association of Zoos and Aquariums (BIAZA) has brought in seven member collections — including Chester Zoo, Longleat, Dudley Zoo and Castle, Wingham Wildlife Park, Whipsnade Zoo, Wildwood and Askham Bryan — to hold biosecure "ark" populations, spreading the genetic stock across enough sites that no single incident, whether a power cut, a disease outbreak or a water-system failure, could wipe the insurance population out alongside the wild one.

What the plan still lacks

The work is backed by a wider £60m UK government-funded programme for threatened species recovery, administered through Defra-linked conservation grants, and combines captive breeding with habitat assessment work on the lake itself. What it does not yet have is a confirmed second wild site. Ark populations buy time; they do not by themselves solve the single-location problem, and finding — or restoring — another body of water clean and stable enough to host a reintroduction is the harder, slower task still ahead. Natural Resources Wales has periodically assessed the Llyn Tegid population's condition since at least 2014, and any reintroduction elsewhere would need similarly close monitoring to avoid repeating the mistakes that wiped the snail out of England in the first place.

The near-term marker to watch is whether any of the zoo-bred stock is released into a second water body in the coming year, which would be the first real test of whether the insurance populations can become more than a holding pattern. Until then, Britain's rarest snail remains a single find away from having nowhere left to be found.
