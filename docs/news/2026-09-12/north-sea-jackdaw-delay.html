Shell's Jackdaw gasfield stuck in limbo as Burnham delays North Sea decisions

A North Sea drilling platform; the Jackdaw gasfield's approval has been left in limbo pending UK by-election politics. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers are set to shelve approval decisions on Shell's Jackdaw gasfield and the Rosebank oilfield until after the October 8 Holborn by-election against Green leader Zack Polanski.

There was a moment, in the last fortnight, when Shell and Equinor allowed themselves to believe the Jackdaw gasfield might finally get its paperwork. Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh was said to be readying a recommendation to approve the field, topsides already towed from Norway and bolted onto the jacket since October last year, first gas targeted for the fourth quarter of 2026. Then the Holborn and St Pancras by-election calendar intervened.

Whitehall officials now say a decision on Jackdaw — and a separate one on the Rosebank oilfield off Shetland — will not land before Parliament rises for conference recess this week, and is unlikely to arrive before 8 October, when voters in Keir Starmer's old constituency choose between Labour's Sagal Abdi-Wali and Green Party leader Zack Polanski. The inference being drawn across the industry and in Westminster alike is not subtle: Downing Street would rather sit on two of the most contentious fossil-fuel calls in Britain than hand Polanski a fresh grievance days before people vote.

A £8bn project waiting on a ballot box

Jackdaw sits within Adura, the 50-50 Shell-Equinor North Sea venture, and is forecast to produce upward of 140,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day at peak — Shell has put the figure at more than 6% of projected UK North Sea gas output, enough, on the companies' own estimate, to heat around 1.4 million homes. The field holds gas equivalent to roughly 200 million barrels of oil. None of that has translated into a green light. Development has been frozen on and off since 2022 amid legal challenges grounded in the Supreme Court's 2024 Finch ruling, which forced regulators to account for the downstream emissions of burning extracted fuel — so-called scope 3 emissions — not just those released during extraction.

Rosebank has fared worse. The Court of Session in Edinburgh quashed its original consent in January, after Uplift and Greenpeace UK argued successfully that the climate impact assessment ignored those same scope 3 emissions — a judgment estimating the field's lifetime output at 254 million tonnes of CO2 equivalent, close to 70% of the UK's entire annual emissions. Adura resubmitted the environmental case in July; it has been sitting in public consultation since. Between them, government estimates suggest, the two fields could supply roughly a tenth of Britain's oil and gas output at peak — which is precisely why neither side of the argument is prepared to let the matter drop quietly.

Why the by-election changes the arithmetic

Holborn and St Pancras is not just any by-election. It was Keir Starmer's seat, and Polanski — elevated to the Green leadership on a platform that put fossil-fuel expansion front and centre — has made Jackdaw and Rosebank his central attack line against the government that succeeded him. "Labour talks about change, but this is change for the worse," Polanski said of the delay, accusing Number 10 of wanting to keep the decision "under wraps" until votes are cast. Campaigners separately put the economic cost of carbon damage from the two fields, if approved, at roughly £336bn — a figure Polanski has used liberally on the stump.

The politics cut in an unusually direct way for Prime Minister Andy Burnham. Approve Jackdaw before 8 October and Labour hands Polanski a live rallying point in a seat it can ill afford to lose to the Greens; delay it, and the party's own conference — where new North Sea development already commands little internal enthusiasm — passes without the row either. Shadow Energy Secretary Andrew Bowie has accused the government of "placing the interests of the Labour party before the interests of Great Britain," arguing that sequencing a regulatory decision around a by-election date is itself the story, regardless of which way the eventual call goes.

The cost of deciding by calendar

What gets lost in the by-election framing is that Jackdaw's operators have done nothing to invite this particular delay. The field's own timeline problems — years of judicial review, the Finch ruling, a resubmitted environmental case — are compounding with a political one that has nothing to do with the North Sea at all. For Shell and Equinor, each month of drift into late autumn narrows the window to hit a fourth-quarter startup and adds carrying cost to an investment already sunk into topsides sitting on a jacket in the North Sea. Industry analysts tracking the project have flagged the more durable risk: if a UK government will visibly time a major energy approval around a single constituency's electoral diary once, investors have to price in the chance it does so again, on the next contentious field, the next by-election, the next conference season.

That is the quieter cost of this particular episode. It is not that Burnham's government has taken a firm position on North Sea development either way — it has, in effect, taken none, and the absence of a position is itself now a variable in project planning. The next concrete date is 8 October. Whichever way Holborn and St Pancras goes, Whitehall will then have run out of electoral cover, and Fahnbulleh's recommendation — on both Jackdaw and Rosebank — will be difficult to postpone a second time without explaining why.
