GTA maker Rockstar accused of putting sacked employee on industry 'watch list'

Rockstar North's former Edinburgh headquarters, at the centre of the union-busting allegations. — Thomas Nugent / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A sacked Rockstar North worker told a Glasgow tribunal the GTA maker kept a 'watch list' of unionising staff before firing 34 of them last October.

A petition about working from home. A Discord server for griping about the boss. Neither sounds like the stuff of industrial tribunals — until, according to one former employee, it becomes the seed of a list.

Dayne Oram, one of roughly 34 workers dismissed from Rockstar North's Edinburgh studio in October last year, told an employment tribunal in Glasgow that the company kept tabs on staff it considered troublesome, starting with a 2023 petition asking bosses to keep remote working after the pandemic. Around 150 employees signed it; only 15 to 20 were union members. Oram testified that Rockstar nonetheless "kept an eye" on that smaller group, and that the petition became, in his words, "the basis of a watch list" for anyone engaged in activities "considered distasteful."

"I believe it was the starting point and used to watch people for activities that were considered distasteful." — Dayne Oram, former Rockstar North employee

From petition to payroll

The claimants — represented by the Independent Workers' Union of Great Britain (IWGB) and its Game Workers branch — say the October 2025 dismissals swept up staff who were IWGB members or active in a Discord channel used to organise. Rockstar's account is different: the company says those let go had shared "highly confidential information, including relating to game features from upcoming and unannounced titles, in an insecure and public social channel," and that the sackings were for gross misconduct, full stop.

That dispute over motive is the whole case. If the tribunal ultimately agrees with Rockstar, the firings stand as a straightforward confidentiality breach involving one of the most secretive projects in entertainment — the long-awaited Grand Theft Auto VI. If it agrees with the workers, Rockstar will have used a leak investigation as cover to gut an incipient union drive, then allegedly kept a list to make sure it stayed gutted.

The case has already had one early skirmish. In January, employment judge Frances Eccles declined to grant the dismissed staff interim relief — a stopgap measure that would have kept them on payroll and their UK work visas active while the case proceeds to a full hearing. Eccles found the workers hadn't shown a "pretty good chance of success" that union activity was the principal reason for their dismissal. That is a lower bar than winning outright, and the union has cast the ruling as a temporary setback rather than a verdict — the full tribunal, now under way in Glasgow, is where the watch-list allegation and Rockstar's leak defence will actually be tested.

Why a list matters more than a sacking

Mass layoffs happen constantly in games, an industry that shed tens of thousands of jobs across the last two years alone. A single wrongful-dismissal claim over a round of cuts is unremarkable. A durable, cross-project watch list — if one exists — is a different animal: it implies infrastructure built to track and exclude specific workers over time, not just react to one bad quarter, and it would sit uneasily beside UK protections against dismissal for trade union activity.

No regulator has yet opened a formal inquiry into the alleged list itself, and Rockstar has faced no finding of liability — this is testimony from a claimant, not an established fact. But the allegation lands at a moment when studio consolidation and repeated layoff waves have made games workers more receptive to organising, and more alert to employers finding quiet ways to head it off.

The Glasgow tribunal is expected to run for several weeks, with a ruling not likely before the case reaches full closing arguments. Whatever it decides about the watch list, the more consequential number may be the one nobody disputes: 34 people fired in a single week, at a studio finishing the most anticipated game in its history.
