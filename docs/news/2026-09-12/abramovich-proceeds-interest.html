Frozen Abramovich Chelsea sale proceeds have earned £175m in interest amid Reeves standoff

Roman Abramovich at Stamford Bridge during his Chelsea ownership; sale proceeds from the 2022 deal remain frozen in a UK bank account. — John Dobson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Abramovich's frozen £2.5bn Chelsea sale proceeds have earned £175m in interest since 2022, and the standoff over releasing them to Ukraine has outlasted the chancellor who fought hardest for it.

Money that nobody is allowed to spend has a way of quietly growing anyway. Since Roman Abramovich sold Chelsea Football Club in May 2022, the £2.3bn in proceeds has sat untouched in a Barclays account — and, through the ordinary mechanics of interest, swollen to nearly £2.5bn. New accounts for Fordstam, the holding company through which the sanctioned Russian oligarch owned the club, show the pile has now earned at least £175m in interest: £63m in 2023, £114m in 2024, and more since. Stefan Barson, head of sport at law firm McCarthy Denning, put it dryly: with the interest it is generating, the frozen account has become, in his words, "the most profitable football-related business in the world."

The joke lands because nothing about the underlying situation is funny. The money was meant, in principle, for the victims of the war Abramovich's associates in Moscow started. More than four years later, not a pound of it has reached them.

A pledge that outlived the deal

When the UK sanctioned Abramovich in March 2022 over his ties to Vladimir Putin, he was forced into a rushed sale of Chelsea and gave a public undertaking: the proceeds would go to "all victims of the war in Ukraine." The government took a narrower reading — that the money should go specifically to Ukrainian humanitarian causes — and the gap between those two phrases has occupied lawyers on both sides ever since.

A licence issued in December 2025 was meant to break the deadlock, giving Abramovich 90 days to direct the funds into a dedicated foundation for Ukraine or face legal action. He let the deadline, mid-March 2026, pass. The government's position, repeated by successive ministers, has been that talks remain open but that it is "fully prepared to pursue this through the courts if required." Abramovich, for his part, took his own grievance to the European Court of Human Rights in April, arguing that a separate Jersey investigation into the original source of his wealth — including profits from the 1995 Sibneft privatisation — amounts to unlawful harassment.

Football finance analyst Kieran Maguire has noted that the ambiguity was baked in from the start: "It has always been unclear whether Abramovich intended to donate all the proceeds from the sale to the Ukraine relief fund, or just the profit." A further £1.4bn claimed by Camberley International Investments, an Abramovich vehicle, as loans owed against the Chelsea business could shrink the final sum still further if it is upheld.

The chancellor who isn't there anymore

The standoff has a peculiar political footnote. It was Rachel Reeves, as chancellor, who made the loudest public push to force the issue, calling it "unacceptable" that more than £2.5bn "owed to the Ukrainian people" could sit frozen in a UK bank account, and joining then-foreign secretary David Lammy in warning they were "deeply frustrated" at the lack of progress. That was the state of play through the first half of 2026.

Reeves is no longer chancellor. Keir Starmer was succeeded as prime minister by Andy Burnham on 20 July 2026, and John Healey — moving over from defence — now holds the Treasury brief, with Ed Miliband installed at the Foreign Office. The legal posture towards Abramovich has not changed under the new government; a spokesperson has continued to insist that "the proceeds from the sale of Chelsea Football Club must be used for humanitarian causes in Ukraine." But the standoff Reeves made her own now belongs to a chancellor who inherited it rather than picked the fight himself, which is its own kind of signal about how settled the government's position has become — durable enough to survive a change of prime minister without a change of strategy.

Interest as its own argument

The £175m figure sharpens the dispute rather than resolving it. It is proof the money has been managed prudently — a sovereign-grade sum sitting safely at a systemically important bank, accruing interest at ordinary commercial rates. It is also, to critics of the delay, evidence that safekeeping has quietly become an end in itself. Every additional month of legal wrangling adds roughly £5-10m to a pot that, on the government's own account, should already be helping rebuild hospitals and homes in a country still under bombardment.

That tension is the real story here, beyond the specifics of one oligarch's fortune. Western governments have frozen roughly $300bn in Russian central bank assets since 2022, and the EU has already begun routing the interest those assets generate towards Ukraine, over Moscow's furious objections. London's own case is smaller and more tangled — a single, named individual's money rather than sovereign reserves, wrapped in a bespoke 2022 sanctions deal rather than a G7-wide framework — but it tests the same underlying question: when a state holds seized wealth in trust rather than confiscating it outright, does stewardship mean maximising its value, or does it mean getting the money out the door as fast as possible, even at the cost of a clean legal argument?

What comes next

The next concrete marker is whatever Healey's Treasury decides to do about the missed March deadline — either an actual filing in the English courts, a fresh negotiating deadline, or continued stalemate while Abramovich's Strasbourg complaint and the Jersey inquiry grind on in parallel. Any of the three would settle, at least for this case, whether frozen-asset interest is treated as a reserve to be preserved or a resource to be spent. With the war entering its fifth winter and Ukraine's reconstruction bill mounting by the month, the answer will be read closely well beyond Fordstam's accounts.
