AstraZeneca's Etcamah fails key late-stage trial days after US approval

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters, where the oncology R&D unit behind camizestrant is based. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's camizestrant missed its primary survival endpoint in the SERENA-4 trial just days after winning US approval on narrower data, sending shares down 3% after-hours.

AstraZeneca spent the better part of two years building a case that camizestrant — brand name Etcamah — represented a genuinely new way to treat advanced breast cancer, one that catches drug resistance before a scan ever shows a tumour growing. On September 4, the US Food and Drug Administration agreed, granting accelerated approval. Seven days later, a much larger trial of the same drug, in a different but overlapping use, missed its main goal entirely.

The timing is the story. AstraZeneca said on September 11 that SERENA-4, a Phase III trial of camizestrant paired with Pfizer's palbociclib (Ibrance), failed to meet its primary endpoint of progression-free survival in previously untreated patients with hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer.[^1] Shares in the Anglo-Swedish drugmaker fell 3% in after-hours trading following the announcement.[^2]

In one sentence: AstraZeneca's camizestrant missed its primary survival goal in the SERENA-4 trial just a week after winning US approval on a narrower dataset, sending shares down 3% after-hours.

Two trials, one drug, different questions

It is worth being precise about what actually failed, because the two trials asked different questions and conflating them overstates the setback. The FDA's approval rested on SERENA-6, a trial that did not give camizestrant to newly diagnosed patients at all. Instead, it monitored patients already on a standard aromatase inhibitor and palbociclib using a blood test for circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA), watching for an ESR1 mutation — a molecular sign that resistance to the aromatase inhibitor is emerging, months before a CT or bone scan would show the tumour growing. Patients who switched to camizestrant at that early-warning point had a median progression-free survival of 16 months, versus 9.2 months for those who stayed on their original therapy — a 56% reduction in the risk of progression or death.[^3] That is the basis on which the FDA, along with regulators in the EU, cleared the drug for a specific, molecularly defined slice of patients.[^4]

SERENA-4 tested something more ambitious: giving camizestrant plus palbociclib upfront, from the moment of diagnosis, to 1,371 patients with no prior treatment for advanced disease, against the standard combination of anastrozole and palbociclib.[^1] AstraZeneca said there was "a numerical improvement" in progression-free survival but that it fell short of statistical significance for the trial's primary endpoint.[^1] Susan Galbraith, the company's oncology R&D chief, put it plainly: "Whilst we are disappointed by the SERENA-4 outcome, it sharpens our focus on maximizing the number of patients who can benefit from Etcamah today based on SERENA-6."[^1]

A regulatory path with a wrinkle already in it

The approval itself was not a clean story to begin with. In May, the FDA's Oncologic Drugs Advisory Committee voted 6 to 3 against recommending camizestrant for approval based on the SERENA-6 data, with panel members raising concerns about relying on a ctDNA switch signal rather than a conventional progression event.[^5] The FDA approved the drug anyway, using its accelerated approval pathway, which permits clearance on a surrogate or early marker with the expectation that further trials confirm clinical benefit. Shares dipped roughly 1.3% on the approval day itself, a muted reaction that suggested investors had already priced in a narrow, ctDNA-gated label rather than a broad first-line win.[^6]

That context matters for reading the SERENA-4 result. Accelerated approval is designed precisely for situations where efficacy is demonstrated in one setting before it can be confirmed more broadly — the pathway anticipates that later trials might disappoint. What is unusual here is the compression: the confirmatory doubts (from the ODAC vote) and the first broader efficacy miss (SERENA-4) landed within the same fortnight, rather than being spaced out over the two or three years such trials typically take to read out.

What it means for the label, and the stock

SERENA-4's failure does not, on its own, touch the approved ctDNA-switch indication — that label is unaffected because SERENA-4 tested an entirely different population and treatment sequence. But it narrows AstraZeneca's near-term ambitions for the drug considerably. A positive SERENA-4 result would have opened the door to camizestrant becoming a genuine first-line, front-of-treatment option — a much larger commercial opportunity than a mutation-triggered switch therapy reserved for patients already failing an aromatase inhibitor. Analysts covering the stock had flagged the first-line setting as the larger prize precisely because it does not require waiting for resistance to emerge.[^7]

The 3% after-hours drop is a modest reaction by biotech standards for a missed primary endpoint, and it likely reflects that the market had already discounted camizestrant's broader ambitions after the rocky ODAC vote in May. AstraZeneca has other camizestrant trials still reading out, including monotherapy studies and further combination work, and the company will need one of them to land cleanly to build the broader franchise it originally pitched to investors.

Watch for AstraZeneca's next earnings update, expected in the coming weeks, for detail on whether the SERENA-6-based label rollout is tracking commercial expectations, and for any confirmatory trial data the FDA will eventually require to convert the accelerated approval into a full one.
