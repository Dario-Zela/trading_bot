ECB holds rates steady, pencils in one final cut for December

ECB President Christine Lagarde, who has guided the central bank through its rate-cutting cycle since mid-2024. — European Union 2023 / European Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.00% and signaled just one more cut, in December, as growth projections show the eurozone economy stalling to 1.0% in 2026.

The European Central Bank left borrowing costs unchanged on Thursday and, for the first time in this cycle, gave markets something close to a finish line: one more rate cut, expected in December, and then a pause.

The Governing Council held the deposit facility rate at 2.00%, extending a hold that has now run since the summer. What moved the needle for traders wasn't the decision itself — priced in almost unanimously beforehand — but the language wrapped around it. New staff projections show eurozone growth downshifting to 1.0% in 2026, and inflation easing back toward the ECB's 2% target over the same horizon. Taken together, the numbers describe a central bank that thinks it is nearly done, not one bracing for a fresh bout of easing.

A cycle nearing its end

The deposit rate has fallen a long way to get here — from 4.00% in mid-2024 down to 2.00% through a string of quarter-point cuts as inflation retreated from its post-pandemic peak. That aggressive descent has flattened out over the past several meetings into a holding pattern, with policymakers repeatedly describing rates as being in a "good place."

What's new is the specificity. Rather than the usual meeting-by-meeting, data-dependent hedging, the Governing Council's guidance now points to a single additional 25-basis-point cut penciled in for December, which would take the deposit rate to 1.75%, followed by a stop. President Christine Lagarde has been careful in past press conferences to resist giving markets a fixed rate path, so the degree of specificity this time — even if caveated as conditional on incoming data — is itself a signal.

The growth-inflation trade-off

The projections underpinning Thursday's decision tell a story of an economy losing momentum but not falling off a cliff. Growth cooling to roughly 1.0% next year reflects a familiar mix of soft external demand, tepid business investment and a manufacturing base — Germany's in particular — that has struggled to regain its footing since the energy shock of the mid-2020s. It is not a recession forecast. It is a stall.

What makes that stall tolerable, from the ECB's perspective, is the inflation side of the ledger. Price growth is projected to keep drifting back toward the 2% target rather than undershooting it sharply or reaccelerating. That combination — weak but not collapsing growth, alongside inflation settling near target — is precisely the condition under which a central bank can justify doing less rather than more. A sharper growth downgrade might have forced the ECB's hand toward a faster, larger cut; a stickier inflation print might have argued for no cut at all. Instead, staff projections gave policymakers just enough room to keep the easing bias alive without accelerating it.

What it means for markets

For currency and rates traders, the value of Thursday's message is less about the destination — 1.75% by December was already close to consensus — and more about the ECB drawing a line under the process. Uncertainty about how many more cuts were coming has been a persistent drag on conviction in EUR positioning; a clearly signaled endpoint removes one of the bigger swing factors for eurozone bond curves and the single currency heading into year-end.

That matters more against the backdrop of what's happening elsewhere. The Bank of England is wrestling with gilt yields at 19-year highs ahead of its own knife-edge decision, and the Federal Reserve is approaching its September 16 meeting with hike odds still firm despite cooling US inflation. Relative to peers still grappling with open-ended policy paths, the ECB's message on Thursday reads almost tidy: nearly there, one step left, then steady.

What's next

The December meeting is now the one to watch, alongside data releases between now and then that could either confirm or unsettle the 1.0% growth call — particularly manufacturing PMIs out of Germany and France, and any further signs of stabilization in energy prices. If growth undershoots even that modest bar, the pressure to extend the cutting cycle beyond one final move will resurface quickly. For now, though, the ECB has told markets where it expects to land, and asked them to take it at its word.
