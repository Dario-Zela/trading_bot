UK gilt yields hit 19-year high as BoE heads into knife-edge rate decision

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the MPC meets Wednesday for a decision markets increasingly see as finely balanced toward a hike. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields hit 5.38%, a 19-year high, as markets bet the BoE's next move is a hike, not a cut, ahead of Wednesday's knife-edge rate call.

The UK's borrowing costs have not been this expensive since the year Northern Rock collapsed. On Thursday the benchmark 10-year gilt yield touched 5.38%, its highest level since 2007, as investors concluded that the Bank of England's next move is more likely to be up than down. The 30-year gilt, which drives the government's long-term financing costs, sits close to 5.9% — a level not seen since 1998.

In one sentence: The 10-year gilt yield struck 5.38%, the highest since 2007, as investors bet the Bank of England's next move is a hike rather than a cut amid resurgent inflation.

This is not a one-day wobble. It is the cumulative result of an inflation print that refuses to cooperate, a central bank that sounds increasingly uncertain of its own next step, and a government whose fiscal room has been quietly eroded by the bond market before it has even delivered its budget.

A rate-setter that no longer looks settled

The Bank's Monetary Policy Committee holds its next decision on Wednesday 17 September, and it arrives in an unusually exposed position. At the last meeting, on 30 July, the MPC voted 6–3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% — but the dissent ran in a direction rate-setters have not had to manage for some time. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill each pushed for a quarter-point rise to 4.00%, arguing that persistent price pressure had already justified tightening rather than patience. That a third of the committee wanted to hike, not cut, is the detail markets have fixated on: it suggests the doves who dominated the easing cycle through 2025 have lost their majority's confidence.

Governor Andrew Bailey has tried to calm the inference that a hike is now inevitable, telling parliamentarians that the Bank's next move will depend on how the data evolves rather than being pre-committed. But his own August Monetary Policy Report undercut the reassurance: it projected headline inflation climbing to around 3.75% in the second half of the year, with a peak near 4.0% in September — twice the Bank's target, over four years after it was supposed to have been tamed for good.

The inflation print that decides it

That forecast now hinges on a single data release. The Office for National Statistics publishes August's CPI figure on Tuesday 16 September — the day before the MPC's decision — making it, in effect, the last input before nine rate-setters cast their votes. July's reading came in at 2.9%, up from 2.6% the month before, driven chiefly by Ofgem's energy price cap increase rather than broader demand pressure. Economists now expect August to show a sharper acceleration, with some estimates pointing toward the mid-3% range, as electricity and gas costs continue to feed through and the after-effects of this summer's Iran-linked oil spike work their way into transport and goods prices.

The Bank has previously argued that energy-driven inflation is the kind of shock a central bank can look through, since it isn't rooted in wage growth or domestic demand. But with headline CPI already at more than four times target and forecast to keep rising into autumn, that argument is becoming harder to sustain politically, even if it remains defensible technically. A committee that voted 6–3 in July, with the minority explicitly citing above-target persistence, is unlikely to treat a further upside surprise on Tuesday as background noise.

Threadneedle Street's problem becomes Downing Street's

The gilt sell-off is not confined to the UK — it has tracked a broader global rise in long-term yields as oil prices, pushed above $100 a barrel by the shuttered Saudi pipeline and Red Sea shipping risk, have revived inflation anxiety across bond markets generally. But Britain has borne the brunt more than most G7 peers, and the reason is political as much as monetary. Prime Minister Andy Burnham's appointment of John Healey as Chancellor in July unsettled investors wary of looser fiscal rules, and every subsequent rise in yields has narrowed Healey's room to manoeuvre before his 28 October Budget. Analysts estimate his fiscal headroom has been roughly halved since taking office, from near £23.6bn to around £13bn, purely on the back of higher debt-servicing costs — a reminder that in gilt markets, the price of borrowing can move faster than any spending review.

The arithmetic is blunt: each quarter-point rise in gilt yields adds roughly £2.5bn a year to the government's debt-servicing bill. That leaves Healey facing a choice between deeper spending restraint or the tax rises that markets already regard as close to certain. Households feel the same squeeze through a different channel. Because fixed mortgage rates track swap rates, which move with long-dated gilts rather than Bank Rate itself, homeowners refinancing this autumn are already paying for a hike that hasn't happened. A 50-basis-point move in gilt yields over the past month alone is estimated to have added roughly £80 a month to the average household's mortgage repayment — pain delivered by the bond market regardless of what the MPC ultimately decides on Wednesday.

What Wednesday actually settles — and what it doesn't

Most economists still expect the Bank to hold at 3.75% on 17 September; a Reuters poll last month found nearly 90% of forecasters anticipating no change through the rest of the year. But holding is not the same as reassuring. Markets are currently pricing a quarter-point hike by December, with two further increases pencilled in for 2027 — a trajectory that would have seemed implausible twelve months ago, when the debate was still about how fast the Bank could cut. Gilt yields at 19-year highs are the market's verdict that the hiking cycle isn't over so much as it never properly ended.

The next fixed points are clear enough: Tuesday's CPI print, Wednesday's MPC vote and minutes, and Healey's Budget on 28 October, by which point the government will know precisely how much of its fiscal headroom the bond market has already spent for it. Until then, every gilt auction is itself a kind of referendum — on inflation, on the credibility of a divided rate-setting committee, and on a chancellor whose numbers are being rewritten by markets before he has said a word.
