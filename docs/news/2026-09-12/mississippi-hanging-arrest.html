Arrest made over death of Black woman found hanged from tree in Mississippi

In one sentence
Jackson police arrested Jarques Ratliff, 51, on a murder charge in the hanging death of Tasia Fortune, 29, after a homicide ruling and a five-week wait that drew national attention.

Jackson police say they have made the first arrest in the death of Tasia Fortune, the 29-year-old Black woman whose body was found hanging from a tree behind a vacant house last month — a case that has reopened one of the rawest wounds in American history and drawn demands for federal intervention.

Jarques Ratliff, 51, was taken into custody on Friday after detectives secured an arrest warrant and two search warrants tied to the investigation. Officers found him walking along Capitol Street in downtown Jackson. He faces a murder charge. Police Chief RaShall Brackney said the arrest followed the Mississippi state medical examiner's determination, delivered Thursday night, that Fortune's death was a homicide — a finding that had taken more than four weeks to arrive after her body was discovered.

"This case remains active and additional arrests will be pending," Brackney told reporters, declining to detail a motive beyond confirming that Ratliff had "an association" with Fortune. Investigators said they had interviewed nearly 15 people and logged hundreds of hours of work before the arrest.

A body, a tree, and a five-week wait

Fortune was found on August 3 hanging from a tree in the backyard of an abandoned home on Road of Remembrance, a street whose name has taken on an unintended weight in local coverage of the case. For weeks afterward, Jackson police would say only that they were treating the death as a homicide investigation "until the medical examiner says otherwise" — a formulation that left Fortune's family, and much of the city, in limbo.

That wait became its own story. Fortune's mother, Christy Spivey, said she had grown frustrated by how little the coroner's office would tell the family, even as speculation filled the vacuum outside it. When the arrest came, she told reporters her reaction was immediate and physical: "I just started crying and told them 'thank you, thank you, thank you.'"

The delay, and the lack of detail police offered while it lasted, became a flashpoint of its own. An online petition demanding an independent review of the state medical examiner's office gathered more than 50,000 signatures. A separate one pressed the FBI to open its own inquiry. Jackson City Councilman Kenneth Stokes was blunter still, arguing that a hanging death in Mississippi "should rise to the top of the food chain" for investigators, regardless of what the evidence ultimately showed. "Whether it's a conspiracy or not," he said, "that's why we got to know what's taking place."

History that will not stay in the past

Mississippi carried out more lynchings than any other state during the era of racial terror that followed Reconstruction — well over 500 documented killings, according to the Equal Justice Initiative, the majority of Black victims hanged from trees in full public view. That history is not confined to archives. In 2018, Willie Andrew Jones Jr., a 21-year-old Black man, was found hanging from a tree in the yard of his white girlfriend's home in Scott County; local authorities ruled it a suicide despite an autopsy that logged the cause of death as ligature strangulation with the manner "undetermined." His family sued and was ultimately awarded $11 million in a civil judgment, though no criminal charges were ever filed.

It is against that backdrop — cases in which official rulings of suicide left families and communities unconvinced — that Fortune's death detonated well beyond Jackson. A group of Democratic lawmakers led by Rep. Ayanna Pressley wrote to Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel demanding a federal accounting of hanging deaths involving Black Americans in the South, arguing the pattern warranted scrutiny above the county level. The Emancipator, a publication focused on the legacy of slavery and racial violence, described Fortune's case as one that had "rekindled trauma" the state has spent decades trying to move past, and never fully has.

Brackney, for her part, has tried to keep the department's public statements narrowly tethered to evidence, resisting both reassurance and alarm. "We have a responsibility to make sure that what we say publicly is supported by our evidence," she said this week, a line aimed as much at the rumor mill as at any accusation of departmental foot-dragging. She has kept in direct contact with Spivey and her husband throughout, she said — an unusual level of access for a family in an active homicide case, and one that suggests the department was alert to how badly a communication breakdown could compound the damage.

What comes next

Ratliff's first court appearance will set the terms of the criminal case — bail, formal charges, and a plea. But Brackney's warning that "additional arrests will be pending" is the detail likely to keep this story open well past a single hearing: it signals police believe more than one person had a hand in Fortune's death, without yet saying who, or how many. The medical examiner's full autopsy report, still not public, will matter almost as much as the arrest itself to a family and a city that spent five weeks waiting for an official word — homicide — that many had already concluded on their own.
