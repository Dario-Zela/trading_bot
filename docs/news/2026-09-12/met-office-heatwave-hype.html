Met Office douses September UK heatwave hype, forecasts near-normal temperatures

The Met Office's headquarters in Exeter, which this month pushed back on tabloid claims of an imminent September heatwave. — Richard Knights / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The Met Office says tabloid claims of a September heatwave are overblown — expect showers and near-normal temperatures, not a repeat of summer's record heat.

The tabloids had already picked their headline: a September heatwave to cap off Britain's hottest summer on record. The Met Office's actual forecast tells a duller story — showers, near-average temperatures, and the kind of unsettled fortnight that rarely sells papers.

In a blog post published this month reviewing its own weather coverage, the Met Office pushed back directly on the speculation. "A return to the exceptional heat experienced earlier this month does not appear to be the most likely outcome," it said, noting that headlines often seize on a single possible outcome buried in long-range guidance rather than the most probable one. Its outlook for the rest of September points to a mixture of showers, rain and sunny intervals, with low pressure dominating and temperatures largely hovering around the seasonal norm — brief warm spells possible, particularly in the south, but nothing resembling the summer just gone.

Met Office meteorologist Craig Snell was blunter still, telling reporters the pattern ahead looks like "nothing like what we have seen during the summer months."

A record summer to live down

The correction matters partly because of what came before it. The Met Office confirmed this month that summer 2026 was the UK's hottest since national records began in 1884, with a mean temperature of 16.5°C — 1.9°C above the 1991–2020 average and 0.4°C clear of the previous record, set only last year. Kew Gardens hit 38.1°C on 13 August, and 54 weather stations across the country broke their all-time daily maximum. England, Wales and Northern Ireland each set new summer mean-temperature records; Scotland had its joint-sixth warmest.

A rapid attribution study by Met Office scientists found human-induced climate change made a summer this hot roughly 130 times more likely — shifting it from a once-in-1,148-years event to one now expected roughly every nine years. Dr Mark McCarthy, who worked on the analysis, called the season "remarkable" and "an example of the extremes we can expect more frequently." Parts of southern England and Wales remain in drought after the driest July on record, despite a wetter end to August.

Why the September story ran anyway

Against that backdrop, it is easy to see why a few warm days in early September were enough to trigger heatwave headlines — readers had just lived through the real thing. But the Met Office's own review flagged the gap between its long-range forecasts, which are framed in terms of probabilities and ranges, and the single confident predictions that headline writers prefer. The same review dismissed parallel claims of an imminent "freezing cold snap," pointing out that isolated overnight lows were being presented as representative of daytime conditions generally.

For now the outlook through the second half of September calls for an unsettled, northwest-to-southeast split, wetter than average in the north and west, with confidence low on any late warm-up. No further heat records are expected.

The next test of the Met Office's messaging comes with its autumn long-range outlook, due in the coming weeks — a period likely to draw the same scrutiny after a summer that made record-breaking headlines feel routine.
