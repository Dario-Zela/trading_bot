Israel says it destroyed a major underground Hezbollah base

In one sentence
Israel demolished a major Hezbollah tunnel complex in south Lebanon with 1,100 tonnes of explosives, testing a fragile June ceasefire ahead of talks in Rome.

The Israeli military said Thursday it had demolished a Hezbollah tunnel and bunker complex running more than two kilometres beneath the Ali al-Taher ridge in southern Lebanon, using some 1,100 tonnes of explosives in a blast large enough to register as a 4.1-magnitude tremor on international seismic monitors. It is the most significant single strike on Hezbollah's underground infrastructure since a fragile ceasefire took hold in June, and it lands at a moment when the wider region — from Saudi oil facilities to Red Sea shipping lanes — is already jumpy.

What was under the ridge

According to the Israel Defense Forces, the complex functioned as the nerve centre of Hezbollah's Badr regional division: command rooms, weapons stores, generator bays, living quarters, showers and a kitchen, built to let fighters shelter and operate for extended periods underground. Israeli officials say troops recovered Iranian-made rockets, drones, anti-tank missiles and hundreds of mines from inside before the detonation, and that construction of the network took roughly two decades, financed and planned with Iranian support.

Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defence Minister Israel Katz framed the ridge — which overlooks the Israeli towns of Metulla and Kiryat Shmona — as a staging ground for what Hezbollah has called its "conquest of the Galilee" plan. The IDF says around 50 Hezbollah operatives were believed to be holed up in the tunnels when the June ceasefire began; it offered them the chance to surrender, which it says they refused, either fighting or attempting to flee. Katz said Thursday that Israel is "ready and prepared to continue the campaign, to continue striking Hezbollah and at additional locations."

A ceasefire that keeps bending

The timing matters. Israel and Hezbollah agreed a ceasefire framework in June, under which Israel retained a roughly 10-kilometre-deep security zone inside Lebanese territory rather than withdrawing fully, and Hezbollah was meant to pull its forces north of the Litani River. Israeli officials say the Ali al-Taher operation, though planned earlier, had been held back to give that framework room to work — and that it was finally carried out only after Hezbollah struck an unmanned Israeli bulldozer with an explosive drone on Wednesday, which Netanyahu and Katz described as a blatant ceasefire violation.

Lebanon's government, led by President Joseph Aoun, has been trying to keep the framework alive rather than let it unravel into open war: Aoun has proposed evacuating Hezbollah fighters from the Ali al-Taher ridge and replacing them with Lebanese army units, a compromise aimed at satisfying Israeli security demands without another round of strikes. Diplomatic talks between Israeli and Lebanese officials are due to resume in Rome next week, and how this operation lands there will say a great deal about whether the ceasefire survives in anything more than name.

Why markets are pricing it in

None of this is happening in isolation. Crude has climbed to around $103 a barrel, its highest since May, as Houthi strikes on Saudi energy facilities and a sharp drop in Saudi output have already put a premium into oil markets ahead of this latest Israeli strike. The tunnel demolition itself is unlikely to move that premium much further on its own — it is a continuation of an operation Israel had signalled for weeks, not a new front — but it reinforces the broader pattern traders have been pricing since the summer: that the Iran-aligned axis running from Lebanon through Gaza to Yemen remains capable of triggering sudden, localised spikes in risk even amid nominal ceasefires.

The more consequential question for investors is less about this specific strike than about sequencing. Israel has now demonstrated, again, that it will act unilaterally against infrastructure it deems a residual threat even while a ceasefire is formally in place — a pattern that keeps a floor under geopolitical risk premiums in oil and in Israeli and Lebanese asset prices alike, without yet crossing into the kind of escalation that would justify a much larger repricing. Whether that floor holds or gives way now depends heavily on Beirut.

What to watch

The Rome talks next week are the next real test: if Aoun's proposal to swap Hezbollah fighters for Lebanese army units on the ridge gains traction, it would suggest both sides still want the June framework to hold despite strikes like this one. If Hezbollah responds instead with another cross-border attack, the cycle of ceasefire-violation-and-retaliation that produced Thursday's blast is likely to repeat — and each iteration raises the odds that Washington and Gulf capitals start treating the Lebanon front, not just Gaza or Yemen, as the trigger for the next serious jump in the region's risk premium.
