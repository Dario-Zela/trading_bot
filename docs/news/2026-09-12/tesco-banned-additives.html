Tesco expands banned-additives list to over 100 items

A Tesco Extra store — the retailer says 114 additives are now banned or restricted across its own-brand range, up from 65 last year. — Matt Harrop / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Tesco has banned or restricted 114 additives in its own-brand food, up from 65 a year ago, getting ahead of any formal UK rules on ultra-processed food.

Tesco has widened the list of additives it bans or restricts in its own-brand food to 114, up from 65 a year ago — a 75% jump that puts the UK's biggest grocer well ahead of any legal requirement to do so, and squarely in a race with rivals to look the healthiest on the shelf.

The additions, confirmed this week, cover ingredients including artificial colours, the sweetener aspartame and monosodium glutamate, all now banned outright from Tesco's private-label range. Others, such as mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids — common emulsifiers in baked goods and margarine — face tighter limits rather than an outright ban. Tesco's guidance to suppliers now favours "kitchen cupboard" ingredients, with additives permitted only where needed for food safety, shelf life or quality, and in the smallest amount that will do the job.

The push against ultra-processed food

Tesco frames the move as a response to demand rather than regulation. Alice Ritchie, the retailer's head of healthy and sustainable diets, said concerns about ultra-processed food (UPF) had moved "beyond trend territory," and Tesco's own research puts the number of customers trying to cut UPF consumption at 81%. The company has added a filter to its online shop letting customers search for additive-free products, and says 65% of its UK and Irish food sales now qualify as healthy, with fruit and vegetable volumes up 10% since 2022.

Tesco is not alone. Marks & Spencer has built out its "Only…" range — products capped at eight ingredients or fewer, with no artificial colours or preservatives — expanding it with a further 12 lines in January. The pattern across UK grocery is the same: premium and "Finest"-tier own-label lines increasingly trade on shorter ingredient lists, even as value ranges lag behind.

Ahead of, not because of, the regulator

The timing matters for how this should be read. There is, as yet, no UK government-mandated additive ban of this kind, and no official definition of "ultra-processed food" in law. The Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition reviewed the evidence earlier this year and found "significant gaps" in the case that UPF status itself — as opposed to salt, sugar and fat content — causes harm, stopping short of recommending a fresh crackdown. What does exist is the HFSS (high fat, sugar, salt) regime restricting promotions and pre-watershed advertising, and a Healthy Choice Framework due to pilot in major supermarkets this year.

Tesco's move, in other words, is pre-emptive rather than compliance-driven. That is the more interesting fact for anyone pricing food manufacturers rather than retailers. Own-label already accounts for more than half of UK grocery volume, with Tesco holding 28.7% of the overall market — its highest share since 2015 — giving it real leverage to dictate additive specifications to suppliers rather than wait for Whitehall to do it. When a customer with that kind of buying power tightens its own rulebook, branded manufacturers selling into Tesco's shelves face the reformulation bill regardless of what parliament eventually decides.

What to watch

The near-term catalyst is the Healthy Choice Framework pilot, expected to test how supermarkets score and display product healthiness before any wider rollout. Manufacturers with heavy exposure to additives now on Tesco's restricted list — colourings, MSG, certain emulsifiers — carry the clearest reformulation and margin risk, since retailer-led standards typically move faster than statute. Whether Sainsbury's, Asda and Morrisons publish comparable additive counts of their own will show whether this is genuine convergence on stricter norms or one retailer's marketing point dressed up as public health policy.
