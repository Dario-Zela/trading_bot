Ipsen weighs US manufacturing investment amid tariff pressure

A pharmaceutical production line packaging tablets (illustrative image, not an Ipsen facility) — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ipsen is weighing US manufacturing investment as Section 232 tariffs push even mid-sized European drugmakers to reconsider where medicine gets made.

Ipsen is weighing whether to build manufacturing capacity in the United States, according to the Wall Street Journal, as the French drugmaker joins a growing list of European pharmaceutical companies recalculating where they make medicine in response to Washington's tariff threats. For a company with roughly €3.7 billion in annual revenue — a fraction the size of Roche or Sanofi — the calculus is different, but the pressure is the same.

The trigger is Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act, which the Trump administration invoked in April to impose a 100% tariff on patented pharmaceuticals and their active ingredients, phased in from July 31, 2026 for large companies and September 29 for smaller ones. Drugs made in the EU get a reduced 15% rate rather than the full 100%. Companies with a Commerce Department-approved plan to build US capacity can cut that further, and the small group that pairs an onshoring commitment with a Most Favored Nation pricing deal with HHS pays nothing at all through January 2029.

That tiered structure is doing exactly what it was designed to do: turning a policy threat into a capital-allocation decision. Ipsen's US-relevant portfolio is not huge — Onivyde, its pancreatic cancer treatment, and Cabometyx, partnered with Exelixis for kidney cancer, are the main US earners, with Onivyde sales up 6.2% in 2025 on an expanded first-line indication. But nearly all of that supply currently originates in Europe: Ipsen makes active ingredients and finished doses at sites in Dreux, France, Wrexham in Wales, and a recently upgraded €52 million facility in Blanchardstown, Dublin. Its US presence, centred on a Cambridge, Massachusetts headquarters, is commercial and R&D-focused, not manufacturing.

A costly hedge for a mid-sized player

The sums already committed by larger rivals illustrate the scale of the industry's response. Roche has pledged more than $50 billion over five years for US manufacturing and R&D; Novartis has put $23 billion behind ten facilities; Sanofi has committed $20 billion through 2030; AstraZeneca and Johnson & Johnson have each signed up for tens of billions more. Across the sector, pledges now total roughly $500 billion, according to Reuters tallies — a figure that dwarfs what a company of Ipsen's size could plausibly spend without straining its balance sheet or delaying the bolt-on acquisitions it has favoured under chief executive David Loew.

That tension is the real story here. Tariff exposure doesn't scale down with company size, but the capacity to build a US plant does. A mid-cap oncology and rare-disease specialist weighing a domestic production line is a signal that Washington's tariff structure is reordering supply-chain economics well beyond the industry's giants — forcing companies to choose between eating a punitive import cost, sharing scarce onshoring capital with much larger competitors, or accepting a margin hit on products in which the manufacturing footprint is already sunk.

The near-term marker is September 29, when the 100% tariff extends to smaller pharmaceutical companies. Ipsen's third-quarter results, expected in late October, should show whether the WSJ's reporting translates into a firm capital commitment — or whether Ipsen, like some peers, opts to lean further into European capacity instead and simply absorb the reduced 15% rate.
