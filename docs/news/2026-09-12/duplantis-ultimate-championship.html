Duplantis headlines glitzy launch of new Ultimate Championship athletics series

In one sentence
Duplantis won track and field's glitzy new Ultimate Championship in Budapest, but the TV-first format's dropped events have split athletes and fans.

Track and field's answer to the shrinking attention span arrived in Budapest this week wrapped in black infield paving, augmented-reality graphics, red-carpet arrivals and, curiously, an original pop song written by a Swedish-American pole vaulter. Armand Duplantis won the thing anyway, clearing 6.20m on Friday to become the inaugural World Athletics Ultimate Championship's first pole vault champion, but the reviews of the event itself were considerably more mixed than his form.

The Ultimate Championship is World Athletics' attempt to solve a problem that has nagged the sport for a generation: it produces phenomenal athletes but struggles to make them appointment viewing between Olympics. The fix, three nights in Budapest running 11–13 September, is an invite-only field of Olympic and world champions, Diamond League winners and top-ranked performers, competing for a $10m purse — $150,000 to each individual gold medallist — inside sessions capped at three hours.

"This was a missing piece for me, to become the Ultimate champion," Duplantis said after seeing off Greece's Emmanouil Karalis (6.15m) and Norway's Sondre Guttormsen (5.95m) for the win.

In one sentence: Pole vault star Armand Duplantis won the inaugural, made-for-TV Ultimate Championship in Budapest, but its glitzy new format has split athletes and fans over what the sport is willing to cut to chase broadcast audiences.

The pitch: sport as show

World Athletics built the event, quite openly, for a screen rather than a stadium. More than 20 broadcasters are carrying it live. The infield has been repainted almost entirely black so that only the event currently under way — picked out under dramatic lighting — draws the eye, a trick borrowed more from stagecraft than sports presentation. Live win-probability graphics run throughout each broadcast, and athletes get red-carpet walk-ins before competing. Duplantis wrote and recorded the championship's official song, titled "Gold," which played to a sold-out crowd of more than 20,000 on opening night — the kind of crossover moment organisers clearly hope becomes a habit rather than a novelty.

The governing body has been candid about the trade-off behind all this. To fit a genuinely TV-friendly window — one session a day, under three hours — the 28-event programme drops shot put, discus, triple jump, women's hammer and the longer track events, including the 10,000m and 3,000m steeplechase, entirely. World Athletics says the calls were made on viewership data and a preference for faster-paced disciplines.

Not everyone is applauding

That data-driven pruning has not gone down well with the athletes it excludes. Olympic discus champion Matthew Denny said the omissions exposed "a bigger issue in our sport" and argued that prize money at this level "can change a career and a life" — a pointed reminder that for throwers and long-distance runners, missing a $10m purse isn't an abstract snub. Triple jump's Pedro Pichardo was blunter on social media: "I really don't understand what your problem is, and even less so making this decision without any explanation."

The counter-argument comes from inside the sport's own marketing machine. Usain Bolt, an ambassador for the event, called it "great to see track and field going in a different direction" and said fans would get "the best of the best." Duplantis, for his part, seemed happy to play along with the spectacle: "The whole show was spectacular... thank you guys, you brought the energy."

The tension underneath

What Budapest actually exposed is a fault line that other legacy sports — cricket's franchise leagues, tennis's mixed exhibitions, golf's shortened formats — have all hit before: paring a sport down to its most photogenic events makes for a tighter broadcast, but it also tells a chunk of the athletes who built the sport's credibility that their event doesn't count when the cameras are rolling. Denny and Pichardo aren't complaining about entertainment value; they're complaining about being edited out of the story World Athletics wants to tell.

Whether that tension is fatal or just growing pains depends on what the ratings say once they're published, and on whether World Athletics widens the programme for the next edition — the event is planned as a biennial fixture, slotting into the calendar between Olympics and World Championships. For now, the governing body has its highlight reel: a sold-out arena, a viral vault, and a pole vaulter with a debut single. What it doesn't have yet is proof that the audience it's chasing will still be watching once the novelty of the black infield wears off.
