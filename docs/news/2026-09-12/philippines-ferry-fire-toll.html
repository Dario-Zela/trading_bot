Death toll from Philippines ferry fire climbs to 35 with dozens still missing

The pier at Coron, Palawan — the ferry hub the MV June Aster was sailing toward when fire broke out offshore — André Héroux / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
The MV June Aster ferry fire off Palawan has killed 35 and left more than 50 missing, despite the vessel having passed a coast guard safety drill in June.

The fire broke out just after 6:45 p.m. on September 9, roughly 1.2 nautical miles off Barangay Marcilla, near Coron in the Philippines' Palawan province. Survivors described two explosions in quick succession, then smoke pouring through the passageways of the MV June Aster before flames took hold. By the time rescue boats reached the scene, the 53-metre ferry — carrying 117 passengers and 17 crew on an overnight run from Manila — was already an inferno.

Three days later, the death toll stands at 35. More than 50 people remain unaccounted for out of the roughly 134 aboard, and coast guard divers are still working through a hull that took nearly two days to cool enough for search teams to board. Forty-three survivors have been confirmed, including two Chilean tourists recovering in a hospital in Coron.

"It's painful to think that your child was asking for help while she was burning," said Lira Daco, whose 20-year-old daughter is among the missing. Another survivor, Arnulfo Nokki Villanueva, recalled the crush that followed: "The fire quickly spread ... people were stepping on me."

A ferry that had just passed inspection

The June Aster was not, on paper, a neglected vessel. Built in 2002 and operated by Atienza Interisland Ferries on the Manila–Coron–El Nido corridor, it underwent a Philippine Coast Guard emergency-readiness evaluation — including a firefighting drill — as recently as June 10. Commodore Noemi Cayabyab, the coast guard spokesperson, said the ship was not overloaded relative to its manifest and that life jackets were on board in sufficient number. "Meron po," she told reporters — there were.

But the numbers sit uneasily next to each other. The vessel's documented capacity varies sharply across records — 277 to 333 passengers depending on which Maritime Industry Authority listing is consulted, against a crew-and-passenger count of 134 on the manifest. Cayabyab herself acknowledged the possibility of undocumented passengers aboard a ship with that much spare capacity, a familiar refrain in Philippine ferry disasters where manifests have historically undercounted the dead. Investigators believe the fire originated in the cargo hold, but the precise cause — mechanical, electrical, or something in the freight itself — had not been determined as of this week.

An old pattern in new hulls

The Philippines runs one of the world's busiest inter-island ferry networks, connecting more than 7,000 islands where flights are expensive and roads don't exist. It also has one of the worst maritime safety records of any seafaring nation. The 1987 sinking of the Doña Paz after a collision with an oil tanker killed an estimated 4,385 people — still the deadliest peacetime shipping disaster on record — and the 2008 capsizing of the Princess of the Stars in a typhoon killed roughly 800 more. In the decades since, no shipping company has been criminally convicted over a fatal ferry accident, according to maritime advocacy groups, even as overloading, aging hulls and inconsistent enforcement keep recurring in official post-mortems.

What makes the June Aster case notable is that it complicates the usual narrative. This was not an ancient, unregistered banca overloaded far past its limits and caught in a storm — it was a licensed operator's vessel that had cleared a safety drill ten weeks earlier, sailing calm September seas. That a ship with a clean recent record could still produce this scale of loss points less to a single derelict company and more to structural weaknesses in how Philippine maritime inspections verify what they claim to verify — passenger counts, cargo manifests, and the actual seaworthiness of onboard firefighting systems under real conditions, not drill conditions.

What the toll means beyond Palawan

For the shipping and insurance sectors that price risk into Southeast Asian maritime transport, disasters like this one tend to move slowly through underwriting rather than markets. Philippine domestic ferry operators are largely locally insured and outside the reach of international protection-and-indemnity clubs that discipline global shippers through premium increases, which is part of why enforcement gaps persist even after high-profile tragedies. Tourism operators around Coron and El Nido — a destination that draws visitors specifically for its islands and reef diving — face a more immediate reputational cost; Palawan's tourism board has not yet said whether the fire will affect ferry bookings into the peak winter season.

The search for the missing continues, and the Board of Marine Inquiry has said it expects a preliminary cause finding within weeks rather than days. That finding — whether it points to cargo, wiring, or something the June Aster's June inspection missed — will determine whether this becomes another entry in a long list of tragedies attributed to systemic neglect, or a rarer case where a specific, correctable failure can be named and fixed.
