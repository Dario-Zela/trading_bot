Zverev to face Ben Shelton in US Open final after Alcaraz and Sinner both fall early

Alexander Zverev, the world number one, is playing his third Grand Slam final of 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Zverev meets Ben Shelton in Sunday's US Open final after Sinner withdrew injured and Alcaraz fell to Shelton in a marathon quarterfinal.

Two years ago Ben Shelton was known, if at all, as a big left-handed serve with a raw game and a famous father. On Sunday he will walk out onto Arthur Ashe Stadium to play for a Grand Slam title, and the man standing across the net will be the world number one — not because Shelton has climbed to the top of a stable hierarchy, but because two of the players who were supposed to be guarding it are not there at all.

Carlos Alcaraz, the defending champion, is out. Jannik Sinner, the world number one for most of the season, never showed up. Between them they have won five of the last six majors. Neither will play in Sunday's final. Instead it is Alexander Zverev, the top seed chasing his first US Open title, against Shelton, the 8th-seeded American who has never been here before.

How the contenders fell away

Sinner's absence was the least dramatic and the most consequential. The Italian, who beat Zverev in the Wimbledon final in July to defend his title there, withdrew from the US Open on August 21st with a right knee injury that had bothered him since that win and kept him out of the entire hard-court swing — Washington, Toronto, Cincinnati, all skipped. It was the first Grand Slam Sinner had missed since his major debut in 2019, and it left the draw without its most dominant player before a ball was struck.

Alcaraz's exit was louder. In the quarterfinals, Shelton beat him 6-7(5), 6-1, 6-3, 1-6, 7-6(7) in a match that did not finish until 3:33 a.m. — the latest finish in the tournament's history, four hours and 28 minutes of tennis that swung entirely on nerve in the final tiebreak. It was Shelton's first win over a top-three player in his career, and his first win over Alcaraz in three meetings. The Spaniard, who had won the tournament the previous year, had no obvious excuse: he simply lost the biggest points to a player who has historically struggled to find any at all against him.

Zverev's long road to the top of the draw

Zverev's path to Sunday has been the season's quieter story. He won his first Grand Slam title at the French Open in June, beating Flavio Cobolli in five sets after three previous major finals had ended in defeat. He followed that with the Wimbledon final, where Sinner beat him for the tenth straight time — a lopsided head-to-head that has shadowed Zverev's career even as the rest of his game has caught up with the ranking.

In New York he has looked less fluent than that record suggests. He beat Karen Khachanov 6-3, 7-6(7), 7-6(6) in the semifinal, and afterwards admitted the tournament had not been straightforward. "After the second round, at about 3 am, we were laughing our asses off about how badly I was playing," he said. "Moving forward I was playing better tennis. Obviously I've been improving, otherwise I wouldn't be in the final." It is his third major final of 2026 — a number that puts him level with anyone in the game this year, Sinner included, but one built on grinding through tight sets rather than the kind of authority Sinner or Alcaraz have shown when healthy.

An American final, sort of

Shelton's semifinal was its own five-set survival act: 4-6, 6-3, 6-3, 7-5 over fellow American Frances Tiafoe, in an all-USA match that briefly raised the prospect of the country's first men's Grand Slam champion since Andy Roddick in 2003. That prospect now rests entirely on Shelton, and the numbers are not kind to him. He and Zverev have met five times on tour, and Zverev has won all five, most recently in straight sets at the ATP Finals in Turin last November.

Zverev, asked about facing an American in front of an American crowd, allowed himself a joke at the country's expense. "Hopefully I can not make the American dream come true on Sunday," he said. It was delivered lightly, but it points at something real: whatever chaos has opened up above him this fortnight, Zverev remains the favourite to close it out, on paper and in practice.

What the draw actually proves

It is tempting to read Sinner's withdrawal and Alcaraz's defeat as evidence that men's tennis has entered a genuinely open era, the sport's version of a changing of the guard. The more careful reading is narrower. Sinner's absence was injury, not form — he remains the man Zverev has lost to ten straight times, and will be back. Alcaraz's loss was one match, decided in a single tiebreak after four and a half hours, the kind of result that happens to great players without unmaking them. What Shelton has actually done is beat one member of the Big Two on a given Tuesday night; what he has not done is show he can do it against the other, or against Zverev, whom he has never beaten.

The more durable story of the fortnight is Zverev's: a player who spent years finishing second finally winning a major in June, reaching a third final in July and a third here in September, arriving at the top of the rankings less by usurping anyone than by simply outlasting the draw in front of him. Sunday will tell us whether Shelton's five-set survival skills stretch to beating the best player left in the tournament, or whether the sport's actual hierarchy — bruised, thinned out, but not toppled — reasserts itself in straightforward fashion. Sinner's knee, and how soon it lets him back on a hard court, will decide how long the gap at the top stays open either way.
