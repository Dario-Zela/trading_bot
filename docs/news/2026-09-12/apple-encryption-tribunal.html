US lawmakers press secretive UK tribunal to open up over Apple encryption case

Senator Ron Wyden co-signed the letter demanding the UK tribunal open up its handling of the Apple encryption case. — United States Senate / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Wyden and Davidson want the UK's secretive surveillance tribunal to stop blocking Apple from briefing Congress on London's push for iCloud backdoor access.

A Democratic senator and a Republican congressman rarely agree on much these days. But Ron Wyden of Oregon and Warren Davidson of Ohio have found common cause in telling one of Britain's most secretive courts to start acting less secretive.

In a letter sent Friday to the Investigatory Powers Tribunal — the body that hears complaints about UK government surveillance — the two lawmakers demanded the tribunal explain how it has handled Apple's long-running fight against a Home Office order to build a way into encrypted iCloud backups. Their complaint isn't really about encryption technology. It's about Congress being shut out of a dispute that touches American users' data and, they argue, American constitutional turf.

How we got here

The row began in January 2025, when the Home Office served Apple with a technical capability notice under the UK's Investigatory Powers Act — legislation that can compel tech companies to help law enforcement access data. The order reportedly asked Apple to build a mechanism letting UK authorities see encrypted iCloud backups belonging to users anywhere in the world, not just Britain.

Apple's answer was blunt: rather than weaken the encryption, it pulled its Advanced Data Protection feature — which extends end-to-end encryption to iCloud backups — from UK customers entirely in February 2025. That meant British iPhone users lost a privacy option available to everyone else, without the government getting the backdoor it wanted.

Apple challenged the original order at the IPT, but the case was dropped after the Home Office narrowed its demand to cover only UK users' data. That might have been the end of it. Instead, in August 2026, Apple filed a fresh complaint at the tribunal, this time challenging the government's underlying authority to issue such notices at all.

Why Congress is involved

The part that has irritated Wyden and Davidson isn't the encryption dispute itself but the gag around it. Apple has asked UK permission to brief the US government on the order and been allowed to talk to the Attorney General, the Vice President and their staff — but explicitly barred from discussing it with Congress.

That restriction, the lawmakers argue, oversteps what a foreign court has any business dictating. "It is wholly inappropriate for a foreign executive body to attempt to dictate the distribution of powers within the US government," they wrote, adding that Congress "is not only a co-equal branch of government, it is the First Branch." No government, they say, should be able to place its actions "beyond the reach of legislative inquiry."

This is not their first letter. Wyden and Davidson, joined by other members, wrote to the tribunal in March 2025 pushing for the case to be heard in open court. Weeks later the IPT rejected the Home Office's bid to keep the entire matter secret, ruling that at least the "bare details" of Apple's claim could be made public — a partial win that set the tone for the latest round.

The bigger picture

Washington and London have historically been close partners on surveillance and intelligence-sharing, most visibly through the Five Eyes alliance. This case exposes a genuine crack: the US intelligence community itself has previously warned that a UK backdoor into iCloud would be "a clear and egregious violation of Americans' privacy and civil liberties" and a ready-made target for hostile hackers, since a hole built for one government rarely stays exclusive to it.

For Apple, the stakes are commercial as well as principled. Encryption is a selling point built into its marketing, and a forced UK exception already means British customers get a lesser version of the product. A public ruling — or a public admission of what's being litigated — would let the company make its case to regulators and customers alike instead of operating under a UK-imposed vow of silence with Washington.

What's next

The IPT has not set a date to rule on Apple's August complaint over the government's notice-issuing authority, and the tribunal historically moves slowly and largely out of public view. The next marker to watch is whether the IPT responds to this week's letter at all, and whether Apple's UK gag on talking to Congress is loosened — either would signal whether the tribunal's culture of secrecy is actually shifting or just absorbing pressure and continuing as before.
