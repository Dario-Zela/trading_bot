Iran/Hormuz: yields ease on de-escalation talk, unconfirmed blasts reported

In one sentence
Yields eased on Trump's claim that Iran wants a deal, even as unconfirmed strikes hit Konarak and Bushehr and Washington denied involvement.

Treasury yields eased for a second straight session Friday, an easing that owes less to any breakthrough than to a single line from President Trump aboard Air Force One: Iran, he said, had "called a little while ago" and "wants to make a deal so badly." The 10-year note, which had jumped more than four basis points to 4.571% on Wednesday after Trump declared the Iran ceasefire "over," slid to roughly 4.54% by Friday morning — second consecutive daily decline, tracking a retreat in oil prices that had briefly reignited inflation fears.

It is a thin reed to hang a risk-off unwind on. Trump paired the deal talk with open skepticism — "I just don't know if they're worthy of making a deal. I don't know if they're going to honor the deal" — and the remark came only hours after the U.S. carried out a second consecutive night of strikes on Iranian targets, which Trump himself described as hitting Iran "20 to 1." Iranian state media reported at least three deaths and widespread power outages from the bombardment. That is not the backdrop of a settled de-escalation; it is the backdrop of a market grasping for any signal that the worst case — a prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz and a sustained energy-supply shock — is off the table.

Explosions without a clear author

Compounding the confusion, Iran's Mehr News Agency reported explosion sounds late Thursday in three separate locations: three blasts in the port city of Konarak on the Gulf of Oman, and six more across Bushehr and Choghadak in the south. Bushehr is home to Iran's only operational nuclear power plant. Local officials offered conflicting explanations — a Bushehr provincial official told state media the site had been hit by "US-Israeli enemy projectiles" before Iranian air defenses engaged, while Konarak's governor said a naval military zone there had been struck by "enemy fighter jets" in two stages. U.S. Central Command told Al Jazeera American forces had not struck Iran "in the past few hours," leaving Israel — whose defense minister said the military was "alert and prepared" for further strikes — as the likelier author, though Israel has not claimed the attacks. None of the casualty, damage or intent details have been independently confirmed.

The pattern this week has been whiplash rather than trend: yields spike on escalation rhetoric, then give it back on a single conciliatory line, often within 24 hours and often from the same source. Friday's rally in bonds reflects that instability as much as any genuine reassessment of risk — traders pricing what Trump says he's hearing from Tehran, not what's confirmed happening on the ground in Konarak or Bushehr.

The next real test is whether Tehran says anything at all. No Iranian official has confirmed Trump's account of a call seeking talks, and Washington has not named a venue or date for resumed negotiations. Until one side puts a specific proposal on the table, expect yields — and oil — to keep swinging on rhetoric rather than settling on a new equilibrium.
