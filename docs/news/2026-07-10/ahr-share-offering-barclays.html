American Healthcare REIT prices 14 million share offering as Barclays starts coverage

Barclays' Canary Wharf headquarters in London. The bank initiated coverage of American Healthcare REIT on July 7 with an Overweight rating and a $61 price target. — Secretlondon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
Barclays initiated Overweight coverage of American Healthcare REIT at $61 a share — the Street's most bullish target — catching up to a stock that raised $705.6 million in fresh equity back in May.

American Healthcare REIT has spent the past two months doing what small-cap real estate companies dream about: graduating. In late June the Costa Mesa, California-based operator of senior housing and medical campuses was promoted out of the Russell 2000 and 3000 into the Russell 1000 and Midcap indices, a reshuffling that forces large index funds to buy the stock whether they have an opinion on it or not. The shares hit an all-time high of $54.68 in the process, capping a run of roughly 50% over the trailing year.

On July 7, Barclays caught up to the story. Analyst coverage from the bank initiated on American Healthcare REIT (NYSE: AHR) at Overweight with a $61 price target — about 13% above where the stock sat that day, and the most bullish number on Wall Street. Truist, RBC and KeyCorp all carry targets in the $56-$58 range; Scotiabank, more cautious, sits at $51. The consensus average is $55.42, according to MarketBeat's tally of analyst notes, which makes Barclays something of an outlier to the upside.

The bull case, and its asterisk

Barclays' reasoning centers on the REIT's exposure to RIDEA structures — the operating leases that let AHR capture upside from senior housing performance rather than collecting a fixed rent check. That exposure has paid off: the company has now posted nine consecutive quarters of double-digit same-store net operating income growth, and first-quarter normalized funds from operations rose 31.6% year over year. Management raised full-year NFFO guidance to $2.03-$2.09 per share on the back of it, with the SHOP segment (senior housing operated by third parties) guided to grow same-store NOI 15-19% for the year.

Barclays also argued that AHR's smaller scale relative to giants like Welltower and Ventas means a given acquisition moves the needle further on FFO per share — a reasonable read when a company is still building scale. The bank flagged leverage of 3.0x net debt to EBITDA as comfortably low for the sector.

The caution: government payers make up 56% of revenue in the Integrated Senior Health Campuses segment, and Barclays worries that skilled-nursing reimbursement rates won't keep pace with rent growth as federal deficits and sequestration pressure Medicare and Medicaid budgets. The bank also cited "stroke-of-the-pen risk" — the possibility that a policy change reshapes reimbursement economics overnight.

The capital raise wasn't concurrent — it mattered anyway

Some recent coverage has implied AHR's 14-million-share offering landed alongside the Barclays initiation. It didn't. That offering priced on May 20 at $50.40 a share, below the prior close, and closed May 22 — about seven weeks before Barclays put out its note. It was structured as a forward sale with BofA Securities, with a subsequent forward agreement for a further 2.1 million shares (the underwriters' over-allotment option) added on May 26, not due to physically settle until 2028.

The timing still matters, just not as a coincidence. AHR raised roughly $705.6 million in gross proceeds while its stock traded near record highs — the point at which selling equity is cheapest for existing holders, in relative dilution terms. Forward sale structures let the company lock in that price without issuing shares immediately, deferring the dilution until settlement. Combined with 3.0x leverage and 6.4x interest coverage, the balance sheet looks built for acquisitions rather than patched together to cover a shortfall.

What's next

The more useful test of Barclays' thesis will be how AHR deploys the roughly $700 million it raised in May. Second-quarter results, expected in early August, should show whether that capital is already working — in acquisitions or development — or still sitting on the balance sheet. Watch too for how the SNF payer-mix risk plays out if Congress revisits Medicare reimbursement formulas later this year; that is the scenario Barclays flagged as the real threat to its $61 call.
