Fed taps Andreessen, ex-central bank chiefs to lead policy task forces

Kevin Warsh, who returned to the Federal Reserve as chair in May 2026 after a first stint as governor from 2006 to 2011. — Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh named 15 outside leaders — Andreessen, ex-central bankers King and Rajan, a Xbox executive among them — to five task forces reviewing Fed methods, due by year-end.

The Federal Reserve does not normally recruit a venture capitalist and a video-game division chief to help think about monetary policy. This week it did both.

Chair Kevin Warsh, five months into a tenure that arrived amid the closest Fed confirmation vote in modern history, named the leadership of five task forces charged with reexamining how the central bank does its job — from the way it communicates decisions to the models it uses to think about inflation. Each panel is co-led by three outside experts, backed by Fed staff, and instructed to report findings to the Federal Open Market Committee by year's end.

"The Federal Reserve's commitment to price stability and maximum employment is unwavering. As is our resolve to pursue our mandate with rigor," Warsh said in the Fed's July 9 statement, adding that the panels would examine "whether policymakers' means and methods, analytical tools and policy approaches can be improved upon."

In one sentence: Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has assembled fifteen outside experts — economists, ex-central bankers and Silicon Valley names — into five task forces to review the Fed's methods, with findings due to the FOMC by year's end.

Five panels, fifteen names

The roster reads less like a central-bank org chart than a conference guest list. The Communications task force pairs former Bank of England governor Mervyn King and former Central Bank of Brazil president Arminio Fraga with ex-Fed official Peter Fisher, and will examine how the Fed conveys its deliberations under uncertainty. Balance Sheet Policy — arguably the most consequential brief, given the Fed still carries roughly $6.7 trillion in assets — goes to Harvard's Karen Dynan, former Reserve Bank of India governor Raghuram Rajan, and former Fed governor Jeremy Stein.

A Data task force under Harvard economist Raj Chetty, former Walmart chief executive Doug McMillon and Chicago's Kevin Murphy will look at the quality and timeliness of the economic signals the Fed relies on. Inflation Frameworks — the panel likely to draw the most scrutiny from markets — falls to Greg Mankiw, Nobel laureate Thomas Sargent, and former Bank for International Settlements adviser William White, tasked with revisiting how the Fed understands what actually drives price changes.

Then there is Productivity and Jobs, which is where the roster stops looking like a central bank exercise at all: venture capitalist Marc Andreessen, Stanford economist Charles I. Jones (currently on leave at Anthropic), and Asha Sharma, the Microsoft executive who runs its Xbox division. Their brief is to assess the economic impact of new general-purpose technologies, artificial intelligence chief among them — a question the Fed has historically left to regional bank research desks, not to the people building the technology.

A chair who campaigned on 'regime change'

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Warsh won Senate confirmation on May 13 by a 54-45 vote — the tightest margin for a Fed chair in the modern era, with only Pennsylvania Democrat John Fetterman crossing party lines. He arrived at the job having spent the prior year publicly calling for "regime change" at the central bank, arguing for a smaller balance sheet and, by extension, room for a lower policy rate. This is also Warsh's second tour at the Fed: he served as a governor from 2006 to 2011, through the run-up to the financial crisis, before decamping to the Hoover Institution and a career of pointed criticism of the institution he has now returned to lead.

Seen against that backdrop, the task forces read less like an eccentric hiring spree and more like the opening move of a chair trying to build an evidentiary record for changes he has already told people he wants to make. The Fed's own framing — that the panels will "operate independently" and "follow the evidence" — is the kind of language an institution uses when it wants findings that look like they arrived at a predetermined conclusion by an unimpeachable route.

What outside advisors can and can't change

The useful comparison is the Fed's 2019-2020 framework review, which was run almost entirely by staff economists and culminated in the average-inflation-targeting regime that arguably left the Fed slow to react to the 2021-2022 inflation surge. Warsh appears to be betting that a review conducted partly outside the building, by people with no institutional stake in defending the last framework, is less likely to produce the same blind spots. Whether three Nobel-adjacent economists and a Xbox executive change actual policy is a separate question from whether they change the paper trail. Task forces report to the FOMC; they do not vote on rates, and nothing in the Fed's statement commits the Committee to adopt their recommendations.

What the composition does signal, clearly, is where Warsh thinks the Fed has been institutionally thin: on how AI and other general-purpose technologies show up in productivity data, on whether the balance sheet has become a policy tool used by default rather than by design, and on whether the inflation framework adopted five years ago survived contact with the inflation it was supposed to help explain.

What to watch

The task forces are expected to report by the end of 2026, which puts their findings on a collision course with the Fed's next scheduled framework discussions. The earlier test comes sooner: the FOMC's late-July meeting will show whether Warsh's rhetorical commitment to the dual mandate translates into any near-term shift in the balance-sheet runoff pace that his own task force has been asked to scrutinize. Markets will be watching less for what the task forces conclude than for how much daylight opens between their recommendations and what the Committee actually does with them.
