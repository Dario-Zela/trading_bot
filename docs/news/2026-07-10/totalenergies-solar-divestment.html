TotalEnergies sells 170MW of European solar assets to focus on larger projects

A ground-mounted photovoltaic installation in rural France, the type of utility-scale project TotalEnergies is prioritising over rooftop solar. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
TotalEnergies exits 170MW of European rooftop solar, betting its renewables growth on utility-scale wind and solar farms instead.

TotalEnergies has completed the sale of roughly 170 megawatts of small-scale solar assets scattered across seven European countries, closing out a business line the French energy major says no longer fits how it wants to grow.

The divested portfolio — mostly rooftop and other distributed generation installations in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Spain, Portugal, the UK and Luxembourg — has gone to two buyers. AMPYR Distributed Energy picked up about 70 megawatts across 17 operational sites in Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, the UK and Iberia. Amarenco took the remaining roughly 100 megawatts. Neither TotalEnergies nor the buyers disclosed a price.

Why the smallest projects had to go

Distributed generation — TotalEnergies' term for projects typically under 3 megawatts, often bolted onto factory roofs or supermarket car parks — sits awkwardly inside a company built to run oil refineries and offshore platforms. In its announcement, TotalEnergies said its "business model is less suited" to that scale than to the utility-scale solar and wind farms that increasingly anchor its renewables arm. Large sites offer economies of scale in procurement, construction and grid connection that a scattering of rooftop arrays cannot match, and they are easier to finance and manage from a head office built around multi-hundred-megawatt projects.

The logic mirrors a broader pattern among the oil majors that piled into renewables over the past decade. Shell, BP and Equinor have each trimmed or reshaped clean-power units this year as returns from early, often subscale bets on wind and solar lagged the double-digit yields available elsewhere in their businesses. TotalEnergies has framed its own approach differently, insisting the renewables push remains core to its strategy rather than a retreat — but Thursday's sale shows the company is still willing to prune the parts of the portfolio that don't scale.

The numbers behind the pivot

TotalEnergies said the divestment will not slow its renewables build-out. The company has added 8 gigawatts of gross renewable capacity over the past twelve months, taking its total to roughly 35 gigawatts of gross installed capacity at the end of March 2026. It says it intends to keep installing at a similar pace through the rest of the decade, putting it on track to exceed 75 gigawatts by 2030 — a target built almost entirely on utility-scale wind and solar rather than the kind of distributed projects it has just exited. Alongside that capacity goal, the company is targeting more than 100 terawatt-hours of net electricity production by 2030.

That framing matters for how the sale should be read. This is not a company stepping back from renewables — it is a company deciding which kind of renewables project it wants to be in. Distributed solar, however useful for decarbonising individual buildings, was never going to move the needle on a 75-gigawatt target the way a single 500-megawatt solar park can.

What it signals for the sector

For smaller developers and the buyers who specialise in commercial-and-industrial solar, the exit of a major oil-and-gas player from the segment is arguably good news: it removes a large, well-capitalised competitor for exactly the kind of assets that AMPYR and Amarenco now want to consolidate. For TotalEnergies, the deal frees up management attention and balance-sheet capacity for the mega-projects — offshore wind arrays, gigawatt-scale solar farms — that fit its engineering and financing muscle.

The next marker to watch is TotalEnergies' half-year results, due in late July, which should show whether the capital freed by this and any further disposals is already being redirected into new utility-scale awards, and whether the 75-gigawatt trajectory is holding up as competitors trim their own renewable ambitions.
