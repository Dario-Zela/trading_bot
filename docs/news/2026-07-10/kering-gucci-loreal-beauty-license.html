Kering hands Gucci's beauty business to L'Oréal for 50 years

In one sentence
Gucci and L'Oréal signed a 50-year global beauty licence, a year early, completing Kering's exit from running fragrance and cosmetics in-house.

Kering said on July 7th that Gucci and L'Oréal have signed a fifty-year exclusive licence covering the creation, development and distribution of Gucci's perfumes and cosmetics worldwide, effective July 1st, 2027, pending regulatory clearance. The date matters as much as the deal itself: it is a year earlier than expected, arrived at by paying off the incumbent, Coty, to walk away from a contract that still had two years left to run.

The headline number is durability — fifty years is close to a generational commitment, longer than most executives' careers — but the more immediate story is what it completes. Kering has spent the past nine months dismantling the beauty operation it built only three years earlier, and handing the pieces to L'Oréal one by one. Gucci was the last and largest piece left on the table.

The mechanics: buying out Coty to move early

Gucci's fragrance and cosmetics line had been licensed to Coty since 2023, in a contract due to expire on June 30th, 2028. Under the new agreement, disclosed alongside the L'Oréal announcement, Coty will accept roughly $400 million to redeem that licence a year ahead of schedule — $250 million payable across 2026, up to $150 million more by September 30th, 2027, and as much as $30 million in performance incentives tied to a smooth handover. For Coty, the Gucci line has generated an estimated $500–600 million a year, a meaningful slice of the $3.8 billion Prestige division that carries most of its margin. Losing it early, even for compensation, leaves a hole its own turnaround plan will need to fill.

For L'Oréal, the payoff is straightforward: Nicolas Hieronimus, the group's chief executive, called the agreement "the start of a 50-year journey, a significant additional growth engine," while Cyril Chapuy, who runs L'Oréal Luxe, said Gucci brings "a unique, highly complementary creative energy" to a portfolio that already includes Yves Saint Laurent, Armani and Prada fragrances. L'Oréal has done this before — it bought YSL Beauté from Kering's predecessor, PPR, in 2008, and has run it profitably since. That history is presumably why Kering was willing to hand over a business Gucci itself once tried to run in-house.

Why Kering is doing this

The Gucci licence is the capstone on a bigger transaction. In October 2025, Kering agreed to sell its entire beauty division — Kering Beauté, built around the 2023 acquisition of fragrance house Creed — to L'Oréal for €4 billion ($4.66 billion) in cash. That deal, which closed on March 31st, 2026, handed L'Oréal the Creed brand outright plus fifty-year licences to develop beauty lines for Bottega Veneta and Balenciaga. Gucci's beauty rights were carved out of that same agreement in principle, contingent on resolving the Coty contract — which is exactly what this week's announcement does.

The logic traces back to Kering's finances. Gucci generated roughly €5.9 billion in revenue in 2025 and, despite eleven consecutive quarters of declining sales, still accounted for 59 percent of Kering's group operating profit. Luca de Meo, the former Renault chief who became Kering's CEO in September 2025, has made reviving Gucci — now under creative director Demna, previously of Balenciaga — his central task, and in April unveiled a plan aimed at doubling group profit. Ceding the beauty licence trades a slower, capital-intensive build-out for guaranteed royalty income and L'Oréal's distribution muscle, freeing Kering's balance sheet and management attention to focus on the harder problem: fixing handbags and ready-to-wear, where the actual damage has been done. Kering's first-quarter 2026 revenue fell 6.2 percent, with Gucci sales down about 8 percent — weakness Kering attributed partly to Middle East disruption and softer tourist spending in Europe.

What the market is pricing in

Kering shares rose roughly 2.6 percent on the announcement, a modest but positive read — investors evidently see the deal as removing an overhang rather than as a concession. The stock remains down about 19 percent year-to-date, however, a reminder of how far sentiment has swung since April, when a single day's 9.3 percent drop on disappointing Gucci numbers wiped out roughly €2.5 billion of market value. Analyst price targets currently span €175 to €360, with a consensus around €282 against a share price near €246 — a wide enough range to signal genuine disagreement about how much of de Meo's turnaround is already reflected in the stock. Bernstein's Luca Solca, one of the more skeptical voices on the sector, has put the tension plainly: "It is easier and faster for the market to believe in a revival, than it is for management to produce it."

The beauty licence does not resolve that argument either way. It converts an operationally complex, capital-heavy business into a cleaner royalty stream, which analysts modeling Kering's margins will welcome, but it also confirms that beauty's high-margin growth from Gucci will now show up on L'Oréal's income statement, not Kering's. The next test comes in two stages: whether EU and other regulators clear the licence transfer without complications, and then, more consequentially, Kering's second-quarter results — due in late July — for the first hard evidence of whether de Meo's broader turnaround is gaining traction beyond the deal-making.
