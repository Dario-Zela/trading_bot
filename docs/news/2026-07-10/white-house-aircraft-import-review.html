White House opens path to tariffs on aircraft and jet-engine imports

Boeing's Everett, Washington plant — the world's largest building by volume — assembles the widebody jets at the center of the aerospace trade fight. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump ordered talks, not tariffs, on aircraft imports after a Commerce probe found national-security risk — leaving a 180-day clock before any duties could follow.

The White House does not usually announce trade actions by explaining, at length, why it isn't taking one. But that was the shape of Thursday's proclamation on aircraft imports: a national security investigation that spent more than a year examining the risks of foreign-built airliners, jet engines and aircraft parts — and that concluded, for now, with a promise of talks rather than a tariff schedule.

President Trump signed the proclamation under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the same statute used to justify tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos, timber and pharmaceuticals earlier in his term. It directs Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer to open negotiations with trading partners over the next 180 days. If those talks fail, stall, or produce agreements Washington judges ineffective, the proclamation leaves the door open to tariffs later. For now, it opens no such thing.

A finding without a remedy

The investigation, run out of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security, arrived at a familiar conclusion dressed in unfamiliar restraint. Its report found that "the U.S. aircraft industry is too reliant on foreign supply chains, raising national security concerns," pointing to decades of what the administration calls unfair foreign market intervention that eroded American producers' global share, thinned out domestic manufacturing capacity, and pushed suppliers toward consolidation. The report also flagged quality-control and counterfeiting risks in imported parts, and argued that competition from lower-cost foreign suppliers has left U.S. firms holding wages flat and hiring cautiously.

That is a serious set of findings by the administration's own account. Yet Lutnick recommended against imposing tariffs immediately — a recommendation Trump accepted. The proclamation frames commercial aircraft as security infrastructure as much as commerce: airliners get requisitioned for troop and cargo transport, emergency response and official travel, and a hollowed-out civil aerospace base means the defense industrial base inherits the same cost inflation, supply uncertainty and order backlogs. The logic points toward action. The proclamation itself does not take any.

Sixty-seven years of zero tariffs

The restraint makes more sense against the backdrop the White House is negotiating around. Commercial aircraft, engines and most related parts have traded duty-free among signatory countries since the 1979 Agreement on Trade in Civil Aircraft, a WTO-era framework the U.S. helped write specifically because Boeing had the most to gain from open access to foreign markets. That arrangement has coincided with a roughly $75 billion annual U.S. trade surplus in aerospace — one of the few manufacturing sectors where America still runs one at that scale.

That surplus is precisely why industry groups pushed back hard once the Section 232 probe was announced last year. Delta Air Lines and major aviation trade associations warned that tariffs on aircraft or parts would ripple into ticket prices and complicate maintenance supply chains; Airbus Americas — which assembles aircraft in Mobile, Alabama, and buys extensively from U.S. suppliers — argued that tariffs would undercut the very U.S. planemaking competitiveness the investigation claims to protect. Boeing, for its part, sources a substantial share of its own airframes from foreign suppliers: engines from CFM International, Rolls-Royce and GE Aerospace's non-U.S. operations, and structural components from Japan, Italy and elsewhere. A blunt tariff on aircraft parts would tax Boeing almost as directly as it would tax Airbus.

There is a second clock running alongside the 180-day negotiating window. A five-year suspension of the long-running WTO dispute over subsidies to Boeing and Airbus — the Large Civil Aircraft case that once produced retaliatory tariffs on both sides of the Atlantic — is due to expire this month. Washington's decision to seek negotiated fixes rather than swing a tariff hammer suggests the administration wants leverage in those talks without reopening a trade fight it has only recently helped wind down.

Boeing's factories, and what comes next

For now the practical effect on aerospace stocks and supply chains is limited, which is more or less the point: the administration signaled it is watching the sector closely without disturbing a tariff-free regime that has served U.S. manufacturers well. Boeing, GE Aerospace, Honeywell and the broader parts-supplier base avoid an immediate cost shock; Airbus keeps unfettered access to the U.S. market and its Alabama assembly line.

What happens next depends on negotiations that have, as yet, no public agenda, no named counterparties beyond "trading partners," and no interim benchmarks. The 180-day clock puts a rough date on the calendar — early January 2027 — by which Lutnick's Commerce Department is due to report back to the President on whether agreements have been reached. Given the size of the sums involved and the industry's history of successful lobbying against aircraft tariffs, the more interesting question may not be whether Washington eventually imposes duties, but what it extracts from Europe and other producing nations in exchange for leaving the zero-tariff framework intact.
