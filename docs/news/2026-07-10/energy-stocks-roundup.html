Energy stocks tread water as oil futures hold near recent lows

An offshore oil platform — energy majors are holding output and payout guidance steady even as crude prices drift near recent lows. — Stephen Gibson / photoeverywhere.co.uk, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oil held near recent lows and Shell, BP shares barely moved, even as Shell cut gas output guidance on Qatar disruption tied to the Iran conflict.

Energy stocks spent the session going nowhere in particular, which is itself the story. Shell and BP shares were little changed in London on Thursday, tracking oil futures that have settled into a holding pattern well below where they started the year — Brent crude near $78 a barrel, U.S. WTI around $72, both still off more than 15% from the mid-$90s levels seen in April.

That quiet surface masks a jumpy few weeks underneath. Brent jumped as much as 5.2% earlier this week — its sharpest one-day gain since May — after the U.S. carried out fresh strikes on Iranian targets and Tehran signaled retaliation near the Strait of Hormuz, the corridor that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Prices have since drifted back down, a pattern traders are calling relief-rally fatigue: each escalation headline moves the tape, but without a confirmed disruption to actual tanker flows, the move fades within a day or two.

Prices hold, drilling doesn't wait

Citi's Francesco Martoccia told clients this month he expects the U.S.-brokered memorandum of understanding with Iran "to hold and turn into a deal over the coming months," and pushed his year-end Brent forecast down to $60–$65 a barrel — a bet that supply fear is overpriced relative to the underlying glut. U.S. drillers appear to be leaning the same way cautiously: Baker Hughes reported the oil-rig count rose by five to 445 in the week to July 3, its highest mark since May 2025 and a third straight weekly increase, as producers add capacity even with prices well off their highs.

Payouts hold, output doesn't

The majors, for their part, are behaving as though nothing has changed. UBS analyst Joshua Stone expects BP to raise its dividend 4% from the prior quarter and Shell to maintain its $3 billion quarterly buyback when both report second-quarter results — Shell on July 30 — with TotalEnergies and Repsol also holding their buyback programs steady. Refining has done the heavy lifting: European refining margins jumped roughly 70% quarter-on-quarter to an average of $25 a barrel, cushioning the hit from softer crude.

Production is where the conflict is actually showing up. Shell trimmed its second-quarter Integrated Gas guidance to 610,000–650,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, down sharply from 909,000 in the first quarter, attributing the drop directly to "the impact of the Middle East conflict on Qatari volumes." It's a reminder that the Hormuz story isn't purely theoretical for oil-major balance sheets, even if crude prices themselves haven't broken out. In Asia, Sembcorp Industries shares fell 3.7% after Citi cut its profit estimates 6–14%, citing the same conflict-driven pressure on regional energy costs.

The next real test comes with earnings. Shell's July 30 update should show whether the trading desk gains it flagged offset the Qatar shortfall, and whether BP follows through on the dividend bump UBS is penciling in. Until then, expect the sector to keep tracking headlines out of the Gulf more closely than any spreadsheet.
