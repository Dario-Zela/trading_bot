Chipmakers lead a tech rally as markets shrug off Iran tensions

A US aircraft carrier transits the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint at the center of this week's US-Iran escalation, even as oil traders shrugged off the risk. — J. Alexander Delgado / U.S. Navy, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Chipmakers and SK Hynix's oversubscribed IPO drove a tech rally Thursday even as US-Iran strikes escalated and oil prices fell instead of rising.

Markets found something to like on Thursday that had nothing to do with geopolitics. The Nasdaq Composite rose 1.30% to 26,206.89, the S&P 500 added 0.81% to 7,543.64, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average edged up 0.27% to 52,487.41 — even as the US and Iran traded a second straight day of strikes and oil, the asset that is supposed to price that kind of risk, went the other way entirely.

Brent crude fell 2.2% to settle at $76.30 a barrel; West Texas Intermediate dropped roughly 2% to $72.08. That happened on a day the Pentagon confirmed strikes on some 90 Iranian targets and Tehran retaliated against US-allied positions across the region. In a market functioning on textbook risk-off logic, oil spikes and growth stocks wobble. Thursday ran the tape backwards.

Chips do the heavy lifting

The rally had a narrow, identifiable engine: semiconductors. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF climbed roughly 2.5% on the day, but the real action was in individual names. Lumentum Holdings jumped 12.3% to close at $794.30, part of a run that has taken the optics-and-laser supplier up more than 115% this year on AI-infrastructure demand. SanDisk rose as much as 9% on a memory-chip rebound. Micron Technology climbed after unveiling a $3 billion investment in its US supply chain, giving investors a concrete data point in a sector that has traded mostly on narrative.

Applied Materials was the standout of the equipment makers, up 9.8% to $626.58 after chief executive Gary Dickerson told Nikkei Asia the company has "tremendous visibility" into customer demand roughly 24 months out, with chipmakers now booking equipment orders that far in advance. Mizuho's Vijay Rakesh took the cue and lifted his price target on the stock from $540 to $650, keeping an Outperform rating. The message from the equipment side of the industry is that the AI capital-spending cycle is not just holding — it is being planned years, not quarters, ahead.

A $28 billion stress test for the AI trade

The clearest read on how much conviction is behind that spending came from SK Hynix, the South Korean memory maker, which priced a US listing of 177.9 million American depositary receipts that was more than seven times oversubscribed. Demand for the roughly $28 billion offering came from global long-only funds, sovereign wealth funds and specialist technology investors — Baillie Gifford, Coatue Management and Situational Awareness Partners were all reported to be seeking allocations worth up to $7 billion combined. SK Hynix says the proceeds go toward new fabrication capacity in South Korea and extreme ultraviolet lithography tools, the same equipment Applied Materials and Lam Research supply.

An oversubscription of that size is not a subtle signal. It says institutional money is willing to pay up for exposure to memory chips at the exact moment a regional war is intensifying a few thousand miles from some of that same supply chain's shipping lanes. Investors, in effect, priced the AI trade and the Iran risk premium separately — and gave far more weight to the former.

Why the oil market didn't confirm the fear

The divergence is the story. Traders in the crude market are, by design, supposed to be the ones most sensitive to a shooting war involving a country that sits on the Strait of Hormuz, the transit point for roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil trade. Instead, Brent snapped a two-day rally, with desks describing the move as a bet that de-escalation, not further supply disruption, is the more likely next chapter. That reading leans on unconfirmed reports of quieter diplomatic channels even as the strikes continued — a wager on tone rather than on any concrete ceasefire.

That leaves two plausible explanations sitting side by side. One is that markets have built up a genuine, well-founded conviction that AI-driven chip demand is durable enough to absorb geopolitical shocks that would once have triggered a broader flight to safety. The other is simpler and less flattering: that repeated rounds of Middle East escalation without a durable supply disruption have desensitized the risk-premium trade, and traders are now systematically underpricing tail risk in energy markets. Both can be true at once, and algorithmic strategies built on historical risk-off correlations — sell equities, buy oil and bonds when conflict headlines hit — are the ones most exposed if the second explanation turns out to be the operative one.

What comes next

SK Hynix's shares are due to begin US trading Friday, and the scale of that debut — against a $28 billion raise and sevenfold demand — will be the next real test of whether Thursday's enthusiasm was a one-day pop or the start of a broader repricing of the memory cycle. Earnings season begins to answer the other half of the question: Applied Materials, Micron and the rest of the chip-equipment complex report over the coming weeks, and their forward guidance will show whether the 24-month visibility Dickerson described survives contact with actual order books. Oil traders, meanwhile, are watching the Strait of Hormuz for any sign that Thursday's pullback in crude was premature.
