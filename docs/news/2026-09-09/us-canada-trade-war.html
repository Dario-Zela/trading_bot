US-Canada trade war escalates as Ottawa hits back and Trump curbs federal purchasing

President Trump, whose administration ordered Canadian goods stripped from federal procurement the same day Ottawa's retaliatory tariffs took effect. — Daniel Torok / White House (public domain)
In one sentence
Canada's $20bn retaliatory tariffs and Trump's ban on Canadian federal procurement escalated the US-Canada trade war on the same day, bypassing USMCA's usual protections.

The trade relationship that Canadian and American officials once described as the most successful in the world is now running on tit-for-tat tariff schedules. On September 8, Ottawa's retaliatory duties on roughly C$27.6 billion (about US$20 billion) of American goods took effect, matching, dollar for dollar, the 50% US tariffs on Canadian imports that came into force after talks between the two governments collapsed. Hours later, President Trump directed federal agencies to strip Canadian-origin products from US government purchasing altogether — a second front opened on the same day the first one went live.

For traders, the significance isn't any single tariff line. It's the pattern: a bilateral relationship built on USMCA's presumption of predictability is now generating fresh escalation roughly every few weeks, with each round wider than the last.

Ottawa's $20 billion answer

Canada's counter-tariffs, unveiled by Prime Minister Mark Carney's government in late August and triggered on September 8, apply duties of 15%, 25% and 50% across more than 700 US product lines — steel, aluminum, dairy, seafood, appliances, wood and paper products, clothing and farm equipment among them. Ottawa says the measures mirror Washington's own tariff structure precisely, a deliberate choice meant to signal proportionality rather than provocation.

The immediate trigger was the breakdown of negotiations on the preceding Friday. Carney was blunt about why: "They asked too much and offered too little," he said, adding that Canada had offered to drop its remaining tariffs on steel, aluminum and autos if Washington reciprocated. Days earlier, describing the broader posture, he had put it more starkly still: "You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked."

What makes this round structurally different from earlier skirmishes is the erosion of the USMCA carve-out. Previous US tariff actions since April 2025 generally exempted goods that complied with USMCA rules of origin. This time, Washington's 50% duty on $20 billion of Canadian imports applies without that exemption — a signal, traders reading the fine print concluded, that the agreement's protections are no longer assumed to hold even for compliant goods. Ottawa has paired the tariffs with a C$7.5 billion domestic support package for exposed small businesses and workers, an acknowledgment that the retaliation carries a real domestic cost.

Washington turns to the procurement lever

The second thread lands on the same day and sharpens the picture of an administration willing to use tools beyond tariff schedules. Trump directed the General Services Administration and the US Trade Representative to remove Canadian-origin products — including alcohol and dairy — from federal procurement lists, and to bar Canadian firms from competing for US government contracts.

In a Truth Social post, Trump framed the move as retaliation for what he called a "Canadian Trade Scam," arguing that Canada already excludes US firms from its own procurement market. The order to GSA is explicitly conditional: agencies are to strip Canadian products from the Multiple Award Schedules "unless Canada restores full and fair reciprocity for American Farmers and Companies" — language that ties the measure to the long-running dispute over Canadian dairy quotas and supply management, a sore point in US-Canada trade talks for years. The White House pegged the value of affected Canadian goods at roughly $50 billion, a figure that dwarfs the tariff package it sits alongside.

The scope is genuinely open-ended. Federal procurement touches defense contractors, infrastructure suppliers and a long tail of subcontractors on both sides of the border; unlike a tariff schedule, an exclusion from GSA's Multiple Award Schedules doesn't come with a published list of affected SKUs, which leaves companies with federal contracts to work out their own exposure.

What it means for markets

The two measures reinforce each other in ways that matter for positioning. The tariff package is priced and dated — traders can model the sector exposure by reading Canada's published product list. The procurement order is not: its conditional phrasing ties resolution to a dairy dispute that has resisted settlement through multiple rounds of USMCA-era negotiation, meaning the measure could persist indefinitely or reverse on short notice.

That asymmetry has shown up in the currency. USD/CAD has climbed through the summer as tariff actions expanded beyond USMCA's traditional protections, with desks flagging that the currency now carries a standing risk premium tied to trade-policy headlines rather than the usual drivers of rate differentials and oil prices. Sector-specific exposure — dairy, steel, autos, farm equipment — adds a second layer of risk for supply chains that had, until this year, treated USMCA compliance as a reliable shield.

The next scheduled catalyst is the formal USMCA joint review, which entered its mandatory six-year assessment period in July and remains unresolved. With tariff exemptions for USMCA-compliant goods already lapsing in practice, the review's outcome will determine whether the agreement's protections are restored, narrowed, or effectively left for dead — a question now more urgent for currency and supply-chain risk than at any point since the pact took effect.
