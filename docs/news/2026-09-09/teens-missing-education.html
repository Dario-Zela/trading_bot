BEYOND THE TAPE · UK · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-09 · 4 min read

Thousands of teenagers missing education 'under the radar', report warns

An empty classroom — colleges in England, unlike schools, are not required to collect or report attendance data. — jen light / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A Children's Commissioner report finds roughly 42,000 English teenagers enrolled in college are attending less than half the time, nearly doubling the real scale of youth disengagement beyond official NEET figures.

There is a particular kind of statistic that comforts policymakers precisely because it is countable: attendance percentages, NEET rates, exclusion figures. They imply the state can see its children. A report from England's Children's Commissioner, published this week, suggests that for tens of thousands of teenagers, it cannot.

Dame Rachel de Souza's office gathered attendance data from 262 college groups in England and found that in spring 2026, an estimated 41,995 16- and 17-year-olds officially enrolled in further education were missing at least half of their expected sessions. They are not dropouts on paper — they remain on college registers, counted as "in education" — but in practice they have vanished from classrooms almost as thoroughly as if they had never enrolled.

"It's under the radar and these kids are not getting the support that they need," Dame Rachel said.

A gap the system wasn't built to see

The blind spot exists by design, in a sense. Schools in England are legally required to record and report daily attendance, feeding a national data pipeline that flags persistent and severe absence in something close to real time. Colleges and sixth forms educating 16- and 17-year-olds face no equivalent duty. A teenager who stops turning up to school triggers alarms; one who stops turning up to college can simply fall out of view; the enrolment record says one thing, the empty seat says another.

The scale, once measured, is hard to shrug off. Beyond the severely absent group, the commissioner's report found 52.2% of 16- and 17-year-old college students had attendance below 90% — the threshold used to define "persistent absence" in schools. Only 5.9% were formally logged as severely absent through existing channels, a fraction of what the college-level data actually showed.

Doubling the official picture

The consequence for national statistics is stark. Officially, 46,520 16- and 17-year-olds in England are classed as NEET — not in education, employment or training. Add the roughly 42,000 who are enrolled but effectively absent, and the population of genuinely disengaged teenagers is close to double what the headline figure suggests. Nationally, about one in eight young people aged 16 to 24 are NEET; the true rate among the youngest cohort may be running well ahead of that.

The reasons teenagers stop showing up read as a familiar list rather than a mystery: mental health difficulties, poverty, unreliable transport, caring responsibilities at home, the jolt of moving from the structure of school to the relative freedom of college, and part-time jobs that pay now for shifts that matter more than a qualification eight months away. Liam Sloan, principal at Bolton College, put it plainly to the BBC — a £30 shift can outweigh, in a 16-year-old's calculus, the promise of a certificate that lies half a year off. Cath Sezen of the Association of Colleges has echoed the concern that the sector has neither the funding nor the mandate to chase absence the way schools do.

Why the sieve matters

None of this means the Department for Education has been careless with its own numbers — the school-age attendance figures it does collect are broadly reliable. The problem is one of coverage, not accuracy: a system built around compulsory schooling was never extended to catch the post-16 population in the same way, and nobody closed that gap when participation in some form of education or training became compulsory to 18. The result is an official picture that looks precise while missing a population roughly the size of a large town's worth of teenagers.

That matters beyond this one report. Funding formulas, intervention programmes and political claims about youth engagement all lean on NEET and attendance statistics as though they were complete counts. If a report like this one is right, those numbers have been undercounting the problem for years — not through any single error, but through a structural gap nobody was required to fill.

The Department for Education has yet to say whether it will extend mandatory attendance reporting to further education providers, which would be the most direct fix. Dame Rachel's report recommends exactly that. Whether ministers act may be the more telling test — of the 42,000, and of how seriously an "under the radar" population gets treated once someone has finally drawn the radar.
