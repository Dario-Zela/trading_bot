Fed hike odds jump to 58% as Chair Warsh turns hawkish

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, whose hawkish Jackson Hole remarks upended rate-cut expectations. — Federal Reserve (public domain)
In one sentence
Futures markets now price 58% odds of a Fed rate hike on Sept 16, up from 36%, after Chair Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech collided with tariff and Hormuz-driven inflation.

With six days to go before the Federal Reserve's September policy meeting, futures traders have done something few expected as recently as August: price in better-than-even odds of a rate increase. Fed funds futures tracked by CME's FedWatch tool put the probability of a 25-basis-point hike on September 16 at roughly 58%, up from about 36% two weeks ago and from near-zero as recently as July, when the consensus still leaned toward a fourth-quarter cut. Kalshi has the hike at 59% against 42% for no change; Polymarket shows 53% for the meeting itself and 71% for a hike somewhere in 2026.

The pivot traces to one man and one speech. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, five months into the job, used his keynote at the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium on August 28 to tell a room of central bankers and academics that summer's softer inflation prints hadn't changed his mind about the underlying trend.

"While this summer's readings were better than expected, they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," Warsh said. "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do."

It was not, on its face, a declaration of intent. Warsh made a point of distancing himself from the practice of pre-committing the committee to a path — "call it an outline, call it a trail map, but please just don't call it forward guidance," he told the audience, drawing a laugh. But markets read the substance over the caveat. Within hours, the two-year Treasury yield had jumped more than 12 basis points to 4.356%, the dollar strengthened, gold slipped, and the implied odds of a September hike very nearly doubled.

Two shocks, one inflation problem

What gives Warsh's framing teeth is that it arrives alongside two supply-side inflation shocks that are, unusually, hitting the economy at the same time. The first is tariffs: duties imposed across a swathe of trading partners this year continue to work their way into consumer prices for goods, from apparel to appliances. The second, more acute in recent weeks, is energy. The war between the United States, Israel and Iran has choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a chokepoint that before the conflict carried roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and a significant share of global LNG. Refined fuel prices are up on the order of 80% year-to-date, and diesel margins in particular have been driven to records — a dynamic detailed in this edition's look at the tanker war in the Gulf.

Warsh's argument, delivered obliquely at Jackson Hole, is that more than half the goods and services in the government's basket are now rising at 3% or faster year-over-year — too broad a pattern, in his telling, to wave away as a pair of temporary shocks that will simply fade. That is a notably different read from the one his predecessor, Jerome Powell, tended to offer about tariff-driven price increases: that they were a one-time adjustment to price levels rather than a sustained inflation problem.

Data released this past week did little to settle the argument either way. August's producer price index landed Thursday and the consumer price index followed Friday, both showing headline inflation still running above 3% and continuing to outpace wage growth — numbers that traders on Kalshi and Polymarket used to push hike bets further into the 60s before settling back toward the high 50s ahead of the meeting.

Not everyone is buying it

The repricing has not gone unchallenged. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC this week that he views the current inflation pressure as a supply shock, and that the Fed has traditionally avoided raising rates into a supply shock unless it sees clear second- or third-order effects feeding into wages and expectations. David Kelly, chief global strategist at JPMorgan Asset Management, went further, arguing the economy doesn't show the momentum Warsh's speech implied and that markets moved too fast in assigning a coin-flip probability, let alone one pushing past 55%.

That tension — a market pricing conviction the data doesn't yet fully support — is itself part of the story. Warsh has been chair only since May, appointed by President Trump after Powell's term expired, and his Jackson Hole remarks were his fullest public accounting yet of how he weighs tariffs, the Iran war and AI-driven investment against the Fed's 2% target. Investors are still calibrating how much of his rhetoric is signal and how much is a new chair establishing inflation-fighting credentials early in his tenure. Warsh himself seemed to anticipate the criticism, telling the Jackson Hole audience the Fed should not "indulge a regime in which market participants are looking primarily to the Fed for their next trade" — an unusual admission, from a sitting chair, that the market's reaction to his own words might be getting ahead of the committee's actual deliberations.

What a hike would mean, and what happens if there isn't one

A quarter-point increase would be the Fed's first hike since 2023 and a sharp reversal of the easing cycle that had been underway. For rate-sensitive trades — long-duration bonds, growth equities, positioning built around further cuts — a hike would force rapid unwinding. Options markets already show elevated implied volatility around the September 16 decision, consistent with a genuinely contested outcome rather than a formality.

A hold, on the other hand, would validate the Bessent-Kelly view that the Fed is looking through a supply shock rather than chasing it, and could trigger its own sharp repricing in the opposite direction — yields lower, dollar softer — simply because so much hike premium has already been built into short-end rates.

Either way, the next six days carry more information than usual. Fed officials enter the blackout period ahead of the meeting this week, meaning Warsh and his colleagues will make no further public remarks before the decision itself lands on September 16, alongside updated quarterly economic projections and a fresh dot plot that will show, for the first time under Warsh's chairmanship, where each policymaker actually sees rates heading.
