NATS air traffic fault leaves UK passengers facing second day of delays

NATS' London Area Control Centre in Swanwick, Hampshire, where flight-planning systems failed on Tuesday. — Peter Facey / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A NATS flight-processing fault grounded nearly 1,000 UK flights Tuesday, with knock-on delays into Wednesday and renewed calls for the CEO to quit.

A software fault at Britain's air traffic control provider grounded hundreds of flights on Tuesday, and the disruption it triggered was still rippling through UK airports on Wednesday, with passengers facing a second day of delays and cancellations.

The problem began at 1:46pm on Tuesday, when NATS — the company that manages air traffic over the UK's airspace — flagged a fault in the system that processes flight plans and distributes them to controllers. It is not a radar or communications failure; the issue sits in the software layer that tells controllers which aircraft are flying where. With that system unreliable, NATS cut the flow of departures to keep the skies safe, and airports from Heathrow and Gatwick to Manchester, Stansted, Edinburgh and Bristol began stacking up delays. Engineers said they had a fix in place by 4:40pm, but full recovery took until around 7:30pm. By then, flight trackers were counting close to 1,000 cancelled or delayed services, and the backlog was too large to clear overnight. More than 1,300 flights were affected across the two days combined, with Wednesday alone adding upwards of 170 cancellations as airlines worked flights and crews back into position.

Ryanair said more than 65,000 of its passengers had been hit, some facing delays of up to eight hours, and its chief operating officer, Neal McMahon, used a statement to call for the resignation of NATS chief executive Martin Rolfe: "How many more failures do passengers have to suffer before overpaid failure Nats CEO Martin Rolfe accepts responsibility for his repeated mismanagement and resigns?" Wizz Air went further, describing the organisation as "not fit for purpose." Rolfe did not resign, telling reporters he "always accepts full responsibility" for the outage, while framing his priority differently: "What I absolutely take responsibility for is keeping people safe and delivering the best service we possibly can on a day like today."

A pattern, not an accident

This is the third failure traced to NATS' Swanwick control centre since the August 2023 bank holiday meltdown, when a single corrupted flight plan crashed both the primary and backup processing systems and stranded hundreds of thousands of travellers. A CAA-commissioned independent review of that episode produced 34 recommendations spanning technology, governance and crisis communication — findings that, aviation analyst John Strickland noted this week, have not stopped a similar fault from cascading through the network again. "Even brief outages cause severe consequences," he said, "because delays quickly cascade through tightly scheduled airline networks." The recurrence is what turns a bad day for passengers into a governance question for regulators: NATS operates as a public-private monopoly provider for UK airspace, and each repeat failure narrows the room to treat this one as bad luck.

What the market angle actually is

Airline share prices showed no sharp reaction to the disruption, and that is itself the story for anyone pricing risk here — the cost of a day of cancellations, largely covered by UK261 compensation rules, is a rounding error for carriers the size of IAG or Ryanair. The real exposure is regulatory, not operational. The Civil Aviation (Consumer Protection and Regulatory Reform) Bill had its Commons second reading on Wednesday, the same day Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander was due to meet Rolfe directly. Any move to hand the CAA sharper powers over NATS, or to force costed resilience upgrades, would matter far more to NATS' cost base — and by extension the fees airlines pay for UK airspace access — than a single day's delays ever could.

The CAA has said it will request a full report on the fault and assess whether further reform is needed. That report, and whatever Alexander says after her meeting with Rolfe, are the next points where this story could move from an operations story into a regulatory one.
