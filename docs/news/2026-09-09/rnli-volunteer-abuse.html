RNLI chief says volunteers face 'unacceptable' abuse amid small-boats tensions

An RNLI lifeboat crew — the charity's stations are staffed almost entirely by unpaid volunteers. — Ben Salter / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
RNLI chief Peter Sparkes says lifeboat volunteers face 'unacceptable' abuse and doxxing after crews helped rescue migrants near Portsmouth.

There is a particular kind of institution that survives on being liked by everyone and disliked by no one — the lifeboat service, the mountain rescue team, the volunteer fire brigade. Its authority comes from standing outside politics entirely. So when the Royal National Lifeboat Institution's chief executive felt compelled this week to publicly defend his own volunteers from abuse, it was a sign of how far Britain's row over small-boat crossings has spread beyond Westminster and into a 200-year-old charity that has never asked who it's saving before it saves them.

Peter Sparkes, the RNLI's chief executive, said the treatment of crews and staff had become "wholly unacceptable in a civilised society," condemning threats from people who "hide behind a balaclava or a keyboard." He pointed to what he called "deliberate misinformation and a misunderstanding" about an organisation that has rescued people at sea, migrants and holidaymakers alike, since 1824.

A rescue, then a backlash

The immediate trigger was an operation on 5 September, when volunteers from the Bembridge and Yarmouth lifeboat stations on the Isle of Wight helped bring ashore roughly 140 people from an overcrowded "mega-dinghy" in the Channel. Two nights later, hundreds of protesters gathered at Eastney, near Portsmouth, trying to block coaches carrying the migrants on to Kent. Police said bricks and rocks were thrown, seven officers were injured, and some demonstrators chased the coaches along the motorway. Officers were later stationed outside the RNLI's Eastney base.

What followed was less a protest than a targeting campaign. Names, faces and other personal details of individual Bembridge and Yarmouth volunteers were circulated online, with two crew members singled out and accused of having "facilitated the invasion of Britain." Social media pages run by unrelated RNLI stations were flooded with hostile messages. Protesters branded the volunteers — unpaid, on-call, trained to pull anyone out of the water regardless of why they went in — "traitors."

The charity, and the government, push back

Sparkes's intervention was unusually direct for a charity that generally avoids political commentary. It was echoed in government: Digital Secretary Lisa Nandy said she was "personally concerned" about the doxxing and had spoken to technology firms about how volunteers' names, addresses and photographs ended up circulating so freely. The Isle of Wight's two MPs, for their part, publicly thanked the Bembridge and Yarmouth crews despite the political heat around illegal Channel crossings — a reminder that support for the RNLI has, so far, cut across the same lines that divide opinion on migration itself.

That cross-cutting support has a precedent. In 2021, after Nigel Farage called the RNLI a "taxi service" for smuggling gangs, online donations to the charity jumped from a typical £7,000 a day to more than £200,000 in 24 hours, and a crowdfunded campaign to buy the charity a hovercraft named "The Flying Farage" eventually raised over £300,000. The same fundraiser reportedly took in another £5,000 in the days immediately after this month's Portsmouth clashes — a small but telling sign that the backlash-to-the-backlash pattern is repeating, five years and one general election cycle later, with the underlying argument no closer to resolved.

What the charity has at stake

The RNLI's position has not moved: its head of lifeboats, Simon Ling, said last year that crews "rescue anyone in trouble at sea… without judgment or preference," a line that captures both the charity's founding ethos and its current bind. It cannot select which emergencies to answer without ceasing to be what it is, yet every Channel rescue now risks becoming a political flashpoint that its unpaid crews did not sign up for and have no way to opt out of.

That is the practical risk beneath Sparkes's statement. The RNLI depends on roughly 5,600 volunteer crew members who train on their own time and drop what they're doing when a pager goes off, for no pay and, evidently now, no guarantee of anonymity. A charity that has weathered storms for two centuries can absorb a bad news cycle; it is less clear it can keep recruiting and retaining crews in coastal towns if doing the job means having your name and address posted online by strangers. The next test will be whether Channel crossings continue at their current pace into the autumn — and whether the political temperature around them cools faster than volunteer patience does.
