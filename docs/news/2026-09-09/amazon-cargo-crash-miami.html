Amazon-branded cargo plane crash in Miami kills five

An Amazon Prime Air Boeing 767-300 freighter, the same model involved in the Miami runway overrun. — formulanone / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A 21 Air Boeing 767 flying for Amazon overran a Miami runway and killed five ground workers, shutting half the airport's runways and its cargo flow for days.

The fire was still smoldering when the first cancellations began scrolling across the departure boards. By Sunday evening, Miami International Airport — the busiest cargo gateway in the United States — had ground-stopped, and it would take the better part of three days to get flights moving again in anything resembling normal volume.

The cause was a Boeing 767 freighter, wearing Amazon's smile logo, that came in too fast and ran out of runway.

What happened on Runway 30

Amazon Air Flight 7598 touched down at Miami International just before 2 p.m. on Sunday, the last leg of a long day that had taken the aircraft from Cincinnati to Miami, on to San Juan, Puerto Rico, and back again. The 767-300, operated on Amazon's behalf by the North Carolina-based charter carrier 21 Air, was travelling at roughly 128 miles per hour when it ran out of paved surface on Runway 30 and kept going — about 1,300 feet past the end of the tarmac, through the airport's perimeter fence, and into a parked commercial van.

The van, a white Ford Econoline registered to Professional Ocean Service Corp., a contractor that cleans aircraft between flights, had seven people inside. Five of them were killed: Rolando Aleman Leon, 55; Yoel Rodriguez Narajo, 53; Julio C. Pineda, 75; Carlos Acosta Fajardo, 53; and Javierkys Reyes Quevedo, 47. The aircraft went on to strike a Toyota SUV outside the fence line before coming to rest and catching fire. Both pilots on board were treated for injuries and released; three of the surviving ground workers remain hospitalized, two of them in critical condition.

"It is utter devastation," National Transportation Safety Board chairwoman Jennifer Homendy told reporters at the scene, describing debris scattered across the crash site and tire marks that "continue through the grass" well beyond the runway threshold. A team of roughly 30 investigators has since combed the wreckage, and both the flight data recorder and cockpit voice recorder have been recovered and sent to NTSB headquarters for analysis. Homendy has said investigators are looking at crew actions, company training and procedures, air traffic control communications, aircraft systems, and weather — though she has been careful not to point toward a probable cause this early. Full NTSB investigations of this kind typically take twelve to eighteen months to complete.

A cargo hub grinds through its busiest season

Miami International is not a conventional passenger hub with cargo bolted on — it is, by tonnage, the largest freight airport in the country, having handled a record 3.4 million tons of cargo last year, most of it international and most of it moving on dedicated freighters rather than in the belly of passenger jets. FedEx and Maersk both run major hubs out of MIA; DHL Express rerouted three aircraft elsewhere on the day of the crash before resuming normal service by Tuesday.

The practical damage has been real but contained. Two of the airport's four runways stayed shut into this week while investigators worked the scene — Homendy has said flatly that "it is not a completely safe scene" and that reopening depends on investigators finishing with the wreckage, not on a calendar. Monday alone brought 181 cancellations and 408 delays. Derek Lossing, founder of the logistics consultancy Cirrus Global Advisors, called it "a significant short-term capacity and reliability event for MIA" rather than a full shutdown, but warned that shippers moving perishable cargo — produce and pharmaceuticals are a large share of what transits Miami to and from Latin America — should stay in close contact with their forwarders while the runway picture remains unsettled. With half the airfield still down, arrival holds have lengthened and connections have slipped; Lossing's estimate is that a quick reopening limits the knock-on effects to a day or three of delay, while a longer closure would compound.

The carrier behind the Amazon logo

The aircraft was painted for Amazon, but Amazon did not fly it. Like much of Amazon Air's network, the 767 was operated under an ACMI arrangement — aircraft, crew, maintenance and insurance supplied by a third party, in this case 21 Air, a Greensboro-based freight operator controlled by Houston Astros owner Jim Crane in a joint venture with the Canadian carrier Cargojet. 21 Air runs a fleet of roughly 21 aircraft, including seven 767-300 freighters, and also flies for DHL Express. Because Amazon does not hold its own air operator certificate for wide-body freight flying, the model lets the company scale cargo capacity quickly by leasing planes to contractors who guarantee minimum flight hours in return.

That structure has drawn scrutiny before. In February 2019, an Atlas Air 767 flying for Amazon crashed into a Texas marsh, killing all three aboard; the NTSB ultimately traced the accident to a first officer's mishandling of an inadvertent go-around and training gaps that a prior employer's records, withheld from Atlas, might have flagged. Amazon later wound down its contract with Atlas altogether, shifting leased aircraft to ABX Air and other partners including Air Transport International and Hawaiian Airlines. Union officials representing cargo pilots have separately complained in recent years that contractors serving Amazon took on more flying than their staffing could support. None of that history establishes what went wrong in Miami — the NTSB has given no indication the two events are connected — but it means investigators, and Amazon's logistics rivals, will be watching closely for any sign that oversight of the airline's patchwork of third-party carriers needs tightening.

What comes next

The NTSB is expected to release a preliminary factual report within 30 days, laying out timeline and instrument data without assigning cause; a final determination is unlikely before late 2027. In the nearer term, the airport's own recovery is the more immediate story — MIA and the FAA have given no fixed date for restoring all four runways, and that timeline, more than the ultimate finding on what went wrong in the cockpit, will decide how much of this week's disruption ripples into a shipping season that was only just getting underway.
