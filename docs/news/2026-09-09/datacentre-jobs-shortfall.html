UK datacentre boom delivered a quarter of the promised jobs, analysis finds

A UK data centre: capital-intensive to build, sparing in permanent staff once running. — Mark Anderson / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Independent analysis finds UK data centres will deliver about a quarter of the jobs claimed by government and developers in planning approvals.

The pitch to planning committees has followed the same script across the country: approve the data centre, and thousands of jobs follow. In Lanarkshire, ministers promised 3,400. In Northumberland, developers cited 4,000, wrapped around a £10bn campus at Cambois, near Blyth. In town halls from Slough to South Lanarkshire, those figures have done heavy lifting — turning warehouses full of servers, which are among the most capital-intensive and least labour-intensive structures ever built, into politically palatable investments.

An independent analysis now suggests the arithmetic behind those numbers doesn't hold up. Action to Protect Rural Scotland (APRS), a countryside charity that has spent the past year totting up job claims against what data centres actually employ once built, puts the realistic figure at roughly a quarter of what's been promised — an overstatement of about 75%.

The multiplier problem

The gap comes down to how a data centre job is counted. A hyperscale facility, once running, is largely unstaffed — racks of servers cooled and monitored by a skeleton crew. APRS's report, drawing on operator disclosures and a decade of investment data from northern Virginia, the world's densest data centre cluster, found that most sites employ between 20 and 50 permanent staff regardless of scale. techUK's own modelling, based on figures from two UK operators, puts the midpoint at 54 full-time roles per site.

Government and industry totals get to the thousands by adding construction jobs — real, but temporary, typically lasting 12 to 18 months — and then layering on "indirect and induced" employment: supply-chain work, spending by newly paid workers rippling through local economies, multipliers of five to seven times the direct headcount. Northumberland's 4,000-job claim, for instance, breaks down as roughly 100 direct operational roles, 1,200 in construction, and 2,700 indirect or induced — a ratio in which the number a resident might picture, someone actually working at the site day to day, is a small fraction of the headline.

"Data centres employ far fewer than a hundred staff, usually 20 to 50 — but proposals suggest thousands of jobs." — Dr Kat Jones, director, Action to Protect Rural Scotland

The Virginia comparison is the sharper number in the report. Over the past decade the state has drawn roughly $71bn in data centre investment against $34bn in manufacturing — yet APRS's analysis found manufacturing generated far more employment per dollar spent, citing a figure of around 168 jobs per $100m invested against roughly three for data centres. However the exact multiple is sliced, the direction is consistent: this is one of the least labour-intensive ways to spend infrastructure capital, even though it is often justified in the language of regional job creation.

Ed Newton-Rex's FOI, and Cambois as the template

The APRS report echoes an earlier challenge from Ed Newton-Rex, a technology commentator who filed a Freedom of Information request with the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology after Technology Secretary Liz Kendall visited Scotland's first AI Growth Zone in Lanarkshire and cited the 3,400-job figure, including 800 described as "high-paying" permanent positions.

The FOI response showed the department had not modelled Lanarkshire employment from scratch. Instead it scaled projections from the Cambois development in Northumberland — which itself claims around 1,200 construction jobs and 400 operational roles — applying employment multipliers of roughly 1.5 to 1.6 across construction and operations to arrive at the Lanarkshire total. In other words, one contested estimate was used to generate another, with the compounding effect of each round of multiplication pushing the headline further from the number of people who will actually clock in once the servers are switched on.

Government officials have defended the methodology. A spokesperson for the department, responding to the APRS findings, said the report "fundamentally misrepresents how jobs are counted for major infrastructure projects," arguing that construction, supply-chain and economy-wide employment are legitimate components of the total and that excluding them risks pushing investment — and the jobs that come with it — overseas. It's a defensible position in principle: energy and transport infrastructure projects are routinely assessed the same way. The dispute isn't over whether multipliers should be used, but over whether the multipliers being used are honest ones, and whether planning committees are told clearly enough which part of the total is durable local employment and which part is a one-off construction bump or a modelled ripple effect that may or may not materialise.

Why the planning system waved it through

That distinction matters because job figures aren't decorative in UK planning applications — they are frequently the central plank of the economic case developers present to secure permission, particularly in areas where councils are weighing environmental costs (water use, grid strain, land take) against promised local benefit. If the employment case is inflated by a factor of three or four, the cost-benefit calculation used to wave through billions in capital investment was built on the wrong numbers from the outset.

The House of Commons Library's own research briefing on data centre planning policy, published in May, arrived at a similar, quieter conclusion: the number of jobs directly created by these facilities is "relatively modest" relative to their footprint and cost. That a cross-party parliamentary research service and a rural campaign charity have landed in roughly the same place, using different data, is the strongest evidence yet that the inflation isn't a one-off exaggeration but a structural feature of how these projects get pitched.

There are more concrete cases in the pipeline. Skills England's 2026 sector assessment found 68% of priority digital-and-technology occupations already in critical or elevated demand — a sign that wherever data centre jobs do land, employers may struggle to fill them regardless of the topline count. And AI Growth Zones beyond Lanarkshire and Northumberland, including further sites earmarked across the north-east, are still working through planning, each carrying employment projections built on the same multiplier logic now under challenge.

The next test is whether any of this changes how councils actually assess applications. APRS is pushing for a moratorium on new Scottish data centre approvals until job-creation methodologies are standardised and independently verified — a campaign that drew a protest at Holyrood in early September. Whether Westminster or Holyrood planning authorities start discounting developer job figures, or keep taking them at face value, will determine whether this analysis reshapes the next wave of approvals or simply becomes another number ignored on the way to a ribbon-cutting.
