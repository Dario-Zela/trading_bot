US agencies accuse six Chinese AI firms of 'industrial-scale' model distillation

Alibaba's Hangzhou headquarters — one of six Chinese AI firms named in the US advisory. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
CISA, the NSA and FBI jointly accused six Chinese AI firms of systematically mining Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok since 2024 to build rival models.

For two years, engineers at Anthropic, OpenAI and Google noticed something odd in their server logs: accounts that queried their chatbots millions of times, in patterns no human customer would produce, then vanished. On Monday, three US government agencies said in public, for the first time, what those companies had long suspected — the traffic was Chinese AI firms systematically harvesting American models to build their own.

CISA, the NSA and the FBI issued a joint advisory naming six China-based companies — DeepSeek, Alibaba, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI — accusing them of running "industrial-scale" knowledge distillation campaigns against Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Grok since at least late 2024. It is the first time Washington's cyber and intelligence agencies have put their names, jointly, to the claim that Chinese AI development runs substantially on copied American work rather than independent research.

"The sheer scale of these campaigns and their sophistication indicate that distillation is not a supplement to these companies' AI model development, but the critical core of it," the agencies wrote.

What distillation actually is

Knowledge distillation is not new, and it is not, by itself, illegal or even unusual. It's a standard machine-learning technique: a smaller "student" model is trained to mimic the outputs of a larger "teacher" model, letting a company deliver decent performance far more cheaply than training a giant model from scratch. Every major AI lab does some version of it internally.

What the advisory describes is different in scale and intent. It says the named firms distributed enormous volumes of queries across many accounts, third-party platforms and cloud intermediaries specifically to extract the reasoning patterns of US frontier models while evading detection and rate limits — tactics closer to industrial espionage than legitimate research shortcuts. According to the advisory and reporting on it, DeepSeek alone queried at least four versions of Claude, two of Gemini, five of ChatGPT and Grok 4 to help train its R1 and V3 models; Moonshot AI drew on 18 different US models, including large volumes of Claude output, to build its Kimi series. In total the agencies describe billions of tokens extracted across millions of exchanges.

CISA's acting director, Nick Andersen, said the agencies "strongly urge AI companies to take immediate steps to safeguard their platforms against knowledge distillation campaigns," and the advisory recommends specific defensive measures: watching for anomalous prompt patterns and account behavior, tracking mismatches between subscription tiers and actual usage, modifying model responses to reduce the payoff from bulk querying, and sharing threat intelligence across labs and cloud providers. White House science and technology policy director Michael Kratsios drew a line between the two: "Legitimate AI distillation used to create smaller, more efficient models plays a vital role in this open innovation ecosystem. However, large-scale, covert industrial distillation aimed at stealing proprietary U.S. technology and undermining American research is unacceptable."

A dispute that's been building for months

Monday's advisory formalizes accusations the labs themselves have been making for a while. Anthropic and OpenAI jointly went public in February with evidence of coordinated distillation campaigns by DeepSeek, MiniMax and Moonshot AI involving some 16 million siphoned exchanges. In June, Anthropic went further, accusing Alibaba of training its Qwen model family directly on Claude's outputs and formally asking Washington to impose export controls on the labs it says are responsible. By July, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was publicly warning Chinese AI firms they could face sanctions or Entity List designation over the practice.

What changed Monday is who is saying it. Corporate accusations from Anthropic or OpenAI carry an obvious commercial motive — both companies stand to benefit from regulatory action against cheaper Chinese rivals. A joint advisory from CISA, the NSA and the FBI is a different kind of statement: it reflects an intelligence-community assessment, not a competitor's grievance, and it explicitly says the scale of the campaigns suggests Beijing was "likely" aware of them. China's embassy in Washington called the earlier round of accusations a "deliberate attack on China's development and progress in the AI industry"; none of the six named companies had responded publicly by Monday evening.

The timing is pointed. The advisory lands roughly two weeks before President Trump is scheduled to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping on September 24, giving Washington a fresh grievance to raise — or a fresh pressure point to use — at a moment when the two governments are already trading barbs over AI trade secrets and chip export rules.

Why this matters beyond the tech press

DeepSeek's arrival in January 2025 rattled US markets partly because it appeared to show a Chinese lab matching frontier performance for a fraction of the reported cost — an argument used ever since to question the scale of American AI capital spending. If a meaningful share of that performance came from systematically querying the very US models it was supposedly out-competing, the efficiency narrative looks considerably less impressive, and the case for treating Chinese frontier labs as fast-follower copyists rather than independent innovators gets official backing.

The practical stakes are threefold. First, enforcement: an interagency advisory is not a sanction, but it is usually a precursor — the kind of documented finding that makes Entity List additions, export controls or civil litigation easier to justify. Second, defense: US labs are already tightening rate limits, geofencing and account verification in ways that will make their products marginally more annoying to use for everyone, not just bad actors. Third, valuation: if regulators start treating distillation as sanctionable IP theft rather than an unfortunate cost of doing business, it changes the risk calculus for investors in both the American labs doing the accusing and the Chinese firms named in the advisory.

None of this resolves the underlying technical reality, which is that distillation is hard to fully prevent — a chatbot that answers questions will always leak something about how it reasons to anyone patient enough to ask it enough questions. The open question now is whether Washington treats Monday's advisory as the end of the story or the opening move, with the Trump-Xi summit on September 24 the first real test of whether it turns into policy.
