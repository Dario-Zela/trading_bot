Grindr to pay £26m to settle UK claim it shared users' HIV status

Grindr's app icon. The settlement covers data practices from before the app's 2020 change in ownership. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Grindr will pay £26m to roughly 12,000 UK users over claims it shared their HIV status and other sensitive data with advertisers before 2020.

For years, Grindr's pitch to advertisers was the same as any app's: know your users, sell that knowledge on. The trouble was who its users are. Grindr is the world's largest dating app for gay, bi and trans men, and among the data it once handed to outside advertising firms, according to a UK legal claim, was whether a user was HIV-positive.

Grindr has now agreed to pay £26m to settle that claim, brought in the High Court of England and Wales on behalf of roughly 12,000 UK users. The company admits no liability. But the size of the payout — and the fact it was willing to write a nine-figure-in-dollars cheque rather than fight the case to trial — tells its own story about how exposed dating and health-adjacent apps now are on sensitive personal data.

What was actually shared

The claim, filed in April 2024 by the London law firm Austen Hays, centred on Grindr's practices before 2020, when the app was owned by the Chinese gaming company Beijing Kunlun Tech. Claimants alleged that Grindr passed data to third-party advertising and analytics firms that could include HIV status, the date of a user's last HIV test, use of PrEP (the drug regimen that prevents HIV transmission), sexual orientation, ethnicity and precise location.

None of this was hidden in fine print nobody read — it came to light in 2018, when Norwegian researchers at SINTEF, working with the Norwegian Consumer Council, examined the app's network traffic and found that a user's HIV status, where entered on their profile, was being sent to two ad-optimisation firms, Apptimize and Localytics, alongside standard device and advertising identifiers. Grindr said within a day that it would stop sending HIV data to those partners. It was not the end of the matter: Norway's data protection authority fined Grindr roughly €10m in 2021 over its ad-data practices more broadly, and the UK's Information Commissioner's Office issued a reprimand in 2022, finding the company had failed to give users "effective and transparent" information about how their data was processed.

The UK claim built on that record. It was an opt-in group action — claimants had to actively register, rather than being automatically included — run on a no-win-no-fee basis, with legal costs capped so claimants keep at least 52% of any award. Roughly 11,000 people had signed up by mid-2025; the final tally settled at around 12,000. The High Court also granted anonymity orders shielding claimants' identities, reflecting the sensitivity of what they were alleging had been exposed.

The settlement, and why it's opt-in rather than class-wide

Under the deal, Grindr pays £13m by the end of 2026 and a further £13m by the end of March 2027. Divided evenly across roughly 12,000 claimants, that works out to about £2,167 each, though individual payouts may vary. A Grindr spokesperson said the settlement "includes no findings or admission of liability," adding that while the company disputes the allegations, it "recognises and acknowledges the distress and loss of trust expressed by some of its UK users regarding that pre-2020 period." Austen Hays said simply that it thanked its clients "for trusting us with this sensitive case."

The opt-in structure matters, and it's a direct legacy of a different case. In 2021, the UK Supreme Court threw out a bid by campaigner Richard Lloyd to bring a US-style representative claim against Google on behalf of millions of iPhone users, ruling that claimants must show they personally suffered financial loss or distress from a data breach — mere "loss of control" of data isn't enough to claim compensation. That ruling effectively closed the door on opt-out, class-wide data claims in England and Wales. Litigants adapted by switching to opt-in group actions like this one, where each claimant is a named party. It's a slower, costlier route to bring, which is partly why £26m settlements over health data are still rare enough to make headlines rather than a matter of routine.

What it signals for the rest of the industry

Grindr's exposure here was always somewhat unusual: HIV status is about as sensitive as personal data gets, and the 2018 revelations gave claimants a well-documented paper trail rather than a hypothetical harm. Most dating, fitness and health-adjacent apps don't generate anything quite that toxic. But the underlying business model — enriching ad targeting with inferred or disclosed health and behavioural signals — is not unique to Grindr, and UK and EU regulators have been steadily narrowing what counts as acceptable practice around "special category" data under GDPR, which covers health information, sexual orientation and similar categories with extra restrictions.

For apps sitting on that kind of data, the practical lesson is less about the size of the cheque than about the pattern that produced it: a technical exposure surfaced by outside researchers, a regulatory reprimand years later, and then a group of claimants able to point to both as evidence a company knew, or should have known, what it was sharing. That sequence — expose, investigate, litigate — is now a template UK claimant firms clearly know how to run, and Grindr's settlement gives them a concrete number to cite the next time they pitch it to a prospective client base.

The first tranche of Grindr's payment is due by the end of this year, which will be the first real test of whether the settlement holds without further wrangling. Beyond that, the more interesting question is whether any other dating or health-app operator becomes the next target — Austen Hays and rival claimant firms have shown the model works.
