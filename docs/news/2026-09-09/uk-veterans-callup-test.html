UK to call up veterans in test of Britain's war resilience

British Army reservists during a training exercise — the strategic reserve of ex-service personnel is now facing its biggest recall test in decades. — Lt Col John Skliros / UK MOD, Open Government Licence
In one sentence
The MOD will test recalling part of Britain's 95,000-strong veteran reserve in a 2027 exercise, its largest mobilisation drill in decades.

Britain will test how quickly it can call a war-ready force back into uniform, with plans to mobilise part of its 95,000-strong "strategic reserve" of former troops in an exercise next year — the largest test of the country's veteran recall system in decades.

Armed Forces Minister Louise Sandher-Jones set out the plan on September 7 in evidence to the House of Lords national resilience committee, telling peers the reserve would be exercised "on a scale we haven't seen for a long time." The point of the drill, due to run in 2027, is blunt: can the Ministry of Defence actually find these people, contact them, kit them out and get them into a unit if a crisis demands it — or does the system on paper collapse the moment it's tested.

A reserve nobody has used

The strategic reserve is distinct from the roughly 32,000 active reservists who already train part-time alongside the Army, Royal Navy and RAF. It is made up of former regular and reserve personnel who, under long-standing rules, retain a legal liability to be recalled to service after they leave — officers for life, others for a period of years. In practice, that liability has been a formality. Sandher-Jones, a former Army Intelligence Corps captain who served in Afghanistan and is herself part of the reserve, told the committee she filed the notice away and forgot about it: "When I left the Army and found out I was in the strategic reserve, I just filed it away and never thought about it again."

That casualness is precisely the problem the exercise is meant to expose. Senior defence figures have acknowledged the MOD does not have a reliable system for tracking many of the veterans who could, in theory, be called up — addresses go stale, contact details lapse, and nobody has stress-tested the process of actually activating tens of thousands of people at once. With the regular Army now numbering around 71,000 trained troops, far below Cold War-era strength, the strategic reserve is being treated less as a legal footnote and more as a genuine mobilisation asset.

Lowering the bar for calling people up

The exercise sits alongside legislative changes moving through the Armed Forces Bill, expected to take effect in spring 2027. The bill raises the maximum recall age for non-officer reservists from 55 to 65, standardises recall liability across the services, and — more significantly — widens the legal threshold for mobilisation itself. Currently the government can only call up reserves in cases of national danger, great emergency or an attack on the UK. The new threshold would extend to "warlike preparations," allowing ministers to begin mobilising before a conflict has actually started.

That shift matters as much as the headcount. It signals that Whitehall wants the option to move early in a crisis rather than waiting for an attack to trigger the legal machinery, a change that tracks with a broader defence posture shift: the government has pledged to raise defence spending to 3.5% of GDP by 2035, and the Strategic Defence Review led by Lord Robertson of Port Ellen has pushed for the UK to be able to generate a much larger fighting force at short notice than its standing army allows.

What it signals

Officials are careful to frame the drill as routine contingency planning rather than a response to a specific threat, but the timing speaks for itself. It comes as European governments broadly recalibrate for the possibility of a larger continental conflict, and as Britain's own defence chiefs have warned that the country's ability to reconstitute forces quickly — not just field them today — is a genuine vulnerability. Chief of the Defence Staff Air Chief Marshal Sir Richard Knighton has made similar points about resilience gaps in recent testimony.

The next marker is the 2027 exercise itself, which will show whether the MOD can turn a dormant legal liability into a functioning callback system — or whether, as officials privately concede, it discovers a reserve force it can locate on paper but not in practice.
