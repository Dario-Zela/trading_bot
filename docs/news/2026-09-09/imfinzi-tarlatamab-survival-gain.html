Imfinzi-tarlatamab combo shows landmark survival gain in lung cancer

AstraZeneca's global R&D headquarters in Cambridge, UK, where Imfinzi's oncology development is based. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Durvalumab plus tarlatamab became the first immunotherapy-bispecific combo to extend survival in frontline small-cell lung cancer — but AZN and AMGN shares fell anyway, for reasons mostly unrelated to the drug.

Extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer is one of oncology's grimmest diagnoses. It spreads early, resists treatment, and returns fast: even with modern immunotherapy, the median patient survives about a year from diagnosis. On September 8th, AstraZeneca and Amgen said a combination of two drugs had, for the first time, moved that number meaningfully — and the stock market shrugged.

The companies announced that IMFINZI (durvalumab, AstraZeneca's PD-L1 checkpoint inhibitor) paired with IMDELLTRA (tarlatamab, Amgen's bispecific antibody) had met its main goal in a Phase 3 trial, extending overall survival compared with durvalumab alone in patients whose cancer had not progressed after initial chemotherapy. Amgen called it a "landmark" result. A Dana-Farber Cancer Institute oncologist involved in the field called the data "among the most compelling survival results I have seen." AstraZeneca shares fell anyway — down roughly 1.3 to 1.6 percent on the day.^1 The gap between the clinical headline and the market's reaction is itself the story.

What the trial actually showed

The study, called DeLLphi-305, randomized 563 patients with extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) who had already completed induction treatment with durvalumab, platinum chemotherapy and etoposide — the current first-line standard. Half then received durvalumab alone as maintenance; half received durvalumab plus tarlatamab. At a pre-planned interim analysis, the combination group lived significantly longer, and also did better on two secondary measures, progression-free survival and objective response rate.[^3][^4]

What the companies have not yet released is the number that matters most to clinicians: the actual median survival gain, or the hazard ratio that quantifies it. Both said full results, including that data, would be presented at a forthcoming international medical congress and submitted to regulators.[^3] Until then, "landmark" is a characterization, not a citation — worth flagging for readers who want the underlying figures before adjusting their sense of the disease's prognosis.

Tarlatamab itself is not new. Sold as Imdelltra, it is a DLL3-targeted bispecific T-cell engager — a lab-built antibody that clips onto both a cancer-cell surface protein and a patient's own T-cells, forcing an immune attack — approved in 2024 for patients whose SCLC had already relapsed after multiple prior treatments. DeLLphi-305 is the trial that tests whether moving it earlier, into first-line maintenance alongside a checkpoint inhibitor, extends life for a broader population. If confirmed, it would be the first immunotherapy-bispecific combination shown to improve survival in this indication.[^5]

Why the safety profile matters as much as the survival curve

Tarlatamab's mechanism carries a well-documented cost: cytokine release syndrome, a systemic inflammatory reaction that occurs as the redirected T-cells activate. In the pooled safety data behind Imdelltra's approval, CRS occurred in 57 percent of patients — mostly mild, but with roughly 2 percent grade 3 or higher — alongside neurologic toxicity in 65 percent of patients (7 percent grade 3 or above, a small fraction fatal).^6 AstraZeneca and Amgen said the safety profile in DeLLphi-305 was consistent with what's already known and flagged no new signals, which is reassuring but not the same as saying the burden is light.[^3]

That burden is logistical as much as medical. Current prescribing guidance calls for a step-up dosing schedule, hour-long infusions, and mandatory in-facility monitoring after the first two doses — patients must generally remain within an hour of a healthcare setting, with a caregiver present, for 48 hours after each of the first two infusions because of CRS risk.[^7] A pharmacoeconomic review modeling US uptake projected only about 25 percent of eligible patients would actually receive tarlatamab in year one, rising to 40 percent by year three — a slow ramp explained less by clinical doubt than by the sheer difficulty of delivering the drug outside specialized cancer centers.[^8] At a list price around $31,500 for the first treatment cycle and roughly $30,000 for each cycle after, cost adds a second layer to that adoption question, on top of the monitoring infrastructure most community oncology practices don't have.[^9]

A share-price move that isn't really about this drug

Here the story gets more interesting than a simple "investors are skeptical" narrative. AstraZeneca's modest dip came on a day the company also released strong Phase 3 results for tozorakimab, an unrelated COPD drug, for which it is now forecasting peak annual sales above $5 billion — hardly the mark of a market in a defensive crouch about the pharma's pipeline broadly.[^10] The more plausible reading is that AZN's SCLC win landed in a noisy news cycle and against a backdrop of a rougher 2026 for the company's late-stage programs: in July, its cardiovascular drug Wainua failed to reduce deaths in a heart-condition trial, sending shares down 9 percent in a single session, and AstraZeneca separately discontinued a lung-cancer combination trial for volrustomig after an independent monitoring committee judged it unlikely to succeed.[^11][^12] Investors may simply be pricing in that positive readouts and negative ones now arrive from AstraZeneca at a similar clip, tempering the reaction to any single one.

Amgen's share move the same day was far larger — the stock fell roughly 9 to 10 percent, with volume many multiples above average — but tracing it to the SCLC data would be a mistake. The bigger driver was a Phase 3 failure announced days earlier by Novartis for pelacarsen, an experimental Lp(a)-lowering drug that cut cholesterol-adjacent lipoprotein levels but did not reduce cardiovascular deaths, heart attacks or strokes. Amgen is running its own late-stage trial of a similar Lp(a)-lowering drug, olpasiran, and investors marked the whole drug class down in sympathy; a same-day downgrade of Amgen by BMO Capital compounded the move.[^13][^14] That Novartis-driven selloff is its own story, rippling into Eli Lilly as well as Amgen. The SCLC combination's stock-market reception, in other words, looks less like a verdict on the drug and more like two unrelated headlines colliding on one earnings-adjacent Tuesday.

The genuine, durable skepticism worth watching isn't in the share price — it's in the adoption curve. A therapy that requires hospital-adjacent monitoring and runs $30,000-plus per cycle will only reshape SCLC's "natural history," to borrow the trial investigator's phrase, if oncology practices outside major centers can actually deliver it, and if payers move quickly on reimbursement once the FDA reviews the frontline-maintenance data. Full DeLLphi-305 results, including the median survival figures still withheld, are expected at an international oncology congress in the coming months, alongside regulatory filings on both sides of the Atlantic — that data release, not this week's stock chart, is the number to watch.
