Trump administration accuses Ford of aiding 'strategic adversaries' via CATL, Geely ties

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, who wrote to Ford CEO Jim Farley criticizing the automaker's Chinese supply-chain partnerships. — U.S. Department of Transportation / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Transportation Department publicly warned Ford's CEO that its CATL battery deal and Geely joint venture aid 'strategic adversaries'; Ford shares fell 4.2%.

Ford Motor Company's stock fell 4.2% on Tuesday after the U.S. Department of Transportation made public a letter accusing the automaker of deepening ties with what it called "strategic adversaries" — a rare instance of a cabinet agency training its fire directly on a single company's supply chain decisions.

The letter, sent by Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy to Ford CEO Jim Farley, cites two arrangements in particular: a technology-licensing deal with Chinese battery maker CATL at Ford's LFP battery plant in Marshall, Michigan, and a joint venture with Chinese automaker Geely at Ford's Valencia, Spain plant, announced in July. Duffy also flagged ongoing talks between Ford and BYD over hybrid vehicle components as a further risk.

What the letter says

Duffy wrote that Ford's use of "imported Chinese technical expertise" at Marshall "challenges the spirit of American national and economic security," and said the department was "deeply alarmed" by the arrangement. On the Spain venture — in which Geely holds a 34% stake to Ford's 66%, producing Ford- and Geely-badged models from 2028 — Duffy argued it "helps strategic adversaries secure a vital foothold in Western markets." CATL itself has been on the Pentagon's list of Chinese military-linked companies since January 2025, a designation made before Trump took office.

Notably, the letter carries no immediate regulatory teeth. It is a statement of concern, not an order, a proposed rule, or a sanction. But it lands from a department that oversees vehicle safety standards, federal highway funding and increasingly, it seems, industrial policy by correspondence.

Ford pushes back

Ford disputed the framing point by point. The company said the CATL relationship is a "limited technology-licensing and services agreement, not a joint venture," with the Marshall plant wholly owned and operated by Ford and supporting 1,700 American jobs. The company has long argued that licensing LFP battery know-how — a chemistry CATL and other Chinese firms dominate — is the fastest route to cheaper, more durable batteries without ceding ownership or IP to a Chinese partner.

That distinction is central to Ford's broader EV cost strategy. LFP cells are cheaper and safer than the nickel-based chemistries Ford previously relied on, and licensing rather than importing was pitched, including to the Biden administration in 2023, as a way to build domestic capacity while still capturing Chinese battery expertise. The Trump administration's letter suggests that argument no longer buys automakers cover.

Why it matters for the sector

The letter is a warning shot rather than a policy change, but it signals where the administration's attention is turning as EV subsidies unwind and trade tension with Beijing persists elsewhere — tariff fights, export controls on rare earths and chips, and a parallel dispute over Chinese AI model distillation flagged by U.S. agencies this week. Ford is not unique in leaning on Chinese battery technology: several U.S. and European automakers have similar licensing or joint-venture arrangements with CATL, Gotion and other Chinese cell makers, making Ford something of a test case for how far Washington is willing to push.

For now, the practical stakes are limited to reputational pressure and a jumpy stock. That changes if the letter is followed by concrete action — a rule restricting licensed Chinese battery technology from federal EV incentives, a national-security review of the Marshall plant, or curbs on the Spain venture's access to U.S. markets. Any of those would force Ford to rework EV manufacturing plans it has already spent billions building around Chinese-originated battery chemistry. Watch for a Ford response beyond the initial statement, and for whether Duffy's letter is echoed by Commerce or Treasury with something more binding attached.
