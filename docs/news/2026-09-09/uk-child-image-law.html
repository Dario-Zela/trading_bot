New UK law would force tech firms to block children sharing nude images

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy, who told MPs current tech industry proposals "do not meet the scale of this crisis." — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Ministers plan a law forcing Apple, Google and children's apps to block nude images at device level, after voluntary fixes fell short.

The UK government says it will write into law what it has spent the past three months asking technology companies to do voluntarily: stop children taking, sharing or viewing nude images on their phones.

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy told MPs on Tuesday that despite "significant commitments" from Apple and Google, the firms' current proposals "do not meet the scale of this crisis." In June, ministers gave the two companies — which between them supply the operating systems on almost every smartphone in the country — three months to build in device-level blocks on nude imagery for under-18s. Both have since made changes to safety tools; neither has gone as far as stopping a phone taking or sending the image in the first place. Nandy's response is to draft legislation that would make the block mandatory, covering not just handset makers but any app children use — so restrictions would follow a child from the camera roll into WhatsApp, Snapchat or wherever else an image might travel.

"Children are facing unacceptable levels of sexual harm online," Nandy said. "They are being groomed, coerced, and manipulated into creating and sharing intimate images." The Internet Watch Foundation, which supplies much of the UK's data on this, says 91% of child sexual abuse imagery it identifies is self-generated — a child photographing or filming themselves, usually under pressure from an adult posing online as a peer. No timetable has been set for the bill; Nandy said only that the government would move "as soon as we can," with the option of standing down if Apple and Google's own fixes catch up first.

Where the AI angle fits

The device-blocking plan lands on top of law that already exists. The Crime and Policing Act, which received royal assent in April, created a standalone UK offence for making, adapting, possessing or supplying an AI "image-generator" built or altered to produce child sexual abuse material — punishable by up to five years in prison. It was, at the time, the first law anywhere to target the tools themselves rather than only the images they produce. A separate amendment added in February requires platforms to remove non-consensual intimate images, including AI deepfakes, within 48 hours of a report, backed by fines of up to 10% of a company's global turnover or the threat of being blocked in the UK.

What's driving the urgency is how fast the AI side of this has grown. The IWF's most recent assessment found 8,029 AI-generated child sexual abuse images and videos in 2025, with the number of videos alone up from 13 the year before to 3,443 — a jump the charity attributes to how cheap and easy the generation tools have become. Nearly two-thirds of those videos fell into the most severe legal category. Girls made up 97% of the children depicted.

Taken together, the two strands of policy — criminalising the generators, then forcing the devices and apps that carry the results to block them at the point of creation — amount to Britain trying to regulate a problem it cannot fully see, since most of this material never reaches a platform's moderation queue at all. Whether device-level scanning can be built without either missing real abuse or flagging innocent family photos is the technical question Apple and Google have been quietly wrestling with since June, and the one that will determine how workable Nandy's bill turns out to be once it is actually drafted.

The next marker is the bill's introduction to Parliament, for which no date has been set. Until then, Apple and Google's own next move — whether they extend Communication Safety-style tools before ministers force the issue — is the thing to watch.
