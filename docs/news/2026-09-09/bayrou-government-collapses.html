Bayrou government collapses, plunging France into fresh crisis before ECB meets

In one sentence
PM Bayrou lost a self-called confidence vote 364-194 over austerity cuts, leaving Macron hunting a fifth PM in two years days before the ECB meets.

The French government fell for the second time in nine months on Monday, when Prime Minister François Bayrou lost a confidence vote he had called himself, 364 votes to 194 — well past the 289-vote threshold needed to topple him. It was a wager Bayrou did not have to make and lost badly, and it leaves President Emmanuel Macron hunting for a fifth prime minister in under two years, three days before the European Central Bank sits down to decide what to do about interest rates.

The timing is the story as much as the vote itself. Christine Lagarde and the ECB's governing council meet Thursday against a backdrop of accelerating eurozone inflation — 3.3% in August, pushed up largely by energy costs — and markets had been leaning toward a further rate move even before Paris lurched back into crisis. Now the council must calibrate policy for the currency bloc's second-largest economy at the exact moment that economy has no functioning government and no passed budget.

A gamble that didn't need making

Bayrou, in office only since December, had not been forced into Monday's vote by any parliamentary maneuver. He called it himself, betting that laying his €44 billion ($51 billion) austerity package on the table — including scrapping two public holidays and freezing state spending — would concentrate minds in a fractured National Assembly and force rivals to either back him or own the consequences of chaos. They chose chaos.

In his speech before the vote, Bayrou leaned hard into the language of reckoning. "You have the power to overthrow the government, but you do not have the power to erase reality," he told deputies. "Reality will remain inexorable. Spending will continue to increase and the debt burden — already unbearable — will grow heavier." He described France's deficit as "a silent, underground, invisible, and unbearable hemorrhage," and cast submission to creditors in the same breath as submission to an occupying army: "Dominated by weapons, or dominated by our creditors... in both cases, we lose our freedom."

The chamber was unmoved. Bayrou becomes the second prime minister in a row — after Michel Barnier, ousted in a similar vote last December — to be broken by the same structural problem: a National Assembly split three ways among Macron's centrists, the left-wing NFP bloc and Marine Le Pen's Rassemblement National, none of them able to muster a majority for spending cuts large enough to matter. Bayrou lasted nine months. His fall makes him the fifth prime minister Macron has had to replace since 2024, a churn rate that is starting to look less like bad luck than a system that no longer produces governments capable of governing.

The euro caught in the crossfire

Currency markets registered the vote well before it happened. EUR/USD slipped toward 1.1750 on Monday as traders priced in the near-certainty of Bayrou's defeat, with technical desks flagging 1.1800 as resistance and 1.1600 as the level a real loss of confidence in French fiscal credibility could open up. It is a smaller move than the political drama might suggest — and that gap between headline chaos and currency reaction is itself telling.

The reason is Thursday. Desks widely expect Lagarde's press conference to spend as much time addressing Paris's fiscal hole as it does explaining the ECB's own rate decision, with markets currently pricing roughly even odds on a move to 2.50% from the current 2.25% deposit rate — a shift itself only possible because the ECB already broke a three-year hold with a hike in June, when the spike in oil prices from the conflict around the Strait of Hormuz first pushed inflation forecasts higher. A rate-setting meeting that would ordinarily be the week's dominant signal is instead being treated by traders as a sideshow to the question of whether France can pass a budget at all — a reversal of the normal hierarchy of eurozone news that says as much about investor priorities as it does about the ECB's credibility.

France's own numbers are not helping. The country's monthly budget balance widened to a shortfall of roughly €146 billion in July, from €107 billion the month before, underscoring exactly the trajectory Bayrou was trying, unsuccessfully, to arrest. With no budget and no government, that gap has no clear path to closing before the next administration — whoever leads it — is forced to have the same fight all over again.

What happens next

Macron has said he will name a new prime minister within days, but the arithmetic that broke Bayrou has not changed: no bloc in the Assembly commands a majority, and none has shown willingness to back the scale of cuts investors and ratings agencies want to see. A snap legislative election remains the pressure-release valve Macron has so far refused to pull twice before. Thursday's ECB decision will be the first real test of how much of this uncertainty is already priced into the euro — and how much more is still to come if Paris cannot produce a working government before its creditors start asking harder questions.
