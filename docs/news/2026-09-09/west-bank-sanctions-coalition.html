Coalition of Western allies joins UK's sanctions on Israeli settlement goods

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who coordinated the 12-nation sanctions push, arrives at 10 Downing Street. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham led 12 nations into sanctions on Israeli settlement goods and briefed Trump first, who did not object — even as Israel shut its Jerusalem consulate and a US ambassador threatened blowback.

Andy Burnham was two months into his premiership when he picked up the phone to Donald Trump. The subject was not tariffs, or Ukraine, or the special relationship's usual friction points. It was the West Bank — and specifically, Burnham's plan to lead a coalition of Western governments in banning trade with Israeli settlements there.

By the account of two sources briefed on the call, Trump did not push back. He did not ask Burnham to change course, or even register strong objection. For a White House that spent its first months back in office rolling back Biden-era sanctions on Israeli settlers, and for a UK-US relationship that watchers had spent the summer bracing for a rupture over exactly this kind of issue, the silence was the story.

What Britain actually banned

The measures, unveiled by Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband, go further than the labelling rules and advisory notices that have defined UK settlement policy for a decade. London will ban imports of all goods produced in Israeli settlements in the occupied West Bank, and bar British companies from providing settlements with financing, construction, infrastructure, real estate or advertising services. The restrictions take effect over six to nine months, giving businesses time to unwind existing contracts.

Miliband's language matched the policy's severity. "The British government agrees that there is ethnic cleansing of Palestinians in areas of the West Bank, perpetrated by settler terrorists," he told Parliament, citing bulldozed houses, destroyed roads and displaced families. It is the starkest language a UK government has used on the issue, and it was designed to travel — Burnham, French President Emmanuel Macron and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney issued a joint statement warning that settlement expansion and settler violence "pose a direct and urgent threat" to the viability of a two-state solution.

A coalition, not a solo act

What distinguishes this from previous UK gestures on Israel-Palestine is the guest list. Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Iceland, Ireland, Norway, Poland, Portugal, Spain and Sweden all joined the UK in announcing national restrictions or committing to push for equivalent measures at the EU level — twelve governments in total, spanning NATO's northern and southern flanks alike. That breadth matters for the policy's bite: a settlement-goods ban that covers only Britain's roughly £340m in annual settlement-linked trade is a gesture, while one that also touches French, Canadian and Nordic import channels starts to look like an actual cost.

It also reframes what might otherwise read as a UK-Israel spat into something closer to a coordinated Western policy shift — one Burnham was able to present to Washington as a fait accompli backed by allies, rather than a unilateral British initiative he was asking Trump to tolerate alone.

Israel's retaliation, and the fight that's actually still live

Israel did not take the news quietly. Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar, with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's approval, announced Israel would close the British consulate in Jerusalem, expel UK personnel from a joint monitoring centre tied to the Gaza ceasefire, end British training of Palestinian Authority security forces, and bar twelve British parliamentarians and other citizens from entering the country.

Washington's tone was more mixed than Trump's silence on the Burnham call suggested. US Ambassador to Israel Mike Huckabee warned publicly that Britain could face American blowback if it followed through, pointing to Florida's anti-boycott statute as a lever that could restrict British companies' business in the state, and calling the UK's move discriminatory toward Jewish people. That is the gap worth watching: the White House itself held its fire, but officials made clear to their British counterparts that they still disagreed with the sanctions even as they declined to block them — reportedly out of their own frustration with the Netanyahu government's settlement policy.

The read for markets

The episode lands as a mild net-positive for the UK-US coordination story that has otherwise dominated headlines this year on trade and defence: rather than the rift some had priced in, Trump's non-interference suggests Washington is willing to let London run its own Middle East policy without retaliating economically. The more durable risk sits elsewhere — in the Israel-UK diplomatic freeze, which could still complicate British commercial and security interests in the region, and in Huckabee's Florida threat, which remains untested. The measures' own six-to-nine-month implementation window is the next real checkpoint: whether the EU's larger economies fold restrictions into single-market rules, and whether Washington's forbearance survives contact with an actual enforcement action against a US-linked firm.
