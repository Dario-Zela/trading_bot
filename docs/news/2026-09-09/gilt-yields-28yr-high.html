UK gilt yields hit 28-year highs, squeezing Chancellor's Budget room

HM Treasury's headquarters at 1 Horse Guards Road, London, where Chancellor John Healey is preparing the October 28th Budget — Wutthichai Charoenburi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Gilt yields hit levels last seen in 1998, eating into roughly half of the Treasury's self-imposed fiscal room just as the OBR's forecast window opens.

Britain's government just borrowed for 30 years at the highest price since the Debt Management Office was created in 1998. On September 1st, the yield on the 30-year gilt jumped 10 basis points in a single session to 5.89%, a level last seen when Tony Blair had been prime minister for a little over a year. The 10-year gilt, the benchmark that does most of the work in day-to-day Treasury arithmetic, climbed to around 5.25% — its highest since the depths of the 2008 financial crisis.

For Chancellor John Healey, who inherited the job from Rachel Reeves in July's cabinet reshuffle under new prime minister Andy Burnham, the timing could hardly be worse. The Office for Budget Responsibility has been commissioned to prepare its full forecast for the October 28th Budget, and that forecast is what will tell Healey — and everyone else — exactly how much room he has left to manoeuvre. Based on where yields are trading now, the answer looks like: not much.

Half the headroom, gone before the Budget starts

The mechanics are unforgiving and largely automatic. The OBR's March forecast assumed 30-year borrowing costs of around 5.1%. Yields are now running roughly 70-80 basis points above that. Every quarter-point rise in gilt yields adds an estimated £2.5 billion a year to the government's debt-servicing bill, according to Treasury analysis cited by economists tracking the forecast. Compound that across the curve and the effect on the fiscal arithmetic is large: Deutsche Bank's chief UK economist, Sanjay Raja, estimates that the roughly £26 billion of headroom Reeves banked against her stability rule at the spring forecast has fallen to about £13.8 billion — a reduction of more than £12 billion, almost entirely attributable to higher interest costs rather than any change in spending or growth assumptions.

That headroom is not a rounding error in Whitehall's books — it is the entire buffer the Chancellor has against breaking the fiscal rules he has publicly pledged to keep. Healey has said the October Budget will be "built on fiscal discipline" and will "meet our fiscal rules," language chosen deliberately to reassure a bond market that has spent the summer punishing governments — Britain's, France's, Japan's — that look fiscally loose. Raja's own read on the acceptable floor: "£10bn to me is the floor. In a perfect world you would want to keep 15." On current pricing, Healey is sitting closer to the floor than the comfort zone, and the OBR has not even finalised its numbers yet.

Why the bond market moved first

The proximate trigger was not domestic. Yields have been rising globally since the US-Iran conflict escalated in early 2026, pushing Brent crude above $92 a barrel and reviving inflation expectations everywhere that imports energy — which includes Britain, structurally. Interest-rate swaps now price in nearly three quarter-point Bank of England hikes by year-end, a sharp reversal from the cutting cycle markets had expected only months ago. Japan's own 10-year yield hit its highest level since 1996 in the same week, and the UK, already carrying government debt equivalent to roughly 94% of GDP, caught the same wave with less room to absorb it.

"Of course, this is red lights flashing."

That is Kathleen Brooks of XTB, summarising the mood among traders watching UK debt costs alongside everything else going on in the Middle East and in Tokyo. The read-through is not that Britain is uniquely reckless — it is that a country with £2.985 trillion of debt and a self-imposed rule to stop that debt rising has almost no cushion left when the global cost of borrowing moves against it.

The Chancellor's narrowing set of choices

Healey's dilemma reduces to three unappealing paths, and none of them is new territory for a Chancellor squeezed by the bond market — but few have faced it quite this early in the job, with quite this little headroom, quite this publicly. He can scale back the spending ambitions he and Burnham have signalled — the pledge to move "money and power" out of Westminster and into every postcode implies fresh outlay, which is now harder to fund without breaching the rules. He can raise taxes further, on top of the £26.1 billion in increases Reeves already pushed through last November, though Labour's manifesto commitments rule out touching income tax, VAT or National Insurance, narrowing the options to smaller, more contentious levers. Or he can loosen the fiscal rules themselves — a move that would likely trigger exactly the kind of gilt-market reaction that forced Liz Truss's resignation in 2022, an episode every subsequent Chancellor has been visibly determined not to repeat.

The government has already made one structural change that matters here: from 2026/27, fiscal rules will be assessed only once a year, at the Budget, rather than twice. That removes one opportunity for markets to mark the Chancellor's homework mid-year, but it also means October 28th carries more weight than a typical autumn statement — there is no spring check-in to correct course if the numbers disappoint.

What happens next

The OBR's forecast will be finalised roughly ten working days before the Budget, meaning the numbers that matter are already close to locked in even as gilt trading continues. Markets will be watching two things between now and October 28th: whether 30-year yields hold near 5.9% or retrace as Middle East tensions ease, and whether the Bank of England's rate path — currently pricing in further hikes — adds to or relieves the pressure on the Treasury's interest bill. A further leg up in yields would push headroom toward Raja's stated floor of £10 billion or below it, likely forcing a choice the government has spent all summer trying to avoid making explicit.
