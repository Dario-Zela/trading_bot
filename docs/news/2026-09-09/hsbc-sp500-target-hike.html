HSBC lifts S&P 500 target to 8,100 on AI earnings surge

HSBC's offices at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London. — Danesman1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
HSBC lifted its S&P 500 target to 8,100 on near-40% EPS growth, but its own list of seasonal and geopolitical caveats hints at thin conviction.

HSBC has raised its year-end target for the S&P 500 to 8,100, up from 7,650, on the back of first-half earnings growth the bank's own analysts call "stellar." The new target implies roughly 5% upside from Tuesday's close of 7,673.52 and an 18% gain for the index over the full year — an aggressive call for a market already trading near record highs.

The case rests on numbers that are, for now, genuinely strong. Nicole Inui, HSBC's head of equity strategy for the Americas, points to S&P 500 earnings-per-share growth running close to 40% in the first half of 2026. Of the 492 index constituents that have reported second-quarter results, 86% beat analyst estimates — well above the long-run average beat rate of 67.5%. HSBC now forecasts full-year 2026 earnings growth of 33%, taking EPS to $360, and applies a price-to-earnings multiple of 22.5 times — a multiple the bank describes as broadly in line with historical norms, even if it looks generous against a slowing-growth backdrop. Continued AI capital expenditure is doing much of the work underneath that forecast, propping up semiconductors and the wider AI-linked trade; HSBC stays overweight technology, financials and industrials, while turning more selective on consumer names.

The catch in the small print

What's notable is how much hedging accompanies the upgrade. Inui's own note flags seasonal softness typical of September and October, inflation data that could unsettle rate expectations, and regulatory pressure building around data centres and social media. She argues that election-driven volatility tends to be short-lived and that geopolitical shocks have so far left aggregate consumer spending largely untouched — but the list of caveats is long enough to suggest the bank is hedging a call it isn't entirely comfortable making without qualification.

That tension matters because the second-half arithmetic leaves little room for error: HSBC's own model needs EPS growth above 25% to hold through the back half of the year, a deceleration from 40% but still a demanding bar. Any wobble in AI-related capital spending, a hawkish turn from the Federal Reserve, or an escalation in any of the geopolitical flashpoints already unsettling oil and shipping markets would puncture the multiple HSBC is applying long before it touches the earnings line.

What it says about the wider bet

HSBC's revision is less a standalone forecast than a proxy for where the entire bull case in US equities now sits: earnings, especially AI-adjacent earnings, have to keep outrunning already-elevated expectations, because valuations offer no cushion if they don't. The bank's willingness to raise its target while simultaneously listing four distinct ways it could be wrong is itself a signal — not of weak conviction exactly, but of a market consensus that is unusually dependent on one narrative continuing uninterrupted.

The next real test comes with third-quarter earnings season in October, when companies will have to show that AI capex is still translating into the kind of profit growth HSBC is underwriting. A Federal Reserve that turns more hawkish than markets currently price — odds of a September hike have already jumped to 58% — would tighten financial conditions and squeeze the multiple side of HSBC's equation regardless of how earnings come in.
