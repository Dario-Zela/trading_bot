Novartis Lp(a) drug failure sends shockwaves through Amgen, Eli Lilly

Novartis's Basel campus, where the Lp(a)HORIZON trial results were announced — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Novartis's pelacarsen trial miss sent Amgen down 10% and Novartis down 14%, with analysts questioning the underlying Lp(a) hypothesis and Amgen's olpasiran pipeline bet.

Novartis told investors on September 4th what cardiologists had spent two decades hoping not to hear: lowering lipoprotein(a) — a genetic cholesterol variant blamed for millions of heart attacks — did not, in a rigorously designed trial of more than eight thousand patients, actually prevent them.

The drug, pelacarsen, did exactly what it was built to do. In the Phase III Lp(a)HORIZON trial, it cut patients' Lp(a) levels by roughly 80%, matching the results of earlier, smaller studies.[^1] What it did not do was reduce the composite rate of cardiovascular death, heart attack, stroke or urgent coronary revascularization compared with placebo — the endpoint the entire trial existed to test.^2 Novartis shares fell 14% on the news, wiping out a year's worth of gains in a single session. The stock closed at $137.63.[^3]

The bigger casualty wasn't Novartis. It was a scientific premise — the "Lp(a) hypothesis" — that underwrites late-stage pipeline bets at Amgen and Eli Lilly worth billions of dollars combined.

A genetic risk factor with no approved fix

Lp(a) is a cholesterol-carrying particle whose blood level is set almost entirely by a person's genes rather than diet or exercise, which is why statins — the workhorse cardiovascular drug of the past thirty years — do essentially nothing to it.[^4] An estimated one in five people worldwide carries elevated Lp(a), and epidemiological studies have long linked high levels to higher rates of heart attack and stroke.[^5] That correlation is what built an entire drug class: if Lp(a) causes cardiovascular disease, then lowering it pharmacologically should prevent cardiovascular events, the same logic that made statins (which lower LDL cholesterol) into one of medicine's most successful franchises.

Pelacarsen, developed by Ionis Pharmaceuticals and licensed to Novartis, is an antisense oligonucleotide — a synthetic strand of genetic material that binds to the liver's Lp(a)-producing messenger RNA and marks it for destruction before the particle can even be made.[^6] It was the furthest along of four rival Lp(a)-lowering drugs in late-stage testing, and the market had treated a positive result as close to consensus. Citi's analysts, reacting to the miss, wrote that "the Lp(a) hypothesis has been weakened," and that it remains unclear whether the failure reflects a flaw in pelacarsen's mechanism, a quirk of the trial's design, or a genuine hole in the underlying science.[^3] That ambiguity is precisely the problem: a clean failure would at least tell rivals what to fix.

Amgen's exposure, and why it isn't identical

Amgen shares fell 10% to $394.38 on the read-through, alongside a ratings downgrade from BMO, even though the company reported unrelated good news the same week — its lung cancer drug Imdelltra, partnered with AstraZeneca's Imfinzi, hit its survival endpoint in the DeLLphi-305 trial.[^3] None of that mattered much to a market suddenly repricing every asset that depends on the same biological bet. Amgen's olpasiran, an siRNA therapy also designed to suppress Lp(a) production in the liver, is running its own Phase III cardiovascular outcomes trial, OCEAN(a)-Outcomes, in patients with established atherosclerotic disease — the same population Novartis just failed in.[^7] Analysts have flagged it as Amgen's most exposed late-stage pipeline asset to a class-wide re-rating.[^3]

The caveat, and it is a real one, is mechanistic: olpasiran uses small interfering RNA rather than an antisense oligonucleotide, silences a different point in the Lp(a) production pathway, and its outcomes trial enrolls a somewhat different patient mix. Pelacarsen's failure does not mathematically predict olpasiran's result. But biotech investors have never waited for mechanistic nuance before selling correlated risk, and Wall Street's message this week was that a shared endpoint and a shared hypothesis are enough to move the stock first and sort out the science later.

Eli Lilly, whose lepodisiran is the least advanced of the three — its 16,700-patient ACCLAIM-Lp(a) outcomes trial is not expected to read out until around 2029 — got off comparatively lightly, dropping about 2% to $1,124.77.[^3] The distance to Lilly's data may be exactly why: the market has years to revise its view before that trial matters, whereas Amgen's readout is closer and the mechanism sits nearer to Novartis's on the same causal chain.

What the failure does and doesn't settle

What makes pelacarsen's miss unsettling to specialists, rather than simply disappointing, is that it clears the two easiest excuses for failure. The drug lowered Lp(a) by a wide margin — this was not a case of insufficient target engagement. And the trial was large and adequately powered, enrolling 8,323 patients with confirmed elevated Lp(a) and established cardiovascular disease, not an underpowered or poorly selected study.^2 That leaves a narrower, more uncomfortable set of explanations: that Lp(a) is a marker of cardiovascular risk rather than a driver of it; that lowering it late in disease, after atherosclerosis has already formed, comes too late to matter; or that the trial simply ran for too short a window to detect a benefit that would show up over a longer horizon. Novartis has not yet published the granular subgroup data that might distinguish between those possibilities, and cardiologists will be picking through it at the American Heart Association's scientific sessions later this year.

The honest position, and the one most cardiovascular researchers not employed by a rival drugmaker are landing on, is that one trial does not kill a hypothesis built on decades of genetic and epidemiological evidence — but it does raise the burden of proof substantially for everyone still running the experiment.^2 Amgen's OCEAN(a)-Outcomes trial is the next test of that exact question, using a different drug and a different mechanism against the same biological premise. Its result, whenever it lands, will tell the market considerably more about whether pelacarsen's failure was a Novartis problem or an Lp(a) problem.
