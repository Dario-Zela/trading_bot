BOJ set to raise growth forecast while holding rates

The Bank of Japan's headquarters in Nihonbashi, Tokyo. — Fg2 / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The BOJ is set to lift its growth forecast on AI demand while holding rates at 1%, but a yen near 40-year lows is pushing officials toward faster hikes.

The Bank of Japan is expected to raise its growth forecast for the current fiscal year on Friday while leaving its policy rate unchanged at 1%, a combination that captures the central bank's central bind: the economy it oversees is doing better than expected, and that is part of the problem.

Policymakers meeting in Tokyo through July 31 are widely tipped to lift their fiscal 2026 growth projection from the 0.5% expansion forecast in April, according to people familiar with the deliberations cited by Reuters and Bloomberg. The upgrade is being attributed chiefly to AI-driven demand — both the chip exports feeding data-centre buildouts abroad and the capital spending it is pulling through at home. The BOJ may trim its core inflation forecast slightly from April's 2.8% projection, largely on the back of falling oil prices, even as it flags AI-linked chip demand as a fresh upside risk to prices.

A hold with an asterisk

The rate decision itself should be uneventful. The BOJ raised its short-term rate to 1% in June — the highest since 1995 — and traders assign it near-zero odds of moving again this week. What has moved markets is the signal underneath the hold. Bloomberg and Reuters have reported that BOJ officials are open to raising rates sooner than the roughly six-month cadence economists have priced in, should inflationary pressure keep building. Markets now put the odds of another hike by October at around 72%, up sharply from a baseline expectation of a December move.

That shift in tone is doing real work. The yen slid past 163 to the dollar last week, its weakest level since 1986, before paring losses on the faster-hike reporting — evidence that jawboning, for now, is achieving what actual policy hasn't. The currency's slide is a function of a wide and stubborn rate gap: the Federal Reserve's benchmark sits at 3.50%-3.75%, roughly 250 to 275 basis points above Japan's 1%, and that spread — not any single Japanese data point — is the structural force pulling the yen lower.

Why the yen is doing the BOJ's job for it

A weaker yen raises import costs, and officials increasingly believe Japanese companies are passing those costs to consumers faster than in past cycles — a sign, in their reading, that inflation is becoming embedded rather than transient. With underlying inflation now sitting close to the BOJ's 2% target, the policy question has shifted from how to generate price growth to how to keep it anchored without letting the currency do the tightening for them, disorderly and unpriced.

Governor Kazuo Ueda is expected to lean on hawkish language this week specifically to cap further yen weakness while preserving the option to act on the bank's own timetable rather than the market's. That is a delicate needle: talk too tough and risk an unwanted rate spike; sound too patient and risk another leg down in the currency and a fresh intervention scare from the Ministry of Finance.

What it means for positioning

For anyone running yen-funded carry trades or hedging Japanese rate exposure, Friday's meeting is less about the headline hold than about how explicitly the BOJ commits to front-loading its next move. A growth upgrade tied to AI demand sounds like an unambiguous positive, and for Japanese equities tied to the semiconductor supply chain, it likely is. But the same AI-driven strength is now a line item in the BOJ's inflation risk assessment, which is the more consequential detail for rates. A central bank that spent a decade fighting deflation is now worried about an overshoot — and telegraphing that it may not wait until December to respond.

The next hard data point is the BOJ's quarterly outlook report, published alongside Friday's decision, followed by the September policy meeting where any acceleration in the hike path would first become visible in the bank's language. Until then, the yen's level near 163 remains the de facto trigger the market will watch.
