Samsung cuts US jobs as tariff war escalates

A Samsung office building in the US. The company is consolidating its North American headquarters in Plano, Texas, cutting hundreds of jobs in New Jersey in the process. — Cristiano Tomás / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Samsung is cutting ~839 US jobs in a Texas HQ move it attributes to tax and AI strategy — not the tariffs it explicitly blamed for freezing Mexico investment.

Samsung is cutting hundreds of jobs at its American operations, and the timing — in the same week Washington rolled out a fresh round of tariffs on 60 trading partners — invites an obvious reading: tariff pressure is now hitting American payrolls. The company's own account of events is more mundane, and worth taking at face value before reaching for the bigger story.

The cuts, in numbers

Samsung Electronics America confirmed on July 19 that 739 positions are affected at its Englewood Cliffs, New Jersey campus — roughly 62% of the roughly 1,200 people who work there — plus about 100 more roles in Plano, Texas, including staff in the struggling mobile division. Most employees were offered relocation rather than shown the door outright, though the company has not disclosed how many accepted versus how many were laid off.

The cuts are the direct consequence of Samsung moving its North American headquarters from New Jersey to Plano by the end of the year — its second US headquarters move in under twelve months, and a jarring one: the Englewood Cliffs campus, a 321,000-square-foot former Unilever building, only opened in September 2025. Samsung says the Texas move puts its executive leadership next to its two largest US manufacturing bets: the Austin semiconductor fab it has run since 1996, and the new Taylor foundry, due online by year-end. The company has described the reshuffle as a "business transformation" tied to tax benefits and workforce alignment for the AI era — not to trade policy.

What the tariffs actually touch

That distinction matters, because Samsung has been explicit about tariffs elsewhere. Facing 25% US duties on steel and aluminum — inputs into appliance manufacturing — the company froze planned investment in Mexico last year and shelved a plan to shift refrigerator production from Gwangju, South Korea to its Tijuana and Querétaro plants, with local reporting putting potential Mexican workforce cuts as high as 30%. That is the kind of specific, attributable, tariff-driven decision the New Jersey and Texas cuts are not.

The broader backdrop is real enough. On July 24, the US Trade Representative imposed Section 301 tariffs of 10% to 12.5% on imports from 60 economies — including the EU, UK, Canada and India — over forced-labor enforcement, the latest escalation in a trade fight that has already reshaped decisions at companies from Shein to the chipmakers now recalibrating supply chains around Section 301 exposure. Samsung's own numbers underline where the pressure is actually landing: its chip division is riding an AI-driven profit surge, while its mobile unit faces a possible first-ever quarterly loss against Apple and Chinese competition — a story about product-line competitiveness, not customs duties.

The headquarters move completes by year-end, alongside the Taylor foundry's start-up — the point at which Samsung's stated rationale, tax and AI-era logistics, gets tested against results. Whether tariff exposure shows up in Samsung's US footprint the way it already has in Mexico is the more interesting number to watch.
