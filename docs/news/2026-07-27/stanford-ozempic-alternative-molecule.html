Stanford scientists find natural molecule that suppresses appetite like Ozempic, without the side effects

Stanford University School of Medicine, where researchers identified the appetite-suppressing peptide BRP. — Robert Skolmen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Stanford found a natural peptide that curbs appetite in mice and pigs like Ozempic, minus the nausea — but it's untested in humans.

Stanford Medicine researchers say they have identified a naturally occurring molecule that curbs appetite in mice and pigs with much the same potency as semaglutide — the compound behind Ozempic and Wegovy — while sidestepping several of that drug's most common complaints. The finding, published in Nature in March and expanded on in subsequent Stanford Medicine reporting, is a mouse-and-minipig result. No human has taken it yet.

In one sentence: Stanford scientists found a natural peptide that suppresses appetite in animals about as well as Ozempic, without the nausea, constipation or muscle loss — but it hasn't been tested in a single human being.

The molecule, called BRP — short for BRINP2-related peptide — is a 12-amino-acid fragment that the body already produces. It was found not by chance but by an algorithm. Lead author Laetitia Coassolo and senior author Katrin Svensson, an assistant professor of pathology at Stanford, built a tool called Peptide Predictor to scan roughly 20,000 human protein-coding genes for the cut sites enzymes use to carve small, biologically active peptides out of larger precursor proteins. The search narrowed 20,000 genes to 373 candidate prohormones and 2,683 possible peptide fragments, which the team then screened in the lab for appetite-suppressing activity. BRP surfaced as the standout. [^1]

A different address in the brain

Semaglutide works by mimicking GLP-1, a gut hormone whose receptors sit not just in the brain but in the pancreas, stomach and gut lining — which is a large part of why it causes nausea, delayed digestion and, in some patients, significant loss of lean muscle alongside fat. BRP, by contrast, appears to act narrowly in the hypothalamus, the brain's hunger-and-metabolism control center, where it activates POMC neurons — a well-established appetite-regulating circuit — through a signaling pathway distinct from GLP-1's. [^2]

In obese mice, a single injection cut food intake by up to 50 percent within an hour; over 14 days of treatment, treated mice lost roughly 3 grams, nearly all of it fat, and showed improved glucose and insulin tolerance. The same effect held in minipigs, whose metabolism and gut physiology more closely resemble a human's than a mouse's does — one reason the Stanford team is treating the result as more than a rodent curiosity. Across both species, the researchers reported, they did not observe the nausea, constipation or substantial muscle wasting associated with semaglutide. [^1] [^3]

What the mechanism claim is — and isn't

It's worth being precise about what BRP targets, because a separate, unrelated study has gotten tangled up with this one in some coverage. Researchers at the University of Virginia, working with NIH's National Institute on Drug Abuse, reported this month that oral GLP-1 drugs — a newer class that includes orforglipron — reach into the central amygdala, a deep-brain region tied to desire and reward, and dial down dopamine release tied to eating for pleasure rather than hunger. [^4] That is a real and notable finding about how semaglutide-class drugs work. It is not, however, what BRP does. BRP's reported action is confined to classic hunger circuitry in the hypothalamus; nothing in the Stanford data speaks to reward or craving pathways. Conflating the two — a natural molecule with fewer side effects, and a separate mechanism for GLP-1 drugs already on the market — overstates how novel either finding is on its own.

The distance to a pill

Svensson has co-founded a company, Merrifield Therapeutics, to pursue development, and has said clinical trials are planned. But BRP's receptor in target tissue hasn't yet been identified, and the peptide's natural half-life is short — both problems that typically take years to solve before a molecule is stable and long-acting enough to dose in humans, let alone file for approval. "Nothing we've tested before has compared to semaglutide's ability to decrease appetite and body weight," Svensson has said. "We are very eager to learn if it is safe and effective in humans." [^1] That sentence carries real hedging: eager to learn, not confident of the answer.

The stakes for getting there are large. Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly's obesity franchises are on track for a combined $120 billion-plus in annual sales by 2030, and roughly 15 percent of patients who quit GLP-1 therapy within a year cite side effects as the reason, with nausea, abdominal pain and vomiting the most common complaints. [^5] A drug that matched semaglutide's efficacy without its gut toxicity would be commercially significant. BRP is not that drug yet — it is early-stage animal data with a promising mechanism and a founder-scientist's financial stake in seeing it succeed, which is worth noting without implying bad faith.

The next real checkpoint is whether Merrifield Therapeutics files for human trials and, if so, what phase-1 safety and tolerability data show — the point at which a mouse-and-pig result either survives contact with human physiology or doesn't.
