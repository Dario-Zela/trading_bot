Trump opens Section 301 probe into EU tech fines after Brussels hits Google with first DMA penalty

President Trump, whose Truth Social post ordering the Section 301 probe called the EU's tech fines an act of 'ROBBING' American companies — Daniel Torok / official White House photo, public domain
In one sentence
Trump ordered an immediate Section 301 trade probe into the EU after Brussels fined Google €890m in its first Digital Markets Act penalty, threatening tariffs to reverse the fine.

The fine landed on a Thursday: €890 million, the European Commission's first-ever penalty under the Digital Markets Act, aimed squarely at Google. By Friday, Donald Trump had turned a competition-law enforcement action into the opening shot of a trade war.

The president ordered an immediate Section 301 investigation into the European Union's treatment of American technology companies, invoking a statute normally reserved for unfair trade practices — dumped steel, stolen intellectual property, currency manipulation — and pointing it instead at Brussels' antitrust regulators. "The European Union will pay a very big price for this illegal and highly unethical conduct, which I have consistently warned them about," Trump wrote on Truth Social, adding that "the United States of America is not a 'PIGGYBANK' for Europe, nor will we allow it to be." He said the EU had been "ROBBING" American companies and, by extension, American taxpayers, and promised a "substantial TARIFF" on the bloc "at the earliest possible moment."

What Brussels actually did

The fine that triggered the outburst was narrower than Trump's framing suggested. The Commission split its €890 million penalty into two findings. The larger piece, €460 million, punished Google for what regulators called self-preferencing — surfacing its own hotel, shopping and transport listings above rivals' in search results. The second, €430 million, addressed restrictions Google placed on app developers seeking to steer Play Store customers toward payment options outside Google's own billing system.

Teresa Ribera, the Commission's executive vice-president for competition, framed the decision as enforcement rather than politics. "Google has fallen short of effective compliance with the Digital Markets Act, and today we have taken decisive yet balanced enforcement action sanctioning these breaches," she said, adding that "the best products should succeed because they're better, not because they're owned by the company running the search engine." Asked about the American reaction, Ribera was blunter still: "Our duty and obligation is to comply with the laws, that our laws are fully respected." Google has 60 days to change both practices or face daily penalty payments of up to 5% of Alphabet's average worldwide turnover.

The DMA, in force since 2023, was designed precisely to produce moments like this one — a case that moves past warning letters and into penalties large enough to matter. This is its first. The law's authors always expected it to draw fire from Washington; they did not necessarily expect a trade statute to be the response.

The bill Trump is disputing

Google's Thursday fine did not arrive in isolation. Trump's Truth Social post tallied a running total of EU penalties against four companies: roughly $15 billion against Apple, $3 billion against Meta, $2.5 billion against Amazon, and now another $1 billion against Google, which Trump said brought the company's cumulative EU fines above $18 billion. Some of those figures compress separate cases — Apple's largest EU penalty stems from a decade-old state-aid ruling over Irish tax arrangements, not the DMA — but the throughline in Trump's account is consistent: Brussels, in his telling, treats American tech balance sheets as a revenue source.

That argument has circulated in Washington for years without producing a Section 301 case. What changed is the mechanism. Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 lets the U.S. Trade Representative investigate whether a foreign government's policies are "unreasonable or discriminatory" toward American commerce, and if so, respond with tariffs, suspended trade concessions, or a negotiated settlement. It is the same authority used to build tariff cases against Chinese intellectual-property practices in Trump's first term. Investigations typically run 12 to 18 months, with public comment and a hearing along the way — a formal process that under prior administrations has moved considerably slower than a Truth Social post promising tariffs "at the earliest possible moment."

Trade law as a shield for antitrust targets

What makes this notable is the category error, deliberate or not. Section 301 exists to police trade practices — tariffs, quotas, market-access barriers. The DMA is a competition law, applied to a company's own conduct within the EU market, not a restriction on what the U.S. sells into Europe. Trump's investigation asks USTR to treat one as the other: to argue that fining a company for anticompetitive behavior is itself an unfair trade practice against the company's home country.

If USTR adopts that logic, the implications extend well past Google. Any regulator anywhere that fines an American company would be recast as a trade adversary, and any tariff threat becomes available as leverage to unwind a fine that has nothing to do with imports or exports. The EU, for its part, has shown no sign of treating the fine as negotiable — Ribera's comments were about legal compliance, not diplomacy. Brussels has weathered tariff threats over the DMA before; what's different now is that a specific enforcement action, with a specific euro figure attached, has become the trigger for a nominally separate trade case.

What comes next

Google's 60-day compliance clock on the search and Play Store findings runs out in late September, which will test whether the Commission is prepared to escalate to daily penalty payments against Alphabet regardless of what Washington does in the meantime. USTR's Section 301 investigation, if it follows the statute's usual timeline, won't produce a formal finding for months, but the office can announce interim steps — public hearings, comment periods — well before then, and Trump has already signaled he doesn't intend to wait for the full process to run before threatening tariffs. The more immediate marker is whether other DMA cases now in the pipeline against Apple, Meta and Amazon draw the same response, which would confirm this is a standing policy rather than a one-off reaction to Google's fine.
