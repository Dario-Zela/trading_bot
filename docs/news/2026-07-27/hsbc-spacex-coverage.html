HSBC initiates SpaceX coverage with a $115 price target

SpaceX's Starship, whose commercial timeline is central to HSBC's cautious valuation, ignites during a test flight. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
HSBC's $115 SpaceX target matched Friday's close not because it called the price, but because the stock fell to meet its caution.

HSBC opened its coverage of SpaceX on Friday with a Hold rating and a $115 price target — a number that turned out to be almost exactly where the stock was already sitting. Shares in Space Exploration Technologies Corp, which trades on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, fell as much as 6% after the note landed and closed the session at $115.07, seven cents above HSBC's target.

That coincidence is the headline, but it understates what's actually going on. SpaceX is barely six weeks removed from the largest IPO in history: a $135-a-share listing on June 12 that briefly pushed its market capitalisation above $2.1 trillion, edging past Tesla. Since then the stock has been in near-continuous retreat — falling below its IPO price on July 15, hitting a fresh low a week later, and shedding roughly $1.16 trillion in paper value from its post-listing peak. A last-second abort of the Starship rocket's 13th test flight on July 16 alone erased close to $100 billion in a single session. Short interest has ballooned from around 40 million shares three weeks ago to roughly 185 million, close to 29% of the tradable float.

HSBC's target didn't move to meet the market. The market moved to meet HSBC.

An outlier, not a consensus view

The bank's analysts used a sum-of-the-parts model with what they called a 2-times "innovation premium" — a deliberate markup for Elon Musk's record of building industries from nothing, applied to Starlink, launch operations and SpaceX's newer AI and data-centre ambitions. Even with that premium baked in, the math topped out at $115. HSBC's own bull case, which assumes Starship turns commercially viable from 2027 and launch capacity doubles, reaches only $293.

That puts HSBC well outside the mainstream. When SpaceX's IPO quiet period lifted in early July, 14 underwriting banks issued ratings simultaneously; 13 were bullish, with targets clustered between $190 and $300 and Raymond James topping the table at $800. Only MoffettNathanson struck a cautious note, at $131. HSBC's $115 now sits below even that, making it the most conservative call yet from a bank with a formal stake in getting SpaceX's valuation right.

The caution isn't about growth — HSBC expects revenue to more than double, to $38.2 billion in 2026 from $18.7 billion last year, powered by Starlink and the AI segment. It's about cash. The bank doesn't see free cash flow turning positive until 2030, after an estimated $106 billion in cumulative burn, and flags SpaceX's newer bets — Starship, the Terafab manufacturing project, lunar-economy work — as capital-hungry ventures priced for success rather than proven by it.

Two dates now matter more than any price target: SpaceX reports its first quarterly results as a public company on August 4, and a lock-up expiration on August 6 could free roughly 900 million insider-held shares for sale — a supply test that will say more about where the stock goes than any bank's spreadsheet.
