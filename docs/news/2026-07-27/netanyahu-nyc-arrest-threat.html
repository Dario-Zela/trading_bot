Netanyahu says he won't be arrested in NYC as Trump intervenes

In one sentence
Trump vowed Netanyahu 'will not be arrested' in NYC after Mamdani said the city lacked authority to enforce his ICC warrant but urged Washington to act.

New York City will not be the place where an international war-crimes warrant catches up with Benjamin Netanyahu — not because the warrant lapsed, but because the President of the United States says so.

The standoff began when Mayor Zohran Mamdani said his administration was in what he called "active conversation" about whether the city could act on the International Criminal Court's arrest warrant for the Israeli prime minister, should he set foot in New York for the UN General Assembly in September. Donald Trump answered within hours on Truth Social: "Benjamin Netanyahu will not be arrested, in any way, shape, or form, while in the United States of America." He added that Netanyahu was "fighting against the Islamic Republic of Iran," casting the ICC warrant as a distraction from a righteous war rather than a legal instrument to be weighed on its merits.

A mayor without the tools

Mamdani's own lawyers gave Trump the argument for him. In a video posted two days later that has since drawn tens of millions of views, the mayor said his administration had "reviewed every avenue available under applicable law to determine whether New York City could execute the International Criminal Court's arrest warrant if Benjamin Netanyahu came here," and concluded "it is clear that we do not have the independent legal authority to enforce this warrant." He still called Netanyahu a "war criminal" and pressed the point where it actually lives: with Washington. "The federal government, however, does" have that authority, he said, urging the administration to join the Rome Statute and act on the warrant it has instead spent a year fighting.

That warrant is not new. ICC judges issued it in November 2024, finding reasonable grounds that Netanyahu bears responsibility for the war crime of starvation as a method of warfare and for crimes against humanity — murder, persecution and other inhumane acts — tied to Israel's campaign in Gaza, along with command responsibility for directing attacks on civilians. Neither Israel nor the United States has ever joined the Rome Statute, the treaty that created the court, which is the foundation of Washington's argument that the warrant carries no domestic force.

The bigger campaign

The Mamdani episode is a small skirmish inside a larger one. Since February 2025, the administration has sanctioned a string of ICC judges and deputy prosecutors under an executive order aimed at officials investigating American or Israeli nationals. Earlier this month, the State Department went further, vowing a "whole-of-government" push to "disable" the court after ICC judges filed a formal complaint over the sanctions — floating visa revocations and travel bans for its staff. Set against that backdrop, Trump's promise to Netanyahu reads less like a one-off political favor and more like the domestic face of a standing foreign-policy position: the court has no claim on America or its allies, full stop.

Netanyahu, for his part, has given no indication he intends to test the question quietly. Meeting Trump at the White House this week — his seventh visit of the second term — he accused Mamdani of "fomenting hate" and said he still plans to travel to New York in September: "I intend to come and speak the truth — speak for Israel and the Israeli-American alliance."

The practical question was settled almost as soon as it was raised — city police were never going to arrest a sitting head of government the federal government wanted left alone. What is left is a test of how far executive fiat can substitute for treaty law when an ally is involved, and whether Washington's campaign against the ICC hardens into something that outlasts this administration. The next marker is September, when Netanyahu is due in New York for the General Assembly and the warrant that supposedly does not apply to him will, for a few days, be standing on the sidewalk outside the UN.
