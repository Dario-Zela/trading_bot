WORLD · WEST BANK · By C. Tanaka · 2026-07-27 · 5 min read

Israeli settlers torch West Bank mosques after deadly weekend clashes

Tyres inside Qusra's mosque after a 2011 settler arson attack — the same village was targeted again this weekend, its new mosque set ablaze. — International Solidarity Movement / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Settlers torched two West Bank mosques in revenge for a clash that killed four Palestinians and two Israelis, one flashpoint in a year that has already left 87 Palestinians dead.

The graffiti went up before dawn, in Hebrew, sprayed across the entrance of an unfinished mosque in the West Bank village of Qusra: "Revenge for Benayahu," alongside a Star of David. By the time residents woke on Sunday, the wooden scaffolding and stonework of the Al-Rahma Mosque — still under construction — had been set alight. In the village of Kur, near Tulkarm, a second mosque burned the same night. In nearby Beit Furik, a home was torched before neighbours managed to put out the flames.

The attacks were retaliation, and the settlers who carried them out did not pretend otherwise. "Jewish blood is not free," read graffiti at the Kur site. The name invoked in Qusra belonged to Benayahu Mellet, a member of a settler civilian security team killed two days earlier in a gun battle near the village of Tal, alongside an Israeli army major. Four Palestinians died in the same clash, which began when a group of settlers entered a restricted area for what the Israeli military later described as an "unauthorized and uncoordinated hike" and encountered armed resistance from villagers. Six deaths in one incident, two mosques burned in reprisal within 48 hours — the arithmetic of the West Bank this summer is brutally direct.

A village that keeps burning

Qusra is not new to this. Settlers have targeted the village of roughly 6,000 people repeatedly since at least 2011, when arsonists first torched tyres inside its mosque during an earlier wave of so-called "price-tag" attacks — reprisals nominally tied to Israeli government actions against settlement outposts but in practice aimed at any nearby Palestinian community. Peace Now activists visited the village during a 2014 solidarity tour documenting the damage. Qusra's mayor, Abdul Azim Wadi, told the Palestinian Authority's WAFA news agency that Sunday's fire destroyed the new mosque's entrance and damaged its stonework — a near-exact repeat of a script the village has lived through before, just with a bigger, more expensive building this time.

The Israeli military said it "strongly condemns incidents of this kind" and that troops combed Qusra overnight but found the attackers had already fled. It is a familiar sequence too: soldiers arriving after the fact, no arrests, an investigation opened and rarely concluded. Rights groups say the pattern holds beyond this single village — the Israeli army's own data recorded 867 incidents of settler and nationalist violence in 2025, up from 682 the year before, and Israeli Army Radio reported a 63 percent jump in attacks in the first half of 2026 alone, to roughly 660 incidents against 405 in the second half of last year.

The numbers behind the cycle

According to the Palestinian Health Ministry, 87 Palestinians have been killed in the West Bank so far this year, 21 of them by settlers rather than soldiers. The UN's humanitarian coordination office (OCHA) had separately logged more than 870 settler attacks across over 220 communities since January — an average above four a day — and said settler violence has already displaced more Palestinians in 2026 than in the whole of 2025. None of this is confined to the immediate aftermath of the Tal clash; it describes a baseline that the weekend's mosque burnings simply spiked above.

What makes the cycle self-sustaining is less the frequency of individual attacks than the near-total absence of consequence on either side. Israeli prosecutions of settler violence rarely result in convictions, according to rights monitors, while Palestinian militant attacks draw swift, large-scale military responses — Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered a broader operation after Friday's clash, with hundreds of Palestinians detained across Tal, Ramallah, Jenin, Hebron, Tulkarem and Bethlehem, and homes in Tal marked for demolition. Each side experiences the other's response as proof that restraint doesn't pay.

No visible off-ramp

International reaction arrived quickly and changed nothing. The UN's special envoy on combating Islamophobia condemned the mosque attacks and called for places of worship to be protected "at all times"; the Council on American-Islamic Relations labelled them acts of terrorism. Statements like these have accompanied nearly every prior mosque burning in the West Bank — including one during Ramadan just months ago — without altering enforcement on the ground. Domestically, opposition politicians accuse Netanyahu's coalition, which includes ministers who have called for outright annexation of the West Bank, of tolerating settler violence it has the power to prosecute. The government denies this while pointing to Sunday's condemnation from the army itself.

What happens next depends less on statements than on whether Israel's promised investigation into the mosque arsons produces an arrest — something that has essentially never happened in comparable cases — and on how the broader military operation launched after the Tal killings unfolds over the coming weeks. Israel's next general election is roughly three months out, a timeline that gives settlement-aligned parties in Netanyahu's coalition little incentive to press for accountability now. The next formal marker will be OCHA's periodic West Bank situation update, which will show whether July's death toll pushes materially past the 87 already recorded, or whether this weekend proves to be, as with Qusra's mosque before it, simply the latest entry in a ledger nobody is closing.
