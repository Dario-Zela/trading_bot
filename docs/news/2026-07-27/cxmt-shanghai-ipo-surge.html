CXMT shares soar 500% in Shanghai's biggest IPO of the year

The Shanghai Stock Exchange building in Pudong, home to the STAR Market where CXMT made its record debut. — 螺钉 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
CXMT raised $8.6bn and its shares surged over 500% on debut, testing whether China's state-backed chip champion can convert an AI-driven memory shortage into lasting market share.

Shares of ChangXin Memory Technologies rose more than 500% within minutes of their Shanghai debut on Monday, turning a company most Western investors had never heard of into China's most valuable listed firm — bigger, on paper, than Industrial and Commercial Bank of China and briefly ahead of Intel.

The memory chipmaker, known as CXMT, priced its initial public offering at 8.66 yuan a share, raising 57.9 billion yuan ($8.6 billion) in the largest share sale by a Chinese semiconductor company on record and Asia's biggest IPO of 2026. When trading opened on the tech-focused STAR Market, the stock immediately jumped to 49.50 yuan and later touched a peak of 55.03 yuan — a gain of more than 535% — before settling to close up over 500%, according to CNBC and Reuters. At the high, the company's market capitalisation reached roughly 3.6 trillion yuan, or about $530 billion.

In one sentence: CXMT raised $8.6bn and saw its shares surge over 500% on debut, a bet by Chinese investors and the state that a Hefei-based memory chipmaker can loosen Korea and the US's grip on AI-era chip supply.

From provincial project to national champion

CXMT's rise traces back to what analysts call the "Hefei model" — a provincial government using public investment funds to seed a strategic industry, then bringing in private capital once the technology proves out. Founded in 2016 with backing from the Hefei Industrial Investment Fund and a minority stake from chipmaker GigaDevice, CXMT is now China's only large-scale producer of modern DDR5, LPDDR5 and LPDDR5X memory, and the world's fourth-largest DRAM maker behind Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron, holding roughly 7.7% of the global market in 2025.

The IPO surpassed Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation's 53.2 billion yuan Shanghai listing in 2020 to become the biggest mainland Chinese chip offering ever. Underlying demand was extraordinary by any measure: retail investors placed 9.4 million orders worth 7.07 trillion yuan, oversubscribing that portion of the deal roughly 212 to 244 times over, depending on the count. Institutional demand, while still enormous at close to 570 times the shares on offer, was comparatively softer — well below the 5,000-times ratios seen in some recent Chinese tech listings — a gap some analysts flagged as a caution sign rather than an afterthought. The debut also landed awkwardly: the STAR Market had fallen roughly 25% from its early-July peak, erasing more than 4 trillion yuan in value, as investors from Seoul to Silicon Valley began asking whether AI-linked valuations had run too far ahead of fundamentals.

Riding — and reinforcing — a global memory squeeze

CXMT's timing owes less to Chinese industrial policy than to a supply crunch that has gripped the memory chip industry worldwide. AI data centres now absorb an estimated 70% of global memory output, and DRAM contract prices have risen roughly 80–95% quarter-on-quarter since late 2025 as manufacturers redirect capacity toward high-bandwidth memory for AI servers, starving the consumer and enterprise market. Micron's chief executive, Sanjay Mehrotra, said in June he expects the shortage to persist through 2027. CXMT's own revenue reflects the windfall: sales in the first quarter of 2026 reportedly rose more than 700% year-on-year to 50.8 billion yuan, and the company, unprofitable as recently as 2024, is now projecting first-half 2026 net profit of 50–57 billion yuan.

That scarcity has produced an unlikely tug-of-war in Washington. Apple has lobbied the Trump administration — Tim Cook has raised it directly with the president, Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent — for permission to use CXMT and Yangtze Memory Technologies chips in devices sold outside the United States, arguing it would ease price pressure that has already pushed Apple to raise MacBook and iPad prices by as much as 20%. Micron, the only large US memory maker, is fighting the request, warning that any opening for Chinese suppliers, regardless of where the products ship, would undercut domestic manufacturing the government has spent years trying to protect. The dispute is complicated by the fact that CXMT sits on the Pentagon's Section 1260H list of Chinese military-linked companies — a designation that restricts US government procurement but stops short of a full export ban or a bar on private commercial dealings.

The test ahead is execution, not enthusiasm

The substance of Beijing's bet is straightforward: memory chips are the one segment of advanced semiconductors where a Chinese firm has a credible claim to global relevance without first cracking the extreme ultraviolet lithography problem that has stalled China's logic-chip ambitions at Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation. DRAM manufacturing is capital-intensive but more forgiving of process-node gaps than cutting-edge logic chips, which is part of why CXMT could reach volume production of DDR5 and LPDDR5X while still lacking access to the most advanced lithography tools, thanks to Dutch and Japanese export controls.

What the IPO does not resolve is whether CXMT can sustain the growth its share price now implies once the current AI-driven memory shortage eases and prices normalise — the same cyclical risk that has periodically hammered Samsung and SK Hynix. Nor does it resolve the geopolitical ambiguity hanging over the stock: a company simultaneously courted by Apple as a supply-chain release valve and flagged by the Pentagon as a military-linked entity is, by definition, trading on a political outcome as much as an operational one.

What comes next

The near-term signal to watch is whether CXMT's shares hold their gains once the retail-driven opening frenzy fades — the pattern of heavy retail oversubscription against comparatively muted institutional demand has preceded disappointing follow-through in other recent Chinese listings. Longer term, the Trump administration's decision on Apple's request to use CXMT chips outside the US will double as a proxy referendum on how far Washington is willing to let commercial pressure erode its China chip-containment strategy, with Micron's lobbying against it setting up one of the more consequential industrial-policy fights of the year.
