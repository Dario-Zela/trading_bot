France stages huge peacetime evacuation as wildfires rage near Bordeaux

Sentinel-2 satellite imagery shows wildfire smoke over the Gironde region of southwest France on July 26, 2026. — Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data 2026, processed by Don-vip using Pierre Markuse's wildfire visualisation script / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Fires near Bordeaux have forced France's largest peacetime evacuation since WWII, with over 300,000 displaced across France and Spain and the EU Civil Protection Mechanism activated.

The phone alerts went out in the night: leave now, head toward Bordeaux. By Sunday morning, France's interior ministry was totting up a number that has no recent precedent — more than 250,000 people forced from their homes in the Gironde and Landes departments alone, with Spain's tally pushing the combined figure across the two countries past 300,000. French officials are calling it the country's largest peacetime evacuation since the Second World War.

The fires began outside Bordeaux on July 22 and have since burned an estimated 42,000 hectares — an area roughly four times the size of Paris — through the pine forests and scrubland of France's southwest. They have not yet reached the city itself, but the smoke has, drifting over a region built on wine, wood and summer tourism.

A country moves

What distinguishes this fire from the wildfires that have become a grim summer fixture in southern Europe is less its size than its speed and the sheer volume of people it displaced overnight. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez said the blaze "again became extremely virulent," generating winds of its own that outran containment lines crews had held for days. Marc Vermeulen, the Gironde fire chief, described the conditions more bluntly: "like being confronted by a hurricane."

By Sunday, 2,500 firefighters, 1,500 soldiers and around 1,200 police were deployed in Gironde, backed by 18 aircraft. Eighty-four firefighters have been injured. President Emmanuel Macron convened a crisis cabinet and told residents his government would "rebuild" and "be there for as long as is needed." Nuñez added a data point that matters more than any single fire: 115,000 hectares have burned across France since January, he said — "far more than anything we have seen in the past."

Across the border, Spain's toll compounded the crisis. More than 75,000 people were evacuated in the Valencia region, where one death was confirmed near Manises, and residents described conditions in terms that echoed Gironde. "It looked like the apocalypse," one evacuee, Rocío Domínguez, said. "You couldn't see anything." Spain's regional government in Valencia said at points the fire was "beyond our capacity to extinguish," a rare admission of the limits of a well-resourced European firefighting service.

The strain pushed both governments to invoke the EU Civil Protection Mechanism, the bloc's channel for pooling scarce firefighting assets across borders. Sweden and Cyprus sent water-bombing aircraft; Slovakia, Croatia and the Czech Republic sent helicopters; Portuguese and Spanish ground crews crossed into France even as their own countries fought fires. It is the kind of reciprocal arrangement the mechanism was built for, but its use this early and this often in a single season — France first activated it on July 5 — points to a firefighting system operating closer to its ceiling than governments would like to admit.

Wine country holds its breath

Bordeaux's vineyards have so far escaped direct damage, and local wine experts say the 2026 vintage is not yet at risk: grapes are still too small and hard this early in the season to absorb the airborne compounds that cause "smoke taint," the phenomenon that can ruin a wine's flavour years after a fire has passed. That is a narrow reprieve, not an exemption — the danger for winemakers rises sharply as harvest approaches in late August and September, when ripening fruit is far more porous to smoke.

Tourism and transport face a more immediate bill. Road and rail links around Bordeaux have been disrupted through the peak summer season, and hoteliers in a region that depends on July and August bookings are already absorbing cancellations. The heat driving the fires is itself a data point worth noting: average temperatures in Aquitaine this July have run at 32.2°C, some 7.3°C above the 1961–1990 norm for the region — a gap large enough that meteorologists no longer treat it as noise.

Why insurers are watching

The financial exposure extends well beyond one region's grape harvest. Reinsurers Swiss Re and Munich Re have already flagged 2025 as Europe's worst wildfire season on record by area burned — more than a million hectares — and cautioned that wildfire losses are not simply growing but clustering, hitting multiple regions in the same years rather than arriving as isolated shocks that diversify a reinsurer's book. Historically, only about a quarter of Europe's natural-catastrophe losses have been insured at all, meaning the uninsured gap — borne by households, farmers and governments — is the larger share of the bill.

That matters for how this fire should be read. A single evacuation order, however large, is a civil-protection story. A second consecutive record season, arriving early enough in July to force a mechanism built for emergencies into near-routine use, is a pricing story — one that reinsurance treaties, agricultural futures and regional tourism revenue all have to eventually reflect. Insurers typically re-model wildfire exposure after seasons like this; the open question is whether the recalibration keeps pace with how fast the fires themselves are accelerating.

The immediate test is meteorological: forecasters have flagged renewed high winds and temperatures in the days ahead, which would determine whether containment lines around Bordeaux hold or whether the evacuation zone grows again. The medium-term test is financial — how Aquitaine's grape harvest fares once the fruit ripens in late August, and whether reinsurers' autumn loss estimates for the 2026 European wildfire season mark a plateau or another step up from last year's record.
