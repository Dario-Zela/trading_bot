Oil slides as US and Iran signal a fragile strike pause

A US aircraft carrier transits the Strait of Hormuz, the corridor carrying roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. — Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class J. Alexander Delgado / US Navy (public domain)
In one sentence
Brent crude fell almost 5% to $92 a barrel as Iran said it would keep halting attacks so long as the US strike pause holds.

Brent crude fell almost 5% on Sunday to $92.02 a barrel, adding to a 3.9% drop on Friday, after Iran signalled through a senior official — cited by Reuters — that it would keep halting attacks on US allies in the Gulf as long as Washington's own pause in strikes holds. US benchmark West Texas Intermediate slid 5.2% to $84.67. Both contracts are now well off the $102 briefly touched last week, the highest level since May and roughly $30 above where Brent traded at the start of the month.

The reversal follows the White House's decision to stand down after 13 consecutive days of American strikes on Iranian targets. Vice President JD Vance and Gen. Dan Caine, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, had raised concerns in internal discussions about the strain sustained bombing was putting on US munitions stockpiles, particularly the interceptors used against Iranian retaliatory missiles. Mike Waltz, the US ambassador to the United Nations, described the pause as giving diplomacy "some space" to work, though officials have pushed back on suggestions that the US arsenal itself is running short.

A market that has been burned before

Traders have reason for caution rather than relief. This is not the first ceasefire of the summer — an earlier truce announced on May 29 collapsed within weeks, sending Brent from under $70 back above $85 in a little over a week once fighting resumed. The current de-escalation rests on the same fragile logic as its predecessor: each side's forbearance is conditional on the other's, with no enforcement mechanism beyond restraint itself.

The underlying dispute has not gone away. Washington and Tehran still disagree over how the Strait of Hormuz — the corridor carrying roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and a large share of its liquefied natural gas — is meant to reopen under the memorandum of understanding the two sides signed on June 17. Tehran promised safe passage through the strait but never specified which shipping lanes vessels should use, a gap Iranian forces have exploited to pressure tankers toward its territorial waters. Lloyd's List Intelligence recorded just 53 vessel transits through Hormuz in the week to July 20, down from 157 the week before, with tanker and gas-carrier crossings falling from 90 to 30. At least nine ships have come under attack since July 6.

The stakes for inflation are not abstract. Energy prices had been running roughly 24% higher year-on-year through the worst of the fighting, feeding directly into headline PCE readings that have hovered near 4% and squeezing out what little room the Federal Reserve had to consider rate cuts. A sustained retreat in crude would ease that pressure; a second collapse of the ceasefire would simply reload it.

For now, markets are pricing the de-escalation rather than the deal. The next test is not a data release but a negotiating one: whether talks referenced by Waltz produce anything more durable than a mutual pause, before the next incident in the strait forces the question again.
