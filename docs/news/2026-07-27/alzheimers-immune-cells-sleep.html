Immune cells identified as driver of Alzheimer's-linked sleep loss

Microglia labeled in a mouse brain section — the immune cells a new study identifies as the driver of Alzheimer's-linked sleep loss. — Syed07 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
University of Kentucky researchers found brain immune cells, not amyloid plaques, drive Alzheimer's-linked sleep loss in mice — but the drug that fixed it isn't safe for long-term human use.

Alzheimer's disease steals sleep long before it steals memory, and for years the assumption was that amyloid plaques were doing the stealing directly — gumming up the brain circuits that regulate rest. A study out of the University of Kentucky's Sanders-Brown Center on Aging, published July 16 in Alzheimer's & Dementia, argues that the plaques are mostly a bystander. The real disruption, the researchers say, comes from the brain's own immune response to them.

The cells in question are microglia — the brain's resident immune sentinels, which swarm amyloid plaques as they form and mount an inflammatory response. Led by Shannon Macauley and first author Nicholas Constantino, the team fitted mice genetically engineered to develop amyloid plaques with EEG and EMG headmounts and tracked their sleep at two ages: six months, when plaques are just emerging, and 18 months, when disease is advanced. Sleep loss appeared early and barely worsened as plaque burden more than doubled — a mismatch that pointed away from the plaques themselves as the direct cause.

What silencing microglia did

To test the immune-response theory, the researchers gave the mice pexidartinib, a drug that blocks a signal microglia need to survive. Over 14 days it wiped out roughly 87 percent of the cells. The plaques stayed exactly where they were — but the mice gained more than two hours of restorative non-REM sleep a night. "Microglia are immune cells that, when they respond to plaques, kick off this elaborate cascade of inflammation," Macauley said, "as if the microglia are partying all night, and keeping the brain awake."

That is a mouse result, not a treatment. Pexidartinib is not a novel compound built for this purpose — it is already FDA-approved as Turalio, marketed by Daiichi Sankyo for a rare joint tumor, and it carries a boxed warning for liver toxicity, including cases requiring transplant. Wiping out nine in ten of a patient's brain immune cells for weeks is not something regulators would wave through for a chronic, decades-long condition like Alzheimer's. Macauley's group says it is now testing gentler options — metformin, the diabetes drug, and the anti-seizure medication stiripentol — that might calm microglial inflammation without eliminating the cells outright. Neither has been tested in this model yet, and no company has announced a program targeting this pathway specifically.

The stakes are not small. An estimated 7.4 million Americans live with Alzheimer's, and sleep disruption affects roughly a quarter to nearly half of them — a symptom caregivers consistently cite as the reason for burnout and, eventually, nursing-home placement. If microglial quieting proves safe in follow-up animal work, it would offer something current Alzheimer's drugs largely don't: a treatable, reversible target for a specific quality-of-life symptom, independent of the slower fight over amyloid clearance. The next marker to watch is whether Macauley's team, funded in part by the Cure Alzheimer's Fund, can show comparable sleep gains with metformin or stiripentol without pexidartinib's toxicity — the step that would actually open a path toward a human trial.
