UK gilt yields hold above 5% for the first time since 2008

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose September rate decision now carries far higher odds of a hike. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields have held above 5% for a week — the longest such stretch since 2008 — after PM Burnham hinted at fiscal 'flexibility', with markets now pricing a 70% chance of a second BoE hike by year-end.

The UK government's cost of borrowing has settled at a level not seen since the year Lehman Brothers collapsed, and it is showing no sign of moving back down. The 10-year gilt yield crossed 5% late last Monday and has not dipped below it since — the longest unbroken stretch above that threshold since July 2008. The 30-year yield, more sensitive to long-run fiscal worries, touched 5.75% this week, its highest in two months and among the highest of any G7 government bond.

The proximate cause is not a spending bill or a budget statement. It is a sentence. Andy Burnham, sworn in as prime minister on 20 July after Keir Starmer's resignation, told reporters on his first day in office that his government would "make full use of any flexibility" within the UK's existing fiscal rules. Bond investors, who parse political language for funding implications the way meteorologists parse pressure systems for storms, read that as code for looser borrowing ahead. Gilts sold off within hours.

"He is playing a dangerous game."
— Matthew Parkinson, senior portfolio manager, Waverton Investment Management

A one-line comment, a market-wide repricing

Burnham's path to Downing Street was unusually compressed. Starmer announced his intention to quit on 22 June after losing the confidence of Labour MPs; Burnham, having returned to Parliament via a special election weeks earlier, secured 379 nominations — more than 94% of the parliamentary party — and was acclaimed unopposed as leader on 17 July. He became prime minister three days later and named John Healey, previously defence secretary and not widely tipped for the job, as chancellor, replacing Rachel Reeves.

Markets initially treated the succession itself as a source of uncertainty rather than alarm. It was the fiscal-flexibility remark that did the damage: the 10-year yield rose 9 basis points to 5.02% and the 30-year jumped 10 basis points to 5.73% in the hours after Burnham spoke, while sterling fell 0.3% against the dollar. The pound clawed back some of that loss after Healey's appointment was announced — investors read a former Cabinet minister with a conventional profile as a mildly reassuring signal on process, if not on substance — but it was still trading down around 0.37% against the dollar, near $1.337, by the end of the week.

The reason a single phrase can move a trillion-pound market is that the UK's fiscal position leaves little room for reinterpretation. Public debt sits at roughly 100% of GDP, and Britain's debt is structured differently from its peers: the average maturity of outstanding gilts is about 14 years, nearly double the G7 average, which means the Treasury refinances and reprices a smaller share of its book each year — but also that long-dated yields, once they move, embed higher costs for longer. Debt-interest payments are already forecast at £109.7bn for the current fiscal year. Against that backdrop, investors treated "flexibility" as shorthand for a government prepared to let borrowing run further than the 2019 fiscal framework — which requires debt-to-GDP to be falling within a five-year forecast window — was designed to allow.

The 2022 echo, and why traders think it's different

The comparison desks kept reaching for was September 2022, when Liz Truss's unfunded tax-cutting mini-budget sent gilt yields into a self-reinforcing spiral and forced the Bank of England into emergency bond purchases. The parallel is instructive mainly for where it breaks down. Truss's episode was triggered by a costed, scored policy document; Burnham's was triggered by an unscripted answer to a question, with no bill, budget or Office for Budget Responsibility forecast attached. That leaves markets pricing a risk premium on uncertainty rather than a specific number on borrowing — which is a more diffuse problem to resolve, since there is no single document whose withdrawal would settle it, but it has so far also produced an orderly repricing rather than the disorderly, liquidity-driven selling that met Truss.

The Bank of England is the other half of the equation, and here the gilt move has done some of its work for it. Sterling money markets are now pricing at least one quarter-point rate rise by year-end, with a 70% probability attached to a second — most immediately at the Monetary Policy Committee's September meeting. A government perceived as loosening fiscal policy while inflation expectations remain unanchored gives the Bank cover, and arguably a mandate, to tighten monetary policy in response — the classic fiscal-monetary trade-off, playing out in real time on trading screens rather than in a committee room.

What happens next

For now, the yield move looks like a market imposing discipline preemptively, on the assumption that words will eventually become numbers. That assumption gets tested soon. Healey has given no date for a fiscal statement, but the OBR's autumn forecast cycle and the government's need to set out spending plans before the new fiscal year put a natural deadline within months. Until then, the immediate catalysts are the BoE's September MPC decision — where a hike is now more likely priced than not — and any further comments from Burnham or Healey that either confirm or walk back the flexibility framing. A government that clarifies quickly could see yields ease; one that lets the ambiguity sit risks the kind of gradual, grinding repricing that is harder to reverse than a single bad headline.
