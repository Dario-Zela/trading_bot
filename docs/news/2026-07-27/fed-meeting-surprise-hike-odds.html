Fed meets with markets pricing an outside chance of a surprise hike

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, where the FOMC meets 28-29 July. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Futures price roughly 25% odds of a Fed hike this week, but Waller's inflation-first pivot suggests rates stay higher for longer regardless of Wednesday's outcome.

The Federal Reserve does not normally make headlines for what it might do at a meeting most economists expect to end in a hold. This week is different. When the Federal Open Market Committee wraps up two days of deliberation on 29 July, the federal funds target range will most likely stay put at 3.50–3.75%. But futures markets are pricing something close to a one-in-four chance it doesn't — a live possibility of the first rate increase since the Fed's tightening cycle ended, at a moment when most of Wall Street had been debating the timing of the next cut, not the odds of a hike.

The repricing has a name attached to it: Christopher Waller. The Fed governor, and the man Donald Trump considered and passed over for the chairmanship in favour of Kevin Warsh, has spent July methodically talking the market out of complacency.

"Unless I see evidence of a significantly weakening labor market, my focus will be on inflation."

That sentence, delivered in a mid-July speech, is a bigger deal than its plain phrasing suggests. The Fed operates under a dual mandate — price stability and maximum employment, held in tension by design. Waller's formulation isn't a tweak to that framework; it's a statement of priority. Inflation wins, jobs data would have to deteriorate meaningfully to change his mind.

Why the bond market is nervous

The mechanics behind the odds are worth tracing, because they've moved fast. CME FedWatch pricing for a July hike sat at roughly 11% in mid-month; by the week of the meeting it had risen several-fold before settling into the mid-20s to low-30s range, depending on the tracker. The proximate trigger was a run of producer-price and core inflation readings that Waller himself flagged as the threshold for action, paired with a fresh leg up in oil prices tied to the Iran conflict — a factor severe enough that Brent briefly pushed back above $100 a barrel before this week's tentative ceasefire signals eased it again.

Treasuries have taken notice. The 10-year yield climbed to around 4.71% last week, its highest since early 2025; the 30-year touched 5.19%, a level not seen in close to two decades. The dollar has pushed to a 40-year high against the yen. None of that is consistent with a market that thinks the Fed's tightening is safely behind it.

Waller has been explicit about why he's alarmed. Core PCE inflation rose from 3.0% in December to 3.4% in May, and he has named three specific drivers: tariffs, elevated energy prices, and AI-related demand pressure on data-centre-adjacent goods and power. In a speech to the New York Association for Business Economics, he put it more bluntly than most Fed officials allow themselves: "If I get another higher [reading], I'm going to treat that as signal, not noise." June's CPI print, released 14 July, actually cooled — headline inflation fell to 3.5% from 4.2%, core to 2.6% — which is precisely why the hike odds haven't gone higher still. But one soft month, after Waller has spent weeks recalibrating what counts as intolerable, has not been enough to unwind the repricing.

The chair problem

There is an irony sitting underneath all of this. Warsh was nominated in large part because he was expected to be the more dovish choice — an official who has argued that AI-driven productivity gains should let the Fed run policy looser than the data alone would justify, and who has pushed back, in public, less aggressively than his predecessor against pressure from the White House for lower rates. In June, Warsh's committee held rates and dropped the Fed's long-standing easing bias entirely. The Fed's own dot plot puts the median year-end 2026 rate at 3.75–4.00% — a quarter point above where the range sits today. Read plainly, the Fed's own projections already assume a hike is coming this year, quite apart from what happens this week. Prediction markets have moved to match: Polymarket has priced better than 84% odds of zero rate cuts in 2026, a dramatic reversal from where positioning sat as recently as the spring.

That leaves Warsh, installed to loosen policy, presiding over a committee where the loudest inflation hawk is a colleague who lost him the job. Whether that tension resolves through consensus or through visible dissent at Wednesday's meeting is itself something the market will be watching the statement and press conference for.

What it reprices

For traders, the practical question is less about this week's decision than about the signal it sends for the rest of the year. A Fed that stays higher for longer than consensus expected forces a reassessment across duration-sensitive assets: equity valuations that had priced in easing-driven multiple expansion, long-dated Treasuries, and rate-sensitive currency pairs all carry different assumptions if the terminal rate is a quarter point — or more — above where futures curves sat even a month ago. Algorithmic and systematic strategies that lean on rate-path expectations for positioning have had to adjust on short notice twice this month already, as the hike probability swung from low double digits to the high twenties and back.

The FOMC's statement and Warsh's press conference on 29 July will matter less for the rate decision itself, which is likely to be a hold, than for the language used to describe the path forward. The next real test comes with July's jobs report in early August and the following CPI print in mid-August — data Waller has already told markets he will treat as signal, not noise, if it runs hot again. The September meeting, not this one, may be where the higher-for-longer thesis is actually settled.
