UK hosepipe bans spread as record heatwave persists into fourth week

A UK reservoir exposed at drought-level water storage, the kind of scene now recurring across southern and eastern England as reservoir stocks run below the long-term average. — Peter Bond / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Eight UK water firms now enforce hosepipe bans as 2026 becomes the first year to hit 35°C in three separate months, exposing supply systems built for a milder climate.

Millions of households across England and Wales are now under hosepipe bans as a heatwave that began in early July grinds into its fourth week, with no sustained rain in the forecast. Thames Water imposed restrictions on its 15 million customers from 23 July, joining South East Water, Anglian Water, Affinity Water, Southern Water, South West Water, Cambridge Water and Welsh Water — eight suppliers now enforcing Temporary Use Bans, the formal term for what customers experience as a ban on garden hoses, sprinklers and paddling-pool fills.

The trigger is a summer without precedent in the UK temperature record. May, June and July 2026 have each produced a day above 35°C — the first time three separate calendar months have done so since records began. Kew Gardens hit 35.1°C on 26 May, Lingwood in Norfolk reached 37.7°C on 26 June — England's hottest June day ever — and Wisley in Surrey touched 35.5°C on 9 July. A joint study by the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, Imperial College London and the Met Office attributed more than 2,700 excess deaths in England and Wales to the May and June heatwaves alone, with researchers calculating that human-caused warming accounted for roughly 42% of that toll.

Reservoirs and rivers under pressure

Behind the bans is a supply system now visibly under strain. National reservoir storage stood at 78.8% in the latest Environment Agency reading, about five points below the long-term average, with Wimbleball, Grafham, Hanningfield and Blagdon among those running especially low. Only 13% of English rivers are at normal levels for the time of year. Eight regions — including Devon, Cornwall, East Anglia, the West Midlands and the Thames catchment — are classed as being in "prolonged dry weather," the stage before an official drought declaration, after England's driest spring in years fed a third heatwave that brought 12 consecutive days above 30°C. Thames Water says demand across London and the Thames Valley is running up to 10% above normal, equivalent to adding a city the size of Manchester onto the network overnight, even as it works through a backlog of leak repairs.

Not every region is stretched equally: Yorkshire Water and Wessex Water have avoided restrictions so far, and national storage, while below average, is not yet at crisis levels seen in 2022. But the pattern — bans arriving earlier in the season, across a wider geographic spread, layered on a company already managing a leakage and investment backlog — is the kind utilities regulators and investors read as a stress test of adaptation planning built around slower-moving assumptions. Ofwat's price review settlements and water companies' capital programmes were framed around historical rainfall and demand patterns that 2026 has now exceeded in a single summer.

The National Drought Group, which coordinates the Environment Agency, water companies and farming bodies, meets again as reservoir levels are reassessed; any move to a formal drought designation would tighten abstraction limits further. For now, the more immediate marker is the weather itself — forecasters see no significant rain across southern and eastern England before early August.
