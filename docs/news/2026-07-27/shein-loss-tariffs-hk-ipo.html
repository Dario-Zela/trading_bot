Shein swings to $99m loss as US tariffs bite ahead of Hong Kong IPO

Shein's corporate logo. The retailer's US business, its largest market, contracted 14.3% in the first quarter of 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shein posted a $99m Q1 loss after the US ended its de minimis tariff exemption, clouding a Hong Kong IPO valued well below earlier estimates.

Shein's accounts for the first quarter of 2026 show a net loss of $99m (£74.2m) — a reversal from the $395m profit it posted in the same period a year earlier. Revenue barely moved, up marginally to $9.05bn, but the US market, still Shein's largest, contracted 14.3% to $2.04bn. The company's own filing points to one cause above all others: Washington's decision to scrap the "de minimis" duty exemption that had let low-value parcels enter the United States tariff-free.

The numbers arrive in a draft prospectus filed as Shein presses ahead with a Hong Kong listing, sponsored by JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley, after the Chinese Securities Regulatory Commission cleared the float in July. It is the fast-fashion retailer's third attempt at going public, following abandoned bids in New York and London.

What de minimis meant to Shein's model

The de minimis rule, which exempted shipments under $800 from import duty, was tailor-made for Shein's direct-to-consumer logistics: garments shipped individually from Chinese factories straight to US customers, skirting the bulk-import tariffs that weigh on conventional retailers. Washington ended the exemption for Chinese and Hong Kong-origin goods on 2 May 2025, and for a stretch that followed, packages faced tariffs as steep as 145% before a temporary US-China trade truce cut that rate to 30%. Shein has since raised US prices to absorb the difference, but the filing makes clear that repricing hasn't been enough to offset the hit — the $99m loss also includes a $328m non-cash fair-value charge on convertible preferred shares, but the underlying trading pressure is the tariff bill.

An IPO built on shakier ground

The timing complicates the equity story. Bankers had once floated a valuation near $100bn when Shein's US growth was still accelerating; the range now under discussion for the Hong Kong deal has narrowed to roughly $40bn-$50bn. Shein has not disclosed deal size, price or timetable in its draft prospectus, leaving the underwriters to build a valuation case around a company that just told regulators its flagship market shrank by double digits. For a business whose entire appeal to investors rested on volume growth funnelled through a tariff-light supply chain, a quarter that shows the opposite is an awkward opening exhibit.

The read-across extends beyond Shein. Its predicament is becoming a template for how trading desks price other Chinese direct-to-consumer exporters — Temu chief among them — now that the loophole underpinning years of ultra-cheap, tariff-free shipping has closed. Tariff exposure, once a footnote in these prospectuses, is now the first line of the risk section.

The next marker is the Hong Kong Stock Exchange's review of pricing and timing, expected in the coming weeks; investors will also be watching whether Washington's 30% truce rate on Chinese goods holds past its current window or reverts higher, which would reopen the same margin question Shein is now trying to explain away.
