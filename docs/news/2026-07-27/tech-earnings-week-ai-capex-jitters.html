Big Tech earnings week opens with Intel's growth beat overshadowed by AI capex jitters

Intel's Fab 12, Fab 22 and Fab 32 complex in Chandler, Arizona — the company posted its fastest revenue growth since 2011 but shares still fell 7.9% on capex concerns — neepster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Alphabet and Intel both beat earnings and got sold off anyway last week — the AI capex reckoning now lands on Microsoft, Meta, Apple and Amazon this week.

Wall Street opens earnings week with a simple question hanging over the country's most valuable companies: how much AI spending is too much? Microsoft and Meta report on Wednesday, Apple and Amazon on Thursday, and between them the four companies carry a combined market capitalisation north of $8 trillion. Their results will settle little on their own — but the tone was already set last week, and it was not a happy one.

On Thursday, Alphabet and Tesla reported first. Alphabet beat revenue estimates, with its cloud business growing 82%, and still got hammered. The company raised its 2026 capital-expenditure guidance to a range of $195 billion to $205 billion, up from $180-190 billion previously, and posted negative free cash flow — minus $5.9 billion for the quarter — for the first time since it went public in 2004. The stock fell as much as 7% in the sessions that followed. Tesla dropped 14% on its own numbers. Together, the sell-off dragged the Magnificent Seven index down 4.8% in a single day, erasing roughly $797 billion in market value — the worst day for the group since the tariff shock of April 2025.

The next evening, Intel added a stranger data point to the same story: a company that beat earnings comprehensively and got punished anyway.

Intel's beat that wasn't rewarded

Intel's second-quarter numbers, released after the close on July 23, were the company's best in years by several measures. Revenue reached $16.1 billion, up 25% year-over-year — the fastest growth pace since 2011 — and non-GAAP earnings of $0.42 a share doubled the $0.21 analysts had pencilled in. The data-centre and AI segment, the part of the business most tied to the hyperscaler buildout, jumped 59% to $6.3 billion, while server average selling prices climbed 48% as chief executive Lip-Bu Tan called it the company's strongest revenue growth in more than fifteen years.

A GAAP net loss of $11 billion, or $2.16 a share, accompanied the release, but that was almost entirely a $12.5 billion non-cash mark-to-market charge tied to the escrowed shares Intel issued the US government under its CHIPS Act arrangement — an accounting artefact, not an operating signal. The stock popped nearly 13% in after-hours trading on the news.

By Friday it had given all of that back and more, closing down 7.9% on the day. The proximate cause was the same one that hit Alphabet: capital spending. Intel confirmed plans to spend more than $20 billion in 2026, up from roughly $18 billion, to keep pace with demand — a modest increase in absolute terms, but one investors read through the same lens now applied to every AI infrastructure dollar. A quarter ago, that guidance might have been cheered as evidence of a turnaround gaining traction. Instead it fed a market already primed to punish spending, whatever the revenue attached to it.

The four reporting this week

Which brings the market to the four names actually on this week's calendar. Analysts, per JPMorgan estimates, expect the five largest US cloud and AI infrastructure providers — Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Oracle — to spend a combined $660-690 billion on capex in 2026, and the four hyperscalers alone (Amazon, Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft) are tracking toward roughly $725 billion this year, up about 77% from $410 billion in 2025. Projections for 2027 run close to $950 billion, even before the companies reporting this week update their own numbers.

Microsoft, down 21% year-to-date, faces the most direct version of the Alphabet question: analysts are modelling fiscal 2027 capex near $262 billion, and the market will want to see that justified against monetisation — Copilot's more than 20 million paid seats — rather than promised against future demand. Meta, off 9.8% this year, has already guided to $125-145 billion in full-year capex; the debate there is whether Reality Labs losses stacked on top of AI infrastructure costs start compressing margins investors can see now, rather than growth they're asked to trust later.

Amazon carries a similar capex load through AWS. Apple is the outlier, and the market has treated it that way: shares are up 23% this year and the company has become the S&P 500's single largest contributor to returns, having largely sidestepped the infrastructure arms race by licensing model capability from outside partners rather than building it in-house. Whether that restraint reads as discipline or as falling behind is itself a question this earnings season is testing.

What broke, and what it means

What changed is not the spending — capex guidance has been rising all year — but the market's tolerance for it. "People are really focused on capex, obsessed with it," Jason Lemire, chief investment officer at Bold Wealth Partners, said following Alphabet's report. "It used to be the more the better, but now it is the less the better." That is close to a full reversal of the logic that drove the AI trade for the past two years, in which aggressive spending signalled confidence and got rewarded with a higher multiple. Intel's Friday session showed that the reversal isn't limited to the four hyperscalers directly building AI data centres — a chipmaker benefiting from the same demand got caught in the same repricing, on guidance that was, by its own recent standards, unremarkable.

For now the broader market is drawing a distinction between revenue growth, which remains largely intact, and capital discipline, which investors are demanding evidence of before extending further credit on the AI thesis. Alphabet and Intel both delivered the first without convincing the market on the second. Microsoft and Meta get the first chance this week to show the two aren't mutually exclusive; Apple and Amazon follow a day later, with Apple arriving as something close to a control group for what happens to a mega-cap that hasn't joined the spending race at all. The next test after that is less a date than a pattern: whether Wednesday and Thursday's numbers calm the capex nerves or extend the sell-off that started with Alphabet and caught Intel in its wake.
