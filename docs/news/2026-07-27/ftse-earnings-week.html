FTSE earnings week: AstraZeneca, GSK, Barclays and Rolls-Royce report

AstraZeneca's Academy House headquarters at the Cambridge Biomedical Campus. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's $80bn ambition and GSK's £3.6bn sales gap headline a week testing whether FTSE pharma still anchors the index.

Five of the FTSE 100's most-watched names report over four days this week, and the two pharmaceutical giants among them are telling almost opposite stories. AstraZeneca opened the week on Monday still chasing an $80bn annual revenue ambition it set for 2030 — a target its own chief financial officer once called "a stretch" but now insists is "very much within reach." GSK, reporting Tuesday alongside Barclays and Unilever, is doing the opposite kind of arithmetic: explaining away a reported £3.6bn hole in its medium-term sales plan.

AstraZeneca's stretch target meets a pipeline setback

Consensus still points to double-digit revenue growth for the Anglo-Swedish drugmaker, whose 2023 revenue of $45.8bn has climbed toward analyst forecasts of roughly $76bn for 2030 — within touching distance of the $80bn goal management laid out that year. Jefferies is more cautious, pencilling in $70bn, and the wider consensus sits nearer £66.8bn-equivalent territory.

The scrutiny this week is sharper than usual because of what happened on 9 July, when AstraZeneca's Phase 3 CARDIO-TTRansform trial of Wainua (eplontersen) failed to hit its primary endpoint in transthyretin-mediated amyloid cardiomyopathy, a heart condition the company had hoped would push the drug's peak sales above $5bn. Shares fell as much as 9.55% that day, erasing close to £19bn of market value in a single session. Wainua remains approved in more than 20 countries for a narrower nerve condition, and that business is untouched — but the setback narrows one of the routes AstraZeneca was counting on to reach $80bn, and investors will want to hear how management plans to close the gap with its other 20-odd late-stage medicines.

GSK's £3.6bn gap and the Nuvalent bet

GSK's shortfall traces back to a decision taken just weeks ago: abandoning camlipixant, the chronic-cough drug it acquired for roughly $2bn in its 2023 purchase of Bellus Health. After reviewing data from the CALM-1 and CALM-2 trials, GSK concluded the drug's benefit was "unlikely to transform patient care" — a blockbuster hope, once pegged at £2.5bn in peak sales, reduced to a write-off. That loss is a meaningful piece of the £3.6bn now missing from the company's push toward £40bn in annual sales by 2031, up from just under £33bn in 2025.

GSK's answer is Nuvalent, the US oncology biotech it agreed to buy for $10.6bn — its largest acquisition in over a decade — plus a specialty medicines portfolio spanning oncology, HIV and respiratory disease. Whether that combination can plug a hole of this size, while the company's general medicines unit continues to erode ahead of looming patent losses, is the question Tuesday's results need to answer. GSK has so far held its 2026 guidance of 3–5% turnover growth.

Barclays and Rolls-Royce complete the picture

Barclays reports the same day as GSK, with attention on net interest income as UK gilt yields sit above 5% for the first time since 2008 — good for margins on paper, but set against mounting bad-debt provisions tied to geopolitical instability and cost pressure in the retail bank. Rolls-Royce follows Thursday alongside Shell, Lloyds, BAE Systems and LSEG; analysts expect first-half operating profit near £1.9bn, roughly 10% higher than a year ago, with full-year guidance of £4.0bn-£4.2bn intact. Its shares have rallied more than 4% recently on stronger engine-utilisation rates and defence spending, though expectations are now high enough that any miss would be felt.

The throughline for the week is less about any single number than what it says about the FTSE 100's defensive core. Pharma has long been the index's ballast; a widening gap between AstraZeneca's growth story and GSK's damage-control one suggests that ballast is no longer uniform. Barclays' numbers Tuesday will show whether elevated gilt yields are a tailwind or a warning sign for UK banks, and Rolls-Royce's Thursday report will test whether the aerospace recovery has room left to run. The next broad read on UK corporate health comes with the Bank of England's rate decision in the weeks ahead, against a backdrop of a FTSE 100 that closed last Friday at a record 10,736.23.
