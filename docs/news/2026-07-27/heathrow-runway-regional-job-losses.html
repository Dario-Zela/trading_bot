Heathrow's third runway would cost other UK regions thousands of jobs, government's own analysis shows

Birmingham Airport, which government modelling shows could lose 7.5 million passengers and around 9,500 jobs a year by 2050 if Heathrow's third runway goes ahead. — Phillip Capper / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
The DfT's own modelling shows Heathrow's third runway would cost Birmingham Airport 7.5m passengers and roughly 9,500 jobs a year by 2050 — undercutting the project's regional-benefit pitch.

The pitch for a third runway at Heathrow has always rested on a simple promise: what's good for London's hub airport is good for Britain. A bigger Heathrow means more routes, more trade, more jobs — and, ministers have long insisted, benefits that ripple out well beyond the M25.

The government's own economists don't entirely agree.

A Department for Transport analysis, examined by the New Economics Foundation (NEF) and reported this week, shows that expansion at Heathrow would strip passengers and jobs from airports across England and Wales as traffic is drawn toward the expanded hub. The single largest loser is Birmingham. By 2050, the DfT's own modelling suggests, Birmingham Airport would carry 7.5 million fewer passengers a year than it otherwise would — a hit equivalent to roughly 9,500 jobs lost at the airport and in its supply chain.

What the government's own numbers say

The figures come from within Whitehall, not from campaigners. The DfT has periodically modelled how a third runway would reshape the geography of UK aviation demand, and that modelling — reviewed and aggregated by NEF's economic policy team — puts the West Midlands' total loss at nearly 10,000 jobs, most of them concentrated at Birmingham. Nationally, the same modelling implies 15,200 aviation jobs that would otherwise have grown up around regional airports, plus a further 6,400 jobs at other London and south-east airports, would instead migrate to Heathrow by 2050.

That is a displacement story, not a growth story. As Dr Alex Chapman, NEF's head of economic policy, put it in the group's assessment of the underlying figures: "jobs created at Heathrow would primarily represent jobs displaced from around the country." NEF's broader review of the department's cost-benefit case has been blunter still, arguing it would be "erroneous to claim a broad distribution of the gains from Heathrow to regions" — and, in earlier work on the department's wider airport-expansion modelling, that the projected national GDP benefit is "only up to 0.05% per year," a rounding error next to the political weight the project is asked to carry.

The Department for Transport disputes that the figures tell the whole story. A spokesperson said the NEF analysis does not account for the more than 60,000 local jobs the department expects Heathrow expansion to generate directly, and pointed to a projected £2.6bn annual boost to the economy, with up to 40% of that landing outside London and the south-east. Heathrow itself has gone further, publishing research arguing that a third runway would support new or restored routes to airports including Leeds Bradford, Liverpool John Lennon, Belfast International and Teesside — a version of expansion that feeds regional connectivity rather than draining it.

Birmingham's stake in the fight

Birmingham's exposure is structural rather than incidental. The airport has spent the past decade positioning itself as the Midlands' long-haul gateway, winning back routes that once had to route through Heathrow or Gatwick. A third runway at Heathrow, with its extra long-haul capacity, threatens to pull that traffic straight back — the same dynamic that prompted business leaders from Greater Birmingham Chambers of Commerce, Marketing Birmingham and the airport itself to write jointly to ministers in past expansion rounds, warning against concentrating growth in the south-east at the expense of a "competitive network" of UK airports.

The pattern repeats, at smaller scale, at airports across England and Wales, wherever a Heathrow connection currently substitutes for direct long-haul service. The DfT's modelling treats that substitution as arithmetic: passengers who would have flown from a regional airport instead route through an expanded Heathrow, and the jobs that go with the passengers follow them south-east.

Why the politics have got harder

The timing sharpens the stakes. The government opened its public consultation on the draft Airports National Policy Statement in June, running through 1 September, with Parliament expected to vote on the final policy statement in the autumn — the step that sets the planning framework a full Development Consent Order will need between 2027 and 2029. Transport secretary Heidi Alexander, who survived Andy Burnham's cabinet reshuffle after he became prime minister on 20 July, remains the minister who will have to defend the project through that vote.

A vote that depends partly on MPs outside London believing expansion pays a regional dividend becomes harder to win when the sponsoring department's own paperwork suggests otherwise. Every regional MP whose constituency airport sits on the losing side of that 15,200-job ledger now has documentary cover to say so. For investors weighing infrastructure exposure to the project — contractors, the airport's institutional shareholders, the supply chain lined up for a 2030s build — the risk is not that the runway gets cancelled outright, but that the coalition required to pass the ANPS vote gets harder to hold together the longer this analysis sits in public view.

What comes next

The consultation closes on 1 September, feeding into the version of the Airports National Policy Statement that goes to a Commons vote in the autumn — the point at which regional MPs will have to decide whether Heathrow's promised £2.6bn outweighs their own airport's place in the DfT's loss column. Watch for whether the department revises or defends its modelling before then, and whether Birmingham, Bristol or the other regional airports named in the analysis escalate from letters to formal objections once the DCO process opens in 2027.
