Christian nationalism in Britain is driven by people who seldom attend church, report finds

In one sentence
A Theos survey finds Britain's most Christian-nationalist-leaning group attends church only slightly more than average, pointing to a cultural movement rather than a religious one.

The people in Britain who most closely match the profile of Christian nationalism are, for the most part, not in church on Sundays. That is the central finding of a survey from the think tank Theos, as reported by the Guardian. It cuts against a common reading of the British right, in which religious conservatism is assumed to follow the pews.

What the survey found

Theos polled 1,765 adults in April 2026 and sorted respondents into five groups according to their attitudes to Christianity and to British national identity. The group whose views come closest to Christian nationalism, which the researchers call "identity integrationists", is the smallest, at 11% of the sample.

Most of them call themselves Christian: 81%. But only 13% attend church weekly, and 31% pray weekly. Just under half, 47%, say they are interested in the Bible. Some 90% say that defending Christian culture and heritage is important to being a Christian.

The contrast is with a larger group, the "belief integrationists", at 13% of the sample. This group has the highest share of practising Christians: 31% attend weekly, 46% pray weekly and 62% are interested in the Bible. Only 18% of them say defending Christian culture is important to being a Christian. Nearly half, 48%, agree that Christian values require welcoming asylum seekers, against 19% of the identity group. It is also the most ethnically diverse group, with 20% identifying as non-white.

The word "seldom" needs care. Average weekly attendance across the whole sample is 11%, so the identity group, at 13%, attends slightly more than the national average. The finding is less that these people never go to church than that church-going does not distinguish them. The people who go most often are not the ones most attached to the nationalist idea.

Idea and practice

Nick Spencer, a Theos senior fellow and one of the authors, framed the divide as "a difference between Christianity as an idea and Christianity as an embodied practice". On that reading, the nationalist group treats Christianity as a marker of belonging to the nation, not as a set of obligations.

Church figures have said much the same. Arun Arora, the Church of England bishop of Kirkstall, observed that "the nationalists… want to take up the cross at marches". The theologian Helen Paynter said: "The Christian faith is not just an identity that we can put on like a coat."

The wider survey data point the same way. It found that 55% of Britons would be unhappy if their local church became a mosque, including 37% who would be very unhappy. It also found that 37% agreed with the "great replacement" conspiracy theory. Whatever those figures measure, they describe unease about demographic and cultural change, not devotion.

Why it matters for the right

Theos's earlier, preliminary work for the same project, published in May, put the headline numbers in perspective. About 17% of UK adults agreed that you need to be Christian to be truly British, and 8% strongly agreed. Looser positions drew far more support: 41% backed law based on Christian values and 37% wanted the government to declare Britain a Christian country. British Social Attitudes data show the share saying that being Christian is important to being truly British fell from 32% in 1995 to 19% in 2023.

The implication is that the audience for Christian-nationalist language on the British right is not a congregation to be courted through churches. It is a looser constituency, culturally attached to a Christian inheritance, and its politics would be better read through identity, immigration and national belonging than through theology. That differs from the American picture, where PRRI data show that frequent attenders are considerably more likely than rare ones to hold Christian-nationalist views.

The usual caveats apply. This account rests on the Guardian's reporting of the survey, not on the full report, whose formal title and technical appendix were not reviewed. How the groups were constructed matters a good deal: a cluster defined partly by agreement that defending Christian heritage is important will tend to select people for whom heritage matters more than practice. Theos itself calls its project's findings tentative; the work runs through 2027 and extends to other European countries, and those comparisons are the next test of whether the British pattern is distinctive.
