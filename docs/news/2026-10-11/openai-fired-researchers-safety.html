Fired OpenAI researchers say they were let go for 'prioritising safety'

In one sentence
Three ex-OpenAI safety researchers say they were fired for putting safety first; OpenAI says it was for mishandling sensitive information, and neither account is independently verified.

Three former OpenAI safety researchers say the company fired them because they put safety ahead of its commercial interests. OpenAI says that is wrong: it says they mishandled sensitive information and broke its trust. Both accounts are on the record. Neither has been tested by an outside party, and the dispute is so far a public argument, not a finding.

What the researchers say

The three are Mikita Balesni, Tomek Korbak and Jasmine Wang. They worked on safety and alignment at OpenAI. They say they were dismissed in the week before they went public on Thursday 8 October, in an open letter and posts on social media.

Balesni wrote on X: "I believe we were fired for prioritizing safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI as a corporation." Korbak says he believes the trigger was his concern that OpenAI was "losing the ability to monitor what AI agents think, one of our best tools for catching when they misbehave." That refers to a technique in which researchers read an AI model's intermediate reasoning to spot when it is heading somewhere it shouldn't.

The letter says the dismissals, and the way OpenAI has described them inside and outside the company, have a chilling effect. Colleagues, it says, may now be "afraid to speak and operate in ways that, until last week, were an integral part of working at OpenAI." The three say they had openly raised concerns and disagreed with colleagues, and that they had been encouraged to work with independent safety organisations. They deny breaking company policy and say their contact with outside safety experts was within the scope of their jobs. AI, they argue, is "not a normal technology" and OpenAI is "not a normal company", so outside collaboration should not carry the fear of retaliation.

What OpenAI says

OpenAI replied on X on Friday 9 October, saying it had "parted ways" with the three the previous week. It said an internal investigation found they had violated policies on handling sensitive information. It described "a significant breach of trust beyond what's outlined in the letter they published" and said it stands by the decision.

It also said: "These decisions were not about raising safety concerns or speaking out." The company says it tolerates good-faith mistakes, does not fire people for raising concerns, and works with third-party safety assessors and independent safety organisations. It said it was saddened by the outcome and credited the three for their contributions to safety. It agreed with the letter on one point: keeping frontier models monitorable "requires an industry-wide commitment" that includes OpenAI.

The two accounts can both be true in part. A person can raise real safety concerns and also break a confidentiality rule. What the public record does not yet show is what information was involved, who received it, or whether the company's account of a breach "beyond" the letter will ever be spelled out. The researchers say sharing with external experts was part of their job. OpenAI says the line was crossed. Without the documents, or an independent review, there is no way for an outsider to settle that.

Why it is being read as a governance test

The timing raises the stakes. Last month OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, SpaceXAI and Nvidia endorsed a voluntary accord, announced by President Trump, that calls for stronger internal safeguards and external audits. Critics said it was nonbinding. OpenAI and Anthropic have also proposed a coordinated international slowdown in AI development, an idea the US and China have rejected. OpenAI recently cancelled the planned release of its GPT-6.1 Astra model because it failed to meet the company's standards for behaving in line with human wishes.

Those commitments rely heavily on staff being able to speak up and work with outside experts. That is why a retaliation claim matters beyond the three people. If a lab's safety promises depend on employees flagging problems, how it treats dissent is part of the evidence for whether the promises mean anything. A poll by AP-NORC found almost two-thirds of Americans think AI is developing too quickly, so the political appetite for scrutiny is there.

This is not the first time OpenAI has faced such questions. Safety researchers at several labs have resigned publicly in recent years, saying they did not want to contribute to the risks. But a resignation is a personal choice, while a dismissal that staff believe was punitive is a claim about the company's conduct.

What to watch

The next developments are whether the three file a formal legal or regulatory complaint, whether OpenAI says more about what it found, and whether any current employees back either side. Regulators who are weighing external audits will also have a practical question: whether those audits would include a route for staff to raise concerns without fear of losing their jobs. For now, the allegation remains an allegation, and the company's rebuttal remains unverified too.
