Houthi strikes reported at Riyadh airport, with flights cancelled and the toll unclear

In one sentence
Saudi authorities say three Saudis died in Houthi-claimed missile attacks on Riyadh's King Khalid airport and a Saudia jet; flights were hit and higher death-toll reports remain unconfirmed.

Saudi Arabia's civil aviation authority says three Saudi citizens, among them an airline pilot, have been killed in missile attacks on Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport and a Saudia aircraft. The Houthis, the Iran-backed movement that controls much of northern Yemen, claimed the strikes. The claim is no longer the only account: Riyadh has confirmed deaths and damage. That puts the Yemen war inside the Saudi capital in a way it has not been before.

What is established

The attack came on Thursday 8 October. A witness told the Associated Press that a large blast shook the airport, that fire and ambulance crews were deployed and that smoke was visible. Departures were halted. Passengers were told to leave checked luggage and evacuate. The Houthis said they hit airports in Riyadh and Najran with ballistic and cruise missiles, offering no evidence for the specifics. Their military spokesman, Yahya Saree, made the claim.

The Saudi-led coalition gave a different picture of the same day. It said it intercepted and destroyed two ballistic missiles aimed at Riyadh and one aimed at Khamis Mushait. Saudi civil defence said debris from an interception damaged a kindergarten and a medical complex, and mentioned no casualties there. Saudi authorities then put the toll from the airport attacks at three dead, all Saudi nationals.

The number needs care, because accounts differ. AP and Times of Israel reporting spoke of one to three dead. The BBC was reported to have given a far higher figure, 12 killed and about 300 injured. I could not find that figure confirmed by Saudi authorities or by a wire service, and it does not match the official count. It is not used here.

Part of the variation is arithmetic. Gulf News and Khaleej Times describe a run of strikes that began earlier in the week. In their account, a Sudanese resident was killed and eight people were wounded at Riyadh airport. Two women, a Moroccan and an Algerian, were killed and 28 injured at Abha airport. Those accounts date the earlier attacks to 6–7 October and give a combined toll of three dead and 36 injured. Reports describe these as the first deaths since the Saudi-led offensive against the Houthis began. Whether the pilot's death belongs to the earlier strikes or to Thursday's is not clear from the material I have. Readers should treat the official three as the current figure, subject to revision.

Flights and the Gulf's air bridge

The disruption is better documented than the casualties. FlightAware reported that hundreds of flights to and from Riyadh were cancelled on Thursday. Cirium data cited by Khaleej Times found that 169 of 378 scheduled departures from King Khalid, or 44.7 per cent, were cancelled or listed as "no fly". Flightradar24 said the airport "closed again" on Thursday afternoon, which implies at least one earlier closure.

Foreign carriers moved faster than the Saudi authorities. Air India suspended operations through Saturday. The Lufthansa Group said its airlines would stop flying to Riyadh until 16 October. A US Embassy alert issued the day before had already warned of possible airspace closures. Saudia said its operations were back to normal from 6pm on Thursday, and the civil aviation authority said air traffic had resumed. A same-day recovery says something about the airport's resilience. The suspensions say more about how airlines are pricing the risk.

That matters because Riyadh is not a peripheral hub. It is the capital of the Gulf's largest economy, and it is the destination for the business, pilgrimage-adjacent and expatriate traffic that Saudi Arabia is trying to grow.

The oil warning and the wider war

The Houthis also warned global airlines "for the last time" about operating in Saudi airspace, and told people in the kingdom to stay away from oil facilities, which they called targets. They say they are enforcing a blockade on Saudi Arabia. The warning is a threat, not an attack, and no strike on an oil facility has been confirmed in the reporting I have. Oil prices jumped after the blasts, according to AP, but no benchmark figure was available, so the size of the move cannot be given. The market context is an energy shock already running; see diesel stays near records as the Iran-war energy shock reaches central banks.

The strikes sit within a ground and sea campaign. The Saudi-led offensive aims to retake territory the Houthis seized a month ago on Yemen's Red Sea coast and at the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the narrow passage through which much of the Red Sea shipping route runs. The coalition said it destroyed a Houthi launch platform in Sanaa used against Riyadh, a claim that could not be independently verified. US support reportedly includes intelligence sharing, target identification and offensive planning.

The diplomatic response has been wide but cautious. Britain, India and the UAE condemned the attacks. Turkey and Pakistan are assessing defensive support for Saudi Arabia, but Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan said: "Turkey will not be sending troops into another country's territory to carry out an attack."

What to watch

Three things will show how far this has moved. The first is whether Lufthansa and Air India extend their suspensions beyond 16 October and Saturday respectively, since airline decisions will show how the risk is judged more quickly than any statement. The second is whether Saudi authorities publish a full casualty list that reconciles the Riyadh and Abha strikes. The third is whether the Houthis follow the oil-facility warning with an attack that can be confirmed, and whether a crude benchmark moves by a measurable amount when they do.
