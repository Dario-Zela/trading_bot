Labour holds Starmer's old seat, but Polanski's Greens face a crisis of their own

In one sentence
Labour held Starmer's old seat by 3,364 votes, but Polanski's Greens, who said he will stay on, now face a fight over whether members or MPs set the party's direction.

Labour held Keir Starmer's old seat in north London on Thursday. The result has done less for Labour than for the party that came second. The Greens, led by Zack Polanski, finished 12 points behind in Holborn and St Pancras, then spent the days around the vote arguing over whether their members or their MPs decide what the party stands for.

A seat held, a lead narrowed

Sagal Abdi-Wali, the Labour candidate, took Holborn and St Pancras with 12,292 votes, or 45%. Polanski, standing in person, polled 8,928, or 33%. The majority was 3,364. Starmer had represented the constituency before the by-election, so Labour was defending a seat with a prime-ministerial name attached to it.

The margin of victory is only part of the story. Reports of a swing of about 13 points to the Greens have not been independently verified, but the direction is clear. A party that was a marginal presence in much of inner London a few years ago came within reach of one of Labour's best-known seats, with its leader as the candidate. Labour will count the hold as a win. It will also read the Greens' share as a measure of how much support its left flank has lost.

Polanski chose to fight the seat himself, which raised the stakes. A leader who stands and loses cannot say that a local candidate was the problem.

The motion

The second blow was self-inflicted, and it came during the campaign. At the party's autumn conference, members passed a motion declaring the Greens an "anti-Zionist party" and saying Zionism should be "treated as any other form of racism". The vote was 1,022 in favour and 813 against. The motion also backs a single democratic Palestinian state in all of historic Palestine, with Jerusalem as its capital.

Polanski, who is Jewish, was not in the hall. He had left the conference to campaign in the by-election. He called the wording "an unhelpful distraction" and said he wanted amendments, but he also said he agreed with it "in substance". Asked after the count whether he still backed it, he said the displacement of the Palestinian people was racist, and he would not take an explicit position on the motion itself.

Israel's foreign ministry called the text "a clear antisemitic text" and announced that Polanski was barred from entering the country. The Jewish press has questioned whether the episode hurt the party's appeal, and the argument has become a story about the Greens, whatever the wording of the motion was meant to say.

Members against MPs

The motion passed over the objections of senior figures. The former leader Caroline Lucas opposed it, as did most of the party's MPs. Ellie Chowns, Sian Berry and Carla Denyer are named in reports as having done so publicly. According to BBC and Guardian reporting, Green MPs have warned that the party must avoid being captured by extremists. The warning is aimed at the activist wing that carried the motion, and by implication at a leadership that was absent when it was put.

The party's own line is that conference motions of this kind have no "material impact on governance or disciplinary procedures". That is a technical defence. Members voted for the policy, the leader will be asked about it at every interview, and an MP who disagrees must say so publicly or stay silent.

The dispute also follows from how the Greens have grown. Rapid membership growth brings in people who are energised, organised and often well to the left of the parliamentary party. Conference is where they have the most influence. MPs and a leader seeking votes in places like Holborn have to win over a wider group, and what that group wants on foreign policy is less clear. The Greens have long managed this gap with a loose structure and a lot of goodwill. A fast-growing party has less of both.

Lucas suggested that defeat could threaten Polanski's position. He answered that he would stay on: "Yes absolutely, that's what leaders do." No challenge has been reported, and the Greens have no easy mechanism for a quick one. His authority rests on his ability to bring in votes, and a second place in a seat like this one does not settle that either way.

What comes next

The immediate test is whether the motion is amended or reopened. Polanski has said he wants changes, and the next conference or a members' ballot are the routes. Whether the four or five MPs press for that, or accept the result, will show who holds the balance in the party.

The wider question is for Labour. The seat stays red, but the Greens' share suggests that a left-leaning voter in London now has a credible alternative. Whether the Greens can keep that vote depends on whether they can show it is not tied to the demands of their most committed members.
