Ex-Deutsche Bank trader's rate-rigging conviction overturned

In one sentence
London's Court of Appeal quashed ex-Deutsche Bank trader Christian Bittar's Euribor rate-rigging conviction, with written reasons still to come.

A London appeals court has quashed the conviction of Christian Bittar, the former Deutsche Bank trader jailed for manipulating Euribor, the euro interbank rate. The ruling came on Friday 9 October. The "Libor-era" label on the story needs a qualifier: Bittar's case concerned Euribor, Libor's euro-denominated sibling, though it belongs to the same family of prosecutions and fell on the same legal reasoning.

The ruling

The Court of Appeal, sitting before Lord Justice Edis, Justice Goose and Justice Moody, said it would publish written reasons later. Until they appear, the court's own account of why Bittar's case fell is not on the record. What is known comes from the arguments heard and from reports by Reuters and PA.

Bittar pleaded guilty on 2 March 2018 to conspiring to rig Euribor and was jailed for five years and four months. Reports at the time put his earnings at as much as £47m a year. He is reported to be the first person convicted of manipulating Euribor or Libor after a guilty plea to have that conviction overturned. His was not a contested trial, which makes the reversal harder to achieve than the earlier ones.

Why it fell

The route runs through the Supreme Court. Last year it ruled that legal errors had undermined the fairness of the first Libor trial, that of Tom Hayes, and of a later trial, that of Carlo Palombo. Their convictions were overturned. Bittar then applied to have his own conviction quashed.

His barrister, Adrian Darbishire KC, argued that Bittar had admitted the offence on the strength of erroneous court rulings about what the prosecution had to prove. A defendant must receive a clear and accurate account of the offence, he said, which is "fundamental to the fairness of any trial process". The Serious Fraud Office, represented by James Waddington KC, countered that by pleading guilty Bittar had acknowledged that he disregarded the proper basis for making Euribor submissions.

The court has not yet said which argument it accepted, or how far. That matters for anyone else who pleaded guilty.

A run of reversals

The ruling follows another on Wednesday, when the same court overturned the convictions of five former Barclays employees: Jonathan Mathew, Jay Merchant, Alex Pabon, Philippe Moryoussef and Colin Bermingham. Reuters counts eight rate-rigging convictions overturned in total. Wednesday's ruling reportedly found that errors in how judges directed juries carried through into later trials.

Jason Williams, head of division at the SFO, said: "We argued for a different outcome but respect the court's decision in relation to Christian Bittar."

What to watch

The written judgment is the next data point. It should show whether the court treated Bittar's guilty plea as tainted by the same misdirection as the trials, and so whether the reasoning extends to other defendants who pleaded. It should also show what is left of the remaining benchmark-rigging convictions. The reversals so far suggest the prosecutions rested on a legal test that the Supreme Court has since found wanting. Whether that holds for every case is for the judgment to say.

This account rests on wire reports of the hearing, not on the judgment itself, and should be read with that limit in mind.
