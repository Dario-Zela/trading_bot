Burnham's cost-of-living test: Budget worries, a VAT cut on electricity and a softer jobs market

In one sentence
Burnham's government faces a Budget that must show living-cost relief while 30-year gilts sit above 6%, a softening jobs market erodes headroom, and the VAT cut saves only about £45 a year.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister since 20 July. His government is already being measured on a promise it has barely begun to keep: that households will feel better off. The autumn Budget is the first big chance to show it. It also lands while the bond market is demanding more to lend to the Treasury than it has for a generation.

The political test

The worry is coming from inside the party. Labour figures quoted by Guardian Politics put it bluntly: "If voters aren't better off by the next election, we're screwed." The quotes are unattributed, and the sources give no detail on what relief they expect or when.

The quote matters to markets because of the constraint behind it. Burnham has repeatedly declined to say whether he will raise taxes. John Healey, who replaced Rachel Reeves as chancellor, has to find room for visible relief inside a fiscal framework that is tightening. One estimate puts the gap in the public finances at about £14bn. Saxo's analysis of the post-conference position says the rise in yields will at least halve the headroom against the fiscal rules, which stood at £23.6bn in March. Both figures are third-party estimates, not official forecasts. The Office for Budget Responsibility's numbers, which will settle the matter, arrive with the Budget itself.

What has been delivered so far

The one concrete measure is small. Domestic electricity has been free of VAT since 1 October. The government puts the saving at about £45 a year per household. Reporting on the measure puts its cost to the Exchequer at around £850m. That is modest next to a £14bn hole. It is also small next to the household budget, where £45 is under £4 a month.

Two questions follow, and neither has an answer yet. The first is whether suppliers pass the full cut through to bills. The second is how much it takes off measured inflation, which depends on the same pass-through. The cut gives the government something to point to, but the political risk is that a saving this size goes unnoticed while energy costs elsewhere stay high.

The jobs backdrop

The labour market is softening, which sharpens the problem. Reports put unemployment at about 5% and wage growth slowing. I could not match those figures to a current ONS release. The latest release I could confirm, covering the three months to January, had unemployment at 5.2% and regular pay growth at 3.8%, down from 4.1%. That is a loosening market with real pay gains thinning out, which is bad ground for a government promising visible improvement.

The policy ideas on offer are mostly small. Former minister Alan Milburn has urged a pre-apprenticeship scheme to close the youth unemployment gap. Burnham has also pledged to curb non-compete clauses, a promise reported as covering finance, tech and professional services. Both are statements of intent. Neither is law, and neither would change household incomes before the Budget.

Where the gilt market comes in

The link between the political test and gilts is real, but it is not the one the Labour insiders imply. Long-dated yields have been rising for reasons that mostly pre-date any Budget. By early October the 30-year gilt yield had passed 6% for the first time since 1998, and the 10-year was above 5.5%, according to Morningstar and others. Some commentary describes this as part of a global bond sell-off. One strategist's verdict, reported in IFRE's coverage, was that "so far, so steady" described Burnham's own contribution. The yields are the highest in the G7.

That sets the terms for the Budget. UBS has argued that 10-year yields have room to rally by at least 20 basis points if the Budget sticks to the existing fiscal rules. The reverse follows from the same logic. A package that loosens the rules, or relies on optimistic forecasts to pay for relief, would probably remove that cushion. Leaning on tax rises to protect the rules would be politically costly. This is the squeeze the insiders fear. Voters need to see gains, and the market needs to see discipline.

The evidence supports that reading only in part. Yields have moved on the global backdrop and on fiscal expectations, and no source separates the two. A weak Budget would not need to cause a sell-off to hurt, because a market already at 6% on long gilts has little slack to absorb a disappointment.

What to watch

The Budget date and the OBR forecast are the two decisive items. The forecast will show whether the headroom is closer to £12bn or to nothing, and whether any room remains for relief beyond the electricity VAT cut. Before then, the next ONS labour-market release will show whether unemployment is at 5% or higher. Supplier pass-through of the VAT cut will show up in the next CPI release. If the Budget cannot offer more than £45 a year, the 6% yield on 30-year gilts will measure what the shortfall costs.
