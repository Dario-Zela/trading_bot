RUSSIA SANCTIONS · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-11 · 4 min read

US suspends Russian diesel sanctions to April 2027 as Kyiv calls it a 'gift to Putin'

In one sentence
The US has licensed Russian diesel imports until 7 April 2027 to ease pump prices before the midterms, drawing Zelensky's charge of a 'gift to Putin' and raising doubts over its own sanctions law.

Washington has spent four years building a sanctions wall around Russian energy. On Friday it opened a door in it, and the reason it gave was the price of diesel at American pumps.

President Donald Trump said on 9 October, after a call with Vladimir Putin, that Russia would immediately supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the United States, with millions of tonnes to follow. The same day the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) issued a general licence authorising transactions linked to the sale, delivery, offloading and import of Russian-origin diesel until 7 April 2027. US diesel futures fell about 4% on the news. Volodymyr Zelensky called the move a "gift to Putin".

What was announced

The numbers come mainly from Trump's own account, and some are conditional. Reports of the call put the schedule at more than 300,000 tonnes immediately, a further 500,000 in November and about 1 million soon after. A further 3 million tonnes was said to depend on the condition of Russian refineries. Trump's headline figure was about 4.8 million tonnes in stages. The White House did not immediately say who would pay for the cargoes, when they would arrive, or whether the November deliveries would land before the 3 November midterm elections.

The legal instrument is narrower than the rhetoric. OFAC's licence runs through 12:01 a.m. Eastern time on 7 April 2027. It is a time-limited authorisation, not a repeal, and the wider US sanctions programme on Russia stays in place. UK and EU measures also remain fully in force. Russia has its own diesel export restrictions, which makes the promised volumes harder to take at face value.

The length of the licence is the unusual part. Earlier Treasury waivers on Russian oil, issued to ease shortages during the Iran war, followed the standard 30-day cycle. This is the first such easing on diesel to run for roughly six months since the Ukraine war began.

Why diesel, and why now

Diesel is the fuel of freight, farming and construction. It has stayed near record levels since the Iran-war energy shock, as covered elsewhere in this edition. Pump prices feed into delivery costs and food bills, and they are a standing grievance for voters. With the midterms three weeks away, the administration has an obvious motive to be seen acting.

That is the reading the evidence supports, though it is an inference. The administration has not framed the licence as a change of policy towards Moscow. It has framed it as a supply deal. The timing, the immediate-delivery pledge and the announcement by the president himself all point at the domestic audience.

The move also arrives awkwardly. Reports note that it came only weeks after Trump signed sweeping legislation targeting Russia's energy sector. That law, passed with bipartisan support, directs the administration to pressure the largest buyers of Russian energy. A licence that lets American importers buy Russian diesel sits uneasily with that intent, and it leaves the threatened secondary measures in doubt. Analysts will be watching whether Treasury enforces the law against third-country buyers while carving out an exception for the United States.

Kyiv's response

Zelensky was blunt. He called the easing a gift to Putin and said that letting Russia sell petroleum products is an investment in a war that should be ended, not prolonged. He added that any easing of sanctions without a clear and lasting de-escalation agreement is a sign of weakness. Ukrainian negotiators were reported to be in talks in Florida as the licence was issued, which gave the criticism an extra edge.

The triage notes for this piece also attributed to Trump a remark that it was "time for Ukraine to get new president". That quote could not be confirmed against an original source and is not relied on here. Trump has previously said it is "time" for Ukraine to hold elections and has questioned Zelensky's standing. Whatever he said this week, the exchange shows how far apart the two governments are on whether sanctions pressure or accommodation will bring Moscow to terms.

The wider cost

Sanctions work partly through credibility. Allies enforcing their own Russia measures have been asked to accept higher energy costs for the sake of a shared policy. If the largest enforcer adjusts its rules when prices bite, those allies have a precedent to cite when their own pressures mount. Energy traders, for their part, will price the licence as a temporary supply loosening rather than a regime change, which fits the modest 4% move in futures.

For Moscow, the benefit is concrete. Sales of refined products bring in revenue, and a US licence gives other buyers cover. Whether it brings concessions at the negotiating table is the question Kyiv is asking, and so far no concession has been announced.

What to watch

The first test is physical: whether the 300,000-tonne cargoes arrive, and at what price, before 3 November. The second is legal: whether Treasury names buyers of Russian energy under the new sanctions law, or lets the deadline pass. The third is the licence itself, which expires on 7 April 2027. Whether it is renewed, as earlier short waivers were, will show whether this was a pre-election measure or a lasting change in policy.
