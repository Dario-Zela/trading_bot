Drones find peat fires smouldering beneath the Cairngorms

In one sentence
Thermal drones are being used to find peat fires smouldering underground in Scotland, but evidence of wide deployment is thin and the Cairngorms details are unverified.

Fires in Scotland's peatlands do not always end when the flames do. Peat is dense, carbon-rich ground that can keep burning below the surface, with little smoke and nothing much to see from above. Thermal-imaging drones are now being used to find these hidden burns, in what the BBC reports is the Cairngorms area. That makes wildfire adaptation a question of detection as well as suppression.

The BBC report is the only source for the Cairngorms deployment. Its specifics could not be independently checked: which agency is flying the drones, how many fires have been found and when the work began. This article therefore avoids stating any of them. The wider picture, though, is well documented elsewhere.

Why peat is hard to see

A moorland blaze can look finished once the surface vegetation has burned off and the fire crews have gone. Peat can go on smouldering underneath. These fires can re-ignite days or weeks later, which is why crews have traditionally returned to burned sites to watch for wisps of smoke. That method depends on someone spotting the smoke, and it is easy to miss in wind or dense ground cover.

The stakes are not only local. Peatlands store large amounts of carbon, and a fire that gets into them releases it. Forestry and Land Scotland (FLS) has cited an estimate that the six-day Flow Country fire in 2019 released about 96,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide.

What the drones do

A thermal camera measures heat rather than light, so a pocket of smouldering peat shows up as a warm patch against cooler ground even when nothing is visibly burning. A Scottish precedent is already on record. After a fire on Dava Moor in the Highlands in July 2025, FLS flew the thermal drone it normally uses to count deer to help the Scottish Fire and Rescue Service (SFRS). Doug Harvey, FLS's East Region manager, said its main contribution was "piloting the thermal imaging drone to help locate the danger areas once the flames were out". According to the FLS release, the imagery showed crews where to apply water. That let the fire service deal with the hotspots, hand the land back to its owners and stand its crews down.

The research points the same way. A 2019 pilot study in the journal Drones, based on work in Indonesia, found that underground peat fires can be detected with thermal infrared sensors on drones. It also found that a simple temperature threshold could flag them automatically. A 2025 paper from Liverpool John Moores University examines how to detect and geolocate such fires with drone-mounted thermal cameras. The 2019 study's authors cautioned that more work is needed on real-world fire detection and firefighting.

An emerging tool, not yet a system

Nothing found so far shows these drones being used at scale across Scottish uplands. The Dava Moor case used equipment borrowed from deer-counting work, which suggests a cheap, opportunistic tool rather than a dedicated fleet. If that holds in the Cairngorms, it is encouraging: a modest piece of kit could cut the time crews spend patrolling burned ground, and could lower the risk of a second fire flaring up after a dry spell.

The open questions are practical ones. They include how many trained pilots are available, whether imagery can be gathered quickly enough in a fast-moving incident, and how reliably thermal cameras separate a smouldering seam from sun-warmed rock. The next evidence to look for is any formal assessment from SFRS or the land managers involved, and whether detection flights become a routine step after upland fires rather than an occasional favour between agencies.
