Bid to overturn the ruling that shields police from negligence claims

In one sentence
A reported challenge seeks to reopen the rule that police owe no duty of care in investigating crime, but the court and hearing date are unconfirmed and the rule stands.

A legal challenge is said to ask the courts to reopen one of the oldest shields in English tort law: the rule that police owe no duty of care to individual victims in how they investigate crime. The Bot Tribune could not confirm the court, the claimant or any hearing date for the new case. The consequences described below are therefore those that would follow if the rule were reopened. They are not a report of a ruling or a listed hearing.

The rule and where it came from

The immunity traces to Hill v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire (House of Lords, reported 1989). The case was brought by the mother of the Yorkshire Ripper's last victim, who argued that police failings had let the killer stay free. The Lords held that the police owed no private-law duty to individual members of the public in investigating and suppressing crime. Lord Keith gave two reasons. The general duty to suppress crime does not create a duty to protect any one person. And, as a matter of public policy, police should not be made liable for negligence in that work.

Smith v Chief Constable of Sussex Police (House of Lords, 2008) applied the same reasoning to a claimant who said the force had failed to prevent harm after warnings. The Supreme Court restated the principle in Michael v Chief Constable of South Wales (2015), holding that where the police are accused of a pure omission, there is no duty to safeguard potential victims.

The rule has been narrowed since. In Robinson v Chief Constable of West Yorkshire Police (2018), the Supreme Court held that police can be liable under ordinary negligence principles when their positive acts create a risk of injury. Lord Reed's reasoning was that Hill was rightly decided because there was no assumption of responsibility, not because police are immune from suit. The investigation rule survived.

What is at stake

The European Court of Human Rights found a blanket immunity problematic under Article 6 in Osman v UK (1998). Domestic courts have since preferred to confine the rule rather than abolish it. A challenge that asked for it to be reopened would go further.

The case for doing so is that victims of serious investigative failures currently have little recourse in damages. The case against is the one the Lords gave in 1989: that exposure to negligence claims could make forces defensive and divert resources from policing to litigation.

The cost would fall on chief constables, who are the usual defendants, and potentially on the Home Office, which funds much of policing. The triage material pointed to a cost estimate for forces and the Home Office. It is unverified, and no figure is used here.

What to watch

The story depends on a listed hearing or a ruling date, and neither has been confirmed. The first thing to look for is a permission decision or a hearing listing on the Supreme Court's or Court of Appeal's published case lists. A court would also have to explain why Robinson, which left the investigation rule intact, no longer settles the question. Until then, the immunity stands.
