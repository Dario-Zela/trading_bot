Lib Dem frontbencher quits over Ed Davey's leadership

In one sentence
Charlie Maynard quit as Lib Dem Treasury spokesman, reportedly to trigger a no-confidence move against Ed Davey, days after Alison Bennett left the frontbench; no formal challenge is confirmed.

A second Liberal Democrat frontbencher in under a week has left Sir Ed Davey's team, and this time the departure appears to be aimed squarely at the leader. Charlie Maynard, the MP for Witney, quit as the party's Treasury spokesman on Friday. The party confirmed he had gone. Maynard has not explained himself publicly and declined to comment.

A resignation with a purpose

Resignations from the frontbench are usually about a brief, a vote or a row over policy. This one is reported to be different. The Financial Times reported that Maynard stepped down in an attempt to trigger Davey's removal, and that he has asked parliamentary colleagues to support a vote of no confidence in the leader. That account comes from newspaper reporting rather than from Maynard, and he has not confirmed it.

The timing adds weight. Four days earlier, Alison Bennett, the party's spokesperson for care and carers, also stood down from the frontbench. Two exits in a week do not amount to a revolt in a party with 72 MPs, the total it won at the July 2024 general election. In a parliamentary party that small, though, each departure is easy to see, and Treasury is one of the senior briefs.

The case against Davey

The frustration is reported to rest on the party's standing in the polls. The latest YouGov survey puts the Liberal Democrats on 11%. Davey has led the party since 2019. He was re-elected leader unopposed in 2024, the year the party won its best Commons result in its modern form. Critics are therefore measuring him against that high point, and the polls have not built on it.

The response

Davey's allies were quick to answer. Deputy leader Daisy Cooper said the "vast majority" of the party's MPs wanted him to stay as leader. Tim Farron, a former leader, also backed him, as did other senior figures, according to coverage carried by the Irish News.

The public statements so far are one-sided. Supporters have spoken on the record, while Maynard has said nothing. The number of MPs willing to back him is therefore unknown. A challenge needs more than one willing frontbencher, and Bennett's resignation has not been linked in any report seen so far to a coordinated effort.

What to watch

Three things will settle whether this is a protest or the start of something larger. The first is whether further frontbenchers resign. The second is whether any MP says publicly that they have backed a no-confidence motion. The third is whether the party's formal rules for challenging a sitting leader are invoked. No report so far confirms a formal no-confidence vote or a leadership contest. If the resignations stop at two and the poll numbers hold at around 11%, Davey probably rides it out. A third name would turn a pair of exits into a pattern.
