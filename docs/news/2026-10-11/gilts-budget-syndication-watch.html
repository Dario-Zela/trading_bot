Gilts: Budget and a long-dated syndication are the next tests after the BoE's QT year

In one sentence
With the Bank of England's £70bn QT year over, a long-dated gilt tender on 13 October and the DMO's November index-linked syndication will test demand ahead of the Budget.

The Bank of England's heaviest year of gilt sales has ended, and the next test of the market's appetite for UK government debt falls at the long end. The Bank's plan for October 2025 to September 2026 was £70bn of quantitative tightening, down from £100bn the year before. Dealers now expect the Debt Management Office (DMO) to sell long-dated gilts through a syndication in October and an index-linked deal in November. If the market asks for a price concession on those sales, it will be read as a verdict on fiscal credibility ahead of the Budget.

What the Bank has just done

The Bank did not simply roll the old programme forward. On 17 September its Monetary Policy Committee voted 9-0 for a multi-year plan, according to press reports of the decision. I could not retrieve the Bank's own statement, so the details below rest on that coverage.

  • All sales are paused until April. In that time the Bank will consult the government on selling gilts directly to the DMO at market prices, rather than through its own auctions.
  • Sales of long-dated gilts are halted entirely.
  • £120bn of gilts maturing in 2049 or later will be kept permanently to back banknotes.
  • £222bn maturing by 2034 will be allowed to run off, and £146bn will be sold.
  • Active sales will run at about £20bn a year. Including maturities, the average annual unwind is about £46bn, compared with £70bn over the past twelve months.

Governor Andrew Bailey said the remainder will be unwound "over the next eight years". The Bank estimates QT has raised gilt yields by about 0.25 percentage points, while some analysts put the effect on 30-year yields nearer 0.75 points.

The halt on long-dated sales matters here. The Bank is no longer adding supply at the part of the curve where private demand is thinnest. That leaves the DMO's own programme as the main source of new long-dated paper.

What the DMO has said, and what dealers want

The distinction between expectation and announcement matters here. GlobalCapital reports that gilt-edged market makers and investors want a long-dated conventional syndication in October, followed by an index-linked deal in November. An earlier GlobalCapital report said a long conventional bond was the overwhelming choice of gilt investors for the third-quarter syndication.

The DMO's published plan is narrower. It schedules one syndication in the third quarter of 2026-27, for a new or existing index-linked gilt in November. It does not list an October long-dated syndication. The calendar says it may change, and the DMO's own announcements are the authority on timing.

Long gilts still arrive in October by another route. The DMO has pencilled in a long conventional gilt tender for Tuesday 13 October, subject to demand and market conditions. It has also asked whether the tender should involve one gilt or two. Investors consulted by the DMO have favoured long issuance, with 4% 2063 mentioned as one candidate. Many urged it to keep the choice of maturities flexible until nearer each date.

Why the long end is the test

Syndications differ from auctions. The DMO appoints banks, markets a single bond to a wide investor base and prices it in one day. The size of the order book and the yield concession needed to clear the deal show how deep demand is. Pension funds and liability-driven investors are the natural buyers of long maturities, so a poorly received deal says something about them as well as about the Treasury.

The QT estimates set the backdrop. If the Bank's removal of supply is worth a quarter of a percentage point on yields, as it estimates, a market with the Bank stepping back should find it easier to absorb new issues. A concession on a long gilt would suggest the market's concerns lie elsewhere, in the deficit, the borrowing path or the Budget arithmetic.

What to watch

The first marker is the 13 October long tender, if it goes ahead: the cover ratio and the tail against the prevailing market level. Next is any DMO announcement of a syndication mandate. Then comes the November index-linked deal. After those sit the Budget and the Bank's consultation with the government on direct sales to the DMO, due to conclude before sales resume in April.
