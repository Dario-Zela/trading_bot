Nikon rules prize-winning image was AI-generated after backlash

In one sentence
Nikon disqualified the winner of its Small World in Motion microscopy contest for using generative AI, after scientists doubted the footage, and says it will review its rules.

A prize in one of photography's more rarefied corners has been taken back. Nikon has ruled that the winning entry in its Small World in Motion contest, which rewards photos and videos captured through powerful microscopes, was made with generative AI and so broke the competition's rules. The entry has been disqualified.

The case is not a conventional photography row. The disputed work was a video, not a still, and it came from a scientific contest rather than an art prize. Some headlines have called it an "image"; the reports describe a video.

What was entered

The winning entry came from Dr Ning Xu of Tsinghua University in China. It showed cilia, the tiny hair-like structures that sweep mucus along the airways, moving in a child's airway. The footage was linked to primary ciliary dyskinesia (PCD), a respiratory condition in which those structures do not work properly.

Small World in Motion has run for 15 years, according to reports based on the BBC's coverage. The reports do not say which year's award this was, or what the prize was.

How it unravelled

The challenge came from scientists, not from Nikon. Several researchers, some of them former judges, questioned the video's authenticity in comments on Nikon's LinkedIn page.

Dr Robert Hirst of the University of Leicester was among the first to speak up. He has spent 20 years diagnosing PCD, and he told the BBC the cells and cilia in the video bore little resemblance to those of patients with the condition. He said he recognised it as fabricated and was not surprised by Nikon's decision.

Nikon said it reviewed the video and supporting materials in detail and consulted the judging panel. It concluded that generative AI had been used in creating the entry, contrary to the contest rules.

Xu acknowledged using AI but said he believed his submission complied with the rules. He cooperated with Nikon's investigation. Nikon stressed that the disqualification was not a judgement on his "professional reputation or scientific contributions."

The rulebook question

The dispute over where "AI-assisted" ends and "AI-generated" begins is the awkward part. Xu says he believed he complied; Nikon says he did not. The reports do not set out the wording of the rule or what Xu did with the tool, so the gap between the two readings cannot be assessed from outside.

The way the case came to light is also telling. The check that worked here was expert eyes. A specialist looked at the footage and saw biology that did not fit. Nikon's own review followed that public challenge, and the reports describe no automated detection. One outlet has described forensic analysis of pixel data and metadata, but the BBC coverage does not mention it, so that detail is unverified.

Microscopy sharpens the problem. Judges of an artful portrait can ask whether the scene was staged. Judges of a scientific video are being asked to trust that what they see is a real specimen behaving as it really behaves, and few of them can tell at a glance.

What comes next

Nikon says it is now reviewing the contest's rules and procedures. The thing to watch is whether the revised rules define what generative AI use is permitted, if any, and whether entrants will be asked to submit raw files or acquisition data alongside their work. Nikon has not said when the review will finish.
