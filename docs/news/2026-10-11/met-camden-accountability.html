Met volunteer chief sacked after AI tool found deleted images; Camden warned over nursery worker

In one sentence
The Met's most senior volunteer officer was sacked after forensic AI recovered deleted intimate images from his work phone, while Camden faces questions over the Vincent Chan nursery case.

The most senior volunteer officer in the Metropolitan Police has been dismissed without notice. The case turned on a detail that matters beyond the man himself: the images that ended his career had been deleted from his force-issued phone, and a forensic system found them anyway.

James Deller, the Met's Special Constabulary chief officer, was sacked after an accelerated misconduct hearing, according to the force and to reports by the BBC, LBC and others. The hearing found that in July and August 2025 he used his police-issued phone to photograph two intimate images of a woman. The images had been sent to his personal phone through WhatsApp's "view once" function, which is meant to stop recipients keeping what they are sent. He then sent the copies back to his personal device and deleted them from the work phone. He is to be added to the College of Policing's barred list, which prevents him serving in policing again.

How the deletion failed

The images came to light during a week-long operation the Met has called Project Galatasaray. The force said investigators used lawful business monitoring together with Palantir's Foundry system, which uses AI. Deller had been suspended in May. Reports have linked the case to a wider inquiry, Operation Artificial, which flagged more than 100 officers and staff after the Palantir software was introduced. The Met has said no criminal allegations arise in his case.

The individual conduct is serious, but the mechanism is the wider story. Deletion on a work phone is not erasure in any sense an employer need respect. A "view once" message, a deleted photo and a cleared gallery all gave the user a sense of impermanence that did not hold on a device the force owned. Public employers that issue phones now have a plain example of what monitoring and recovery tools can reconstruct, which bears on device policy, on what staff are told about monitoring, and on how misconduct inquiries gather evidence.

It also raises oversight questions the Met has not yet had to answer in public. Who audits a tool that scans staff devices at scale? What are the false-positive rates? The Met's earlier experience of image-recognition software mistaking desert photographs for nudes is a reminder that such systems err. In this case a hearing tested the evidence, and the outcome does not appear to rest on the software alone. As the monitoring widens, that safeguard will matter more.

The case is not isolated. Another Met officer, Detective Inspector Ben Coogan, was dismissed without notice this year after a routine audit of his force-issued phone found sexually explicit images of a woman taken between May and July 2025. Two dismissals, with the device as the common witness, suggest the force is now looking at phones as a matter of course.

A different failure in Camden

A separate institutional question is playing out in north London. Camden council faces scrutiny over the case of Vincent Chan, the nursery worker jailed for 18 years in February after police found about 26,000 indecent images of children. Reports say a colleague reported concerns about videos he was making of children on a work iPad to both the nursery and the council in May 2024; he was arrested the following month. Parents have told the BBC they warned the nursery operator, Bright Horizons, months earlier and felt dismissed.

The point at which any warning reached the council, and what it did with it, is still unsettled. A published safeguarding practice review is under way, and the council has referred the case to the Health and Safety Executive. The claim that Camden was warned "years" before the arrest could not be confirmed from available reporting.

What ties them together

Both cases ask whether an institution can see what its own systems already hold. The Met found what a deleted file left behind; Camden is asked why a report did not stop an abuser sooner. The next markers are the safeguarding practice review in Camden and any Met account of how its monitoring is governed.
