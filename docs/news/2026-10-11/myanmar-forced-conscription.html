Young men in Myanmar fear being seized and forced into the army

In one sentence
Four years after the coup, Myanmar's junta is filling its ranks through street seizures and trafficking, rights groups say, a sign of manpower shortages and a daily threat to young men.

Four years and eight months after the generals seized power, military service in Myanmar has become something young men try to dodge on the street. The junta's conscription law is formally a call-up system, but rights groups and journalists describe a cruder practice of seizures, bribes and sales. Young men say they now plan their journeys to work, to market and to the next village around the chance of being taken.

From statute to street

The army, known as the Tatmadaw, took power on 1 February 2021, ending the elected government led by Aung San Suu Kyi. It has been fighting armed opposition ever since. In October 2023 the Three Brotherhood Alliance of ethnic armed groups launched Operation 1027, and the military lost towns and bases it had held for years.

The People's Military Service Law followed on 10 February 2024. It was the country's first conscription law. It covers men aged 18 to 35 and women aged 18 to 27. Refusing service carries up to five years in prison, according to rights groups. Soldiers who abscond after enlisting are reported to risk far harsher penalties, including death.

On paper this is a lottery-style draft. The reporting suggests it has not worked that way.

Seizures and sales

The Asian Parliamentarians for Human Rights (APHR) said in a May statement headed "Stealing a generation" that young people have been abducted while walking home from work or taken from their homes. It cited reports that up to 120,000 people have been forcibly recruited since the law took effect. That figure comes from an advocacy group relying on secondary reports, not an independent count, and should be read as an estimate.

Radio Free Asia's Burmese service has gone further. Its September investigation found thousands of young men captured, sold and conscripted, with brokers and some officials profiting. The exiled National Unity Government's human rights ministry has concluded that the practice may amount to forced labour and human trafficking. Defectors and conscripts quoted in these reports say recruits are sent to active front lines with minimal training. Many of these sources are opposition-aligned, and the junta has little reason to confirm their accounts. Their findings are nonetheless consistent with one another.

What it says about the army

Compulsion on this scale points to a manpower problem. An army that can fill its ranks with volunteers does not need to pay brokers or snatch men at checkpoints. The shift from the law's tidy age bands to opportunistic seizure suggests quotas are being met by whatever means are available, and that losses and desertions are running ahead of recruitment.

The consequences spread beyond the front lines. Young men who can afford to leave try to cross into Thailand or elsewhere, which adds pressure on neighbouring states and on the migrant workers who already send money home. Those who cannot leave stay indoors, move between towns less, or pay to be left alone. In effect, the costs of the war are being moved from the battlefield to the civilian population.

What to watch

The next markers are documentary rather than dramatic: whether UN human rights bodies formally classify the seizures as trafficking, whether Thailand and other neighbours change their treatment of men crossing the border, and whether the junta's planned electoral timetable is accompanied by any easing of recruitment. Until then, the evidence comes mainly from the testimony of those who were taken, and those who ran.
