Teledyne to acquire Varex Imaging for $1.1bn

An X-ray tube — the type of imaging component at the core of Varex's business, alongside digital detectors and high-voltage interconnects. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Teledyne will pay $18.90 a share — a 52% premium — for X-ray component maker Varex Imaging in an all-cash, $1.1bn deal.

Teledyne Technologies has agreed to buy Varex Imaging, the Salt Lake City-based maker of X-ray tubes and imaging detectors, in an all-cash deal valuing the company at $1.1 billion. Teledyne will pay $18.90 a share — a premium of roughly 52% to Varex's $12.41 close on August 7, the last full trading day before terms leaked ahead of Monday's announcement. Varex shares jumped nearly 50% on the news, closing in on the offer price as arbitrage traders priced in a high probability of completion.

The price tags Varex at about 1.3 times its expected 2026 revenue of roughly $870 million, a modest multiple for a components supplier but a rich one relative to where the stock had been trading. Varex's 52-week high before the deal was $14.56; for much of the past year the shares had languished nearer $10, weighed down by soft Chinese demand and inventory overhang across the medical-imaging supply chain. The offer clears both marks comfortably, which is presumably the point — Teledyne is paying up to remove any doubt about shareholder approval.

What Teledyne is buying

Varex was spun out of Varian Medical Systems in January 2017, inheriting a lineage that traces back to Eimac Products in the 1930s and Varian's subsequent decades as an X-ray-imaging pioneer. Today it supplies X-ray tubes, digital flat-panel detectors, high-voltage interconnects and imaging software to original-equipment manufacturers such as GE Healthcare, serving medical diagnostics, industrial non-destructive testing, security screening and vehicle inspection. It employs about 2,400 people across North America, Europe and Asia.

That is a narrower, more component-level business than Teledyne's own imaging operations, built up through the 2011 acquisition of DALSA and the 2017 purchase of e2v. Robert Mehrabian, Teledyne's executive chairman, framed the fit as deliberately complementary rather than overlapping: Teledyne makes X-ray detectors but not for high-radiation environments like oncology, he said, while Varex is one of the few suppliers of advanced photon-counting detectors. Varex chief executive Sunny Sanyal called the deal a match for the company's X-ray technologies and "a substantial premium for our shareholders."

Financing and timeline

Teledyne is funding the purchase from its own balance sheet rather than issuing equity, a straightforward call for a company sitting on around $285 million of free cash flow generated in the second quarter alone and carrying leverage of roughly 1.1 times earnings — comfortably below levels that would worry ratings agencies. Neither company has disclosed a debt or revolver breakdown for the transaction. Both boards have approved the deal unanimously; it still needs Varex shareholder approval and antitrust clearance, and Teledyne expects to close in early 2027, a timeline that leaves several months of regulatory review and integration planning ahead.

The consolidation angle

The deal fits a broader pattern among industrial-technology conglomerates: rather than build niche imaging capabilities organically, larger players are buying specialist suppliers outright once valuations soften. Varex's shares had spent much of the past year trading well below where analysts thought they belonged — the five-analyst consensus target sat at $18, barely under Teledyne's final offer — making it a candidate for exactly this kind of opportunistic bid. For Teledyne, the acquisition adds scale in a segment — medical and industrial imaging components — where demand has stayed resilient even as broader industrial capital spending has been patchy.

The near-term risk sits in execution rather than price. Varex's own recent results flagged soft demand in China and inventory digestion among its OEM customers, headwinds that will now become Teledyne's problem to manage through the transition. Investors will get their next read on integration planning and financing detail when the merger proxy is filed ahead of the Varex shareholder vote; regulators' response to the tie-up, given the limited product overlap both companies have emphasized, is the other variable to watch before the early-2027 close.
