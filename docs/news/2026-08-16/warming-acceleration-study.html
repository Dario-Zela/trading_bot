Study finds sharp acceleration in global warming rate over past decade

Stefan Rahmstorf, PIK climatologist and lead author of the warming-acceleration study. — Frédéric Batier / Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (CC BY-SA 3.0 DE)
In one sentence
Potsdam Institute researchers find global warming has nearly doubled to 0.35°C per decade since 2013-15, with 98% statistical certainty across five datasets.

The rate at which the planet is warming has nearly doubled over the past decade, according to a new statistical analysis from the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research (PIK) — a finding its authors say clears a threshold of confidence that earlier warming-acceleration claims could not.

Grant Foster, a US statistics expert, and Stefan Rahmstorf, a PIK climatologist, examined five independent global temperature datasets — from NASA, NOAA, the UK's HadCRUT, Berkeley Earth and the European Copernicus service's ERA5 — after stripping out the short-term noise caused by El Niño cycles, volcanic eruptions and the solar cycle. What was left, they report in Geophysical Research Letters, is a warming rate of roughly 0.35°C per decade since around 2013–15, against an average of just under 0.2°C per decade between 1970 and 2015. The acceleration shows up across all five datasets with more than 98% statistical certainty — the kind of consistency that has been missing from previous attempts to detect a speed-up in real time.

"We can now demonstrate a strong and statistically significant acceleration of global warming since around 2015," Foster said. Rahmstorf was blunter about the implication: at the current pace, the world would breach the Paris Agreement's 1.5°C long-term threshold before 2030.

What might be driving it

The paper's leading explanation is not a mystery new forcing but an old one going into reverse. Reduced aerosol cooling — chiefly the sharp cut in industrial sulphur pollution from Chinese power plants and, separately, tighter international shipping-fuel rules — has stripped away some of the atmospheric haze that had been masking greenhouse-gas warming for decades. Anthropogenic radiative forcing, the study estimates, has risen roughly 50% since 2000, with that aerosol unmasking as the dominant contributor.

The result is not without pushback. Because the raw, unfiltered temperature record shows no statistically significant acceleration on its own — only the adjusted series does — some climate scientists, including Andrew Beaulieu's 2024 analysis, have argued that a genuine "warming surge" is hard to isolate from natural variability with confidence. Foster himself has floated a more conservative estimate, closer to 0.33°C per decade, than the paper's central figure.

For markets that price long-duration climate risk — carbon allowances, green bonds, insurance-linked securities, utility and energy-transition equities — the study's core contribution is less the headline number than the statistical rigour behind it. Risk models built on a 1970–2015 baseline rate now have a specific, peer-reviewed alternative to test against. The next checkpoint is the UN's periodic stocktake ahead of COP31, where negotiators will confront how much closer these numbers put the 1.5°C limit than official projections currently assume.
