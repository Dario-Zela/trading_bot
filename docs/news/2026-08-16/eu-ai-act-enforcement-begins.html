EU AI Act transparency rules now in force as enforcement begins

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which houses the AI Office now enforcing the bloc's transparency rules. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
The EU's AI Act moved from law on paper to active enforcement this month, with fines up to €15m for AI systems that fail to disclose they're AI.

Brussels spent three years writing the rulebook. On August 2nd it started reading companies their rights — or rather, informing them of the fines.

The European Commission's AI Office, working alongside national market watchdogs in each of the 27 member states, began enforcing Article 50 of the EU AI Act: a set of transparency duties that require chatbots to admit they're machines, deepfakes to carry a label, and AI systems that read emotions or classify people by biometric data to say so upfront. Two weeks in, the rule is live, the fines are real, and the first test of whether Brussels can make good on Europe's most ambitious piece of tech law is quietly under way.

What the rule actually says

Article 50 is narrower than it sounds. It doesn't ban anything or vet any model before launch — it just insists people aren't fooled about what they're dealing with. Four obligations apply now: chatbots and voice assistants must disclose they're AI at first contact, unless that's already obvious from context; providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark that output in a machine-readable format; deepfakes need a visible or audible disclosure, not a buried metadata tag; and emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation tools must tell the people being scanned that it's happening.

Companies that don't comply face fines of up to €15 million, or 3% of global annual turnover — whichever is larger. That formula matters: for a small AI startup, €15 million could be existential; for a hyperscaler, the turnover-based figure bites harder. The Commission says more than 180 companies have already signed its voluntary Code of Practice on AI-generated content, a sign that some of the industry pre-committed rather than wait to be caught out.

A narrower net than originally planned

The rules that took effect this month are the ones that survived a rewrite. Under industry pressure — executives from ASML, Airbus, Ericsson, Nokia, SAP, Siemens and Mistral AI had warned publicly that Europe risked regulating itself out of the AI race — the Commission agreed in its June "digital omnibus" package to push back the AI Act's toughest provisions. Obligations on standalone high-risk systems, covering things like hiring algorithms, credit-scoring tools and border-control systems, now don't apply until December 2027, a 16-month delay from the original date. Product-embedded high-risk AI gets until August 2028.

Digital rights groups weren't pleased. Stefi Richani of the Equinox Initiative for Racial Justice argued the delay would let predictive systems keep operating in migration and asylum contexts with essentially no oversight, calling for an outright ban rather than a postponed rulebook. What's left standing after the trims is exactly the transparency layer now in force — the part regulators judged easiest to police and hardest for industry to credibly object to.

Compliance as a cost of doing business in Europe

For AI companies, the practical effect is a new line item. Industry estimates put first-year compliance costs for a limited-risk chatbot or assistant at roughly €15,000 to €30,000; a genuinely high-risk system, once those rules bite in 2027, could run €50,000 to €80,000-plus once conformity assessments are included. A CCIA Europe survey of founders this year found nearly a quarter of respondents were already spending more than 30% of their budgets on regulatory compliance, and about the same share said they'd considered relocating away from the EU.

That's a real, if modest, headwind against the AI investment story dominating markets elsewhere — Nvidia alone is reportedly assembling a $500 billion financing platform to keep the infrastructure buildout going. European-exposed AI firms now carry a compliance and breach-risk premium that their US counterparts, absent equivalent federal rules, largely don't. It's not enough to reprice the sector on its own, but due-diligence timelines on European AI deals are already stretching by four to six weeks as investors check regulatory readiness before term sheets go out.

What to watch

The AI Office has enforcement powers but hasn't yet announced a first case; national regulators, who share responsibility for policing deployers, are still standing up their own units. The real signal will be the first published fine — its size, and which kind of violation it targets, will tell companies how literally to take the €15 million ceiling. After that, the next deadline that matters is December 2027, when the high-risk obligations the Commission just delayed are due to land for real.
