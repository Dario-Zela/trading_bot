Airbus probes rare A320 triple hydraulic failure after Air India near-miss

An Air India Airbus A320neo, the same model as the aircraft involved in the August 4 incident. — Ayan Zaman Photography / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Airbus confirms an Air India A320neo briefly lost elevator and aileron control on August 4 after all three hydraulic systems failed at once, injuring 24.

Somewhere over the Andaman Sea on August 4, Air India flight AI2379 dropped 300 feet in four seconds. The Airbus A320neo, en route from Phuket to Delhi with 145 people aboard, had just lost hydraulic pressure in not one, not two, but all three of its independent flight-control systems at once. The elevators and ailerons — the surfaces that control pitch and roll — briefly moved on their own, the autopilot kicked off, and 24 people were hurt before the crew wrestled the jet back and landed safely in Delhi. Seventeen needed hospital treatment.

Airbus has now confirmed what investigators feared: the triple failure was real, not a sensor artefact, and it is rare enough that the manufacturer cannot yet explain it.

What the flight data shows

The A320's redundancy is the whole point of the design: three separate hydraulic circuits — Green and Yellow, driven by the engines, and Blue, with its own electric pump and a ram-air-turbine backup — exist precisely so that no single failure can take away control. Each normally runs at 3,000 psi. On AI2379, all three lost pressure in rapid succession. Airbus's preliminary read of the digital flight data recorder found the elevators and ailerons had "no reported hydraulic availability" for roughly four seconds, with pressure restored within seven. The aircraft, registration VT-EXO, an A320neo delivered in 2018, experienced g-forces above its structural limits during the excursion and has been grounded since for inspection.

Crucially, Airbus says the flight control computers themselves appear to have behaved correctly — the surfaces entered a mechanical "damping" response consistent with a genuine loss of hydraulic power, not a software fault. That narrows the question but doesn't answer it: did three physically separate systems really fail together, or did something upstream — a shared sensor, switch or wiring run — make them look like they had? India's Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau is leading the inquiry as a "serious incident," with Airbus and France's BEA providing technical support. Airbus has asked Air India to inspect hydraulic components, pressure sensors, switches and wiring fleet-wide.

Why a rare event matters at scale

The A320 family is the best-selling commercial jet in aviation history, with more than 10,000 delivered and thousands more on order; Airbus is pushing production toward 75 a month by 2027. That scale is precisely why manufacturers treat a mechanism this improbable — three redundant systems failing near-simultaneously — as urgent rather than isolated. It is also not the year's first A320 hydraulics scare: EASA issued an emergency airworthiness directive in November 2025 covering a flight-control-computer software vulnerability, since superseded by a broader AD, and regulators separately widened inspection mandates on A320-family landing-gear-door actuators earlier this year. None of those is confirmed to share a root cause with AI2379, but the pattern has put hydraulic and control-surface reliability on the A320 squarely in regulators' sights.

For Airbus, the financial exposure hinges entirely on scope. Absent evidence of a fleet-wide design or manufacturing defect, the cost sits inside routine warranty and support budgets — inspections, parts, engineering hours. If investigators trace the fault to something systemic, the calculus changes fast: mandatory retrofits, temporary operating restrictions, or a broader airworthiness directive across an in-service fleet running into the thousands, plus the reputational drag on an aircraft that airlines and lessors have treated as the industry's safe default for three decades.

What's next

AI2379 remains grounded pending structural and systems inspection, and the AAIB has not set a timeline for a preliminary report. The next concrete marker is whether Air India's fleet-wide checks, requested by Airbus, turn up a repeat fault pattern — that finding, more than anything in the initial data readout, will determine whether this stays a one-off or becomes the industry's next big airworthiness directive.
