Nvidia lines up $500bn AI financing platform with Wall Street giants

Nvidia's headquarters in Santa Clara, California, the staging ground for its pivot from chipmaker to infrastructure financier. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Nvidia enlisted six Wall Street giants to mobilise $500bn-plus for AI infrastructure, recasting chips as an 'investable asset class' days before its Aug 26 earnings.

Nvidia has spent three years selling chips faster than the world could rack them up. Now it wants Wall Street to help pay for the racks, too.

On August 10, the company announced memorandums of understanding with six of the largest pools of capital on the planet — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to build financing platforms capable of mobilising more than $500 billion in third-party money for AI infrastructure. The idea is straightforward even if the plumbing is not: instead of hyperscalers and cloud providers funding every data centre, power plant and server farm off their own balance sheets, outside investors — pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurers — put up the capital, take a claim on the future revenue those chips generate, and Nvidia keeps selling silicon into a market that no longer has to wait for any single company's capex budget to catch up with demand.

An asset class, not a line item

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, has been road-testing a new vocabulary for what his company sells. "We began by building chips," he said announcing the tie-up. "Today, we are helping create a new class of productive, investable infrastructure: AI factories." The framing is deliberate. A chip that depreciates on a five-year schedule is a cost. A data centre that generates metered, usage-linked revenue for a decade — continually refreshed through Nvidia's CUDA software rather than junked at the end of its warranty — is something an insurance company can underwrite.

The partners obliged with similar language. Jim Zelter, Apollo's president, called modern compute "a scarce, mission-critical asset class with compelling investment characteristics." Bruce Flatt, Brookfield's chief executive, said compute is "fast becoming the essential layer of infrastructure," putting it in the same sentence as toll roads and power grids — the traditional territory of infrastructure funds, not chipmakers. Larry Fink, BlackRock's chairman, framed it as deepening "our relationship with Nvidia... to connect long-term capital to essential infrastructure."

Each of the six firms will run its own platform rather than pool capital jointly, and the agreements are non-binding — subject, in the companies' own wording, to "final agreement execution." But the direction is clear: Nvidia wants a standing, self-replenishing pipeline of institutional money aimed at its customers, so that a Blackwell or Vera Rubin order is no longer gated by how much cash an individual cloud provider is willing to spend in a given quarter.

The circularity question

The timing invites scrutiny as much as enthusiasm. Nvidia is already juggling a web of financing arrangements with its own customers — most notably a discussed $100 billion equity commitment to OpenAI and talks over guaranteeing tens of billions more in OpenAI debt tied to chip purchases. Short sellers Michael Burry and Jim Chanos have both argued publicly that Nvidia's revenue is increasingly propped up by capital it helps arrange itself, a dynamic critics label vendor financing: Nvidia funds the buyer, the buyer buys Nvidia chips, and the resulting revenue makes Nvidia look healthier than organic demand alone would justify.

Huang has pushed back directly, telling investors this month that "the capital is not Nvidia revenue" and insisting "the demand is real." The distinction matters here in a way it didn't with the OpenAI arrangements: BlackRock, Goldman Sachs and the rest are not Nvidia customers being financed to buy Nvidia products — they are third-party allocators raising capital from pensions and insurers who have no existing stake in Nvidia's income statement. If that capital genuinely arrives at the scale promised, it dilutes the circularity critique rather than feeding it. If the platforms struggle to attract outside money at the promised rates, the announcement will read as another data point in what Burry and others call a AI credit bubble rather than a rebuttal to it.

Why the timing is not an accident

The announcement lands two weeks before Nvidia reports second-quarter fiscal 2027 results on August 26, a print Wall Street already expects to show revenue near $91–95 billion, up roughly 96% year-on-year, with data-centre sales — which topped $75 billion as recently as the April quarter — doing most of the lifting. Every Nvidia earnings call this year has drawn the same question from analysts: how long can growth at this rate continue before the pool of companies able to fund AI infrastructure out of pocket runs dry? A $500 billion financing platform is Huang's answer before the question is asked — evidence, timed for maximum visibility, that the constraint on Nvidia's next several years of growth is not capital availability but manufacturing capacity and power supply.

What happens next is less about Nvidia's balance sheet than about whether institutional money actually shows up at the terms described. The August 26 print will offer the first read on demand in the current quarter; the more telling test comes over the following two or three quarters, as the financing platforms either close real deals with frontier labs and cloud operators — or quietly stall while remaining, for now, a set of signed intentions.
