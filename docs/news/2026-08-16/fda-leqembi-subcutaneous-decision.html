FDA sets August 24 decision date for subcutaneous Leqembi

The Philip A. Sharp Building in Cambridge, Massachusetts, headquarters of Biogen, which co-develops Leqembi with Eisai. — Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The FDA approved Leqembi's at-home subcutaneous starting dose five weeks ahead of its August 24 deadline, letting Alzheimer's patients skip IV infusions entirely.

The Food and Drug Administration was not supposed to rule on this until August 24. It ruled five weeks early.

On July 13, the agency approved a subcutaneous starting-dose formulation of Leqembi, the Eisai-Biogen antibody drug for early Alzheimer's disease, clearing the way for patients to begin — and now complete — an entire course of treatment through at-home injections rather than hospital infusions. The August 24 date that had been circulating in analyst notes and this newspaper's own forward calendar was the regulatory deadline, not the decision itself; the FDA simply acted before it had to, a signal, according to the drug's original acceptance filings, that reviewers had "not raised any concerns... regarding the approvability" of the application even as they pushed the deadline back three months from its original May 24 target to allow time for what the companies called a "major amendment" to the filing.

What the approval actually changes

Leqembi, known chemically as lecanemab, works by clearing beta-amyloid plaque from the brain, and in the pivotal Clarity AD trial it slowed clinical decline by 27 percent over 18 months compared with placebo — a result published in the New England Journal of Medicine in January 2023 and the basis for the drug's original approval. Until now, that treatment began with biweekly intravenous infusions, a real burden for patients who need a caregiver to drive them to an infusion centre every other week for a year and a half.

The newly approved product, branded Leqembi Iqlik, lets patients start on 500 mg once weekly, delivered as two 250 mg injections from an autoinjector, each taking about 15 seconds, administered at home. It pairs with a subcutaneous maintenance dose the FDA cleared back in August 2025, so Eisai and Biogen can now claim what they describe as the first anti-amyloid Alzheimer's therapy administered entirely outside a clinical setting, start to finish. A U.S. launch is planned for late August, through specialty pharmacy channels, with Eisai's patient assistance program covering eligible uninsured patients.

The drug still carries the FDA's most serious warning: a boxed alert for ARIA, brain swelling or bleeding that shows up on imaging, usually asymptomatic but occasionally serious or fatal, particularly in patients who carry two copies of the ApoE4 gene variant. Regular MRI monitoring, unaffected by the injection route, remains part of the regimen.

The competitive stakes

Leqembi's first-quarter 2026 sales rose 74 percent year-on-year to roughly $168 million, ahead of Eli Lilly's rival antibody Kisunla (donanemab), which brought in about $124 million over the same period, according to the companies' disclosures. Kisunla holds one edge — patients can stop dosing once scans show plaques have cleared — while Leqembi has required indefinite treatment. An at-home injection across the full course narrows the practical gap between the two drugs considerably, which analysts at BMO Capital Markets and Jefferies both flagged as a meaningful boost to Leqembi's competitiveness on approval.

The next marker to watch is not a regulatory one: Eisai reports fiscal results later this year, and the company has pencilled in roughly $900 million in Leqembi revenue for the fiscal year — a target that will hinge on how quickly clinicians and patients take up the home-injection option once it reaches pharmacies this month.
