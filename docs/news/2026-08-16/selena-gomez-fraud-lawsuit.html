Selena Gomez sued for alleged fraud over mental health company

In one sentence
Investors are suing Selena Gomez, her mother and co-founder for fraud over the collapsed Wondermind startup, alleging a $95 million valuation built on deals that never existed.

Selena Gomez is one of the most-followed people on the planet — more than 500 million people across her social accounts — which is precisely what a group of investors say they were buying when they put nearly $1.2 million into her mental-health startup, Wondermind. Now they want their money back, and they are accusing Gomez, her mother and her business partner of fraud to get it.

The lawsuit, filed Thursday in federal court in Delaware by two investment vehicles — Wondermind SRS 44 and Bespoke Wondermind SPV — names Gomez, her mother Mandy Teefey and co-founder Daniella Pierson. Wondermind launched in November 2021 promising mental-fitness content, tools and eventually an app, and by 2022 it was raising money from investors at a claimed $95 million valuation. The complaint alleges that valuation rested on representations that never held up: partnerships with major brands, advertising deals, celebrity magazine tie-ins, and an app that, in the plaintiffs' words, "was never built."

"The partnerships did not exist. The initiatives never materialized," the complaint states, adding that Gomez "purported to sign a contract obligating her to perform and then ignored it." Investors say they were told Gomez would be "intimately involved" in the business as its head of marketing — a role the suit claims she never meaningfully filled. The complaint also alleges Teefey told investors that Pierson used company funds to help cover roughly $60,000 a month in New York rent, and that the company's internal dysfunction was concealed for more than three years, only surfacing publicly after a September 2025 investigation in The Cut detailed a rift between Pierson and Teefey that pushed Teefey out of the brand in early 2023.

Gomez's attorney, Mathew Rosengart, called the allegations against her "completely meritless, both factually and legally," and said the team would move to dismiss. Pierson told the New York Times she "categorically denies" wrongdoing and invested her own money without drawing a salary. Teefey has not commented.

The founder-liability gap

Celebrity-backed startups have leaned on star power as a fundraising shortcut for years — a famous name substitutes for the track record a first-time founder would otherwise need. Wondermind's pitch followed that playbook closely: investors say the $95 million valuation was built substantially on the promise of Gomez's reach and involvement, not on audited revenue or signed contracts. The lawsuit argues those claims — partnerships with named institutions, specific product deadlines — were the kind of thing a routine due-diligence call could have checked before money moved.

That gap matters beyond one startup. Regulators and plaintiffs' lawyers have increasingly treated a celebrity's active involvement in fundraising materials as grounds for personal securities-fraud exposure, not just reputational risk — the same logic that has snagged crypto endorsers in recent years. A motion to dismiss, which Gomez's team says is coming, will test how far that theory reaches when the celebrity is a co-founder rather than a paid spokesperson. Wondermind itself did not respond to requests for comment; its current operating status is unclear.
