Met Police apologises after data breach exposes Al Fayed accusers' emails

The rotating sign outside New Scotland Yard, headquarters of the Metropolitan Police. — Matt Brown / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
A Met Police email error exposed the addresses of roughly 140 women alleging abuse by Mohamed Al Fayed, prompting an apology and an ICO notification.

Roughly 140 women who came forward with allegations of sexual abuse against the late Harrods owner Mohamed Al Fayed have had their email addresses inadvertently exposed to one another, after the Metropolitan Police failed to conceal recipients on a routine mailing.

The force sent a monthly update to complainants on Tuesday but did not use blind carbon copy — the standard method for mass emails where recipients should not see each other's contact details. Instead, the addresses were visible to everyone on the list. The Met said the error was caught quickly and that everyone affected was contacted directly on the day of the breach. The UK's Information Commissioner's Office, the data protection regulator, has been notified and is now reviewing the incident.

"Due to human error, recipients' email addresses were visible to others on the distribution list," the force said in a statement, adding that it was "truly sorry" for any distress caused and would review its processes to prevent a repeat.

A breach inside an already fraught investigation

The timing compounds the damage. The mailing list exists because of the scale of the Met's investigation into Al Fayed, who died in 2023 at the age of 94. Allegations against him — of rape, sexual assault, exploitation and trafficking, most alleged to have occurred between 1977 and 2014 — surfaced publicly in a BBC documentary in 2024 and prompted a wave of women to come forward. More than 150 have now reported allegations directly to police, and Harrods' own redress scheme, set up after the documentary aired, has received claims from over 250 people.

Many of the women involved say they were selected for jobs within the Fayed business empire, then subjected to invasive medical examinations whose results were passed to Fayed himself — part of what investigators have described as a systematic pattern stretching across decades. Detectives are separately examining whether associates of Fayed, including former staff, enabled the alleged abuse; several people have been questioned under caution, though no one has yet been arrested.

Scrutiny already mounting

The breach lands on a force already facing formal scrutiny over how it has handled the case. The Independent Office for Police Conduct is investigating a serving and several former Met officers over the force's response to complaints made between 2018 and 2024, after survivors separately complained that opportunities to act on earlier reports were missed. For women who spent years deciding whether to come forward, having their identities exposed to fellow complainants — however inadvertently — cuts against the basic assurance of confidentiality that such investigations depend on.

The Met has not said whether the error will be referred internally for disciplinary review, nor detailed what technical or procedural safeguards will change. The ICO's assessment, once complete, will determine whether the breach amounts to a reportable failure under UK data protection law and whether any enforcement action follows — a process that typically takes weeks rather than days. In the meantime, the episode adds one more item to the list of grievances survivors' lawyers are compiling about the Met's stewardship of the case.
