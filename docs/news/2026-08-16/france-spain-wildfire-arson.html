Hundreds arrested for arson as wildfires rage across France and Spain

A wildfire burns on the slopes of Peña Montañesa in northern Spain, July 2026. — Azahara00 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nearly 500 people have been arrested over wildfires in France and Spain this summer, as attribution scientists tie the underlying heat directly to climate change.

Firefighters in southwestern France and swathes of Spain are entering a second month of near-continuous mobilisation, as a summer of record heat produces two crises at once: fires that keep breaking containment lines, and a wave of arson arrests large enough to alarm the lawyers defending the accused.

France's interior ministry says 474 people have been detained since 1 July on suspicion of starting wildfires, 183 of them minors. Around seven in ten are suspected of deliberately setting fires; the rest are accused of reckless causes — a discarded cigarette, machinery sparks, an untended barbecue. A 15-year-old boy is among those held, linked to a blaze near Bordeaux airport in July that killed two firefighters. Muriel Gestas, a lawyer who has represented several of the accused, told reporters the volume of arrests "feels excessive," noting that prosecutors have often released few details on what evidence ties individual suspects to specific fires.

The fires themselves have already made history. France had burned upwards of 115,000 hectares by late July — about 40% more than its previous modern-day record year — with the Landes and Gironde region losing over 50,000 hectares in a single stretch near Arcachon Bay and forcing roughly 220,000 people from homes and campsites. Spain's toll is larger still: close to 257,000 hectares burned so far in 2026, 170% above the 20-year average, including a fire near Ávila now the largest single blaze in the country's recorded history at around 50,000 hectares. Madrid declared Spain's first-ever national wildfire emergency after fires forced more than 11,500 evacuations; a further 31,000 hectares burned in Huelva province in the south.

What the attribution science shows

A rapid analysis by World Weather Attribution, using the Canadian Fire Weather Index to model conditions against a pre-industrial baseline roughly 1.4°C cooler, found the hot, dry and windy weather behind France's fires was at least twice as likely because of human-caused warming — and the conditions driving Spain's fires at least 20 times more likely. The culprit isn't a single heatwave but a compounding sequence: four heatwaves rolled through Europe between May and late July, on top of soil moisture left anomalously low despite a wetter-than-usual winter. France recorded its hottest June on record, surpassing the previous mark set in August 2003.

An institutional squeeze

The two crises are feeding the same bottleneck. Investigators, courts and fire services are drawing on the same stretched personnel during the same weeks, even as scientists note that roughly nine in ten French wildfires originate from human activity of some kind — the large majority accidental, not criminal. That gap between the scale of arrests and the harder question of intent is likely to surface again as cases move through French courts this autumn, testing whether the arson dragnet holds up under scrutiny once the smoke clears.
