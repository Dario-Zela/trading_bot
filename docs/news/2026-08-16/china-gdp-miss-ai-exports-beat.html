China's economy: GDP growth misses as AI exports beat forecasts

In one sentence
China's Q2 GDP grew just 4.3%, missing forecasts, as a property-driven consumption slump offset a 117% surge in AI chip exports.

China's economy grew 4.3% in the second quarter, the government said this week, undershooting the 4.5% consensus forecast and marking the weakest pace of expansion since the tail end of 2022. It is the first time since Beijing stopped publishing a formal annual growth target during the pandemic that output has come in below the informal 4.5%-5% range officials have been signalling to markets — a symbolic threshold as much as a statistical one.

The headline number would be easier to wave off if it weren't part of a pattern. Retail sales fell in May for the first time since the economy reopened from Covid lockdowns in late 2022, and fixed-asset investment contracted more sharply than expected over the first five months of the year. Property investment, the sector still doing the most damage to household balance sheets, was down more than 16% year-on-year — a decline that has now stretched into its third year.

Set against that is a genuinely strong export sector, and within it, an unambiguous boom in AI-related hardware. The two data points arrived within weeks of each other and describe an economy pulling in opposite directions: a state-directed industrial and technology complex performing roughly as advertised, and a household sector that isn't spending.

The chips are working

July trade figures, released in the first week of August, showed exports rising 23.9% year-on-year in dollar terms — beating a Reuters poll forecast of 22.2% and holding up despite a fresh round of US tariffs. The composition of that growth is the more interesting number. Semiconductor exports surged 117% year-on-year, a pace that accelerated even past the first half's already-strong run, and high-tech exports overall were up 40.7% for the first seven months of the year, with computer and related-parts shipments climbing 45.2% over the same stretch.

Part of this is memory chips: Samsung and SK Hynix have spent much of 2026 redirecting DRAM production toward high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, a product global server builders cannot get enough of, and Chinese assemblers and exporters sit downstream of that demand. Part of it is also exporters front-loading shipments ahead of anticipated US tariff increases — a distortion that inflates the near-term number without necessarily reflecting a durable change in trend. Even so, the trade surplus for July, at $112.5 billion, came in above the roughly $107 billion analysts expected, even as it narrowed from June's $125.6 billion.

Why the tech boom doesn't show up at home

The puzzle for Beijing is that none of this manufacturing and export strength has translated into a China that spends. Michelle Lam, greater China economist at Société Générale, summarized the quarter bluntly: growth cooled "as weak consumption and property activity outweighed resilient exports." That is the structural story in one sentence. Semiconductor fabrication and AI server assembly are capital-intensive and comparatively labor-light relative to the property and construction sectors they're being asked to replace as growth engines — they generate export revenue and headline GDP contribution, but not the broad-based wage income and household confidence that used to flow from a booming real-estate sector.

That gap matters because it isn't obviously fixable by the technology itself getting better. A country can lead in AI chip exports and humanoid robotics — LG and Nvidia's expanded manufacturing alliance is one recent marker of how central Chinese supply chains remain to that build-out — and still have a consumer who won't buy a sofa, because the transmission mechanism from industrial output to household income has narrowed. Retail sales' brief return to 1% growth in June, after May's outright decline, suggests some stabilization, but not the kind of reacceleration that would close a full percentage point of GDP shortfall on its own.

What Beijing does next

Economists are split on whether a miss this size forces Beijing's hand. The People's Bank of China has limited room for aggressive rate cuts without pressuring the yuan, so the more likely lever is fiscal — the government has signalled that any new stimulus will be "targeted and limited," language that markets have learned to read as a promise of incrementalism rather than a bazooka. That calibration reflects a real tension: broad-based stimulus risks reinflating the property bubble policymakers spent three years trying to deflate; withholding it risks letting a quarter's soft patch harden into a lower trend growth rate that Beijing has to publicly accept.

For traders, the read-through extends beyond China's own equity and bond markets. Commodity demand — iron ore, copper, energy — tracks the investment and construction side of the economy that's contracting, not the chip-export side that's expanding, so a genuine structural slowdown argues for softer industrial-metals demand even as AI hardware demand stays firm. Semiconductor and AI supply chains, meanwhile, are increasingly reliant on Chinese assembly and export capacity precisely at a moment when Washington is trying to constrain that dependency through tariffs — a tension likely to keep generating trade-policy headlines through the autumn.

The next major data points are August's trade and activity figures, due in September, along with any signal from the Politburo's autumn economic work conference on whether "targeted and limited" stimulus becomes something larger. Until then, the two halves of China's economy — a world-class export machine and a household sector still nursing a property hangover — look set to keep moving in opposite directions.
