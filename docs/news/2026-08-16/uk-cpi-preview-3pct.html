UK inflation set to approach 3% as energy bills surge

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where policymakers are split on how to respond to an energy-driven inflation shock. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK inflation is expected to climb toward 3% on Wednesday as surging energy bills squeeze households and test the Bank of England's resolve to hold rates.

Wednesday brings a number that will do more to shape the Bank of England's next move than anything said in Threadneedle Street this month. The Office for National Statistics publishes July's inflation reading at 7am, and economists expect it to show the headline rate climbing from June's 2.6% to somewhere close to 2.9% — its highest since early last year and within touching distance of the 3% threshold that would have seemed alarming twelve months ago and now looks almost pre-priced.

The driver is not a broad-based reacceleration in prices. It is energy, arriving in a familiar and fairly mechanical way: Ofgem's quarterly price cap rose 13% in July, and that single adjustment is estimated to add around 0.44 percentage points to the headline CPI figure on its own, according to Thomas Pugh, chief UK and Ireland economist at RSM UK. "The cost of living squeeze is set to return to the headlines," Pugh said, adding that the increase is "adding fresh pressure to household budgets and complicating the outlook for interest rates."

Why gas and electricity are doing the work

UK inflation has spent much of the past year drifting down from the 3.8% peak it reached last year, helped along by falling energy costs and easing wage growth. That disinflation has now stalled. Wholesale gas prices have stayed elevated through the summer, partly a function of continued volatility tied to the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz, and Ofgem's cap — reset quarterly against a backdrop of those wholesale costs — has pushed a typical dual-fuel household's annual bill to £1,663 for the July-to-September period.

There is a partial offset already banked for the autumn. The government's decision to remove VAT from electricity bills, worth an estimated £45 a year to the average household, is expected to soften the October reset: Cornwall Insight's forecast for the cap due to take effect on 1 October has come down to roughly £1,700, from a previous estimate north of £1,900. But that relief lands after Wednesday's print, and it works on the level of bills, not the trajectory of the annual inflation rate, which compares this year's prices with last year's regardless of what happens next quarter.

The Bank's dilemma, in one vote

The number matters because the Bank of England is not united on what to do about it. At its July meeting the Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% — a split that has moved from 7-2 in June, meaning three members, Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann, now want an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%. The Bank's own projections, published alongside that decision, show inflation peaking at around 3.2% in the fourth quarter of this year, a forecast built on energy market pricing that assumes no further escalation.

That is the credibility problem in miniature. Governor Andrew Bailey has consistently framed energy-driven inflation as the kind of external shock the Bank should look through rather than fight with higher rates, since raising borrowing costs does nothing to lower a gas bill and instead adds a second squeeze — on mortgages and business credit — on top of the first. The hawkish minority argues the opposite: that if households and firms start expecting inflation to sit above target for another year, the shock stops being temporary and starts feeding into wage claims and pricing decisions, which is exactly the dynamic a central bank exists to prevent. Victoria Scholar at interactive investor expects the Bank to land closer to the hawks' view by year-end, pencilling in one rate rise. Pugh's own base case is a hold through 2026, but he is explicit that it "depends heavily on energy prices easing" — which, with the Hormuz situation unresolved, is not guaranteed.

What a hot print would do to markets

A reading at or above 2.9% would land at an already jumpy moment for UK rates markets, with 30-year gilt yields sitting at their highest since 1998 on fiscal concerns unrelated to Wednesday's data. An inflation surprise on top of that would tend to push short-dated gilt yields and sterling higher on repriced rate expectations, while adding to the narrative that the UK's inflation problem, unlike the more benign pictures now visible in some peer economies, still has a way to run. A softer print — anything closer to 2.6% — would ease that pressure and hand the doves at the MPC a stronger hand heading into the September meeting.

Either way, the report due at 7am on Wednesday is only the first data point in a run that matters more as a sequence than a single reading. RSM's base case has inflation averaging 3.1% this year and peaking near 3.5%, before falling back to 2.5% in 2027 — a path that assumes the current energy shock fades rather than compounds. The next test comes at the September 17 MPC meeting, by which point Ofgem will also have confirmed the actual October price cap, replacing the current range of forecasts with a number households can plan around.
