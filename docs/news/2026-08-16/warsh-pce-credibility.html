Fed Chair Warsh's credibility questioned over inflation-metric comments

Kevin Warsh, who took over as Federal Reserve chair in May, has floated dropping PCE as the Fed's primary inflation gauge. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors (public domain)
In one sentence
Warsh's suggestion the Fed could ditch PCE for a trimmed-mean gauge has fed a bond-market repricing and revived credibility questions.

Kevin Warsh has been Federal Reserve chair for three months, and the argument he has picked is not with the White House or with Congress. It is with a spreadsheet.

At his confirmation hearing, Warsh dismissed the Fed's long-standing preferred inflation measure, the personal consumption expenditures index, as "rough swag." At his press conference on July 29 he went further, floating a preference for trimmed-mean and median PCE variants — measures compiled by the Dallas and Cleveland Fed regional banks that strip out the largest price swings before averaging the rest. He has since set up task forces to formally reconsider how the Fed measures inflation. Trimmed-mean PCE read 2.2% in June, comfortably below the 2.6% core PCE figure the Fed has used as its benchmark for a decade. Warsh insists the 2% target itself is not moving — "there is no soft target," he told reporters — but which yardstick gets used to judge distance from that target is very much in play.

A credibility problem, not just a technical one

Bill Dudley, the former New York Fed president who now writes for Bloomberg Opinion, argued this month that Warsh's troubles run deeper than an awkward start. Dudley's objection isn't to trimmed-mean measures on their own merits — plenty of economists like them — but to a Fed chair musing about swapping targets in public while declining to explain how he'd actually respond to changes in the outlook. Markets and the central bank each end up waiting on the other for a signal, Dudley wrote, and that mutual deference "leads to indeterminate policy expectations" and a loss of credibility. Bank of America's economists used blunter language, warning that Warsh's comments had produced a "central bank inflation credibility shock" and cautioning that redefining the inflation gauge "opens the door for cherry picking of indicators to justify a dovish stance."

What traders have already priced in

The bond market reacted the way it does to emerging-market central banks that talk out of both sides of their mouth: a bear steepening. The gap between 10- and two-year Treasury yields widened from 0.27 percentage points in June to 0.47 points by July 31 — among the sharpest post-FOMC steepenings since the mid-1990s — as two-year yields slipped roughly 4 basis points on the press conference while 30-year yields pushed to 5.21%, their highest since 2007. Inflation breakevens rose in step, the dollar softened, and equities wobbled, the classic signature of a market pricing in more inflation risk premium rather than less. The irony BofA points to is that Warsh's attempt to sound dovish may force his hand the other way: Polymarket odds of a September rate hike — not a cut — climbed to 56% after the press conference, and the Federal Open Market Committee's 9-3 vote to hold rates, its widest split in nearly a decade, shows the confusion has reached the committee table as well as the trading desk.

The next test lands with the August jobs and PCE releases ahead of the September FOMC meeting, where Warsh will need to show whichever gauge he settles on can still anchor expectations rather than unsettle them further.
