SEC abruptly cancels crypto capital-raising rules vote as CLARITY Act odds slide

The SEC's Washington headquarters, where a scheduled vote on new crypto capital-raising rules was abruptly pulled this week. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The SEC scrapped a vote on new crypto fundraising rules as Galaxy Digital slashed its odds of the CLARITY Act passing in 2026 to 10%.

The Securities and Exchange Commission was due to vote Friday on the first formal rulebook for how crypto startups raise money without registering as full securities issuers. On Thursday, it cancelled the meeting. The stated reason was an "unforeseen scheduling issue," with no elaboration and no rescheduled date offered.

The timing invites scepticism. The cancellation landed just as the Senate left Washington for a five-week recess without acting on the CLARITY Act, the digital-asset market structure bill the industry has spent two years lobbying for. Hours after the SEC's notice went out, Galaxy Digital's research desk cut its odds of that bill passing in 2026 to just 10% — down from 50% in late June, 60% in early June and 75% in May. Two regulatory tracks that were supposed to converge on an answer for crypto capital-raising have instead stalled within days of each other.

What the SEC pulled back

The scrapped proposal, dubbed Regulation Crypto Assets internally and running to roughly 400 pages, would have created three pathways for crypto projects to raise capital outside the traditional securities-offering regime: a startup exemption capped around $5 million over roughly four years, a broader fundraising exemption up to $75 million annually, and a "decentralization" safe harbor letting sufficiently mature tokens exit securities classification altogether. Chair Paul Atkins had framed it as building on the work of Commissioner Hester Peirce's crypto task force, telling colleagues the agency intended "a tailored offering regime for crypto assets that are part of or subject to an investment contract." The proposal, filed under RIN 3235-AN38, remains listed as pending on Reginfo.gov rather than withdrawn.

Reporting since the cancellation points to friction the SEC didn't put in its notice. Some accounts trace the delay to White House unease that a sweeping rulemaking might undercut the case for passing the CLARITY Act at all — why would Congress act if the SEC can write the rules itself? Others point to the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association, which has pushed back on exemptive relief in favor of formal rulemaking that keeps Wall Street's existing compliance apparatus relevant. Either way, a vote that looked routine a week earlier evaporated without a fight in public.

Why the legislative math got worse

Galaxy's downgrade rests on arithmetic as much as sentiment. The Senate returns September 14, and by Galaxy's count lawmakers will have only two to three working weeks before the session's other priorities crowd out floor time — meaning the CLARITY Act would need to "dominate basically the entire working session" to reach a vote, let alone pass. The bill cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May, but disputes have hardened rather than eased: Democrats and banking-industry lobbyists object to provisions that would let crypto firms offer stablecoin yields without the capital and consumer-protection rules banks operate under, and ethics questions about officials' personal crypto holdings remain unresolved. More than 200 crypto companies petitioned for passage in June; the petitioning has not moved the Senate calendar.

The gap this leaves

What's left is a vacuum on both fronts. The SEC's rulemaking, which industry figures had started treating as a plausible substitute for legislation, is now open-ended — no hearing date, no public timeline. Commissioner Peirce, the architect of the agency's crypto-friendly posture, is due to leave the Commission in November, adding a real clock to whatever window remains for the current majority to act. And the CLARITY Act, once framed as the year's signature crypto win in Washington, is down to what Galaxy calls long odds with barely a month of realistic floor time left on the calendar.

For crypto issuers hoping 2026 would bring a settled framework for raising money in the U.S., the practical answer for now is the same one they've had for years: proceed under existing exemptions, offshore structures, or ambiguity, and wait. The next concrete marker is the Senate's return on September 14 — if the CLARITY Act doesn't move quickly once lawmakers are back, Galaxy's 10% figure will start to look generous rather than cautious.
