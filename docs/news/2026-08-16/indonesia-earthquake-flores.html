Magnitude 7.7 earthquake kills at least 47 in Indonesia

A shopfront building collapsed onto the street in Flores after Saturday's magnitude 7.7 earthquake. — Indonesia National Agency for Disaster Countermeasure / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A magnitude 7.7 quake off Flores killed at least 47 and set off 235+ aftershocks, with damage costs still unassessed in a region where most homes carry no earthquake insurance.

A magnitude 7.7 earthquake tore through the waters off Flores island before dawn on Saturday, killing at least 47 people and setting off a scramble across Indonesia's eastern archipelago that has yet to produce a clear tally of the damage — or of what it will cost to repair.

The quake struck at 5:58am local time, its epicentre roughly 68 kilometres north-northwest of Ende, a port city on Flores in East Nusa Tenggara province, at a shallow depth of around 10 kilometres, according to the US Geological Survey. Shallow, high-magnitude quakes of this kind tend to produce the most violent shaking at the surface, and this one did: the Indonesian meteorological agency BMKG recorded a peak intensity of VIII on the Modified Mercalli scale — "severe" — in Manggarai Regency, strong enough to collapse buildings not engineered to withstand it.

Most of the dead were asleep when their homes came down, officials said, a detail that has shaped the recovery effort as much as the death toll itself: search teams sent from Bali and West Nusa Tenggara, including canine units, have spent the days since sifting through rubble for survivors and bodies alike. "The quake was massive, the shock was so strong, we were resting at home with family," one resident told reporters, describing a scene repeated across the province.

A fault with a long memory

Flores sits on a back-arc thrust fault that has ruptured repeatedly and lethally over the past two centuries — in 1815, 1857, 1917, 1979 and, more recently, 2018 and 2021. Researchers have counted 79 earthquakes above magnitude 6.0 in the wider region since 1900, eleven of which triggered tsunamis. Saturday's event fits the pattern: BMKG issued a tsunami warning within two minutes and sixteen seconds of the shaking, covering East Nusa Tenggara, West Nusa Tenggara, and South and Southeast Sulawesi. Waves as high as 1.6 metres were measured at Reok, on Flores' north coast, before the agency lifted the warning around 90 minutes later, having found no sea-level changes large enough to threaten a wider evacuation.

What followed was a swarm rather than an aftershock or two. More than 235 aftershocks had been logged by early afternoon, ranging up to magnitude 6.1 — itself a sizeable quake in its own right — and seismologists briefed by BMKG expect elevated aftershock activity to persist for two to three weeks before tapering off. That sequence complicates an already difficult rescue: several roads on Flores were blocked by landslides, four regional airports sustained damage, and communications in some districts remain patchy.

The bill nobody can write yet

The human toll has outpaced the physical one in official reporting so far — more than 113 injured, over 1,300 homes damaged, and some 2,000 villagers moved into temporary shelters — but the harder number, in dollar terms, does not yet exist. Catastrophe modellers typically need one to two weeks of ground survey before publishing loss estimates, and none has yet been issued for this event.

What is already known is the shape of the exposure. Indonesia has one of the widest earthquake protection gaps in Asia: the vast majority of homeowners in disaster-prone regions like East Nusa Tenggara carry no earthquake cover at all, leaving state and local budgets, and eventually foreign disaster relief, to absorb most of the cost of rebuilding. Jakarta has been extending insurance to government buildings since 2020 but has not built anything resembling Turkey's catastrophe insurance pool, which lifted homeowner earthquake coverage there above 50 percent after a series of deadly quakes. For reinsurers, that gap cuts both ways — it caps the industry's direct claims exposure to this particular quake, but it also means the region remains largely unpriced and under-modelled risk, a fact underwriters will be revisiting as they set 2027 renewal terms for Indonesian and broader Asia-Pacific catastrophe treaties.

The more immediate marker to watch is the death toll itself, which officials have repeatedly cautioned will keep climbing as search teams reach areas still cut off by landslides. A first damage assessment from Indonesia's national disaster agency, BNPB, is expected within the coming week, and will be the first real basis for estimating what the Flores quake will actually cost — in lives, and eventually, in money.
