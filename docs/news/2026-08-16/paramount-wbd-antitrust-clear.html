Paramount Skydance's $110bn Warner Bros Discovery takeover clears US and UK antitrust review

The US Department of Justice, which cleared the merger in June without requiring any divestitures. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Paramount Skydance's $110bn Warner Bros. Discovery deal cleared DOJ and UK CMA review, but a 12-state antitrust suit still blocks closing.

The last two governments capable of blocking Paramount Skydance's takeover of Warner Bros. Discovery have both said no thanks — they won't stand in the way. The US Department of Justice cleared the $110 billion deal in June without demanding a single divestiture. On August 6, Britain's Competition and Markets Authority followed, declining even to open the deeper Phase 2 inquiry that had loomed over the transaction since spring. Between them, the two decisions closed out a global regulatory sweep spanning 68 jurisdictions — the European Commission, Australia, Canada, Brazil, China and, as of August 14, Mexico — with not one government finding grounds to block the combination.

That is, on paper, the whole antitrust story. What it leaves standing is a domestic one: twelve US state attorneys general are still suing in federal court to stop the deal, and until that case resolves, the merger cannot close.

What the regulators actually found

The DOJ's review ran eight months and ended with a finding that the tie-up was "likely to enhance competition" across streaming, television and film rather than diminish it — an unusually permissive framing for a deal that fuses two of the six major Hollywood studios, welds Paramount+ to HBO Max, and puts CBS and CNN under one roof. No behavioral remedies, no asset sales, no ongoing monitoring.

The CMA's reasoning, laid out in its clearance notice, was narrower but pointed the same way: the combined company "would continue to face competition from" Disney, Universal and Sony, "and a range of other smaller studios," in the UK theatrical market, so the deal did not risk a substantial lessening of competition there. Regulators in both capitals, in other words, looked at a $110 billion consolidation of legacy media assets and concluded that streaming's crowded field — Netflix, Amazon, Disney, Apple — was competition enough to keep the merged giant honest.

How Ellison got here

The deal itself was the product of a bidding war Paramount Skydance, run by chief executive David Ellison, very nearly lost. Warner Bros. Discovery had agreed in December to sell its studio and streaming arms to Netflix for roughly $72 billion in cash and stock — about $82.7 billion including debt. Paramount came back with an all-cash offer for the entire company, eventually settling at $31 a share, plus a quarterly "ticking fee" to compensate Warner shareholders for delay. Netflix walked away on February 26, saying the price Paramount was prepared to pay no longer made financial sense for it to match. Warner's board signed the definitive agreement the next day; shareholders approved it in April.

The prize is a company that would sit alongside Disney, Comcast and Fox at the top of the entertainment industry: more than 200 million combined streaming subscribers, a film library north of 15,000 titles, and — by one estimate — control of roughly a quarter of the $223 billion US media market once HBO, CNN, Discovery Channel, MTV, Nickelodeon and BET all sit under the Paramount Skydance roof alongside CBS and the Paramount film studio.

What's still in the way

The regulatory sweep answers the question antitrust economists ask; it does not answer the question a California courtroom is now asking. On July 13, a coalition of twelve states led by California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued in the Northern District of California to block the merger outright, arguing the combined company would wield excessive market power over what Americans watch. Eleven days later, Paramount and Warner agreed by stipulation not to close the deal — or begin integrating operations — until five days after a ruling on the merits, or June 1, 2027, whichever comes first. Separately, more than 5,500 actors, directors and other industry figures, including Florence Pugh, Pedro Pascal, Edward Norton and Joaquin Phoenix, have signed a letter opposing the tie-up on concentration-of-power grounds, though that carries no legal force.

Markets have treated the federal and UK clearances as good news rather than decisive news: both stocks moved only modestly — Paramount Skydance shares up roughly 0.3%, Warner Bros. Discovery up about 1% — on the CMA announcement, consistent with a deal whose regulatory risk had already been mostly priced out but whose litigation risk has not been.

What the DOJ and CMA decisions do say clearly is that neither Washington nor London currently regards vertical media consolidation — studio plus network plus streamer plus news operation — as the kind of concentration that draws a structural remedy. That is a signal the rest of the industry, still working through the wreckage of the streaming wars, is likely to read as an invitation.

The next marker is procedural rather than economic: a scheduling order in the states' case, which under the July stipulation effectively sets the outer bound for closing at June 1, 2027. Until a trial date and merits ruling arrive, Paramount Skydance holds every approval it needs except the one that matters most.
