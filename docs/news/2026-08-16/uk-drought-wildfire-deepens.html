Drought and wildfire emergency deepen across England and Wales

The scarred aftermath of a UK grass fire that spread into homes — a scene echoed across England and Wales this August as wildfires and drought compound. — Blythwood / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
England and Wales face a record drought and wildfire season — 37 fires burning, troops deployed, disposable BBQs banned, and up to half of some harvests at risk.

Britain spent the middle of August fighting fires with soldiers. On Friday, Prime Minister Andy Burnham confirmed that around 200 military personnel — from the Queen's Own Gurkha Logistic Regiment and the 22 Engineer Regiment — had been sent into South Wales at the request of South Wales Fire and Rescue Service, which was battling ten large fires simultaneously and had run out of civilian capacity to dig firebreaks. Millions of phones across England and Wales buzzed with an official emergency alert warning of "a very high risk of wildfires nationally." The government banned the sale of disposable barbecues nationwide, with no end date attached.

"Britain is a tinderbox right now," Burnham said, touring Stourbridge in the West Midlands, where a grass fire had torn through more than 500 acres two days earlier, destroying 19 homes and damaging 18 more. "Setting a fire in any outdoor setting is a risk to people's homes and other people's lives." West Midlands Ambulance Service treated 54 patients from that single blaze, 19 of whom were hospitalised. Fire chief Simon Tuhill called it one of the most significant incidents his service had ever handled.

A record fire season, not an isolated blaze

The Stourbridge fire was not a one-off. Around 37 wildfires were smouldering across the country as Burnham spoke, part of a season that has already broken records: fire services in England and Wales logged 966 wildfire callouts between January and 10 August, ahead of the equivalent period in both 2022 and 2025. July alone brought 458 incidents, the busiest month on record, and crews responded to a further 185 in the first ten days of August. Friday was provisionally the tenth day of 2026 with temperatures above 35°C — itself a record — and Kew Gardens hit 38.1°C on the day Stourbridge burned, the hottest reading of the year.

Drought spreads faster than the fires

The fires are a symptom of a drier problem. The Environment Agency's National Drought Group confirmed on 10 August that 71.3% of England — an area covering roughly 45 million people — is now in official drought, alongside the whole of Wales, following the driest July on record in several regions. The consequences are already showing up on farms rather than waiting for autumn: the Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board reports the earliest harvest in 20 years, with early yield data for wheat, spring barley, oats and grassland all running below average. Growers have warned the drought group that as much as half of the potato, carrot, apple and hop harvest could be lost. In East Anglia, regulators imposed a 50% cut to farm water abstraction licences from 10 August to 31 October under Section 57 emergency powers, on top of more than 1,500 abstraction restrictions already in force nationally. Shadow environment secretary Victoria Atkins accused the government of moving too slowly given weeks of visible warning signs, and pointed to the knock-on risk to food prices.

What the deployment signals

Sending troops to dig firebreaks is a small operation in military terms, but as a policy signal it is not small at all. The UK reaches for the armed forces domestically only when civilian emergency capacity is exhausted — the same threshold crossed during the 2022 heatwave, when London's fire brigade described its busiest day since the Second World War, but not since repeated at this scale or this early in a season. What has changed since 2022 is duration: this is not a single freak week but a summer-long compounding of heat, drought and crop stress across a G7 economy with, on paper, some of the world's more resilient emergency and agricultural infrastructure. That resilience is now visibly straining — evacuations, hospitalisations, abstraction bans and yield warnings arriving simultaneously rather than sequentially.

The practical test now shifts to the harvest and to autumn rainfall. The National Drought Group meets again in the coming weeks to assess whether reservoir stocks can recover before winter, and the AHDB's fuller harvest figures — due through September — will show whether the potato, carrot and hop losses flagged this month materialise at the scale growers fear. If they do, the story moves from fire crews and soldiers to supermarket shelves and insurers, which is usually where climate-linked shocks leave their longest mark.
