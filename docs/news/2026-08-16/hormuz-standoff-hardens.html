Washington hardens Hormuz stance as Trump vows to claim the strait 'as territory'

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil and LNG once passed before the 2026 Iran war. — Perry-Castañeda Library Map Collection, University of Texas at Austin / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Washington says its Hormuz blockade of Iran could run indefinitely and Trump wants to claim the strait as US territory, hardening Brent above $88.

Almost six months into the war between the United States, Israel and Iran, Washington's message on the Strait of Hormuz has shifted from a promise of eventual resolution to something closer to a standing order: the blockade stays until Tehran breaks, however long that takes. Brent crude closed at $88.52 a barrel on Thursday, up 1.7% on the day, after two of the Trump administration's senior officials made clear in the space of 24 hours that the chokepoint through which a fifth of the world's oil and gas once flowed is not reopening on any schedule they're willing to name.

A blockade with no exit date

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth was the more blunt of the two. Asked how long the US Navy could keep Iranian ports sealed off, he didn't hedge. "Indefinitely the United States Navy can maintain a blockade like that," he told reporters, "because we'll rotate ships in and out, as we have, and we'll continue to." It was a statement aimed as much at Tehran's calculus as at reporters — a signal that Washington sees this as a war of attrition it is structurally built to win, with the USS Abraham Lincoln now more than 260 days into a deployment that has become the centerpiece of the Gulf campaign.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent supplied the financial half of the message. In an interview on Newsmax on Thursday, he said the administration would soon roll out measures amounting to "economic isolation... like have never been seen in the history" of a sanctioned country, layered on top of the naval cordon. "Watch this space for more announcements coming next week," he said. Between the two statements, the intended effect was unmistakable: no timeline for the blockade, and new financial pressure still to come.

The strait has been effectively closed since the opening days of the war. When Operation Epic Fury began on February 28 with joint US-Israeli strikes on Iran's nuclear and military infrastructure, Hormuz shipping collapsed within 48 hours as insurers pulled cover and tankers rerouted. Traffic has crept along at a fraction of normal levels ever since — commercial tracking has shown periods of roughly 25 vessels a day against a pre-war baseline nearer 100, with some recent days registering close to zero transits. For a waterway that carried around a fifth of global oil and LNG supply before February, that is not a disruption markets can treat as transitory, which is a large part of why Brent has held above $88 even as broader demand signals have softened.

Trump's claim on the strait itself

The blockade rhetoric was compounded on Friday by a more startling claim from the president. Speaking in New York, Donald Trump said that once Iran — which he described as being "very badly defeated" — is finished as a military threat, the United States would declare the Strait of Hormuz US territory. The strait has never been under American jurisdiction; it is bordered by Iran to the north and Oman's Musandam exclave to the south, and international law treats it as an international strait subject to transit passage rights, not a possession up for claim by the country patrolling it.

Iran's response came within a day. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi said the strait "has been Iranian, is Iranian, and will remain Iranian," adding that it "cannot be seized by tweet, nor by aircraft carrier, nor by issuing an order, nor by an election speech." Iranian officials have separately warned that the proposal risks prolonging the conflict rather than ending it — a sovereignty dispute layered directly on top of the ceasefire talks that have, so far, gone nowhere.

A parallel front in the Gulf

The strait's two flanking states have their own unresolved dispute simmering alongside the main conflict. Qatar shot down two Iranian Su-24 bombers over the Gulf on March 2, and the two governments have offered contradictory accounts of what happened to the crews ever since. Doha says the pilots were killed and that it has located and is arranging the handover of one set of remains, having invited an Iranian team to inspect the search-and-rescue operation in April — an invitation Tehran has not taken up. Iran's military, in a letter to the International Committee of the Red Cross, alleges three pilots survived and are being held by Qatari forces, a claim Doha calls misleading. It is a narrower, quieter story than the blockade or the territorial claim, but it points to the same underlying condition: even nominally neutral Gulf states are now entangled in unresolved, contested incidents with Tehran, with no forum currently capable of settling them.

The floor under oil

Taken together, the three threads describe a conflict that has stopped behaving like an acute shock and started behaving like a structural feature of the energy market. An indefinite blockade removes the assumption that Hormuz traffic snaps back once a ceasefire is signed. A presidential claim on the strait's sovereignty adds a second, open-ended dispute that would need resolving even after the shooting stops. And unresolved incidents like the Qatari pilot standoff show how the war has widened the population of grievances that any eventual settlement will have to clear. None of that guarantees Brent stays above $88 — prices eased earlier in August on demand worries even as the blockade rhetoric hardened — but it does mean the geopolitical premium in oil is no longer pricing a temporary event. The next marker will be Bessent's promised sanctions package, expected within the week, and whatever Tehran does in response.
