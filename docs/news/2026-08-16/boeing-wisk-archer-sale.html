Boeing sells Wisk Aero, SkyGrid and Insitu units to Archer Aviation

Wisk Aero's Generation 5 Cora eVTOL aircraft, photographed at the 2022 NBAA-BACE event. — ZLEA / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Boeing is offloading Wisk Aero, SkyGrid and Insitu to Archer Aviation for a 19.75% equity stake rather than cash, exiting eVTOL development while keeping IP access.

Boeing has agreed to hand over three of its advanced-air-mobility businesses — Wisk Aero, SkyGrid and Insitu — to Archer Aviation, taking a near-fifth stake in the eVTOL startup rather than cash. The all-stock deal, announced August 10 and expected to close by the end of 2026 pending antitrust clearance, marks Boeing's exit from direct development of self-flying air taxis after seven years and hundreds of millions of dollars invested.

Under the terms, Boeing will receive Archer Class A shares equal to 19.75% of the company's outstanding stock, plus warrants to buy up to $200 million more over the following one to four years. Boeing has also committed to putting up to $55 million into Archer's next funding round and will get to nominate a board director so long as it keeps a minimum stake. No cash changes hands at closing.

Three businesses, one buyer

The package is an odd bundle on paper. Wisk Aero builds autonomous, pilotless eVTOL aircraft and traces its lineage to a 2019 joint venture between Boeing and Kitty Hawk, the flying-car venture backed by Google co-founder Larry Page; Boeing bought out Kitty Hawk's stake in 2023 to take full ownership. SkyGrid is a digital airspace-management platform built with Boeing's HorizonX unit. Insitu, by contrast, is a mature, profitable maker of combat-tested unmanned aircraft that generates more than $200 million a year in revenue — the one asset in the trio that was never a bet on urban air taxis.

Archer, which has yet to generate meaningful commercial revenue from its own four-passenger Midnight aircraft, is effectively acquiring scale, defense-market exposure and autonomy technology in one transaction. Archer CEO Adam Goldstein said the fit was natural given design overlap between Wisk's and Archer's aircraft: "If ever two companies could come together in the eVTOL space, it's Archer and Wisk." The deal also feeds Archer's hybrid-electric drone program with Anduril Industries, where Insitu's unmanned-systems expertise is a more direct asset than anything eVTOL-related.

Boeing keeps a foot in the door

For Boeing, the logic is less about air taxis than balance sheet discipline. Boeing chief executive Brian Yutko framed the move as refocusing engineering resources: "We can now take all of the technologies and the IP and whatnot that's been developed, and then focus them back on our core Boeing products." A cross-licensing arrangement lets Boeing keep access to Wisk's autonomous-flight software for future commercial and defense aircraft — useful, executives suggested, as the company eventually works toward a successor to the 737.

That structure — exit the capital-intensive frontier business, keep an equity call option on its upside, retain the IP that's actually reusable — reads as an admission that Boeing no longer sees value in running an eVTOL developer itself, even as it isn't ready to write off the sector entirely. Building and certifying a new aircraft type from scratch is exactly the kind of program Boeing has struggled to execute cleanly in recent years, on commercial jets and defense contracts alike.

Market reaction was mixed

Archer's stock jumped 18.6% on the announcement before giving back some of the gain, closing out the week down 5.2% as investors weighed roughly $1 billion in stock consideration against the value of three businesses of very different maturity. Cantor Fitzgerald reiterated an Overweight rating and $11 price target on Archer shares. The skeptical case centers on Archer's cash burn and uncertain FAA certification timeline for Midnight — concerns the Boeing assets don't resolve on their own, even if Insitu's steady revenue offers some ballast.

The deal still needs antitrust sign-off, and the companies have set a year-end target to close. Watch for whether regulators scrutinize the concentration of eVTOL and unmanned-aircraft IP in a single company, and for Archer's next funding round, where Boeing's promised $55 million investment will be one signal of how much conviction the deal actually carries.
