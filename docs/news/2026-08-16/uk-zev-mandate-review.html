UK government reviews electric-car sales targets after carmaker pressure

A public EV charging point in the Scottish Borders — the charging network manufacturers say the ZEV mandate was meant to justify. — Walter Baxter / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A UK consultation could cut the 2030 pure-EV sales target from 80% to as low as 50%, after carmakers said the mandate cost them over £10bn in discounts.

The government opened a consultation on 14 August that could let carmakers sell as few as half their new cars as fully electric by 2030 — down from the 80% currently written into law — after the industry's trade body said manufacturers had ploughed more than £10bn into EV discounts just to stay on the right side of the rules.

The Zero Emission Vehicle mandate, in force since 2024, requires an escalating share of each manufacturer's UK sales to be battery-electric, rising to 100% by 2035 — the effective point at which new petrol and diesel cars stop being sold. The Department for Transport's consultation, running until 23 October, sets out four ways to ease the path to 2030: trajectories reaching 70%, 60% or 50% zero-emission sales, or keeping the 80% headline figure but with more generous compliance flexibilities. It also revisits the so-called PHEV loophole, under which manufacturers can count a slice of plug-in hybrid sales toward their target using a "utility factor" calculation that critics say overstates how much time those cars actually spend running on electricity alone.

The price of compliance

The trigger is money, not sentiment. Battery-electric cars still take roughly a quarter of the new-car market — well short of this year's mandated share — leaving manufacturers who miss their target with three options: buy compliance credits from rivals who have overshot, pay a fine to the Treasury, or discount EVs heavily enough to shift the extra volume themselves. The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders, fronted by chief executive Mike Hawes, says the industry has chosen the third route to the tune of over £10bn in discounts across the past two years, and argues that pace is not sustainable without an urgent rewrite of the targets.

That figure is doing a lot of work in the debate, and it is worth treating carefully. A £10bn subsidy pool implies an average discount approaching £11,800 per EV sold — roughly double the £5,000-to-£5,600 range that data providers JATO and Autotrader have calculated for the same period. SMMT has not published a methodology behind its number, and more recent statements from the body put the cumulative figure above £12bn, without reconciling the gap. None of that makes the underlying pressure fictional — margins on discounted EVs are visibly thinner than list prices suggest — but the precise scale of the damage is closer to industry advocacy than audited fact.

Government under pressure, critics unconvinced

Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander framed the review as pragmatism rather than retreat: "It's right we keep targets under review to ensure they're practical and back British industry. The end goal hasn't changed — but we need to take business with us on the journey." She pointed to £7.5bn in government EV support, including a grant scheme she said has helped over 160,000 buyers switch, as evidence the state is still pulling its weight.

That framing has not satisfied either side. Charging-infrastructure groups warn that a 50% target, if the gap is filled by petrol and diesel sales instead of hybrids, could add close to 19 million tonnes of lifetime carbon emissions from the extra combustion vehicles sold — and that watering down a mandate barely two years old undermines the investment case for the charging network the mandate was partly designed to justify. SMMT, for its part, wants faster movement than an October consultation close allows, having pushed for the government's previously scheduled 2027 review to be brought forward.

The practical stakes for traders sit less in the politics than in the arithmetic: a confirmed shift toward 50-60% by 2030, rather than 80%, would push back the adoption curve analysts have been pricing into EV supply-chain and legacy-automaker margin forecasts, and would keep manufacturers' EV divisions loss-making on volume for longer than consensus currently assumes. The consultation closes on 23 October; a government response and any revised targets are expected to follow before the end of the year.
