White House commits $700m in federal funds to new coal plants and an export terminal

John E. Amos Power Plant on the Kanawha River, Poca, West Virginia — one of three states targeted for new coal construction under the federal matching-grant programme — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The Trump White House will direct $700m under the Defense Production Act to coal plant upgrades, new construction grants, and the long-contested Oakland export terminal — a federal bet on a fuel the market is pricing for decline.

The White House said Wednesday that President Trump will commit roughly $700 million in federal funds to coal infrastructure — upgrading existing power plants, matching corporate capital for new construction, and reviving a West Coast export facility that spent the better part of a decade fighting California municipalities in the courts. The package, expected to be announced at an Oval Office event Thursday, represents the administration's largest concentrated financial commitment to a fuel that has shed two-thirds of its share of the American electricity market in the past twenty years.

The legal vehicle is the Defense Production Act of 1950, a Cold War statute originally designed to mobilise industrial output for national emergencies. The Trump administration has invoked it repeatedly to channel funds into energy projects, and the coal package follows an established pattern of framing fossil-fuel support as national security policy rather than sector subsidy. The administration has cited AI data-centre power demand — the Department of Energy estimates data centres consume 10 to 50 times more energy per floor space than conventional office buildings — and the goal of limiting strategic leverage held by foreign adversaries over fossil-fuel supply chains.


Coal handling infrastructure at John E. Amos Power Plant along the Kanawha River, West Virginia — one of the states targeted for new construction funding. (Wikimedia Commons)

How the $700 million is structured

The money is divided into three tranches. The largest — more than $350 million — covers upgrades at 13 existing coal-fired power stations. A second block of $185 million is structured as matching grants: the federal government will put in capital alongside corporate funds to support two new plants in Alaska and West Virginia and restart one in Maryland. The third piece, $75 million, goes to the long-proposed West Gateway export terminal at Oakland's former Army Base, a project that cleared its final major legal hurdle last year when the California Supreme Court declined to review a lower-court ruling that had paved the way for construction.

The Alaska project is the most-watched element. Terra Energy Center — a proposed 1.25-gigawatt facility backed by a $500 million equity commitment from Koreit, a South Korean private equity firm — would, if completed, be the first new coal-fired plant commissioned in the United States since Sandy Creek, a 932-megawatt station in Texas, came online in 2013. Terra has signed a supply agreement with Hyundai Heavy Industries Power Systems for boilers. The coal mine that would feed the plant, however, does not yet exist, and the project's economics depend heavily on how quickly AI-driven power demand materialises in Alaska's grid.

Betting against structural decline

The competitive backdrop is difficult to ignore. Coal now accounts for roughly 16 percent of U.S. electricity generation, down from more than half in 2000. The Energy Information Administration projects a further 50 percent decline from 2024 levels by 2030, and a 93 percent decline by 2035. Levelised cost estimates for new coal generation run $68 to $166 per megawatt-hour — against $28 to $117 for utility-scale solar and $23 to $139 for onshore wind. The gap is structural, driven by cheap natural gas and fast-falling renewable capital costs, not regulatory headwinds alone.

U.S. coal production itself is forecast to fall from an estimated 512 million short tons in 2024 to 467 million by 2026 as power producers continue to dispatch cheaper alternatives. Federal grants — even at this scale — do not change the dispatch stack. Without complementary policy that shifts the competitive economics directly, such as tariffs on competing generation equipment, grid-reliability mandates that value dispatchable baseload at a premium, or long-term military off-take agreements, the $700 million functions more as a floor under existing capacity than a genuine reversal.

The export angle carries its own complications. Global seaborne coal trade volumes contracted in 2025 and are expected to contract again in 2026 — what would be the first consecutive two-year decline this century. China's coal imports fell roughly 10 percent year-on-year in early 2026, even as India's rose nearly 9 percent. The Oakland terminal's commercial case rests primarily on Asian demand, and the long-run direction of that market's largest buyer is not in coal's favour.

Market read: priced in, not transformed

Coal equities had already priced in substantial policy tailwind well before Wednesday. The Range Global Coal Index ETF (COAL) had returned 64 percent over the prior twelve months and was up 22 percent year-to-date before Wednesday's session, when it dipped 0.7 percent alongside utilities broadly. Peabody Energy (BTU), Core Natural Resources (CNR), and Alliance Resource Partners (ARLP) are the names most directly exposed to near-term volume and price movement.

Peabody's balance-sheet moves are instructive. The company priced a $250 million convertible note offering at 0.50 percent last week, using proceeds to retire higher-cost paper rather than fund expansion. Even producers aligned with administration policy are treating this environment as one to manage credit, not to bet large on new supply.

The administration has invited governors and lawmakers from Wyoming and West Virginia to Thursday's Oval Office event — a staging that underscores the political geography of the initiative as much as its commercial rationale. Both states have economies that remain materially exposed to coal royalties, employment, and tax revenues.

What to watch next

The practical test for these projects is not the announcement but the financing close. The Alaska plant still needs its mine permit, a complete regulatory stack, and a bankable offtake agreement before federal matching grants translate into construction starts. The Oakland terminal faces California's permitting agencies and local governments, which retain meaningful leverage over utility interconnections, rail access, and environmental review — instruments that have stalled similar projects for years.

The near-term price catalyst for thermal coal is less likely to be the grants themselves than any trade action that accompanies them. If the administration couples this package with tariffs on competing generation equipment or tightens grid-reliability rules in ways that advantage always-on baseload, the competitive math shifts. If it does not, the $700 million is a subsidy programme for a sector the market is still pricing for long-run contraction. Investors should watch the fine print of any executive order language released Thursday alongside the announcement.
