Nvidia-Siemens data centre partnership forces a rethink of Fluence Energy's market position

Server racks inside a modern data centre. AI factories running Nvidia's Vera Rubin platform require battery storage to bridge grid gaps and condition power for dense GPU workloads. — Carl Lender / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Fluence Energy's stock surged 44% after being named sole battery partner in Nvidia and Siemens' AI factory blueprint — but analysts remain split on whether the spec translates into durable orders.

On 2 June, Siemens published a blueprint. The document — a reference architecture for an AI data centre running Nvidia's Vera Rubin NVL72 platform — specified, to the bill of materials, exactly what a 103-megawatt AI factory should look like. Among the named components was Fluence Energy's SmartStack battery storage system, the only battery energy storage partner listed across all seven infrastructure OEM designs in the Nvidia ecosystem.

The market reacted immediately. Shares in Fluence, the publicly traded pure-play energy storage firm spun out of Siemens and AES in 2021, jumped roughly 44 per cent in a single session, reaching $27.

The architecture is more than symbolic. The Siemens design specifies a two-to-three-hour battery duration — above the one-hour threshold analysts had previously assumed as a baseline for AI data centre applications. For a company that sells modular battery blocks by the megawatt-hour, that spec change carries real revenue implications: Mizuho estimates the longer duration alone implies $2 to $4 of per-share upside relative to earlier models. The core function of Fluence's SmartStack in the design is to sit between the grid and the AI compute cluster, smoothing erratic power draws, riding through voltage and frequency fluctuations, and providing black-start capability if the grid fails entirely. As GPU clusters grow denser — Vera Rubin NVL72 systems draw power at extreme and uneven densities — that kind of battery-backed conditioning becomes an engineering necessity rather than an optional upgrade.

Named partner, contested moat

The complication is what "named partner" actually means in practice. Mizuho, which held an Underperform rating and a $15 price target through the rally, made the point plainly: the DSX ecosystem is both crowded and non-exclusive. Fluence's appearance in a reference document carries no revenue commitment. The bank already models a 10 per cent share for Fluence in the data centre battery market and said it wants to see actual order intake before reconsidering that position.

Canaccord Genuity took the opposite view, reiterating a Buy and a $28 target. The argument: being the sole named battery partner embedded across seven OEM reference designs creates real procurement inertia. Engineers building AI factories to the Nvidia specification are more likely to default to a listed vendor than to re-evaluate the battery tier independently.

Both positions are defensible, and the gap between them maps onto Fluence's own recent numbers. The company's backlog reached a record $5.6 billion in the second quarter of fiscal 2026, with its total pipeline up roughly 30 per cent to $30 billion since September. But quarterly revenue of $465 million came in 26 per cent below forecasts, and gross margins remain thin at 11.7 per cent — not the profile of a company with much room for further execution slippage.

The next legible test arrives before year-end. Fluence's fiscal 2026 guidance of $3.2 billion to $3.6 billion in revenue leaves enough space for the data centre thesis to either confirm or disappoint. What analysts are watching for is whether hyperscalers and co-location operators placing orders under the Vera Rubin spec convert the blueprint into Fluence purchase orders — or simply use the reference design as a starting point before shopping the battery tier.
