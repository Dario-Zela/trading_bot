Anglo American restructuring outruns analyst consensus, leaving valuations fragmented

Sculptures outside Anglo American's London headquarters at 20 Carlton House Terrace, Westminster. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Anglo American's stock trades above the analyst consensus target as a GBX 1,900–4,500 price-target spread reflects genuine uncertainty over its post-restructuring value.

Anglo American's shares are trading well above the average analyst price target — a reversal of the usual hierarchy that points to something real: the company's portfolio restructuring has moved faster than Street models have kept up. As of this week, the stock sits at roughly 4,167p on the London exchange while the analyst consensus target stands around 3,437p, implying the market has priced in an outcome that a majority of covering analysts haven't yet committed to on paper.

The gap between highest and lowest targets is harder to explain away. Deutsche Bank raised its target to 4,500p on 3 June with a Buy. JPMorgan, also revising in the past ten days, moved its Underweight target up to 3,160p — still nearly a quarter below where the stock trades. Somewhere further back sits a low-end outlier at 1,900p. The range from floor to ceiling runs to roughly 2.4 times: a spread that, on a FTSE 100 constituent with a decades-long analyst following, reflects genuine disagreement rather than modelling inertia.

A company mid-metamorphosis

The valuation confusion has a straightforward cause. Anglo American is not the same company it was eighteen months ago, and it has not yet become the company it intends to be. The demerger of Valterra Platinum — formerly Anglo American Platinum — completed in June 2025, removing the group's largest revenue line by volume. In May 2026, Anglo agreed to sell its Australian steelmaking coal portfolio to Dhilmar for up to $3.875 billion: $2.3 billion upfront and a price-linked earnout of up to $1.575 billion, with the transaction expected to close early next year. De Beers remains in process — the formal sale is described as "continuing" with no date announced.

What remains after these exits is a tighter business built around copper, premium iron ore, and crop nutrients, with management targeting copper at roughly 60 to 70 percent of group EBITDA. That goal is legible in outline. What analysts are still working out is what multiple a semi-transformed miner deserves while the transformation is unfinished.

Barclays, maintaining an Overweight rating through the period, flagged the arithmetic tension directly: post-restructuring EBITDA margins are projected to rise sharply — reaching 47 to 55 percent over 2026–28 — but absolute EBITDA will be 22 to 26 percent lower than pre-restructuring figures as the asset base shrinks. A leaner, higher-margin business commands a different P/E than a sprawling diversified miner. Analysts are not yet agreed on where that multiple lands.

The stock market, characteristically less patient than sell-side schedules, has already voted. Whether that vote reflects a justified re-rating of a copper-focused Anglo or a momentum overshoot ahead of the De Beers resolution is the question portfolio managers are now sitting with.

The next concrete data point is the De Beers sale timeline. Management has said it expects to provide an update "through the course of 2026"; given the coal deal's May announcement, the logical window for a De Beers development narrows as the year progresses. Until that uncertainty clears, the spread between bull and bear targets is unlikely to compress.
