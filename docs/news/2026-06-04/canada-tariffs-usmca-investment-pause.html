Canada extends US metals tariffs as USMCA uncertainty freezes border-region investment

The Ambassador Bridge linking Windsor, Ontario to Detroit, Michigan — the busiest commercial land crossing in North America — frames the bilateral trade stakes as USMCA renegotiations reach their deadline. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Ottawa confirmed it will extend retaliatory tariffs on US steel and aluminium through mid-2027, as the July 1 USMCA review deadline approaches with border-region investment already stalling.

Ottawa's decision to extend retaliatory tariffs on American steel and aluminium past their current expiry marks a hardening of Canada's negotiating posture at a moment when the USMCA trade architecture — the framework governing nearly $2 trillion in annual North American commerce — is approaching a mandatory review deadline of July 1.

Canada's Finance Ministry confirmed this week that tariff-rate quotas on imported steel and aluminium relief measures will remain in place through mid-2027, with steel quotas extended to June 27, 2027 and aluminium tariff protections running to June 30, 2027. The measures, which penalise non-USMCA importers with a 50% surtax on volumes exceeding strict quota limits, were initially imposed as direct countermeasures to Washington's 50% tariffs on Canadian steel, aluminium, and copper — themselves a significant escalation from the original 25% rate levied in March 2025.

Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne framed the extension in domestic terms. "Supporting Canada's steel and aluminum industry means strengthening our regional economies and the future of shared prosperity," he said in a statement accompanying the announcement.

The timing is pointed. Canadian Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc met US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer in Washington on June 2, accompanied by chief negotiator Janice Charette, presenting a proposal for a 16-year USMCA renewal alongside parallel talks on sectoral tariffs. Canada is pushing to resolve the steel and aluminium dispute within the broader renegotiation framework. Washington has so far shown limited enthusiasm for bundling the two tracks.

A standoff measured in steel


Steel coils at U.S. Steel's Gary Works, Gary, Indiana — output from integrated mills like this sits at the centre of the bilateral tariff dispute. (Ken Lund / CC BY-SA 2.0)

The tariff arithmetic is not small. Canada's original March 2025 countermeasures covered C$12.6 billion in US steel imports and C$3 billion in aluminium — a combined C$15.6 billion package structured to mirror Washington's own measures in dollar-for-dollar terms. Since then both governments have layered on additional measures: Canada tightened non-USMCA steel quotas from 50% to 20% of 2024 import volumes in December 2025, and imposed a 25% tariff on steel-derivative products covering approximately $10 billion in imports annually, applying to the full value of goods including doors, windows, fasteners, and structural components.

Through it all, USMCA-compliant trade has continued largely unimpeded — over 85% of Canada-US trade retains tariff-free status under the agreement's exemptions. But that exemption is precisely what is under renegotiation. The US is seeking to raise regional content requirements for vehicles to 82%, with half of that production located in the United States. Canada's current steel and aluminium measures function both as a negotiating lever and as insurance against further escalation.

For businesses operating across the border, the uncertainty compounds. Canada's horizontal remission for steel goods used in manufacturing and food packaging expired January 31, 2026 — meaning companies that had relied on tariff relief for US-origin steel inputs have had to absorb costs or switch suppliers since February. The automotive and aerospace sectors retain exemptions until July 1, a date that now coincides with the USMCA review deadline.

Capital that won't cross the border

The trade policy stalemate has generated a second-order effect that is now attracting attention from within the Federal Reserve system. Lorie Logan, President of the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, speaking at the University of Texas at El Paso on June 3, flagged a measurable deceleration in capital investment across US border regions tied directly to USMCA review uncertainty.

"A noticeable slowdown in investment as uncertainty looms," was how Logan characterised conditions in the Dallas Fed's service territory — a district that spans Texas and significant portions of the US-Mexico economic corridor. The observation carries institutional weight: the Dallas Fed surveys hundreds of companies operating in cross-border manufacturing, logistics, and energy supply chains, giving its president ground-level visibility into investment timing decisions that aggregate economic data lags by months.

The picture Logan described tracks with what analysts elsewhere are observing. Foreign direct investment in Mexico — the third leg of the USMCA triangle — has declined roughly 10% year on year, according to estimates cited in recent assessments. Nearshoring projects that were accelerating through 2024, as manufacturers sought to reduce dependence on Asian supply chains, have entered what one assessment called a "holding pattern." New greenfield manufacturing investments have largely stalled. Manufacturing employment in border communities is declining.

For companies weighing multi-year capital commitments in facilities that depend on integrated North American supply chains, the calculus is straightforward: until rules of origin, tariff structures, and investment protections are settled for the next renewal period, project approvals are deferred. The result is an investment gap that will not be recovered simply by eventually reaching agreement — the months of inaction are already a sunk cost.

Tony Stillo of Oxford Economics put it directly: "Our forecast for growth to ramp up in H2 and through 2027 depends on a favourable USMCA renegotiation."

What the July 1 deadline actually means


The Ambassador Bridge carries hundreds of thousands of trucks annually between Windsor and Detroit, making it a barometer of North American supply-chain health. (Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0)

If all three USMCA parties fail to agree on a renewal path by July 1, the agreement does not expire — it shifts to mandatory annual reviews through 2036, a mechanism that by design generates continuous uncertainty rather than resolution. For businesses and investors, that outcome is arguably worse than a clean renegotiation, however contentious, because it substitutes a defined policy horizon with rolling ambiguity.

Canada's position has hardened partly as a function of domestic politics and partly as a reflection of where negotiations stand. Ottawa was excluded from recent US-Mexico bilateral talks, putting it in a position of receiving a proposed deal rather than shaping one. PM Mark Carney has shown little appetite for pre-conceding on tariff issues. Canada's Q1 2026 GDP contracted at an annualised 0.1% rate, marking a second consecutive quarter of negative growth — a figure that simultaneously increases the economic pressure to reach a deal and raises the political cost of appearing to concede under duress.

The steel and aluminium tariff extension, in this context, is less a standalone trade measure than a signal. Ottawa is communicating to Washington that it enters the July 1 review window with its retaliatory tools intact and operational, not wound down in anticipation of goodwill. Whether that posture accelerates a deal or delays one depends on how Washington reads Canadian resolve — and whether the investment contraction that Lorie Logan is now cataloguing generates sufficient domestic pressure in the United States to move the talks forward.

The next visible marker: LeBlanc and Greer are expected to meet again before the July 1 deadline. If a framework agreement on USMCA extension terms emerges from those talks, the steel and aluminium measures could become a bargaining chip. If not, both sides enter the annual-review regime with tariffs fully armed.
