AAR extends Airbus A320 slat repair contract in MRO market expansion

An Airbus A320 on approach with flaps and leading-edge slats deployed — the slats are among the most frequently overhauled components on the narrowbody type. — Bin im Garten / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
AAR Corp adds Airbus A320 leading-edge slat repair to its Chonburi, Thailand facility — a routine capability extension with no disclosed financial terms.

AAR Corp has added leading-edge slat repair for the Airbus A320 family to its authorized component maintenance offering in Asia-Pacific, deepening a decade-old partnership with the European planemaker and widening the American services group's footprint on the dominant narrowbody type.

The capability will run out of AAR's established facility in Chonburi, Thailand — an Airbus-authorized single-source service centre — and covers both the classic A320ceo and the newer A320neo variant. Slats sit on the leading edge of the wing; they extend at low speeds to increase lift during takeoff and landing, making their periodic overhaul a near-certain recurring cost for every A320 operator. AAR's Chonburi site already handles rudders, flaps, and sharklets for the type; slat repair slots in as the next logical addition to that cluster. The announcement, made on May 19, also marks the tenth anniversary of AAR and Airbus's collaboration in the region.

"We are strengthening A320 MRO support infrastructure across Asia-Pacific as airlines modernise their narrowbody fleets," said Rahul Shah, AAR's senior vice-president for strategic growth and business development in APAC and MENA.

Routine maintenance in an expanding market

The news carries no disclosed contract value, no stated duration, and no financial guidance specific to the deal — characteristics more typical of a capability press release than a headline award. AAR management did not update investor guidance in connection with the announcement; the full-year fiscal 2026 target of 17–19% revenue growth, reaffirmed at the company's May 12 investor day, predates it.

That broader guidance, however, contextualises why the move makes sense. AAR reported third-quarter fiscal 2026 revenue of $845 million, up 25% year-on-year, with adjusted EBITDA climbing 26% to $102 million. The repair and engineering division — the segment that houses component MRO — has been a consistent growth driver alongside a parts supply business that grew 45% in Q3.

The A320 family is the obvious vehicle for incremental repair volume in Asia-Pacific. Airbus holds roughly 60% of the global narrowbody market, with a backlog exceeding 7,000 A320neo-family aircraft. The Asia-Pacific region accounts for roughly 46% of projected global new-aircraft demand over the next 20 years, and Airbus forecasts that total aviation services demand in the region will reach $138.7 billion by 2044, growing at a 5.2% CAGR. Against that backdrop, a service centre in Thailand adding one more line item to its Airbus-authorized repair menu is less surprising than the absence of it would be.

The absence of financial specifics is not unusual at the component-MRO level; the industry rarely discloses per-contract revenue. What it signals is strategic direction — AAR pushing further into Airbus proprietary repair rather than third-party channels — rather than a discrete earnings catalyst.

The next quantifiable milestone will be AAR's fourth-quarter fiscal 2026 results, due in mid-July, at which point management will face the test of whether the 17–19% full-year guidance holds. Slat repair in Thailand is unlikely to merit a line in those results; it would need time to mature into volume first.
