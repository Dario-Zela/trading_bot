Nvidia buys Kumo AI to push beyond chips into enterprise software and data infrastructure

Nvidia's headquarters in Santa Clara, California. The chipmaker has been aggressively acquiring software and model companies to extend beyond hardware. — Kevin McCarthy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Nvidia has bought graph-ML startup Kumo AI for $400M+, adding predictive analytics for enterprise structured data to its growing software stack — but integration plans remain undisclosed.

Nvidia has acquired Kumo AI, a startup that builds predictive models for enterprise structured data, for more than $400 million — its latest move to extend its reach from chip manufacturing into the broader software and data infrastructure market. The deal was announced via a LinkedIn post by an Nvidia executive and confirmed by The Information and Fortune on Wednesday; Nvidia declined to discuss financial terms.

Kumo AI is four years old, employs a small team, and had raised $37 million from Sequoia Capital in 2022. Its three co-founders — CEO Vanja Josifovski, head of engineering Hema Raghavan, and chief scientist Jure Leskovec — have already transitioned to Nvidia. The company's customers include DoorDash, Reddit, and Sainsbury's.

What Kumo actually does

Kumo AI sits in a gap that most enterprise AI tools have left open: making useful predictions from the messy, interconnected tables of data that companies actually store — customer records, payment histories, supply chain logs, churn indicators. General-purpose large language models are poorly suited to this kind of structured, relational data. Kumo's answer is graph neural networks, which treat each data point not as an isolated row but as a node in a web of relationships.

The company's latest product, KumoRFM-2, launched in April 2026, is billed as the first foundation model to outperform traditional supervised machine learning on relational data. It requires no additional training: a data team points it at their existing data warehouse — Snowflake or Databricks are the primary integrations — defines the prediction task in plain English, and gets an answer. The company claims it can process data at 5 gigabytes per second and handle tables of 500 billion rows.

Leskovec, a Stanford computer science professor who pioneered graph neural networks and co-created PyTorch Geometric (the most widely used library in the field), demonstrated the pitch succinctly: "With the foundation model, you point it to your data, you define what you mean by churn, and a second later, you get the prediction."

Reading Nvidia's acquisition playbook

This deal fits a well-established Nvidia pattern. Since 2024, the company has bought Run.ai (GPU orchestration, approximately $700 million), SchedMD (Slurm workload scheduling, December 2025), and Illumex (enterprise ontologies, approximately $60 million in February 2026), in addition to the far larger $20 billion IP deal to absorb Groq's inference chip assets. The common thread is vertical integration: Nvidia wants to own not just the silicon, but the software layer that runs on it, the scheduling layer that allocates it, and increasingly the AI models themselves.

Jensen Huang has framed this strategy in terms of the "AI factory" — the idea that the enterprise data center is no longer a passive storage layer but an active production facility for intelligence. If Nvidia controls the GPU, the software stack that runs inference on that GPU, and the predictive models being inferred, it becomes much harder for enterprise customers to route workloads to competitors. The Kumo acquisition adds a model layer directly targeted at the structured data that sits at the core of most real businesses.

The timing is deliberate. Enterprise adoption of AI has accelerated sharply in 2025 and 2026, but most deployments remain narrowly focused on document summarisation and code generation. Predictive analytics on operational data — the kind of use case Kumo was built for — represents a large and underserved market that incumbent vendors, including Microsoft and Salesforce, have so far addressed only partially.

What the announcement doesn't say

For all the strategic logic, the disclosure is thin. Nvidia has not explained how it plans to integrate Kumo's technology: whether the models will be folded into its AI Enterprise software suite, offered as standalone NIM microservices, or used primarily as research and development capability by Leskovec's team. Kumo's annual recurring revenue — the figure that would tell you whether this is a product acquisition or a talent acquisition — has not been disclosed.

The $400 million figure, if accurate, is meaningful but not decisive for a company of Nvidia's scale. It represents a modest premium on what is still a very early-stage commercial operation; by comparison, Run.ai was generating substantial enterprise revenue before its sale. The absence of a clear integration roadmap raises the question of whether Nvidia bought Kumo for its customers, its technology, its team, or some combination of all three in proportions that have yet to be worked out.

Kumo's website, as of Thursday, made no mention of the acquisition.

The clearest signal of intent will come when Nvidia begins incorporating Kumo's capabilities into its enterprise product lineup. Nvidia's next developer conference and its coming quarterly earnings calls are the natural venues. Until then, the Kumo deal is another data point in Nvidia's accumulation of enterprise software assets — directionally coherent, but strategically incomplete.
