Gulf incidents push crude traders to reprice geopolitical risk

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG supply normally transits — effectively closed since late February 2026. — Kleptosquirrel / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Iran claimed to strike a US Navy destroyer in the Gulf of Oman; CENTCOM called it a lie, but Kuwait airport attacks and stalled peace talks kept crude risk premiums elevated.

Iran's state broadcaster IRIB reported Wednesday that Iranian Navy forces had struck the "command and control centre" aboard a US destroyer operating near Iranian territorial waters in the Gulf of Oman. US Central Command's response was terse and unequivocal. "Iran is lying," the Pentagon's regional headquarters said in a statement, adding that its military assets at sea "continue to fly, sail, and operate safely and unimpeded."

No independent verification of the alleged strike exists. Iran's claim offered no damage assessment, no imagery, no corroboration from a neutral party — the operational specifics that would ordinarily anchor such a claim were absent.

The incident did not arrive in isolation. Less than 24 hours earlier, Iranian missiles and drones had struck Kuwait International Airport, damaging Terminal 1, killing one person, and injuring 63 others. Kuwait Airways suspended all flights; other carriers diverted. The airport, which had briefly reopened after an earlier closure, was shut again before partially resuming operations from a second terminal. Crude traders were left absorbing both pieces of information simultaneously — a claimed strike on a US warship followed within hours of a confirmed attack on a Gulf civilian airport.

What the market is absorbing

Brent crude futures were trading around $97 per barrel on Thursday after three consecutive sessions of gains, with WTI holding above $95. Both contracts have become the primary barometer of Gulf risk since the US-Israel air campaign against Iran began in late February, effectively shuttering the Strait of Hormuz — a chokepoint for roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG supply. Approximately 10.5 million barrels per day of Gulf production came offline in April alone.

The options market tells a starker story. Implied volatility in crude contracts has averaged 78% since the conflict escalated, with isolated sessions topping 100% — levels last seen during acute supply-shock episodes. The gap between implied and realized volatility has turned negative in recent weeks, meaning traders are paying above recent-move rates for protection. That is a sign of latent anxiety rather than current dislocation: the market is not crashing, but it is buying insurance as if it might.

Analysts put the embedded geopolitical risk premium in Brent at $8–$12 per barrel. That range implies crude would trade in the low-to-mid $80s if the Strait reopened and verifiable ceasefire commitments emerged from back-channel talks between Washington and Tehran. Neither condition currently holds.

The market has grown calibrated to discount unverified Iranian military claims until independent evidence appears — this conflict has produced several such claims. What distinguishes this week's cluster is the pace: an alleged warship strike, a confirmed civilian airport attack, and diplomatic talks described as stalled, all within 72 hours. Individual claims can be discounted; the accumulation of incidents raises the baseline risk that markets embed in prices, and that baseline has been drifting higher since late February.

The next catalysts to watch: whether neutral observers — UN monitoring, satellite imagery, allied signals intelligence — corroborate the warship claim; whether Kuwait airport achieves a sustained reopening; and whether the diplomatic channel referenced in recent weeks by both sides can be revived before the next incident forces the risk premium higher still.
