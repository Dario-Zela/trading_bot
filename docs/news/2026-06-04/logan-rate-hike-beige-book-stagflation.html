Dallas Fed's Logan puts a 2026 rate hike back on the table as Beige Book signals stagflationary crosscurrents

Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas headquarters in downtown Dallas, where Lorie Logan serves as president and CEO. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Dallas Fed's Logan says current rates aren't restraining growth, puts a 2026 hike on the table, and warns the trim-mean PCE is masking true inflation.

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas hosted a town-hall-style conversation in El Paso on Tuesday, but Lorie Logan used the forum to deliver a message considerably sharper than the format suggested. Speaking alongside University of Texas at El Paso President Heather Wilson, the Dallas Fed chief said current interest rates are failing to brake the economy — and explicitly raised the possibility of a rate increase in the second half of 2026.

"Monetary policy is not restraining the economy," Logan said, pointing to accommodative financial conditions, robust corporate earnings, and AI-driven capital spending that shows no sign of slowing. Her reading of the Fed's target range of 3.5% to 3.75% was blunt: policy is "neutral or perhaps even a bit loose." The corrective she is seeking is "at least mildly restrictive policy to finish the job."

The remarks represent the sharpest public positioning Logan has taken this cycle. In late April, she was one of three FOMC voters — alongside Cleveland's Beth Hammack and Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari — who dissented against a statement carrying an implicit easing bias. By June, that dissent has resolved into something closer to direct advocacy for higher rates.

When the Ruler Warps

Logan's most technically significant claim may be one that received less attention than the rate-hike headline: that the Dallas Fed's own signature inflation measure, the trimmed-mean PCE, is no longer a reliable policy guide.

"Trimmed-mean inflation is not currently a reliable signal," she said — a notable concession given that the Dallas Fed publishes the series and Logan herself cited it favourably in her February address.

The problem is structural rather than coincidental. The trimmed-mean methodology works by excluding the extreme price movers at both ends of the distribution — the top 31% and bottom 24% of items by price change — and averaging what remains. In normal conditions, those extremes are genuine outliers, and the residual reflects underlying trend. Under broad-based tariff pressure, that logic breaks down. Because import duties pushed up a wide swath of core goods simultaneously, the upper tail of the distribution is no longer populated by idiosyncratic noise but by a systematic policy effect. The trimming process, designed to cut noise, is cutting the signal instead.

Dallas Fed researchers estimated in April that tariff effects added roughly 0.80 percentage points to 12-month core PCE in March 2026. Absent those effects, core inflation would stand near 2.3%. The trim-mean, by stripping the tariff-driven spike at the top, nudges reported inflation toward the lower figure — suggesting convergence toward target when the underlying dynamics are more complicated. Logan's point is not that inflation is dramatically higher than reported; it is that the measure the Fed leans on could, taken at face value, justify easing before price pressures have actually subsided.

Two Speeds, One Bind

The Federal Reserve's Beige Book, released the same day as Logan's El Paso appearance, provided field-level corroboration. Price pressures accelerated across most of the twelve Fed districts compared with the prior survey period — a marked shift from earlier in the year — with energy costs a primary driver. Crude prices have risen as Persian Gulf military tensions escalated, and those costs are filtering through to shipping rates, trucking, and grocery shelves.

Consumer spending, meanwhile, is softening. Lower- and middle-income households are pulling back, even as overall economic activity has held at "slight to moderate" across ten of twelve districts, supported by corporate capital expenditure and upper-income consumption. Employment is barely moving: eleven districts reported little or no change.

The resulting picture is uncomfortable in its symmetry. Growth is modest but not contracting. Inflation is above target and trending higher in the Beige Book's snapshot. Employment is neither weakening enough to compel a cut nor strong enough to argue away price pressures. The corridor the Fed must navigate between its two mandates has narrowed materially.

Logan's comments on Fed independence add an institutional dimension to the rate debate. Jerome Powell has recently warned publicly against political interference in central bank decision-making, and the April dissents — from Logan, Hammack, and Kashkari simultaneously — read in context as a signal that the rate path will follow data rather than external pressure. A central bank that is defending its independence has particular reason to avoid the appearance that rate decisions are driven by anything other than price and employment outcomes.

What to Watch

The next FOMC meeting falls in late July, leaving roughly two months of incoming data before a policy decision. May's PCE report, due later in June, is the immediate test: if headline and core measures again diverge meaningfully from the trimmed mean, it will strengthen Logan's case for looking past the smoothed series when calibrating policy. A second Beige Book release before the July meeting will update the consumer spending picture.

For markets priced around the assumption that the Fed's next move is a cut, Logan's remarks in El Paso are an explicit caution. The rate cycle is not closed in either direction — and, depending on how the June data land, the next incremental move could be upward.
