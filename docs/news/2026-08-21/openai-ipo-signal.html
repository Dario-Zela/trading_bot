OpenAI CFO says company will go public 'in 2027 or sooner'

Sarah Friar, OpenAI's CFO, previously took Square public in 2015 and Nextdoor public in 2021. — Stephen McCarthy / Sportsfile via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar told staff the company expects to go public 'in 2027 or sooner,' the firm's most concrete IPO signal yet.

OpenAI's chief financial officer has put a number on something the company has spent two years dodging questions about. At an all-hands meeting on 19 August, Sarah Friar told employees that OpenAI expects to be a public company "in 2027 or sooner" — the most concrete timeline the ChatGPT maker has ever given for an IPO, and a marked shift from the standard non-answer executives have offered since 2023.

Friar was careful with her framing. The listing could come earlier, she said, if the business "continues to inflect" — company shorthand for revenue accelerating rather than merely growing. She was equally careful with what the IPO is not. "The IPO is not a finish line," she told staff, according to people who described the meeting to reporters, "it is a milestone, another fundraise." That line matters more than it sounds: it's an attempt to stop employees, and by extension the market, from treating a public listing as the moment OpenAI's story resolves into a stable, matured company.

Why now

The timeline isn't coming out of nowhere. OpenAI confidentially filed a draft S-1 registration with the Securities and Exchange Commission back in June, a routine early step that lets a company start the regulatory clock without committing to a date or tipping its numbers to competitors. Confidential filings can sit for months before converting into a public prospectus, so Friar's comment is best read as OpenAI signalling where that process is headed, not as a listing date.

The numbers she showed staff explain the confidence. Revenue run rate has climbed 35% quarter-to-date, she said, with the enterprise business up 50% over the same stretch, and the company's AI coding and workplace tools are pulling in 20 million weekly active users. That builds on a March funding round — $122 billion from investors including Amazon, Nvidia and SoftBank — that valued OpenAI at $852 billion, making it the most valuable private company in the world by a wide margin.

Set against that is a less flattering set of numbers. OpenAI is still burning cash at a scale with few precedents: internal projections reported this year point to roughly $14 billion in losses for 2026 alone, with cumulative losses potentially reaching $115 billion before the company turns profitable sometime in the 2030s, largely a function of the enormous compute and data-centre commitments it has signed. A CFO announcing IPO plans while the loss column widens is not a contradiction so much as the actual pitch: come in now, own the growth curve, and don't expect profitability to arrive on a normal software-company timeline.

The Anthropic subplot

Friar's remarks came with a pointed aside about OpenAI's closest rival. Anthropic has also filed confidentially for an IPO and, by some accounts, could go public within weeks — potentially as soon as September, ahead of OpenAI. Asked how the company felt about that prospect, Friar told employees: "That's OK, we are running our own race." It's a line calibrated for an all-hands room, but it also does real work outside it: it pre-empts the market narrative that would otherwise write itself if Anthropic beats OpenAI to the bell — that the smaller, newer competitor got there first because it had less to prove.

Friar has done this before. She was chief financial officer at Square through its 2015 IPO and chief executive of Nextdoor through its 2021 listing via SPAC, giving her more public-market experience than most people in OpenAI's building. Before that she spent time in finance and strategy roles at Salesforce, Goldman Sachs and McKinsey. She joined OpenAI as CFO in mid-2024, arriving with an explicit brief to professionalise a finance function that, until then, had been running a company worth hundreds of billions of dollars with the internal machinery of a much smaller startup.

What a listing would actually mean

An OpenAI IPO at anything close to its current private valuation would be among the largest public offerings in history, and it would land at a moment when AI spending — on chips, power and data centres — is already the single biggest driver of US capital markets activity. It would also force a level of disclosure OpenAI has avoided as a private company: audited financials, a clear accounting of its unusual capped-profit corporate structure, and a public paper trail on exactly how it plans to fund the trillion-dollar-plus infrastructure commitments it has made to partners like Microsoft, Oracle and Nvidia.

None of that happens on Friar's say-so alone. A confidential S-1 has to be updated, refiled publicly, and cleared by the SEC before any roadshow can begin, and OpenAI's unusual governance — a nonprofit board sitting above the for-profit entity — adds a layer of legal complexity that a normal tech IPO doesn't carry. What Friar's comments do is set an expectation, both for staff weighing equity decisions and for the investors who have poured over $100 billion into the company on the promise that a listing eventually arrives. The next marker to watch is whether that confidential filing converts to a public one — and whether Anthropic, or OpenAI, rings the opening bell first.
