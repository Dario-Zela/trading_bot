NHS A&E attendances hit record high amid summer heatwaves

The entrance to an NHS Accident and Emergency department — England's A&E units recorded their busiest month on record in July 2026. — Geograph.org.uk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
England's NHS logged a record 2.5 million A&E attendances in July as heatwaves pushed ward temperatures to 41.8°C, exposing a near-£16bn estates repair backlog.

England's Accident & Emergency departments had their busiest month on record in July, according to NHS England performance data, with 2.5 million attendances logged as two summer heatwaves pushed hospital ward temperatures as high as 41.8°C. May, June and July now rank as the three busiest months for A&E in the health service's history — a run health economists say reflects both a genuinely hotter summer and a system with too little slack to absorb it.

The headline figures are not uniformly bad. NHS England noted that 75.4% of patients were seen, treated or discharged within the four-hour target in July, an improvement on June, and category 2 ambulance response times fell to just under 30 minutes. Health Secretary Yvette Cooper pointed to a record 2.65 million diagnostic tests carried out in a single month as evidence of underlying productivity gains. But more than 150,000 people waited over 12 hours in A&E during the month, and ambulance services fielded 898,503 emergency 999 calls, up 6.5% on a year earlier. "In July, there were a record 2.5 million A&E attendances, representing huge pressure for staff and uncomfortable waits for patients in unbearably hot conditions," said Sarah Scobie, deputy director of the Nuffield Trust think tank.

Buildings built for a cooler country

The temperature data is the more striking thread. Almost every hospital trust in England recorded internal temperatures above the NHS's own recommended ceiling of 28°C at some point in July, with an average peak of 31.9°C across sites — and isolated readings, including the 41.8°C figure cited by the Royal College of Surgeons of England, in wards with no mechanical cooling at all. Tim Lane, the college's president, said "across the NHS, surgical teams are struggling to deliver high-quality care in ageing hospital buildings," describing an estate "struggling to cope with continuing heatwaves." He linked the strain directly to a maintenance backlog he put at close to £16bn — a figure roughly consistent with NHS Digital's Estates Returns Information Collection, which recorded a £15.9bn backlog for 2024–25, up from £13.8bn the year before. Lane said operating theatres were especially exposed, with surgeons working under intense lighting and heavy protective equipment reporting dehydration, fatigue and dizziness severe enough that some had to step away mid-procedure. Part of the bind, according to reporting on NHS capital planning, is that net-zero guidance has favoured natural ventilation over air conditioning in newer builds — a design assumption now colliding with successive heatwave summers.

Workforce strain, and what it costs

The Royal College of Nursing has gone further, with general secretary Nicola Ranger describing conditions in some units as an "impossible environment," and reporting nursing staff collapsing on shift in facilities she said were "not fit for humans." The RCN is pressing for updated NHS capital guidance on climate adaptation, mandatory heat risk assessments and faster installation of cooling equipment — asks that, if acted on, point toward incremental capital spending layered on top of an already stretched estates budget. For an employer of England's size, sustained heat exposure, short-staffing on hot shifts and rising overtime and agency use are the kind of pressures that show up later as pay and recruitment costs, a consideration for anyone pricing NHS-adjacent labour markets or public sector wage settlements.

The next formal marker is the ERIC estates data due later this year, which will show whether the backlog has grown past £16bn as flagged, and the Autumn Budget on 28 October, where capital allocations for NHS estates — including any dedicated heat-adaptation funding — will be tested against wider fiscal constraints.

[Figures are drawn from NHS England's July 2026 performance statistics and the Royal College of Surgeons of England; readers should note that estate-condition figures, including the £16bn backlog estimate, are college and industry projections rather than official Treasury or NHS Digital confirmed totals for the current financial year.]
