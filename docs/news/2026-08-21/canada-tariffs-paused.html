Trump pauses 50% Canada tariffs at 11th hour on tentative trade deal

President Trump greets Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney at the White House. — Gabriel B. Kotico / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Trump paused 50% tariffs on ~$20bn of Canadian goods two hours before they hit, citing a deal that still isn't signed and expires today.

President Trump paused a set of 50% tariffs on Canadian goods less than two hours before they were due to take effect, saying Washington and Ottawa had struck a trade deal that merely needs signatures. That deal does not yet exist in writing. The reprieve — three days, expiring today — buys time for negotiators but does nothing to resolve the dispute that produced the tariffs in the first place, and it leaves roughly $20bn of annual trade exposed to a levy that could still land.

A law dormant since 1949

The tariffs Trump paused were unusual before they were even due to bite. On July 20 he invoked Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a provision last used, by any president's administration, in 1949 — to impose the duties via three separate proclamations covering motor vehicles (439 tariff lines), dairy (52 lines) and alcoholic beverages (63 lines). All three were scheduled to enter force on August 19. Together they touched about $20bn of Canadian imports, roughly 5.2% of the $382bn in goods the US bought from Canada in 2025, according to figures cited by trade lawyers tracking the proclamations.

The administration's stated rationale leaned on grievance rather than macroeconomics. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer argued Canada had pulled American alcohol off store shelves in some provinces, given the European Union better access than the US for dairy exports, and capped how many American-made vehicles could be sold into the Canadian market. Trump separately pointed to the $46.4bn US goods deficit with Canada in 2025. Notably exempted from the Section 338 tariffs: oil, gas, potash, critical minerals, fish, and anything already caught by the separate Section 232 tariffs on steel, aluminum and copper, which range from 15% to 50% and remain in force regardless of what happens with this deal.

The 11th-hour reprieve

Trump announced the pause on Truth Social the night of August 18, roughly two hours before the tariffs were set to kick in at midnight. "I have paused the 50% Tariffs against Canada, that were scheduled to kick in tomorrow morning for a three day period, based on the fact that Canada and the U.S.A., subject to the finalization of documents, have a DEAL!" he wrote, adding — without elaboration — that the shelved Keystone XL pipeline "may be awoken from the grave!" Greer followed with a statement describing an agreement that would deliver "comprehensive market access for all American goods, economic security commitments, digital trade alignment."

Canada's account was more guarded. Prime Minister Mark Carney's office said "substantial progress has been made, although there is important work still to be done," and Dominic LeBlanc, the minister leading the Canadian side, said Wednesday that "efforts remain ongoing" and negotiators were "working collaboratively towards a finalized agreement." Neither side has disclosed what Canada is giving up to get the tariffs lifted, or whether the auto-export cap, dairy access and alcohol shelf-space complaints that triggered the Section 338 action are actually being addressed.

A muted market response

Currency markets treated the pause as good news, but not conclusive news. The Canadian dollar climbed to a two-and-a-half-month high, with USD/CAD easing to 1.3824 from 1.3889 the prior session — helped as well by firmer Canadian GDP, a strong employment report and higher oil prices. But the move was measured rather than a relief rally, and analysts at Monex Europe framed the range explicitly: a durable, signed deal could carry the loonie toward 1.37, while a collapse and reimposition of tariffs could send it back above 1.40. That asymmetric framing is the clearest signal of how traders are actually pricing this — not as resolved, but as a live coin flip with a three-day fuse.

For positioning in autos, dairy and cross-border industrials, the practical effect of the pause is to remove the tail risk of a same-day 50% cost shock. It does not remove the tariffs as a standing variable. Section 232 duties on steel and aluminum stay in place either way, and nothing in the pause touches them.

What's next

Today's deadline is itself a marker of how provisional this is: the pause runs out August 21, and either the two governments produce signed text extending or replacing it, or the Section 338 tariffs default back into force. Beyond that, the dispute sits inside a larger, slower-moving fight — the USMCA's mandatory six-year joint review took place July 1, and the US declined to commit to the agreement's 16-year extension option, triggering a rolling annual review process instead of locking in trade terms through 2042. Canada and Mexico both indicated they wanted the extension; Washington did not. That leaves the Canada relationship exposed to exactly the kind of unilateral, short-notice tariff action that produced this week's scramble, deal or no deal.
