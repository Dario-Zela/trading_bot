Drought grips over 70% of England as wildfire risk climbs toward a sixth heatwave

In one sentence
Over 71% of England is in drought and wildfire callouts have surged, with Europe's linked hydropower shortfall raising gas-market tail risk into autumn.

Drive through Hampshire or the Isle of Wight this month and the fields tell the story before any statistic does: cracked clay where pasture should be, hedgerows browned off six weeks early, rivers reduced to a trickle over gravel that is usually submerged. Parts of Hampshire, the Isle of Wight and West Sussex have gone 59 days without a drop of rain. The Met Office and the Environment Agency confirmed this month that 71.3% of England — almost three-quarters of the country — is now formally in drought, after the driest July in a series dating back to 1836.

The headline number matters less than what sits behind it. This is England's third drought in five years, and the pattern is no longer an anomaly to be explained away — it is becoming the shape of the English summer.

A landscape running dry

July brought England just 10% of its long-term average rainfall, the driest such month on record. Reservoir storage nationally has fallen to 65.9% of capacity, 13.8 percentage points below where it should be for late August. Five reservoirs — Clatworthy, Clywedog, the Dove Group, Hanningfield and Wimbleball — are classed as exceptionally low; South West Water's Wimbleball is down to 46% full. Fifteen river-gauging sites are exceptionally low, and only 5% of monitored sites sit at normal levels.

Eight water companies have imposed temporary use bans, leaving more than 27 million people under some form of restriction, and several have escalated to "operational drought level 2" — the stage at which supply resilience, not just customer inconvenience, becomes the concern. In East Anglia, regulators have cut spray-irrigation licences by half under Section 57 powers, affecting 288 farm licences at the tail end of a growing season that needed water most. Tony Grayling, who chairs the Environment Agency's National Drought Group, put it plainly: the dry weather "is taking a real toll on farming and there will be long-lasting impacts on our environment." The government has warned conditions could persist into October.

Fire risk keeps pace with the heat

Where the ground dries, it burns. Fire and rescue services across England and Wales have logged well over a thousand wildfires so far in 2026 — more than 200 in a single fortnight in late July alone — prompting an emergency mobile alert on 14 August and a COBRA meeting the following day, at which the Prime Minister described parts of the country as a "tinder box." The National Fire Chiefs Council's Phil Garrigan noted that "more than 200 wildfires in just 12 days shows how quickly the risk can escalate when hot, dry conditions take hold," adding that the great majority are preventable — discarded cigarettes, barbecues, glass left in dry grass.

By scale, 2026 has not matched the record-breaking 2025 season — Europe's fire-monitoring service EFFIS puts this year's burned area in the UK at roughly half of last year's — but that is cold comfort set against a decade-long trend, and fire chiefs are unanimous that the underlying risk, driven by longer dry spells and record summer heat, keeps ratcheting upward regardless of any single year's tally.

Why traders are watching the taps

The drought's relevance stretches beyond gardens and grasslands. Reduced river flow and low reservoir levels curb the cooling water available to thermal power stations, and drought is doing the same to Europe's hydropower fleet on a much larger scale: Austrian output ran at 51% of normal in July, Switzerland at 48%, Germany at 63%, France at 68%. Hydropower supplies roughly 28% of EU electricity, and the shortfall has already pushed EU gas-fired generation up 8% year-on-year as utilities burn gas to cover the gap. Michael Strugl, chief executive of Austria's Verbund, summed up the arithmetic: "We are simply missing one-third of the water volume that our hydropower plants would normally process."

Britain is not as hydropower-dependent as the Alpine states, but it sits inside the same interconnected gas and power market, and the mechanism is the same one that helped drive last year's price volatility when supply shocks met tight capacity: dry weather raises cooling-water and generation constraints at precisely the moment air-conditioning and irrigation pumps push demand higher. UK-listed utilities — National Grid, SSE, United Utilities, Severn Trent — face a narrower version of the same exposure, through both water-network stress and the regulatory scrutiny that follows a summer of hosepipe bans and low reservoirs. None of this guarantees a repeat of last year's swings, but it adds a tail-risk that gas and power traders, and utilities-exposed portfolios, are pricing rather than ignoring.

What comes next

The Met Office's own outlook is deliberately hedged: forecasters say a further, sixth heatwave in September is "one possible outcome" among several, and "far from certain," with low pressure and wetter conditions also plausible into early autumn. What is not in doubt is the accumulated deficit — a reservoir system already 14 points below normal cannot be refilled by a single wet fortnight. The next National Drought Group update and the Environment Agency's monthly water situation report will show whether autumn rain starts closing that gap, or whether England heads into winter still carrying this summer's shortfall — and, with it, the pressure on gas and power markets that comes from a continent short of water.
