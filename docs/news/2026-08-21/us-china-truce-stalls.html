US-China trade truce stalls as summit deliverable slips

Trump and Xi walk together at Zhongnanhai during the May 2026 Beijing summit, where the two sides chartered a since-stalled investment forum. — The White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A May summit pledge to open a US-China investment forum has stalled completely, even as new US forced-labor tariffs hit China at a 12.5% rate.

The trade truce Donald Trump and Xi Jinping struck in Beijing this May was never billed as a grand bargain — officials on both sides called it a stabilisation, not a resolution. Three months on, even that modest framing looks generous. A flagship deliverable from the summit, a joint forum meant to unlock Chinese investment in the United States, has yet to hold a single working session. And Washington has just imposed fresh tariffs that hit Chinese goods among dozens of other trading partners.

A forum going nowhere

At the May summit, Trump and Xi chartered two new bodies to manage the relationship: a Board of Trade for non-sensitive commercial flows, and a Board of Investment meant to give both governments a standing channel for discussing capital flows between the two economies. The trade board has made some headway. The investment board has not moved at all.

"Neither have really initiated the consultation process," Sean Stein, president of the US-China Business Council, told the South China Morning Post, adding that neither government looks ready to sit down with the private sector on the issue. The holdup, according to people familiar with the discussions, traces to unresolved disagreement inside the Trump administration and Congress over how much Chinese foreign direct investment the US should welcome at all — a live political question that a Beijing summit communiqué was never going to settle.

That matters because the investment board was one of the few concrete, forward-looking commitments to come out of May, alongside Chinese purchases of US soybeans and Boeing jets and the reopening of the beef and poultry trade. Those purchase pledges are transactional and self-executing. The investment board required both bureaucracies to actually build something together — and three months in, neither has.

Tariffs land anyway

Meanwhile the trade war's older machinery has kept running. On 24 July, the US Trade Representative imposed new Section 301 tariffs on 60 economies following a forced-labor investigation, replacing an expiring global duty. China was assessed the top rate, 12.5%, alongside Brazil, Vietnam and Russia; the UK, India, Mexico and Canada drew a lighter 10%. The forced-labor tariffs sit apart from — and stack on top of — the reciprocal tariff truce Trump and Xi extended through November, the one born at their Busan meeting last October that also parked China's rare-earth export controls for a year. Beijing has since resumed needling that truce's edges, blacklisting two US rare-earth firms, MP Materials and USA Rare Earth, from Chinese supply chains in June.

None of this technically breaches the May agreement — the forced-labor tariffs are a separate legal track, aimed broadly rather than at China alone. But the optics are hard to miss: a summit sold as opening China trade wider is instead presiding over a stalled investment channel and a fresh round of tariffs that catch Beijing regardless.

Xi is expected in Washington on 24 September for the reciprocal leg of this year's summit diplomacy, with AI cooperation set to dominate the agenda. Whether the Board of Investment has so much as met by then will be one of the clearer signals of whether May's truce is deepening or merely holding.
