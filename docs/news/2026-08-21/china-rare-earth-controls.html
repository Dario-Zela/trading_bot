China tightens rare earth export controls further, hitting Japan and US supply chains

A NASA satellite image of the Bayan Obo mining district in Inner Mongolia, the source of the bulk of China's rare-earth production. — NASA Earth Observatory
In one sentence
Beijing's new reporting rule turns rare-earth export curbs into standing enforcement, deepening supply squeezes for Japan, the US and allied chip and defence makers.

Beijing has spent the past sixteen months turning rare-earth exports into a lever it can pull at will. This summer it started bolting that lever to the wall.

On 24 June, China's Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) issued Announcement No. 26 of 2026, which took effect 1 July. It does something the earlier rounds of export curbs didn't: it formalises how violations get caught. Any organisation or individual, the ministry said, now has "the right to report conduct suspected of violating relevant laws," with rewards promised for confirmed tips and reduced penalties dangled in front of companies that self-disclose. The mechanism covers the obvious offences — exporting without a licence, misdeclaring goods, routing through third countries — but also reaches into the supply chain around a shipment: freight forwarders, customs brokers, e-commerce platforms and banks that knowingly support an illegal export are now exposed too.

From leverage to law

The distinction matters more than it sounds. China's rare-earth licensing regime, introduced in April 2025 and expanded through Announcement 18 that year to cover seven additional medium- and heavy rare earths — samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium — always functioned as a control valve Beijing could open or shut depending on the state of trade talks with Washington. A reporting-and-reward system is different in kind. It recruits the private sector into enforcement, embeds compliance risk into every logistics and financial intermediary touching a Chinese mineral shipment, and outlives whatever diplomatic mood produced it. Enforcement has already produced casualties: two Japanese nationals were detained in Dalian in May on suspicion of smuggling export-restricted goods, and the chairman of a Chinese precision-optics firm was separately charged with disguising germanium-containing lenses as ordinary glass to dodge licensing.

Tokyo bears the brunt

Japan is the clearest test case for what formalised enforcement looks like in practice. Beijing's restrictions on Japanese-bound dual-use minerals tightened sharply from January 2026, in the wake of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's November 2025 remarks on Taiwan, which Beijing treated as a provocation. The numbers since are stark: China shipped no dysprosium or terbium oxide to Japan at all between November 2025 and May 2026. Japan's total dysprosium imports for the first half of 2026 came to just 13 tonnes — down 82% on the same period two years earlier — with monthly imports flatlining at zero in January, February, May and June, according to Japanese customs data reported by TrendForce.

Dysprosium and terbium are what keep neodymium-iron-boron magnets from losing their magnetism at high temperature — the property that makes them usable in EV motors, industrial servos and missile-guidance systems rather than just fridge magnets. China still supplies roughly 80% of Japan's rare-earth imports and controls about 90% of global magnet-grade refining capacity, so there is no quick substitution. Magnet makers Shin-Etsu Chemical, Proterial and TDK are drawing down inventory to keep customer deliveries flowing, but none can do so indefinitely without qualified oxide from a non-Chinese source — and little of that exists at scale.

The bill comes due elsewhere

The price signal is already showing up outside Japan. Neodymium-praseodymium oxide prices rose roughly sixfold between January and June 2026; tungsten concentrate tripled; antimony doubled. Heavy rare earths are carrying the steepest premiums, and meaningful new non-Chinese processing capacity isn't expected before 2027, according to S&P Global. CSIS has reported that European defence contractors are struggling to secure magnets for missile-guidance systems, while China's June decision to add ten US entities — including rare-earth miners MP Materials and USA Rare Earth — to its own control list shows Beijing is willing to squeeze the companies built specifically to route around it.

Washington's answer so far is to build a buffer rather than a bypass. In February, the US hosted a Critical Minerals Ministerial in Washington that produced the FORGE forum and Project Vault, a $12 billion public-private stockpile — $10 billion from an Export-Import Bank loan, the largest in the agency's 92-year history, plus roughly $2 billion in private capital from firms including Boeing, GE Vernova, General Motors and Alphabet. The reserve is meant to cushion manufacturers against price spikes and supply gaps, not replace Chinese supply, which remains the underlying constraint.

What to watch

The formal reporting mechanism has been live barely seven weeks; how aggressively MOFCOM actually pursues tips, and against whom, will show whether this is primarily a deterrent or the start of a genuine crackdown on transshipment. Japan's monthly customs releases remain the clearest gauge of whether the dysprosium and terbium taps stay shut, and Project Vault's first stockpile drawdowns — expected once the reserve's initial mineral purchases are finalised — will indicate how much slack the US buffer can realistically absorb before chip and defense manufacturers feel the pinch directly.
