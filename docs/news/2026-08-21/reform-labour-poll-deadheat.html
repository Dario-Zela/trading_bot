Reform UK and Labour locked in dead-heat as poll averages converge

Nigel Farage, whose Reform UK party has surged 14 points since the 2024 election to draw level with Labour in polling averages. — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Reform UK and Labour are statistically tied at roughly 25% each in the latest poll average, with the Conservatives trailing near 20%.

Britain's political map has flattened into a three-way scramble, and the numbers now say so plainly. The latest seven-poll average tracked by BritPolls.co.uk puts Reform UK at 24.8% and Labour at 24.6% — a gap of two-tenths of a point, well inside any pollster's margin of error. The Conservatives sit a further five points back at 19.6%, a level that would have seemed catastrophic for a governing party only two years ago and now barely registers as news.

The averages, compiled from YouGov, Ipsos, Survation and Redfield & Wilton surveys, describe a collapse without precedent this early in a parliamentary term. Labour's vote share has fallen by roughly 15 points since the party's 33% landslide in July 2024. Reform, by contrast, has gained about 14 points over the same stretch — a mirror-image swing that has taken Nigel Farage's party from fringe insurgency to co-frontrunner in under two years.

A different Labour, a diminished lead

Much of Labour's recent stabilisation traces to a change at the top. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July after Keir Starmer's departure, and his net approval rating of +15.2% is a marked improvement on the -23.8% Starmer carried into his final weeks in office. That bounce appears to have arrested Labour's slide rather than reversed it: the party is no longer haemorrhaging support week to week, but it has not recovered the ground lost since the election either. Burnham's early moves — including a restructuring that stripped the Treasury of some growth-policy authority in favour of a new Manchester-based hub — suggest a prime minister trying to signal change without yet shifting the topline numbers.

Reform's rise has come partly at Labour's expense and partly at the Conservatives', with the Greens also picking up roughly eight points since 2024 as parts of the left splinter further. The result is a five-party field in which no single formation commands anything close to a working majority in polling terms, and where the traditional two-party swing that has shaped British elections for a century no longer describes what is happening.

Why markets are watching

A genuine three-way contest removes the kind of predictability that currency and bond investors price into UK assets. Sterling has already shown sensitivity to the shifting political mood this year, slipping to multi-week lows around bouts of speculation over Starmer's position before his departure, and gilt yields have stayed elevated as investors weigh the fiscal implications of a government led by a party with no track record of setting departmental budgets. A dead-heat poll average does not by itself move markets, but it raises the odds that any single data point — a by-election result, a misstep at the despatch box, a fiscal announcement — could be read as a turning point, and gilts and sterling have tended to react accordingly.

The next test comes domestically before it comes electorally. Chancellor Rachel Reeves' Budget is set for 28 October, and with inflation holding at 2.9% and gilt yields hovering near 5%, the fiscal statement will be parsed as much for what it signals about Labour's ability to govern from a position of polling weakness as for its tax and spending detail. Beyond that, there is no scheduled general election before 2029, but a poll average this tight, sustained for months, keeps speculation about an early contest — or a leadership challenge inside one of the three main parties — very much alive.
