Taiwan proposes record $35bn defence budget amid China tensions

An attack helicopter flies over live-fire drills during Taiwan's Han Kuang military exercise. — Presidential Office / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Taiwan's cabinet approved a record $35.2bn, 18%-higher defence budget for 2027 — above 3% of GDP for the first time — but must still clear an opposition-controlled legislature.

Taiwan's cabinet approved a record NT$1.1225 trillion (US$35.2 billion) defence budget for 2027 on Thursday, pushing military spending above 3% of gross domestic product for the first time in the island's history. The 18% jump from the current year is the sharpest single-year increase Taipei has posted in decades, and it lands at a moment when Beijing's military activity around the island shows no sign of slowing.

President Lai Ching-te framed the figure as more than an accounting milestone. "This is an investment in peace," he said Monday, previewing the number before the cabinet's formal sign-off. It is also, more plainly, a bet: that spending heavily now on missiles, drones and air defence can hold off a Chinese military that has spent the past four years normalising the kind of pressure it once reserved for moments of crisis.

What the money buys

Of the total, NT$691.9 billion sits in the core Ministry of National Defense budget, with a further NT$218.2 billion in special budgets, NT$60.7 billion in non-profit special funds, NT$103.8 billion for military pensions and NT$47.9 billion for the Coast Guard. Layered on top is a separate eight-year, roughly $40 billion special budget Taipei approved earlier this year for longer-term procurement — meaning the headline $35 billion figure understates the full multi-year commitment.

The funding priorities read like a checklist against a specific threat. Around NT$40.8 billion goes to AI-related defence programmes. Money is earmarked for the domestically developed Chiang Kung, or "Strong Bow," surface-to-air missile system, and for the T-Dome project, a layered air and missile defence architecture Taiwan has been assembling in pieces — radar, interceptors, command-and-control — rather than buying off the shelf. Drone funding, which had been stripped from an earlier version of the budget amid legislative wrangling, has reportedly been restored. There is also NT$39.2 billion for pay rises across the military, civil service and teaching corps, a detail that matters because Taiwan's forces have long struggled to recruit and retain personnel even as the equipment budget balloons.

The spending sits against a backdrop of accelerating Chinese activity that Taiwan's own Ministry of National Defense has been logging in near real time. PLA aircraft sorties into Taiwan's air defence identification zone rose from 1,669 in 2023 to 3,615 in 2024, more than doubling in a single year, and 2025 tracked even higher, with over 4,000 incursions logged between January and September alone. May 2026 brought 274 sorties in a single month, with roughly 79% penetrating the ADIZ — the highest monthly penetration rate this year, even if still below 2025's peaks. Taiwan's own Han Kuang exercise, its largest annual drill, wrapped up on August 14 after ten days that for the first time folded in unscripted scenarios, tests of internet-infrastructure resilience under simulated attack, and mass mobilisation drills that put army convoys on city streets and reservists in classrooms.

A parliament that isn't writing blank checks

The budget's approval by the cabinet is not the end of the story — it is barely the middle. Taiwan's Legislative Yuan, where the opposition Kuomintang and Taiwan People's Party hold a working majority, must review and pass the plan before it takes effect, and recent history counsels against assuming that will be smooth. This year's regular budget passed only after a record 266-day delay, and the KMT-TPP bloc has blocked elements of the special defence budget from committee review on repeated occasions, arguing — among other things — that the government first fund pension increases for retired police and firefighters before writing new checks for weapons. Opposition lawmakers insist they are not opposed to defence spending in principle; they say they want it scrutinised rather than rubber-stamped, a distinction that has nonetheless produced months of gridlock in each of the past two budget cycles.

That friction points to the deeper question hanging over Thursday's announcement: whether Taiwan can actually sustain this trajectory. Lai has pledged to lift defence spending to 5% of GDP by 2030, a target that would very roughly double the burden already being absorbed at 3.01%. Taiwan's tax-to-GDP ratio sits around 14.7%, low by developed-economy standards, which leaves relatively little fiscal slack to expand defence spending without either raising taxes, running larger deficits, or squeezing the health and welfare budgets that a rapidly ageing population increasingly depends on. Defence outlays have already climbed 61% since 2019 under Lai's Democratic Progressive Party predecessors and himself — a run rate that has been achievable so far partly because Taiwan's chip-driven economy has kept growing, but one that becomes harder to justify politically the longer it continues without a parallel reckoning on where the money comes from.

The stakes beyond the ledger

For Washington, the budget is likely to be read as a partial answer to years of pressure on Taipei to spend more on its own defence rather than relying on the presumption of American intervention. For Beijing, which has never renounced the use of force to bring Taiwan under its control, a defence budget explicitly framed around deterring PLA pressure is unlikely to be received warmly, even if Chinese officials have so far reserved their sharpest public criticism for the Han Kuang exercises rather than the budget itself, calling the drills a "wasteful charade" that "stoked war panic."

The practical test now shifts to the legislature. Lawmakers are expected to take up the budget before the end of August, and given the precedent of the past two cycles, a clean, swift passage would itself be notable. Watch for whether the drone and AI-defence line items survive committee review intact, and whether the KMT-TPP coalition again ties defence approval to unrelated domestic spending demands — a pattern that, more than any Chinese incursion count, may end up determining how much of this record budget actually reaches Taiwan's military in 2027.
