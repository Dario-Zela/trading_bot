MHRA seizes 28.5 million doses of counterfeit weight-loss drugs in record bust

Pens of Wegovy (semaglutide), a legitimate GLP-1 weight-loss injection of the type counterfeiters have been imitating with tirzepatide and retatrutide. — Nelson R. de Lima Filho / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
MHRA's 2025–26 report logged a record 28.5m doses of illegal medicines seized, headlined by the UK's first counterfeit weight-loss drug factory bust.

In one sentence: MHRA's annual report reveals a record 28.5 million doses of illegal medicines seized in 2025–26 — with a first-of-its-kind counterfeit weight-loss drug factory as its marquee case.

The figure is easy to misread, so it is worth being precise about it. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency's 2025–26 annual report, published 16 July, disclosed that its Criminal Enforcement Unit removed 28.5 million doses of illegally traded medicines from circulation over the year — a record for the agency, with an estimated street value of £64.4 million.[^1] That total spans the full range of what the black market moves through Britain: sedatives, painkillers, erectile-dysfunction pills. It is not 28.5 million counterfeit Mounjaro pens. But the case the MHRA chose to headline its report with was, for the first time, a weight-loss drug operation — and it illustrates just how fast that category has grown.

The Northampton factory

On 24 October 2025, MHRA investigators and Northamptonshire Police raided a warehouse on an industrial estate on the edge of Northampton. It was, the agency said, the UK's first known illegal factory manufacturing unlicensed weight-loss injections.[^2] Inside, officers found more than 2,000 pens pre-filled with tirzepatide and retatrutide — ready for dispatch — alongside tens of thousands of empty pens, raw chemical ingredients, packaging and manufacturing equipment, and roughly £20,000 in cash suspected to be trafficking proceeds. The finished stock alone was valued at more than £250,000. The MHRA described it as the largest single seizure of trafficked weight-loss medicines ever recorded by a law-enforcement agency anywhere in the world.[^3]

The two drugs found in the pens matter for different reasons. Tirzepatide is the active ingredient in Eli Lilly's approved Mounjaro and Zepbound. Retatrutide is not approved anywhere in the world — it remains in Lilly's late-stage clinical trials — which means any retatrutide reaching a UK consumer is, by definition, unlicensed and untested at scale.[^4] Then-Health Secretary Wes Streeting called the raid "a victory in the fight against the shameless criminals who are putting lives at risk by peddling dangerous and illegal weight-loss jabs to make a quick buck," adding that the products were "made with no regard for safety or quality" and posed "a major risk to unwitting customers."[^2] Andy Morling, who heads the MHRA's Criminal Enforcement Unit, put it more plainly: "Medicines regulation isn't discretionary — it exists to protect people."[^5]

A market growing faster than it can be policed

The Northampton bust was not an isolated event but the leading edge of a trend the MHRA's own numbers show accelerating. In a separate report covering calendar year 2025, published in January 2026, the agency said more than 5,000 illegally traded GLP-1 products had been seized that year alone — a sharp jump from October 2024, when MHRA officials described weight-loss drugs as making up only a "very small part" of the illicit medicines trade.[^6] Two further operations this year targeted the same criminal network: raids across Lincolnshire and Nottinghamshire in February, and a May raid on a country estate near Northampton that had also been used as a wedding venue, where roughly 12,000 further doses were recovered and two 29-year-old men were arrested under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012.[^7]

The supply-demand mismatch behind this is not subtle. Eli Lilly raised UK list prices for Mounjaro by as much as 170% in September 2025, and a month's private supply now runs from roughly £120 to £340 depending on dose.[^8] NHS access remains tightly rationed by design: of the estimated 3.4 million patients who could qualify under national criteria, only 250,000 are due to be brought onto the health service pathway over three years, with NHS England itself acknowledging a full rollout could take up to 12 years.[^9] Against that backdrop, counterfeit pens circulating for as little as £20 a shot are not a marginal temptation — for many patients priced out or stuck on a waiting list, they are the only accessible option.[^9]

Lilly goes to court

The manufacturer whose drugs are being counterfeited has started fighting back through the courts rather than relying on regulators alone. In August, Eli Lilly filed six US lawsuits against medical spas, compounding pharmacies and online sellers — including Aesthetic Envy, Legendary Peptides, Striker Pharmacy, Texas Peptides and Lone Star Peptide — accused of selling retatrutide by exploiting a "research-use only" labelling loophole intended for laboratory chemicals, not human injections.[^10] Lilly's lab analysis of seized product found doses ranging from zero to well above the intended amount, wrong active ingredients including insulin, and non-sterile manufacturing conditions; the company said some supply chains traced back to organised criminal networks in China. Reports collected by Lilly include cases of liver failure among users of black-market product. "What is being sold on the black market is not a medicine — it is entirely unverified, unapproved, and not worth the risk," the company's chief medical officer said.[^10]

What the Northampton case and Lilly's lawsuits share is a common diagnosis: neither a single national regulator nor a single manufacturer can fully police a global supply gap this large on its own. The MHRA's next full-year figures are due around January 2027, and will show whether 2026's run of raids — Lincolnshire, Nottinghamshire, the Northampton estate — dents the trend or merely confirms that seizures are rising in step with demand. Lilly's civil suits, meanwhile, move through the US courts over the coming months, a parallel track that will test whether litigation can close a gap that criminal enforcement alone has not.
