Russia intensifies strikes on Ukraine as Zelenskyy faces domestic pressure

Aftermath of a Russian missile strike on a residential building in Kyiv earlier this year — a recurring scene as barrages have intensified through 2026. — Prosecutor General's Office of Ukraine / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
A Russian barrage killed at least 18 in Kyiv as Ukraine's Patriot interceptor stockpile — just 1% of US reserves — runs critically thin before winter.

Kyiv had already spent most of the night listening to the drones when the ballistic missiles arrived. By the time the all-clear sounded, close to eleven hours after the first air-raid siren, at least 18 people across Kyiv and the surrounding oblast were dead and dozens more pulled from rubble, some still trapped beneath a collapsed nine-storey apartment block in the capital's Solomianskyi district. It was, by the numbing arithmetic of this war, an unremarkable night — the third mass barrage on the city in three weeks.

What made it notable was what didn't happen. Ukraine's air force said it intercepted 39 of more than 44 missiles and drones fired at Kyiv — a respectable tally on paper, but officials and residents alike have grown used to a starker admission trailing every such night: that the interceptors needed to stop Russia's ballistic missiles, the fastest and deadliest part of any barrage, are running out.

A shortage with a number attached

Ukraine now holds roughly 1% of the United States' stockpile of Patriot interceptors, according to figures cited by Ukrainian officials to CNN this month — well short of the 5% Zelenskyy has publicly asked Washington to release to get the country through winter. Lockheed Martin, the sole manufacturer of the PAC-3 interceptors that give the Patriot system its ballistic-missile capability, built around 620 of them in 2025, a production rate of less than two a day against a war that has, in recent months, absorbed missiles far faster than that. The Pentagon signed a seven-year deal in January to lift output toward 2,000 a year, but that horizon is 2030, not this winter.

The scarcity shows up in blunt, human terms at the launch sites. "It's really a terrible sight when you have the equipment, but you can't intercept a single missile," a Ukrainian launcher-unit engineer identified only as Dmytro told CNN, describing empty racks where interceptors should sit. Zelenskyy has gone further than describing a supply problem — he has suggested it may not only be one. "Perhaps these are political steps to make Ukraine, let's say, more receptive to certain demands," he said earlier in August, declining to rule out that reduced deliveries reflect diplomatic leverage rather than simple scarcity, even as he acknowledged the Middle East remains the only reason his partners have officially given for the shortfall.

Russia has adjusted its tactics accordingly. Zelenskyy has said Moscow is now firing roughly twice as many ballistic missiles a month as it did a year ago, aimed squarely at the gap in Ukrainian defenses that interceptor scarcity has opened. Thursday's strikes hit two fuel depots and warehouses outside Kyiv along with the residential blocks, a combination that fits a pattern of pairing infrastructure damage with civilian casualties as autumn approaches and heating season looms.

A government under strain from within

The barrage landed on a president already absorbing pressure from a different direction. Parliament confirmed Yevhenii Khmara, a former security service officer, as defense minister by a lopsided 312-2 vote the day before the strikes — filling a post Zelenskyy had left vacant since firing Mykhailo Fedorov in mid-July after a dispute involving then-commander-in-chief Oleksandr Syrskyi, who was himself dismissed days later.

Fedorov's removal has not faded quietly. It touched off recurring demonstrations dubbed the "Cardboard Maidan" — protesters in Kyiv, Lviv, Dnipro, Odesa and a dozen other cities holding hand-lettered signs, now in their second month, demanding his reinstatement. Fedorov had built a reputation cracking down on procurement corruption and made weapons-buying more transparent; his departure coincided with a $3.4 million money-laundering investigation into senior officials in Zelenskyy's office, which prompted the president to dismiss his own deputy chief of staff. On the day of Khmara's confirmation, Fedorov released a video calling the situation a "systemic crisis of governance" and urging elections.

Zelenskyy's approval numbers have moved with the scandal. Kyiv International Institute of Sociology polling put his trust rating at 58% in early August, down from 65% in June — the third straight monthly decline, which the pollster's executive director called "a worrying signal that requires attention and thoughtful decisions from the authorities." He remains, notably, the most trusted political figure in the country by a wide margin, and Ukrainian law bars elections under martial law regardless of where his numbers sit. But the combination of battlefield strain and a corruption scandal reaching his inner circle is the most sustained domestic pressure Zelenskyy has faced since the 2022 invasion.

What the shortage tests

The interceptor gap is not simply a logistics problem for Kyiv; it is a live test of how far Western military commitments extend once they collide with finite production lines and competing theaters — the Gulf coalition alone burned through roughly 225 interceptors a day during a four-day exchange with Iran earlier this year, a demand shock that has left US stockpiles depleted well below pre-war levels. Ukraine is pushing to build its own interceptor manufacturing capacity, a project Zelenskyy calls essential regardless of how the war ends, but that too is a multi-year undertaking measured against a shortage that is acute now, with winter approaching and Russian strike tempo rising.

For markets, the signal running underneath the human toll is a widening question about sustainability rather than intent: allied governments have not wavered rhetorically on support for Ukraine, but the physical capacity to arm that support is proving harder to scale than the political commitments made around it. That gap — between what has been promised and what can be manufactured — is what will shape decisions in Washington and European capitals over the coming weeks, as Ukraine heads into a winter it will spend defending both its cities and its skies with less than it had a year ago.

The next marker is practical rather than diplomatic: whether Patriot deliveries pick up before the first hard frost, when Russia's strikes on energy infrastructure typically intensify. Khmara's handling of procurement, and whether the Cardboard Maidan protests widen or fade, will shape whether Zelenskyy heads into that winter with a united government behind him or one still absorbing its own aftershocks.
