LVMH and European luxury sector selloff deepens

Louis Vuitton's flagship store on the Champs-Élysées in Paris, the most visible storefront of a group whose shares have shed roughly 30% of their value this year. — Jorge Láscar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH shares have fallen roughly 30% this year as weak China demand, rising European bond yields and Middle East tension squeeze the luxury sector, with Kering also under pressure though less severely.

Bernard Arnault built the world's largest luxury conglomerate on a simple premise: rich people keep spending, recessions or not. That premise is being tested more severely than at any point in LVMH's history. Shares in the Paris-listed owner of Louis Vuitton, Dior and Moët Hennessy have fallen roughly 30% since the start of 2026, from above €640 in January to around €446–451 in mid-August — the worst run in the company's history as a listed stock, and one that has wiped tens of billions of euros off its market value.

The slide is not a single-company story. It is a sector-wide repricing of an industry that spent three years insisting its post-pandemic boom was structural rather than cyclical, now confronting evidence that it might have been the latter all along.

A slowdown that outran the results

What makes the decline unusual is that it has not tracked LVMH's actual operating performance particularly closely. Half-year results released this month showed revenue of €38.64 billion and an operating margin still above 22%, hardly the numbers of a company in crisis. The fashion and leather goods division — Louis Vuitton and Dior, the profit engine — returned to organic growth in the second quarter after seven consecutive quarters of decline, even if only by a single percentage point. US sales accelerated to 6% growth in the second quarter from 3% in the first.

Yet the stock has kept sliding. On August 18 alone, LVMH fell a further 2.7% and Kering 4.3%, in a session driven less by company news than by a jump in European government bond yields. That divergence between the earnings tape and the share price is the clearest sign that investors have stopped pricing LVMH as a growth stock and started pricing it as a bond-proxy asset exposed to two compounding risks: a Chinese consumer who has not come back, and a cost of capital that keeps rising.

The China problem hasn't gone away, it's just changed shape

China still accounts for something like 30–40% of global luxury spending once outbound tourism is included, and it remains the unresolved variable in every luxury earnings call. LVMH executives describe the domestic Chinese recovery as gradual rather than absent — a stabilization, not yet a rebound — while Middle East tension has layered a fresh, quantifiable drag on top: LVMH said the conflict cut roughly a percentage point off growth in the first quarter, and Kering's Middle East retail sales fell 11% over the same period, concentrated almost entirely in the weeks after the war escalated in March.

Arnault himself has framed 2026 as a binary outcome rather than a soft landing. At LVMH's April annual meeting he told shareholders the year would either end in "a world catastrophe" or a gradual return to normal business "as we all hope" — an unusually stark formulation from an executive normally allergic to hedged, one-word predictions.

Kering's pain is sharper, if narrower

Kering, the owner of Gucci and Saint Laurent, has had a rougher and more idiosyncratic year. Shares are down closer to 14% year-to-date through mid-August — a smaller headline drop than LVMH's, but from a smaller, more concentrated base, and punctuated by violent single-day moves: a 9.3% collapse in April wiped roughly €2.5 billion off its market capitalisation in one session after first-quarter results showed Gucci revenue down 14.3% and confirmed that the brand's turnaround under new leadership is running well behind the timetable the market had priced in. Group revenue fell 6.2% in the same quarter. A modest, one-percentage-point return to growth in the following quarter has been treated by investors as tentative stabilization rather than proof of recovery.

What the two companies' divergent scale of pain reveals is that this is not a uniform sector collapse. Hermès, by contrast, grew 6.7% in the second quarter and continues to trade at a valuation premium, insulated by a supply-constrained model that manufactures scarcity rather than chasing volume. Brunello Cucinelli posted 14% organic growth in the first quarter under the same macro backdrop that saw Gucci decline 8%. The luxury trade is fracturing between houses built on genuine exclusivity and those, like Gucci and to a lesser extent Louis Vuitton's core leather goods lines, that scaled aggressively during the 2021–2023 boom and are now more exposed to a pulled-back aspirational buyer.

Why bond yields matter as much as China

The second leg of the story is less visible but arguably more structural. European government bond issuance is on track to approach €1.4 trillion in 2026 as governments refinance debt sold cheaply during the post-2021 low-rate era, and that supply has pushed yields higher even as central banks signal gradual easing — the 10-year Bund sat near 2.68% in mid-August, with Italian BTPs above 3.4%. Higher yields do two things to a stock like LVMH simultaneously: they raise the discount rate applied to future luxury-brand cash flows, compressing the price-to-earnings multiple the market is willing to pay, and they raise the financing cost of the debt-funded acquisitions — Tiffany, Christian Dior Couture, a steady diet of smaller maisons — that have been central to LVMH's growth model for two decades.

That second channel matters because it is not cyclical in the way a Chinese spending pause is. A slower China can recover with the next stimulus cycle or a stronger yuan. A structurally higher cost of capital across Europe, driven by heavy sovereign issuance rather than temporary inflation, changes the arithmetic of how much LVMH and Kering can pay for growth going forward — and investors appear to be repricing the whole sector for that possibility rather than betting on a near-term reversal.

What's next

The next hard data point is Kering's and LVMH's third-quarter trading updates, due in October, which will show whether the second quarter's tentative stabilization in fashion and leather goods carries through the traditionally strong autumn gifting season in China and the US. Beyond company results, the direction of European bond yields — shaped by the pace of ECB easing and the scale of continued government issuance — will determine whether the sector's de-rating is a temporary overshoot or a lasting reset in how the market values luxury growth. A sustained rebound in LVMH's share price, on current evidence, requires both threads to turn at once.
