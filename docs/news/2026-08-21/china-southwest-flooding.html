Severe flooding kills 10, forces over 20,000 evacuations in southwest China

The swollen Yangtze River in Chongqing, in China's southwest — the same broader region hit by this month's deadly flooding. — Huangdan2060 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Torrential rain killed 10 and forced over 20,000 evacuations across Sichuan and Guangxi, as the storm system now pushes north toward Beijing.

Torrential rain tore through China's southwest this week, collapsing a home during a family celebration in Sichuan and triggering a landslide at a construction site in Guangxi. Ten people died. More than 20,000 were moved out of harm's way as local governments raced ahead of rivers still rising.

The worst single incident came in Changning county, Sichuan, where accumulated rainwater brought down a roof and wall during a dinner marking a child's college entrance results — a customary summer gathering turned fatal. Three people died at the scene and two more later in hospital; 17 were injured. In Guangxi, a landslide struck a construction site, killing five. Elsewhere in Sichuan, heavy rain triggered geological hazards that cut National Highway G350 and stranded roads through the mountainous Garze and Aba prefectures, prompting authorities to pre-emptively evacuate 21,784 people across 64 counties, according to Reuters reporting relayed by Carbon Brief.

A summer of repeat flooding

This is not an isolated event. Guizhou, just east of Sichuan, has already logged three separate flooding episodes since May — one killing 22 and displacing 24,000, another evacuating nearly 10,000 from Qianxi and Zunyi in June, a third hitting Guangxi in July. China's meteorological administration and Ministry of Water Resources have issued red alerts for flash floods and mountain torrents across the region multiple times this summer, and President Xi Jinping said in April that torrential rain and flooding disasters are shifting in "temporal and spatial distribution" as the climate warms — an unusually direct acknowledgment from Beijing of climate-driven risk to domestic infrastructure.

The rain system now appears to be pushing north. Authorities evacuated thousands from Beijing ahead of heavy rainfall expected in the capital, according to Carbon Brief's latest briefing — a second front opening even as southwestern provinces are still counting damage.

What's exposed

Sichuan and its neighbors sit at the intersection of several supply chains sensitive to disruption. The province draws roughly 80% of its electricity from hydropower, and Carbon Brief reports that despite this month's heavy rain, plant operators are prioritising water storage over generation because national precipitation for the year overall is still tracking below average — a reminder that a wet week doesn't erase an underlying drought risk that has forced factory shutdowns in the region before. The cut roads through Garze prefecture also touch China's lithium supply chain: the area hosts the Jiajika deposit, one of the country's largest, and the recently restarted Ganzizhou Rongda spodumene mine. Rice, rapeseed and tobacco farmland across Sichuan and Guangxi faces separate losses from waterlogging, though full crop-damage estimates have not yet been published.

The next marker is Beijing's own rainfall, already underway, and whether the wider system that has now cycled through Guizhou, Guangxi and Sichuan continues its drift north through the Yangtze basin in the coming days.
