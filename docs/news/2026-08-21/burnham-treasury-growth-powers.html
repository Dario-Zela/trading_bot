Burnham strips Treasury of growth powers, hands them to new 'No.10 North' hub

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government has stripped HM Treasury of its central role in growth policy design. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham has moved economic growth policy from HM Treasury to a new Downing Street hub in Manchester, testing bond-market patience ahead of the 28 October Budget.

Andy Burnham has spent his career threatening the settled order of British government, first from Whitehall, then from a mayor's office in Manchester, and now from Downing Street itself. On Wednesday he made it official: economic growth policy, the function that has defined HM Treasury's authority since the department's creation in 1660, is moving out of London and into a new political hub called No.10 North.

The announcement, confirmed in interviews with The Times and Bloomberg, strips the Treasury of its lead role in designing and delivering growth strategy. In its place, Number 10's Manchester outpost — first opened in July, when Burnham and Chancellor John Healey worked their inaugural day there — will take direct charge of the government's stimulus and regional-investment agenda. The Treasury keeps the ledger: public spending discipline, tax policy, the fiscal rules. Growth becomes a separate, and explicitly political, project.

A 300-year-old department loses its centre of gravity

For as long as anyone can remember, Treasury has run both halves of the UK's economic operation: keeping the public finances in order and setting the direction for growth. Burnham's argument, made explicitly in his briefings this week, is that combining the two jobs has quietly strangled the second one. A department whose institutional instinct is caution — watching the deficit, protecting the gilt market's confidence, saying no to spending asks — is not built to also be the department that takes risks on growth.

So he has split the mission. Treasury retains its 2,000-odd London staff and its historic role minding the public purse. But hundreds of posts tied to growth policy are expected to move out of Whitehall and into the new operation, with No.10 North serving as the delivery arm for fiscal support, industrial strategy and the devolution agenda Burnham built his mayoral career on. He is said to plan working from the Manchester site roughly one day a week for the remainder of his premiership — a habit as symbolic as it is administrative, given his years insisting that Westminster's economic thinking has drifted too far from the regions it governs.

The move sits alongside a longer-running rebalancing already under way at the civil-service level: the Darlington Economic Campus, which brings together Treasury and six other departments outside London, is due to open in early 2028. No.10 North is a more overtly political creature — headquartered in the city Burnham has run for nine years, and answering to Downing Street rather than to the Treasury permanent secretary.

The bond market question Burnham can't fully answer yet

This is the part that matters for anyone pricing UK risk. Burnham arrives in office carrying a history of comments that unsettled gilt investors before he ever reached Number 10 — most notably suggesting Britain had been left "in hock" to the bond markets by decisions to hand away control of energy, water and housing. He has since walked the framing back rather than the substance, telling interviewers in the spring that he has "never said you can just ignore the bond markets," while still describing an economic vision built around reasserting public control over utilities and infrastructure.

Gilts wobbled during that period and have traded warily since, hostage to a UK political cycle that has already produced one change of prime minister this year. Yields have stayed close to 5% into the summer, with the market's attention now fixed on the 28 October Budget, where Healey is expected to set out how — or whether — fiscal rules survive the split of powers announced this week.

That is the asymmetry investors now have to price. If No.10 North is mostly a rebranding exercise — a political headquarters for initiatives Treasury would have designed anyway — the market impact is limited to noise and a marginal loosening of discipline. If it is the first concrete sign that growth policy will be run by conviction rather than by Treasury orthodoxy, the calculus changes: looser fiscal discipline is a gilt-negative story, but the same stimulus expectations could lift domestically exposed equities, particularly in regions and sectors that have waited years for a growth agenda with teeth.

What Burnham is actually betting on

Burnham's political identity was built almost entirely outside Westminster. Elected Labour MP for Leigh in 2001, he cycled through Cabinet posts under the last Labour government — Chief Secretary to the Treasury among them, a detail with some irony given this week's announcement — before leaving Parliament in 2017 to become the first mayor of Greater Manchester. Nine years, two re-elections and a reputation as the unofficial "King of the North" later, he took the Labour leadership and then Number 10 in June, in circumstances that owed as much to his predecessor's collapsing poll numbers as to any single policy platform.

What he has consistently offered since is a critique of London-centric government: that growth policy designed inside Whitehall, answerable to Treasury orthodoxy, structurally underserves everywhere that isn't the South East. No.10 North is the institutional expression of that critique. Healey's public backing for the change — notable, since it is his own department losing a core function — suggests this was negotiated rather than imposed, and gives Burnham some cover against the charge that he is simply hollowing out fiscal oversight by decree.

Whether that cover holds depends on what No.10 North actually does with its new mandate. A regional-investment programme with real fiscal firepower would confirm this is a serious redirection of economic policy toward the north of England, at some cost in bond-market patience. A hub that mostly convenes existing initiatives under a new letterhead would confirm the opposite — and gilt investors, who have spent a volatile year pricing Burnham's rhetoric, are likely to withhold judgment until they see money move.

What comes next

The first real test lands on 28 October, when Healey delivers the Budget and is expected to spell out how the fiscal rules Treasury still owns will interact with a growth mandate it no longer controls. Between now and then, watch for the first tranche of Whitehall postings out to Manchester — the scale of that move will be the clearest early signal of how much authority, and how many pounds, are actually following the announcement north.
