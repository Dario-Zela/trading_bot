UK fiscal watch: inflation holds at 2.9%, gilts steady near 5%, Budget set for 28 October

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose rate-setters split 6-3 to hold in July. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK inflation hit 2.9% in July as expected, the BoE's rate-hold vote split 6-3 toward hawks, and gilts brushed 5% ahead of the 28 October Budget.

UK inflation did exactly what economists said it would in July, which was the problem. The Consumer Prices Index rose 2.9% year-on-year, up from 2.6% in June, matching every major forecast on the Bloomberg survey. No surprise, no relief — and for a Bank of England already nervous about the inflation path, a matched forecast at a four-month high is its own kind of bad news.

The increase was driven largely by energy: Ofgem's price cap rose 13% in July, sending gas prices up 14.7%, the sharpest jump since October 2022, with electricity up 3.6%. Housing and household services inflation jumped to 4.1% from 2.7%. Furniture, clothing, alcohol and health costs all firmed too. The Bank's own projections, published alongside its 30 July rate decision, now show CPI peaking near 3.2% in the fourth quarter — 60% above the 2% target, with the energy shock from the Middle East conflict cited as a key uncertainty.

A committee losing patience

That rate decision is the real story behind the July print. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, but the dissent mattered more than the outcome. Three members — chief economist Huw Pill, Catherine Mann and Megan Greene — voted for an immediate quarter-point hike to 4%. The split had been 7-2 in June; by July, a third of the committee wanted to tighten. Markets read it as a committee losing confidence that inflation is under control, and are now pricing a hike by year-end with another possible by next April — a sharp reversal from the cutting cycle traders had been betting on earlier in the year.

Gilts test 5%, budget battle lines drawn

Bond markets have been trading that repricing in real time. The 10-year gilt yield touched 5.06% this week on oil-driven inflation fears before rallying sharply on softer labour-market data — unemployment holding at 4.9% but payrolled employment down 86,000 year-on-year — a reminder of how thin the margin is between a hawkish BoE story and a growth-scare one. The Debt Management Office still found buyers: a £4bn sale of 2036 gilts cleared with steady demand despite the higher-rate backdrop.

All of this lands three weeks before Chancellor John Healey's first Budget on 28 October, when the Office for Budget Responsibility will publish a fresh forecast that is expected to show growth pinned near 1.4% alongside persistent fiscal pressure. Healey is under pressure from the TUC to raise the bank surcharge — proposals range from 16% to 35% — after Barclays, HSBC, Lloyds and NatWest posted £29.2bn in combined first-half profits, with roughly £13.7bn returned to shareholders. UK Finance, representing all four, has warned that a surcharge increase would damage Britain's competitiveness as a financial centre. Healey has said nothing publicly either way.

The pieces point the same direction: sticky inflation, a central bank edging toward its next move rather than away from it, and a Chancellor who must reconcile both with a budget already boxed in by the OBR's numbers. The next Bank Rate decision lands 17 September; the Budget, and the OBR forecast that frames everything the Treasury can and can't do, follows on 28 October.
