Hormuz tanker attack and oil spill send Brent above $94 as Trump threatens new Iran sanctions

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes. — US Central Intelligence Agency (public domain)
In one sentence
Two ADNOC tankers were attacked transiting the Strait of Hormuz and a roughly 500-square-mile oil spill spread off Oman, sending Brent crude above $94 a barrel as a US-Iran truce lapsed.

Brent crude pushed above $94 a barrel on Friday after two tankers owned by Abu Dhabi's state oil company were attacked transiting the Strait of Hormuz, adding a fresh shock to a market already absorbing a spreading oil spill off Oman and the collapse of a fragile US-Iran truce. The strait carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Five months into a war that has repeatedly promised to close it and repeatedly failed to, the market's reflex — sell first, price the geopolitics later — is intact even if the physical flow of crude mostly isn't interrupted.

A chokepoint that keeps not quite closing

The attack, which the UAE's foreign ministry attributed to Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps and called "an act of piracy," struck an ADNOC-owned vessel early Saturday as it moved through the strait. No casualties were reported in that specific incident, but ADNOC says 15 of its ships have now been hit by missiles or drones since fighting began in March, killing one crew member and injuring 20. Saudi Arabia called the latest strike "a grave breach of international law"; Qatar said the strait should not be used as "a bargaining chip."

That fighting has run far hotter, and far longer, than the headline figure suggests. Since March 1, strikes attributed to Iran have hit Oman's ports of Duqm and Salalah and industrial sites at Sohar and Mina Al Fahal, killing at least 17 people. The US has lost an A-10 and an Apache helicopter in the fighting. Two separate ceasefires — in April and June — briefly reopened the strait before unravelling. Brent has swung from $63 in January to $126 in late April and back below $90 in between; Friday's move above $94 is escalation, not a new high.

What has changed is the traffic. VLCC transits through the strait have averaged two to three a day since early July, versus roughly eight before the war, according to shipping data cited by CNN — a de facto rationing of the world's most important oil corridor by insurance cost and owner nerve rather than by blockade. That gap between "still open" and "functioning normally" is the real story underneath every price spike: the strait hasn't been sealed, but it has been throttled, and throttling is enough to keep a risk premium permanently loaded into the price of crude.

A spill nobody planned for

Layered onto the tanker attacks is an accident with its own momentum. The Caroline Bezengi, a sanctioned tanker in Russia's so-called shadow fleet carrying close to a million barrels of crude, reported an explosion off Yemen on June 8 and later grounded off Oman's Sharbithat coast. The resulting slick has since spread, by Greenpeace's estimate, across roughly 500 square miles of the Arabian Sea — other satellite-based estimates from Copernicus and Planet Labs have put it closer to 800 square kilometres — and has now reached beaches at Ras Madrakah, threatening a marine reserve and closing on Masirah Island.

The spill is a reminder that Hormuz-adjacent risk isn't purely a function of who is shooting at whom. Sanctioned tankers, often uninsured to normal commercial standards and crewed to cut costs, have proliferated as buyers of Iranian and Russian crude route around Western restrictions; when one fails, there is no clean chain of liability or cleanup capacity behind it. Oman is managing the response largely on its own.

Washington's sanctions gambit, and its limits

The truce that had briefly restored shipping through the strait lapsed this week, and President Trump responded not with military escalation but with an economic one. In a Wednesday Truth Social post he promised the "MOST CRUSHING ECONOMIC OPERATION EVER TAKEN AGAINST ANY COUNTRY," warning of "TREMENDOUS Economic Consequences" for any nation, bank or airport that gives Iran a financial lifeline — what he called an "Economic D-Day." The administration says it has already choked Iranian tanker loadings to a fraction of February-to-April levels through a port blockade.

The trouble with an economic-warfare pivot is that it depends on cooperation Washington doesn't fully control. Iran, according to CNN's reporting, already has roughly 80 million barrels sitting on the water outside the blockade — most of it committed to Chinese buyers — which at current prices works out to about $1.5 billion a month in revenue, or roughly four months of export income at a discharge rate of 650,000 barrels a day. The blockade slows the money; it does not stop it.

China's answer: keep buying

That is because China has simply declined to participate. Beijing imports roughly 1.3 to 1.4 million barrels a day of Iranian crude — more than 80% of Iran's total exports — much of it through the "teapot" refineries that process discounted, sanctioned barrels into fuel and petrochemicals. In May, when Washington sanctioned five of those refiners, China's commerce ministry issued a blocking order instructing firms not to recognise or comply, and has since said publicly it will not bow to pressure to cut Russian or Iranian purchases, framing the trade as lawful commercial activity between sovereign states. Enforcement against Chinese banks and refiners that would actually bite — cutting them off from dollar clearing — has not materialised.

What the price is actually pricing

Taken together, the three threads describe the same structural problem from different angles: a chokepoint that can be harassed but not closed, a sanctions regime that can be threatened but not enforced against its largest evader, and a market that reprices instantly on headlines while the underlying barrels mostly keep moving, just more slowly and expensively insured. That combination — thin physical disruption, outsized price reaction — is what a risk premium looks like when nobody involved has the incentive or the leverage to end the standoff. Iran can't fully close the strait without cutting off its own remaining buyer; Washington can't fully sanction China without a fight it hasn't chosen to have; and Oman is left cleaning an oil spill that belongs to neither side's war.

The next catalysts are concrete rather than atmospheric: whether Trump's Treasury actually sanctions a Chinese bank rather than a refiner, whether VLCC transit counts recover from two-to-three a day, and whether Brent tests the $100 level it briefly cleared in July. None of those require a new attack — only the absence of the de-escalation everyone keeps promising and failing to deliver.
