Gaza ceasefire strains as Kushner reportedly restrains IDF strikes

Jared Kushner, the Trump adviser leading US mediation on Gaza, pictured in 2025. — Andrea Hanks / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Kushner asked Netanyahu to limit Gaza strikes to genuine "imminent threats," but a deadly strike followed within hours and the Board of Peace stayed silent.

The request was polite, according to officials briefed on it, and entirely unenforceable. Days ago, envoys for the US-backed Board of Peace reportedly asked Israel to confine its Gaza strikes to targets posing a genuine "imminent threat." On Monday, Jared Kushner delivered a version of that message in person, telling Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu during a four-hour meeting in Jerusalem that the exception should not be read broadly — it covers live emergencies, not a standing justification for hunting down anyone connected to the October 7 attacks.

Netanyahu did not accept the distinction. Officials familiar with the meeting described a genuine clash: the prime minister insists Israel will keep striking October 7 participants regardless of how the ceasefire's terms are drawn, and no agreement was reached. Kushner, notably, did not rule out such strikes altogether — he told Netanyahu he would not object to hits on people who present an authentic imminent threat, so long as Israel does not stretch that label to cover routine operations.

A test arrived almost immediately

Hours after Kushner left, the IDF carried out one of its deadliest strikes in Gaza in months, hitting what it described as a gathering of Hamas commanders. At least six people were killed, including a child, with more than ten wounded. Hamas called it an attempt to sabotage the ceasefire process outright.

The Board of Peace — the Trump-appointed body chaired in practice by Kushner, with Tony Blair and Nikolay Mladenov among its senior figures — said nothing publicly about the strike. That silence is not new. The board has consistently declined to object strongly to ceasefire violations when the IDF frames the target as an imminent threat, even as it has grown visibly uneasy about the mounting civilian toll of strikes carried out under that umbrella.

What the request actually tests

The episode is less a rupture than a first look at whether Washington's restraint request has any teeth. Kushner and Netanyahu did agree, in the same meeting, to set up a deconfliction mechanism meant to assess whether Israeli claims of emerging threats are genuine or could be resolved without force — a process, not yet a constraint, and one with no strikes yet run through it to test whether it changes outcomes on the ground.

That leaves the "imminent threat" standard doing a lot of work with very little definition. Israel has, in practice, applied the label to strikes the Board of Peace itself has questioned. Absent a public rebuke or a case in which Washington visibly stops a strike from happening, the request functions as guidance Israel can accept in principle and interpret however it chooses in the field.

The test to watch is not another meeting but another strike: whether the deconfliction mechanism actually intercepts a targeting decision, and whether the Board of Peace is willing to say so — publicly — if it doesn't.
