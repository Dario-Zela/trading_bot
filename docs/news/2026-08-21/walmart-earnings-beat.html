Walmart beats on earnings, raises guidance despite GLP-1 drug-price drag

A Walmart storefront. The retailer beat Q2 earnings estimates and raised guidance, but shares fell 9% on slowing US sales growth. — Walmart Corporate / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Walmart beat estimates and raised guidance, but a 2.6% US comp-sales slowdown driven by falling GLP-1 drug prices sent shares down 9%.

Walmart made more money last quarter than Wall Street expected, sold more goods than Wall Street expected, and told investors the rest of the year would be better than it had previously promised. Its stock fell nine percent anyway — the worst single-day drop for the retailer in years, and on its own responsible for roughly a tenth of that day's decline across the Dow.

The headline numbers, released Thursday, were unambiguous. Revenue for the quarter came in at $187.9 billion, up 5.9 percent from a year earlier and about $1.15 billion above forecasts. GAAP earnings per share landed at $0.80; on an adjusted basis, EPS rose 19 percent to $0.81, beating the $0.74 consensus by a wide margin. Walmart raised its full-year guidance, lifting expected net sales growth to a range of 4 to 5 percent from 3.5 to 4.5 percent, and nudging adjusted EPS guidance up to $2.80–$2.87.

Buried inside that beat-and-raise was the detail that mattered more to investors than the headline: comparable sales in the United States grew just 2.6 percent, the slowest pace in more than six years and a sharp deceleration from 4.1 percent last quarter and 4.6 percent a year ago. The single largest drag on that number wasn't tariffs, wasn't grocery inflation, and wasn't a weakening consumer. It was the price of weight-loss drugs.

The GLP-1 arithmetic turns negative

For two straight fiscal years, GLP-1 drugs — semaglutide and tirzepatide, sold as Ozempic, Wegovy and Zepbound — were a tailwind for Walmart's pharmacy business, adding roughly 100 basis points to company-wide comparable sales as prescription volumes climbed. That has now flipped. "In fiscal year '27, the benefit from GLP-1 is expected to be roughly half that amount, as script count growth is more than offset by price-mix headwinds," chief financial officer John David Rainey told analysts on the earnings call.

The mechanics are straightforward. Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk have both cut list and cash prices repeatedly over the past year — Wegovy's cash price fell 30 percent to $349 a month, and Lilly now sells discounted single-dose Zepbound vials for pickup at Walmart pharmacies through its direct-to-consumer LillyDirect program — as the manufacturers compete with compounding pharmacies and chase volume ahead of Medicare's second round of negotiated pricing, due to take effect for Ozempic and Wegovy in January 2027. More people are filling GLP-1 prescriptions at Walmart than before. Each one now brings in meaningfully less revenue. Health and wellness was the only major category at Walmart to post a comparable-sales decline in the quarter; every other category — grocery, general merchandise, apparel — grew.

Why a beat still got sold off

Rainey and chief executive Doug McMillon spent much of the call arguing that the underlying business is healthy: global e-commerce sales rose 23 percent, US e-commerce 24 percent, advertising revenue 38 percent, and membership income 17 percent. Operating income jumped 28.8 percent to $9.4 billion, helped by tariff refunds. Excluding health and wellness, core merchandise comparable sales rose 3.4 percent — a perfectly respectable number in isolation.

But investors weren't isolating it. They were watching a company that has beaten estimates in 15 of its last 16 quarters suddenly post its weakest US growth since before the pandemic recovery, at the same time management flagged plans to keep cutting prices to defend market share — a strategy that supports foot traffic but squeezes margin from the other direction. The market's read was less "pharmacy problem" than "deceleration, and the company's own answer to it involves giving up more margin." Walmart's move also came a day after Target posted its own beat-and-raise, EPS of $2.46 against a $2.25 estimate, suggesting the broader discretionary consumer isn't obviously buckling — which made Walmart's specific comp slowdown harder to wave away as a sector-wide story.

A structural problem, not a one-quarter one

What distinguishes the GLP-1 drag from ordinary quarterly noise is that it isn't going away. Kroger's chief executive, Rodney McMullen, has made a similar point about his own pharmacy business: GLP-1 "sales dollars are a lot bigger than the margin dollars," because branded weight-loss drugs carry thin reimbursement even at high volume. CVS Health, which runs both retail pharmacies and a pharmacy-benefit manager, has flagged "continued pharmacy reimbursement pressure" even as GLP-1 prescription volume climbs. Every large retailer with a pharmacy counter is running the same experiment: can rising script volume outrun falling per-script economics? For now, at Walmart's scale, the answer is close to a wash — a rounding error against $187.9 billion in quarterly revenue. For smaller regional chains and independent pharmacies without Walmart's grocery and e-commerce businesses to lean on, the same price compression lands with much less cushion underneath it.

The next data points arrive on two separate clocks. Medicare's negotiated prices for Ozempic and Wegovy take effect January 1, 2027, which will reset the price-mix math again — likely for the worse, from a pharmacy-margin standpoint, before it stabilizes. And Walmart itself reports again in November, by which point analysts will want to see whether the 2.6 percent US comp figure was a floor or a new trend line.
