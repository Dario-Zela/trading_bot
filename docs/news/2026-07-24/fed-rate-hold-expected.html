Fed widely expected to hold rates at July 28-29 meeting

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC meets July 28–29. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Markets price a near-certain Fed hold on July 29, but hike odds — not cut odds — are the real tension beneath the consensus.

The Federal Reserve is all but certain to leave interest rates unchanged when it meets July 28–29, and traders have already moved on to arguing about something else entirely.

On Polymarket, contracts tied to the meeting put the odds of no change at roughly three-in-four to four-in-five, with most of the remaining probability sitting on a quarter-point hike, not a cut. That is a striking place for a betting market to land less than three weeks after Fed Chair Kevin Warsh stood on a panel in Sintra, Portugal, and said flatly that "we've all looked around, and we've seen that prices are too high." A market pricing near-certain inaction from a central bank whose own chairman is complaining about inflation is not, on its face, an obvious bet — until the rest of the data comes into view.

June's consumer price index rose 3.5% year-over-year, down from 4.2% in May and below the 3.8% economists had forecast — the headline number actually fell 0.4% on the month. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, held at 2.6% annually, still well above the Fed's 2% goal but no longer accelerating. Meanwhile the June jobs report added a soft 57,000 payrolls with unemployment steady at 4.2%, retail sales ticked up 0.2% (the smallest gain in five months but still a fifth straight increase), and the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow tracker has bounced around all month before settling near 1.7% growth for the quarter. None of it screams overheating. None of it screams recession, either. That is precisely the combination that makes a hold the path of least resistance: growth solid enough that the Fed doesn't need to rescue anything, inflation cool enough that it doesn't need to slam the brakes.

A chairman who won't tip his hand

Warsh took the gavel in May, and his first meeting as chair in June produced a unanimous vote to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% — alongside a statement roughly a third the length of anything his predecessor issued, stripped of the forward guidance markets had grown used to parsing line by line. He has kept that posture since: inflation is "too high," but he has declined to say what the Fed will do about it next. Fed Governor Christopher Waller struck a similar note, saying it would take several months of favorable readings before he is convinced inflation is genuinely headed back to target. That is a committee signaling patience, not urgency — which is exactly what a hold requires and a cut does not.

The hike odds nobody's talking about

What makes the current pricing more interesting than a simple consensus is how recently it wasn't one. On the CME's FedWatch tool, the odds of a July hike touched 46.5% on July 13 — essentially a coin flip — before cooling to roughly 36% by the eve of the meeting as the softer CPI print landed. Polymarket's own hike contract still carries something like a one-in-four to one-in-five chance, far from negligible. The market isn't really betting on a placid, uncontested hold; it's betting that the Fed's inflation hawks lose an internal argument that was live just ten days ago. What almost nobody is pricing is a cut — Warsh has given no indication the committee sees room to ease while core inflation sits at 2.6%, and futures markets have all but zeroed out that outcome for July.

The decision lands at 2 p.m. Wednesday, followed by Warsh's press conference. The next real test of the market's confidence comes with the July jobs report on August 7 and the August CPI print roughly two weeks after that — either could reopen the hike debate the Fed just spent two weeks trying to close.
