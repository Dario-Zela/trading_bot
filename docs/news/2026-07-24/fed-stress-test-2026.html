All 32 large US banks pass 2026 Fed stress test

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the central bank's Board of Governors is headquartered. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
All 32 big US banks passed the Fed's 2026 stress test, but four GSIBs are now holding capital buffers right at the regulatory floor.

All 32 of the United States' largest banks cleared the Federal Reserve's 2026 stress test, the central bank said on June 24th, with every institution ending the hypothetical ordeal above its minimum capital requirement. Regulators framed it as a clean bill of health. The underlying numbers tell a more textured story — and the interesting part is not that the industry passed, but where the margins actually got tight.

The test imagined a severe global recession: unemployment surging to 10 percent, commercial real estate prices falling 39 percent, home prices down 30 percent, and gross domestic product contracting 4.6 percent. Layered on top, for the banks with large trading books, was a global market shock — a commodity-driven jolt of higher oil and gold prices and violent moves across currencies, rates and credit markets, the kind of stagflationary shock the Fed itself has flagged as a live risk for 2026 given where inflation currently sits.

Across the 32 banks, the Fed projected $708 billion in hypothetical losses — about $200 billion from credit cards, $160 billion from commercial and industrial loans, and $75 billion from commercial real estate. Aggregate common equity tier 1 capital fell from an actual 12.8 percent at the end of 2025 to a trough of 11.2 percent, before recovering to 12.7 percent by the end of the projection window. A 1.6 percentage-point peak-to-trough decline, comfortably above the 4.5 percent regulatory floor, and — notably — a smaller hit than 2025's 1.8-point drop, even though this year's scenario was tougher on paper: equity prices were assumed to fall 58 percent rather than 50, and credit spreads were widened by 4.7 percentage points instead of 3.9.

Where the cushion thinned

The aggregate figure flatters the picture. Stress capital buffers — the bank-specific add-on that determines how much capital each firm must hold above the bare minimum — compressed to the regulatory floor of 2.5 percent at Bank of America, JPMorgan Chase and Wells Fargo this cycle, with Wells Fargo posting the steepest slide, down from 3.70 percent a year earlier. Morgan Stanley's buffer fell to 2.5 percent from 3.0 percent. Goldman Sachs was the outlier, edging up to 2.9 percent from 2.7 percent. No bank came within two percentage points of breaching its minimum, so the passes were not narrow in the sense of nearly failing. But four of the country's most systemically important lenders are now running with essentially no spare capital buffer above what supervisors deem required — a thinner cushion than they carried a year ago, even as the headline scenario grew harsher.

That compression matters more in the current environment than it might have in a low-inflation year. Sticky consumer prices have kept the Fed on hold, and elevated-for-longer rates and commodity volatility are exactly the ingredients in the global market shock component applied to the trading giants. The core recession scenario itself is technically disinflationary — the Fed's model has CPI inflation falling from 2.5 percent to around 1.0 percent before drifting back up — but the stagflation-flavored market shock, and the real-world backdrop it is meant to approximate, is where the inflation story actually lives in this test.

The regulatory footnote

The Fed left capital requirements unchanged, as it had already signalled it would, pending a broader rework of the testing framework due to take effect in 2027. Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman called the results a sign of banking-system strength, while also flagging the Fed's own discomfort with the process: officials have proposed averaging stress-test results over two years and disclosing more about model changes, an acknowledgment that the exercise currently produces what the Fed itself has described as excessive year-to-year volatility in capital requirements.

Markets read the results as a green light. JPMorgan raised its quarterly dividend 10 percent to $1.65 a share and authorised a new $50 billion buyback programme; Goldman Sachs lifted its payout 11 percent to $5.00 a share; Morgan Stanley and Wells Fargo followed with increases of their own. None of that required regulatory permission beyond clearing the test, and none of it is unusual — banks nearly always pass, and shareholder payouts nearly always follow.

The next checkpoint is less theatrical but more informative: the Fed's rate decision on July 28–29, where policymakers are widely expected to hold, and the 2027 rollout of the revised stress-testing methodology, which will determine whether buffers this thin become the new baseline or a one-year anomaly.
