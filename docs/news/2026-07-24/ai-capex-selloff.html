Alphabet and Tesla lead tech selloff as AI spending bill comes due

Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai; the company raised its 2026 capex guidance to $195-205 billion, spooking investors despite a strong quarter. — Lukasz Kobus / European Union, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Alphabet's $205bn capex guidance and Tesla's first negative-cash-flow quarter in two years dragged the Nasdaq down 2.15%, as markets questioned AI spending's payoff.

The bill for the artificial intelligence boom landed on Wall Street's desk on Thursday, and investors did not like the number. Alphabet, the world's most profitable search engine, told shareholders it now expects to spend up to $205 billion this year building AI infrastructure — tens of billions more than it had guided just three months ago. On the same day, Tesla disclosed that it burned through cash rather than generating it, the first time that has happened in more than two years. The Nasdaq Composite fell 553 points, or 2.15%, to 25,137.69 — its worst single session in a month — as traders concluded that two of the market's biggest AI bets were starting to look like liabilities rather than assets.

The two companies have almost nothing in common operationally. One sells search ads and cloud computing; the other sells cars. But both are now defending the same proposition to investors: that spending enormous, rapidly escalating sums on AI compute and infrastructure will eventually pay for itself. Thursday was the day a meaningful slice of the market said it wasn't sure that was true anymore.

Alphabet's math gets harder to defend

Alphabet's underlying business, on paper, had a strong quarter. Revenue rose 24% year-over-year to $119.8 billion, operating income climbed 30% to $40.8 billion, and cloud revenue — the unit most directly tied to the AI build-out — surged 82% to $24.8 billion, with a backlog of signed contracts reaching $514 billion. Under most circumstances, that would be a stock-popping quarter.

Instead, Alphabet shares fell roughly 7% in the session, wiping out tens of billions in market value, because of a single line in the outlook: full-year capital expenditure guidance rose to a range of $195 billion to $205 billion, up from the $180-190 billion the company had signaled as recently as its previous quarter. At the midpoint, that's an increase of roughly $15 billion — spending that shows up on the balance sheet now, against returns the company says will materialize over a multi-year horizon.

Chief financial officer Anat Ashkenazi told analysts on the earnings call that the increase reflects "an acceleration in the delivery of capacity to meet growing demand," adding that Alphabet remains "in a supply-constrained environment" and is "seeing very strong demand both from external cloud customers as well as across the business." Pressed on how the company decides when enough is enough, she said: "Our goal is to invest as long as we see an attractive return on that investment. We do take a long-term view."

That answer is the crux of the market's discomfort. Alphabet spent $44.9 billion on capex in the second quarter alone — more than double what it spent a year earlier — and is telling investors the number will keep climbing before it can prove the return. For a company long prized for converting revenue into free cash flow with minimal drama, that is a meaningfully different investment case than the one shareholders bought into two years ago.

Tesla's cash flow turns negative

Tesla's quarter told a related but distinct story, and the market punished it more severely: shares dropped roughly 12-14% on the day, erasing more than $140 billion in market value in what was one of the steepest single-day losses in the company's history.

Tesla delivered record revenue of $28.24 billion, up on the back of 480,126 vehicle deliveries, a 25% increase from a year earlier. But profitability collapsed underneath that top line. Operating income fell 57% to $398 million, and operating margin dropped to 1.4% from 4.1% a year ago. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $0.33, well short of Wall Street's $0.50-0.53 estimate.

The figure that most alarmed investors was free cash flow, which swung to negative $1.1 billion — Tesla's first cash-flow deficit in over two years, and a sharp reversal from the $1.44 billion surplus it posted just one quarter earlier. The driver was capital expenditure, which jumped 142% year-over-year to $5.79 billion as the company poured money into AI infrastructure, data centers and other projects alongside its core auto and energy business. Tesla has guided to more than $25 billion in capex for all of 2026 — nearly triple the $8.5 billion it spent in 2025 — and management has said spending will keep rising for the next two to three years.

Unlike Alphabet, Tesla doesn't have a cloud backlog or an advertising business to point to as evidence the spending is already generating revenue. Its AI ambitions — autonomous driving, robotics, data-center-scale compute — remain mostly promises. Electrek summed up the market's mood bluntly, describing the reaction as a sign that Elon Musk's "just trust me" pitch to investors "doesn't hold anymore."

A sector-wide reassessment, not two isolated stock moves

What turned two earnings misses into a market event was the breadth of the reaction. The Nasdaq's 2.15% decline dragged down the S&P 500 by 1.21% and the Dow by 0.97%, with weakness spreading well beyond the two companies that reported. Oil prices, pushed higher by the widening conflict around Iran, added to the pressure, but traders and strategists pointed to the earnings as the proximate trigger for the tech-specific slide.

The read-through is straightforward: if Alphabet — with genuine cloud revenue growth and a swelling contract backlog — and Tesla — with record deliveries — both saw their stocks marked down for spending too much on AI infrastructure, then the market is applying that skepticism to the sector generally, not to company-specific missteps. Every hyperscaler and AI-adjacent manufacturer now faces the same question analysts were asking about Alphabet and Tesla: at what point does capex intensity stop being evidence of confidence and start being evidence of an arms race with no clear finish line.

That question has been building for months, as capex guidance from Microsoft, Amazon and Meta has ratcheted upward alongside Alphabet's. What changed Thursday is that the market, for the first time in this cycle, treated a large guidance increase as a negative rather than a sign of demand strength — even from a company, Alphabet, that beat estimates on every other line.

What happens next

The next test comes quickly. Microsoft, Meta and Amazon report earnings within days of each other in the coming weeks, and each will face the same question Ashkenazi fielded on Alphabet's call: how much more, and for how long, before the AI infrastructure spend converts into visible profit. If those companies raise capex guidance again without a matching acceleration in revenue tied directly to AI — cloud bookings, enterprise AI contracts, anything concrete — Thursday's selloff will look less like a one-day correction and more like the start of a broader repricing of what AI spending is actually worth. The Federal Reserve's July 28-29 meeting, where rates are widely expected to hold, adds a second variable: any signal on the path of borrowing costs will matter more to capital-intensive balance sheets than it did a year ago, when the AI trade still ran on enthusiasm rather than arithmetic.
