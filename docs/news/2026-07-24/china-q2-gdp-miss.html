China's Q2 growth slows to 4.3%, missing Beijing's target

Shanghai's Pudong skyline — export-driven growth has masked a deeper slump in domestic demand. — DvTor8303 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
China's GDP grew 4.3% in Q2, missing Beijing's 4.5-5% target as exports surged but household spending and property investment stayed stuck.

China's economy grew 4.3% in the second quarter from a year earlier, official data showed this week — the slowest pace since late 2022 and the first time growth has fallen below Beijing's own target band since the pandemic. The government had set a 4.5-5% goal for 2026. It missed the floor.

The headline number obscures a sharper story underneath. Exports, boosted by a rush of semiconductor and computer-parts shipments, rose 27% in June alone and 17.6% over the first half of the year. Factories kept humming. What didn't show up was spending at home — the household consumption Beijing has spent three years promising to make the engine of growth instead of the caboose.

"No domestic demand, all about exports — it's really quite unsustainable, to be frank," said Alicia Garcia-Herrero, chief economist for Asia Pacific at Natixis, summing up a divergence that has become the defining feature of China's post-stimulus economy.

A quarter of two economies

The first quarter of 2026 had looked, briefly, like vindication for Beijing's approach: GDP grew 5%, inside target, front-loaded by early stimulus disbursements and a burst of trade-in subsidies for autos and appliances. That momentum didn't survive into summer. First-half growth came in at 4.7% — still technically within the annual band, but only because Q1 did the heavy lifting. The second quarter's 4.3% undershot even the 4.48% consensus forecast among economists surveyed ahead of the release.

Retail sales, the most direct gauge of household appetite, told the story in miniature: they fell in May for the first time in more than three years, then limped back to 1% growth in June — nowhere near the pace needed to offset the drag elsewhere. Fixed-asset investment shrank 5.7% over the first half. Property investment, the biggest single component of that decline, plunged 18%, extending a rout that has now pushed new-home prices down for 20 consecutive months and wiped out roughly 90% of the market value of listed property developers since their 2021 peak. Evergrande's liquidators are still fighting creditors in Hong Kong courts; Country Garden, Sunac and Kaisa remain mid-restructuring; even Vanke, long treated as the safe, state-linked developer, needed exceptional support to stay solvent through 2025-26.

Stimulus that isn't landing

Beijing has not been idle. Trade-in subsidies, targeted consumption vouchers and a deficit set at roughly 4% of GDP for 2026 — paired with heavy bond issuance — were all meant to cushion the property downturn and coax households to spend. The Q2 numbers suggest the cushioning has been partial at best: growth this quarter was, in the words of one Beijing-based analyst, "very much powered by manufacturing" rather than the consumption-led rebalancing officials have targeted since at least 2023.

Part of the shortfall is external. Oil and other resource prices spiked as the war involving Iran widened over the quarter, squeezing input costs for Chinese manufacturers even as it did nothing to help consumers already nursing weak confidence over jobs and housing wealth. But the deeper issue predates Iran: a property slump now well into its fifth year has hammered the household balance sheet channel — most Chinese family wealth is tied up in real estate — leaving consumers reluctant to spend even where subsidies exist.

The timing puts pressure on the Politburo's late-July meeting, the closely watched venue where Beijing typically signals its policy stance for the second half. Expectations have shifted toward firmer easing language and faster fiscal disbursement rather than a large new stimulus package. Notably, several analysts now expect the leadership to keep tilting state resources toward AI, quantum computing and advanced manufacturing — treating consumption as a byproduct of industrial upgrading and employment rather than a lever to be pulled directly. That would extend, rather than resolve, the imbalance the Q2 data just exposed.

Why the miss travels

A China growing below target reverberates well beyond its own statistics agency. Weaker Chinese demand for commodities has already been a drag on prices for iron ore, copper and other industrial inputs this year, a dynamic now complicated by the Iran-driven spike in oil. Multinational exporters and commodity producers calibrate earnings guidance around Chinese demand assumptions; a persistent shortfall forces those numbers down. And a China that leans harder on exports to hit its growth target — rather than fixing the domestic side — invites more of the trade friction already visible in Washington's tariff actions and Brussels' scrutiny of Chinese industrial policy.

The more immediate test comes at the Politburo meeting itself, where markets will be parsing language for any hint of consumption-specific stimulus rather than the industrial-policy tilt analysts currently expect. Barring a policy surprise, the next real data point is Q3 GDP, due in mid-October — the quarter that will show whether faster fiscal spending in the second half actually reaches households, or whether China's growth keeps riding on a single, narrowing export lane.
