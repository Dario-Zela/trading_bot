Fed launches five task forces to review its own conduct of policy

Kevin Warsh at his swearing-in ceremony as Federal Reserve Chair, White House, May 22, 2026 — Daniel Torok / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
The Fed under new Chair Kevin Warsh launched five outside-led task forces to review its policy tools, with no stated catalyst and findings not due until fall.

The Federal Reserve has set up five task forces to examine how it makes monetary policy, the clearest sign yet that Chair Kevin Warsh intends to remake the institution he spent years criticizing from the outside.

The panels, announced July 9, will each look at one piece of the Fed's machinery: communications, balance sheet policy, the quality of the economic data the Fed relies on, productivity and jobs in an era of AI-driven change, and the framework the Fed uses to think about inflation. Each is co-led by outside experts rather than Fed staff — a structural choice that put former Bank of England governor Mervyn King, Nobel laureate Thomas Sargent, former Fed governor Jeremy Stein, and venture capitalist Marc Andreessen on the same masthead as career central bankers like Raghuram Rajan and Arminio Fraga. Harvard economist Raj Chetty and former Walmart chief executive Doug McMillon will co-lead the data task force; Microsoft Xbox chief Asha Sharma joins Andreessen and Stanford's Charles Jones on productivity and jobs.

Warsh's own explanation was thin on specifics: the U.S. economy "has changed significantly over the last generation," he said, and the task forces will assess whether the Fed's tools and methods have kept pace. The Fed has not said what specific episode or failure prompted the review, nor whether any of the five areas is expected to yield binding changes to how the Federal Open Market Committee sets rates. Findings are due in the fall, with conclusions expected by year-end.

A chair with a prior verdict

The timing matters. Warsh was confirmed in a 54-45 Senate vote in May — the narrowest margin for a Fed chair on record — after years of public criticism of the very framework he now oversees, including the Fed's 2020 shift to average inflation targeting and the scale of its post-2008 and post-2020 balance sheet expansion. That history has shaped how the review is being read on Capitol Hill. Rep. Nydia Velázquez called it an effort to "offload Fed objectives to clandestine task forces with no clear transparency," and Senate Democrats used Warsh's mid-July testimony to press him on his ties to President Trump rather than the review's substance. Some economists, by contrast, credited Warsh for stacking the panels with genuine expertise — including Obama-era appointees and foreign central bankers — at a moment when other federal agencies have been hollowed out.

What the review will not settle, at least not this week, is the more immediate question hanging over Fed watchers: rates. The FOMC meets July 28-29 and is widely expected to hold steady. The task forces' findings, when they arrive, will say more about what a Warsh-era Fed does differently in 2027 than about anything the committee decides next week — but investors parsing his public statements for a preview of the direction of travel will have five separate documents to read once the fall reports land.
