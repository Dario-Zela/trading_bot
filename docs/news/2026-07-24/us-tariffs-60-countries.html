US reimposes tariffs on 60 countries as forced-labour exemptions lapse

Container stacks at the Port of Rotterdam — a symbol of the global trade flows now facing renewed US duties. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Washington resurrected a broad tariff wall, hitting dozens of trading partners with 10-12.5% duties as temporary levies expired on sturdier legal footing.

At one minute past midnight on Friday, Washington's tariff wall — torn down by the Supreme Court in February, patched back up with a stopgap in March, and now rebuilt on new foundations — went back up. Duties of 10% to 12.5% took effect on imports from 60 trading partners, covering by the administration's own count 99.4% of everything the United States buys from abroad. The justification this time is not a national emergency or a trade deficit. It is forced labour.

The timing was not incidental. The new duties, issued under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, landed at the exact moment a separate 10% global levy — imposed under Section 122's balance-of-payments authority after the Court struck down the president's emergency tariff powers in February — expired at its statutory 150-day limit. One tariff regime died at 12:00am; another was born at 12:01.

From emergency powers to labour law

The pivot matters more than the rate. Section 122 was always a blunt, time-limited instrument — Congress capped it at 150 days precisely to stop a president from running an indefinite tariff regime on emergency authority alone. Section 301 carries no such clock. It requires a formal investigation into a specific unfair trade practice, which the Office of the US Trade Representative duly opened in March and delivered findings on in June: that 60 economies had failed to "impose and effectively enforce" a ban on goods made with forced labour, something Ambassador Jamieson Greer's office says the United States has done, in some form, for nearly a century.

The rate a country pays reflects that finding. Trading partners judged to have adopted at least the framework of a forced-labour import ban — Mexico, the UK, Canada, India, and, at minimum, the European Union and Taiwan — face 10%. Those found to have no such prohibition at all, including China, Vietnam, Japan, Switzerland, South Korea and Brazil, face 12.5%. A senior administration official called it "the most sweeping international labour rights action the United States has ever taken, that any country has ever taken." Oil, gas, fertiliser, autos and metals already under separate Section 232 duties, and goods compliant with the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement, are carved out.

A rationale few trading partners accept

Governments from Canberra to Brasília rejected the premise within hours. Brazil, hit with the 12.5% rate, called the tariffs "arbitrary" and "unjustified"; President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva said Brazil would look elsewhere for buyers rather than negotiate under the forced-labour label. Most partners, notably, stopped short of retaliation and signalled they would keep talking — a sign that after a year and a half of tariff whiplash, capitals have concluded that absorbing the duty is cheaper than a trade war.

Equity markets registered the shift alongside a separate selloff in AI-linked megacaps: the S&P 500 fell 1.2% Thursday, its worst session in a month, with the Nasdaq 100 down 1.9%. Untangling how much of that belongs to tariffs versus tech-spending jitters is difficult on a single day, but the direction of travel for import-exposed sectors — retail, apparel, electronics assembly — was unambiguous.

Built to last, or built to lose in court

The durability question is the one that matters for anyone pricing 2026 and 2027 margins. The Peterson Institute for International Economics argues the Section 301 foundation is scarcely more solid than the emergency powers the Supreme Court just rejected: the statute requires a showing that a practice "burdens" US commerce, an odd claim to sustain against economies representing 90% of global trade, and its history is built around country-specific findings, not a simultaneous levy on five dozen partners at once. If a court agrees, Washington would be searching for its fourth legal hook in under two years.

But the pattern itself is the story. Two legal foundations have now given way — IEEPA emergency powers in February, the Section 122 balance-of-payments clock this week — and each time the administration has simply reached for the next available statute rather than let the tariff wall come down. For markets trying to decide whether 10-12.5% import duties are a durable feature of the operating environment or a cyclical irritant to be timed and traded around, that persistence is the more informative data point than any single court's verdict on Section 301's technical merits.

The next test arrives quickly: importers of record have until July 28 to clear goods already in transit under the old rates, and legal challenges to the new duties are expected within weeks. South Korea, notably, faces the prospect of an additional levy pending a separate USTR review — a reminder that even a 60-country list drawn up in a single week is, in this administration, treated as a floor rather than a ceiling.
