Iran war widens: oil tops $100, Houthis threaten Bab al-Mandeb, Trump vows to bill Tehran

The Bab al-Mandeb strait, the Red Sea chokepoint now at the centre of Houthi blockade threats. — NASA/METI/AIST/Japan Space Systems, and U.S./Japan ASTER Science Team — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brent crude jumped 7% to $100.69 as Houthi attacks near Bab al-Mandeb, Trump's asset-seizure threat and UK war jitters widened the Iran conflict's economic front.

Oil markets have not seen a day like Thursday since the spring. Brent crude jumped 7% to settle at $100.69 a barrel — touching $102 intraday — its highest level since May, as a widening war between the United States and Iran opened a second front against global energy supply. The trigger was not Iran's own coastline but a stretch of water 1,300 miles away: the Bab al-Mandeb strait, the narrow gateway between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden through which 12 to 15% of the world's maritime trade, worth more than $1 trillion a year, normally passes.

A second chokepoint opens

Until this week, the market's chief worry was the Strait of Hormuz, where Iran's forces have kept shipping on edge through repeated near-closures. Now Yemen's Houthi movement, Iran's most active regional proxy, has given traders a second one to price in. Earlier in the week the Houthis announced a blockade of Saudi-linked shipping through Bab al-Mandeb, retaliation, they said, for a Saudi blockade of Yemen and an attack on Sanaa's airport. On Thursday they made good on the threat, striking two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, setting both alight. Houthi military spokesman Yahya Saree said the vessels were targeted "for their violation of the blockade decision issued by the armed forces." No casualties were reported, but the message landed: a group that spent much of the past two years disrupting Red Sea traffic on and off is now doing so explicitly in Iran's war, not as a sideshow to it. The strikes came as the US carried out its twelfth consecutive night of bombing raids against Iranian targets, underscoring how far the conflict has spread beyond its original front.

Washington's ledger, and London's watch

The financial front has widened too. Donald Trump said on Truth Social that the US would now draw on frozen Iranian assets to cover shipping losses, writing that "any and all damages done to Ships, Cargo, or anything related thereto, will be paid for by Iranian Money that the United States has in its possession, and controls." The declaration folds roughly $24 billion in restricted Iranian funds — money that a now-collapsed ceasefire framework would have released back to Tehran — into a de facto insurance pool for Gulf shipping, without specifying a claims process or seeking congressional sign-off. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, called the move an "incendiary precedent," warning that seizing one nation's assets to cover unrelated future claims sets a template other states will not welcome either.

The spillover has reached Britain, too. After Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps warned that "any base used for aggression against Iranian territory constitutes a legitimate target," naming RAF Fairford as a launch point for US bombing runs, a UK government spokesperson said the country's armed forces stood "ready to keep the United Kingdom safe from any kind of attacks, whether it's on our soil or from abroad." The warning followed Prime Minister Andy Burnham's recommitment to the long-standing arrangement letting the US fly missions from British bases — a routine defence posture that Iran's escalation has turned into a live threat.

The thread that ties it together

Taken separately, a tanker fire, a Truth Social post and a Whitehall statement look like three unrelated news items. Together they describe one mechanism: a war that began as a bilateral US-Iran confrontation is now pricing itself into oil, shipping and sovereign balance sheets simultaneously, through actors — the Houthis, the Treasury, the IRGC — that Washington does not fully control and cannot easily deter. Markets are treating Bab al-Mandeb the way they once treated Hormuz alone: as a chokepoint whose closure is no longer hypothetical. Whether it stays a threat or becomes a sustained blockade is now the single biggest swing factor for inflation expectations heading into next week's Federal Reserve meeting on July 28-29, where policymakers already face a growth slowdown without a fresh oil shock to complicate it.
