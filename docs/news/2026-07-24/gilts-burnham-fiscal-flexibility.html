Gilts sell off to G7-high yields as Burnham signals looser fiscal stance

The Bank of England building in London, whose Monetary Policy Committee meets 30 July as gilt volatility complicates the rate-cut debate. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields hit a G7-high 5.04% and mortgage rates rose after new PM Andy Burnham signalled looser fiscal policy on his first day in office.

The bond market did not wait for a honeymoon. Hours after Andy Burnham was confirmed as Britain's seventh prime minister in a decade, benchmark 10-year gilt yields jumped eight basis points to 5.04%, the highest among G7 economies, while the 30-year yield climbed to 5.75%, its highest level in two months. The trigger was a single line to reporters outside Downing Street: Burnham said his government would "make full use of any flexibility" within the UK's existing fiscal rules.

That was enough. Investors read "flexibility" as shorthand for more borrowing, and the sell-off in gilts — UK government bonds — was immediate and broad-based, dragging the pound lower alongside it.

A one-line signal, an immediate repricing

Burnham was sworn in on 20 July, replacing his predecessor after Labour's latest leadership churn, and named John Healey — a 66-year-old veteran who served as a junior Treasury minister between 2002 and 2007 — as chancellor, taking over from Rachel Reeves. Healey inherits an economy where the bond market has already made its expectations clear before he has set a single policy.

Traders' logic is straightforward. The UK's fiscal rules cap the pace at which government debt can grow relative to the economy; "flexibility" within them still means more gilt issuance to fund it. More supply, all else equal, means lower prices and higher yields — and with UK debt already north of 95% of GDP, investors are pricing in less patience for slippage than they might have shown a decade ago. By Thursday, 10-year yields had touched 5.09%, their highest since 20 May, with the 30-year at 5.78%, a level last seen in mid-May.

Ed Yardeni, the Wall Street economist who coined the term "bond vigilantes" in 1983, framed the dynamic bluntly before Burnham had even taken office: investors know it is the bond market, not the prime minister's office, that calls the shots in a £2.9 trillion economy. JPMorgan chief executive Jamie Dimon weighed in too, praising Reeves's fiscal discipline and telling the incoming chancellor that growth, not spending signals, is what markets want to see.

Who pays for the flexibility

Higher gilt yields are not an abstract market indicator — they set the price of the government's own borrowing and ripple directly into household finances through swap rates, the wholesale pricing benchmark lenders use to set fixed mortgage deals. Within days of the yield spike, five major UK lenders — NatWest, Nationwide, Virgin Money, Barclays and Coventry Building Society — raised their fixed mortgage rates, ending a fortnight of gradual cuts. Others, including Skipton, Lloyds, Halifax and Leeds Building Society, repriced upward too, with increases of up to 0.35 percentage points on some products. Average two-year fixed rates now sit around 5.57%, five-year fixes around 5.60%, both a one-month high.

The mechanism is the one that has defined UK mortgage pricing since the 2022 gilt crisis under Liz Truss: two-year swap rates, which had dipped below 4% earlier in July and allowed lenders to cut, jumped back to around 4.18% as the political risk premium returned, with five-year swaps near 4.26%. For a borrower coming off a fixed deal this autumn, the timing is unwelcome — refinancing now costs more, just as the market had been pricing in relief.

That leaves the Bank of England navigating from an awkward angle. Bank rate has held at 3.75% since June, and the Monetary Policy Committee's next decision lands on 30 July — six days from now. Markets had been leaning toward the case for a cut building through the summer; a fresh bout of gilt volatility driven by fiscal politics, rather than inflation data, complicates that calculus without giving the MPC any new tool to address it directly. The Bank sets the short end of the curve; the long end, where mortgage pricing and government borrowing costs actually live, is currently being set by traders reacting to a prime minister's press-conference phrasing.

What it means going forward

The episode is a reminder, delivered within hours of Burnham entering office, that Britain's fiscal room to manoeuvre is now priced in real time. Every signal toward looser spending will be tested immediately against a gilt market already charging the UK the highest borrowing costs in the G7, and every basis point of that repricing eventually shows up in a mortgage offer. Healey's first substantive test will not be a Budget — it will be whether his early statements can calm the market Burnham's did not. The next checkpoints: the MPC decision on 30 July, and whatever fiscal detail the new chancellor puts on paper first, which the bond market will treat as the real policy statement.
