DOJ drops subpoenas against NYT reporters in leak probe

The New York Times Building in Manhattan, where the paper's lawyers fought the subpoenas as an assault on press freedom. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The DOJ withdrew subpoenas against New York Times reporters after a judge said the requests broke the department's own last-resort rules for compelling journalists' testimony.

The Justice Department has backed away from one of the more aggressive press subpoenas in recent memory, telling a federal judge Thursday it would withdraw grand jury subpoenas against New York Times reporters over their coverage of security gaps in President Trump's new, Qatari-donated Air Force One.

The retreat came at the end of a hearing in the Southern District of New York, where U.S. District Judge Arun Subramanian had made plain that the subpoenas would either be withdrawn voluntarily or quashed by the court. Facing that choice, a DOJ lawyer, Sean Buckley, conceded the department had made errors. "That was an error, judge, which we own," he told Subramanian. "It was a consequence of trying to move quickly." The judge was unmoved by the framing: subpoenas to journalists, he reminded the room, citing the department's own regulations, "are the last step, not the first step, but the last step."

The case began in early July, after the Times published reporting — bylined by Julian E. Barnes, Eric Lipton, Tyler Pager and Eric Schmitt — on why Trump used the old Air Force One rather than the new Boeing 747-8 gifted by Qatar to leave a NATO summit in Turkey, and on the newer jet's thinner security features, including its lack of the anti-missile systems fitted to the aircraft it is meant to replace. Days later, federal agents showed up at reporters' homes to serve subpoenas demanding grand jury testimony about who had leaked the details. The requests went further than the bylines: prosecutors also sought phone records for two reporters' spouses and for one reporter's mother, a mental-health professional with confidentiality obligations to her own clients, and asked for records stretching back to January 1 — months before the articles that supposedly triggered the investigation.

Last resort, not first

That mismatch between the leak under investigation and the sweep of the subpoenas became the case's central problem. Longstanding Justice Department policy treats compelling journalists' testimony or records as a measure of last resort, used only once other investigative avenues are exhausted — a norm meant to keep leak inquiries from turning into fishing expeditions through newsrooms. Bruce D. Brown, president of the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, said the subpoenas "break from longstanding Justice Department practice to protect the public interest and press independence," calling the home visits from federal agents "an extraordinary assault on the freedom of the press." David McCraw, the Times' deputy general counsel, was similarly blunt in court filings, saying the sight of law enforcement on reporters' doorsteps "should shock the conscience of any American who believes in the Constitution."

With the subpoenas now void, McCraw struck a more measured note: "Today's proceeding was an important affirmation of our country's commitment to a free press." The department, for its part, is not conceding the underlying fight — a spokesperson said the investigation "remains ongoing" and that it would continue to "pursue justice against those threatening national security by leaking classified information," while also complaining that Subramanian had blocked prosecutors from laying out their case.

That tension is the story to watch. Nothing stops the department from opening a narrower, better-documented subpoena the next time a leak investigation reaches a newsroom's door — the underlying authority is untouched. What changed is the cost of getting it wrong: a judge willing to call the tactic out in open court, and a Justice Department that, faced with sanctions, chose retreat over a fight it might have lost on the merits.
