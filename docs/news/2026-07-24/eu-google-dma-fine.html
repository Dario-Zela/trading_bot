EU hits Google with record €890m fine in first major DMA strike

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which issued the record DMA fine against Google. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brussels fined Google €890m — its first major Digital Markets Act penalty — for search self-preferencing and Play Store restrictions, risking US tariff retaliation.

Brussels has spent two years insisting the Digital Markets Act had teeth. On Thursday it used them. The European Commission fined Google €890 million — about $1 billion — for breaching the bloc's flagship tech-competition law, the first major penalty issued under a regime that until now had produced mostly warnings, workshops and specification documents.

The fine splits into two decisions. Brussels found Google favoured its own services — Shopping, Flights, Hotels, local transport results — over rivals' in Google Search, in breach of the DMA's self-preferencing ban, and fined the company €460 million for it. A second decision, carrying a €430 million penalty, addressed the Play Store: regulators concluded Google kept blocking app developers from telling users about cheaper offers available outside Google's payment system and from directing them to alternative stores, despite a legal obligation to allow exactly that kind of steering.

From statute book to enforcement

The DMA took effect in 2024 as the EU's answer to a familiar complaint: that ordinary antitrust cases move too slowly to constrain platforms that can out-litigate regulators for a decade while the underlying market tips irreversibly in their favour. Instead of proving harm case by case, the DMA pre-designates "gatekeeper" firms — Google, Apple, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, ByteDance — and imposes a fixed list of dos and don'ts: no self-preferencing, no blocking of third-party stores, no using rivals' data to compete against them.

Critics, including gatekeepers themselves, argued the law was more theatre than threat until it produced an actual bill. Thursday's decision answers that. Teresa Ribera, the Commission's executive vice-president for competition, put it plainly: "Google has fallen short of effective compliance with the Digital Markets Act, and today we have taken decisive yet balanced enforcement action sanctioning these breaches." Google now has 60 days to bring both products into compliance or face daily penalties that can reach 5% of Alphabet's global turnover — a figure that dwarfs the fine itself and turns this from a one-off cost into an open-ended liability if Google drags its feet.

Google says it will appeal. Kent Walker, Alphabet's president of global affairs, argued the ruling forces the company to "strip away real-time search features Europeans love — like instant pricing and direct availability for hotels, flights, and restaurants — and dismantle safety protections on Google Play," framing compliance itself as the thing that degrades the product. That argument has surfaced before, in Google's pushback against the EU's 2017 Shopping case and the UK's ongoing search remedies process, and regulators have consistently rejected it as a description of lost competitive advantage dressed up as a user-experience concern.

The trade wire gets pulled taut

What makes this fine different from Brussels's earlier tech cases — the €4.1 billion Android decision, the €2.95 billion adtech fine — is the political moment it lands in. Washington has treated DMA enforcement against American firms as a trade issue since the law's early drafting, and this decision arrived with the US Trade Representative's office already primed to say so. USTR Jamieson Greer said the fine "poses a real risk to the continuation of transatlantic stability with respect to trade," and a group of 25 Republican members of Congress wrote to President Trump urging retaliation, naming Nokia, Axel Springer, Volkswagen, BMW, Ikea and Airbus as possible targets for countermeasures.

That list matters more than the rhetoric. It signals that any US response would not be confined to digital services — it would reach into European industrials and autos, sectors already exposed after Washington's broader tariff actions this year. A Commission official maintained the fine was "nondiscriminatory" and "based on solid evidence," the standard line Brussels uses to insist DMA enforcement is not a disguised tax on American tech. Whether Washington accepts that framing is now a live question sitting alongside the tariff disputes already running between the two blocs.

What the fine buys, and what it costs

For Alphabet, €890 million is not a balance-sheet event — the company generates that in roughly two days of revenue. The more consequential number is the 60-day compliance clock and the 5%-of-turnover ceiling behind it, which is the mechanism that actually disciplines behaviour going forward rather than just taxing the past. For the rest of the gatekeeper list, the message is that the Commission is now willing to fine first and litigate the appeal later, rather than treating designation as the end of the process.

For Brussels, the case is a proof of concept it needed. A law with no enforced penalties invites exactly the compliance-in-name-only response gatekeepers have been accused of since 2024. A law that produces a billion-dollar fine, a hard deadline and an escalating penalty structure is a different kind of signal to every other firm on the gatekeeper list, and to the US administration weighing how hard to push back.

The next marker is Google's 60-day compliance deadline, which lands in late September and will show whether the company redesigns search results and Play Store steering or heads straight to the EU courts while paying accruing penalties. Alongside it, watch whether Washington converts its warnings into an actual trade measure — the Congressional letter's target list of European exporters gives an early guide to where that would land if it comes.
