Trump ties Saudi nuclear deal to Israel recognition

Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, whose government insists normalization with Israel still requires a pathway to Palestinian statehood. — Saudi Press Agency / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A day after signing a landmark US-Saudi nuclear deal, Trump said it's "totally subject" to Riyadh joining the Abraham Accords — a condition Saudi Arabia says was never part of what it signed.

Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman shook hands in Riyadh on Wednesday over a signed agreement: a civil nuclear cooperation pact, the kind of long-gestating infrastructure deal that typically takes years to negotiate and decades to pay off. Washington would back a Saudi civilian nuclear power program, giving American firms priority access to supply reactors and fuel for a partnership expected to be worth billions of dollars over its lifetime. Saudi Arabia would not be permitted to enrich uranium on its own soil — a red line the US has held since talks began.

By Thursday, President Trump had added a condition nobody in Riyadh remembers agreeing to.

"Will be approved, but is totally subject to Saudi Arabia joining the very respected and successful Abraham Accords," Trump wrote on Truth Social, tying the nuclear pact to Saudi normalization of relations with Israel. White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt confirmed the stakes bluntly: without accession to the Accords, in her words, the nuclear "deal is off."

A day of daylight between the signature and the price tag

The sequencing matters. Had the condition been part of the original announcement, it would have been unremarkable — a known Trump preference for bundling deals, disclosed up front. Instead, the administration presented the nuclear pact on Wednesday as a freestanding energy and technology agreement, the kind of deliverable that gets read as evidence Washington and Riyadh are deepening ties independent of the Israel question. Then, less than 24 hours later, Trump reframed it as leverage.

A Saudi official's response, relayed to reporters, was terse: "It was signed. So nothing to renegotiate. Tweets don't overturn signed agreements." That is a defensible legal position — the text goes to Congress for review regardless, and a social media post does not amend a signed intergovernmental accord. But it also captures the credibility problem the reversal creates. If a headline deal can be re-priced by presidential post the morning after signing, every other agreement bearing Trump's signature invites the same question: is this final, or provisional pending whatever gets attached to it next.

Why Riyadh isn't expected to fold

Saudi Arabia's position on Israel has been consistent for years and hasn't moved: Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has said repeatedly that normalization requires a credible pathway to Palestinian statehood, not just a ceremony. That is politically load-bearing for Riyadh in a way it wasn't for the UAE, Bahrain or Morocco, which signed the original Abraham Accords in 2020 without extracting comparable commitments on the Palestinian question. Saudi Arabia, as custodian of Islam's two holiest sites and the Arab world's largest economy, has more domestic and pan-Arab political exposure than any of those three — and less to gain from moving fast.

The optics have also shifted since October 2023. The kingdom has spent the past two years positioning itself as a steady hand in a region racked by the Gaza war's aftermath and now the widening confrontation with Iran — not as a supplicant likely to fold under a nuclear-deal deadline. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, for his part, welcomed Trump's framing without hesitation, calling Saudi accession to the Accords a potential "historic leap forward for peace in the Middle East. Peace through Strength." That eagerness on Israel's side only sharpens the contrast with Riyadh's silence — MBS has not been reported in contact with the White House since the condition was announced.

What's actually at stake beyond the headline

The nuclear agreement itself is a genuine strategic prize for Washington, not a minor sweetener. A so-called "123 agreement" under the US Atomic Energy Act, paired with a bilateral safeguards accord, locks American firms in as the preferred suppliers for a nuclear buildout that Saudi Arabia has signaled it will pursue with or without the United States — China and Russia have both courted Riyadh's civilian nuclear business in recent years, without insisting on the enrichment ban Washington has secured. Losing the deal to a Saudi walkout wouldn't just cost American contractors billions in reactor and fuel contracts; it would hand Beijing or Moscow a foothold in a program with obvious dual-use sensitivities in a region where Iran's own nuclear ambitions remain the reason enrichment limits exist at all.

That is the tension Trump has now introduced into his own leverage play. The Abraham Accords condition assumes Saudi Arabia wants the nuclear deal badly enough to move on Israel. But if Riyadh calculates that Washington wants the nuclear deal — and the strategic foothold that comes with it — just as badly, the condition could backfire, pushing Saudi Arabia toward alternative suppliers rather than toward Jerusalem. Congress, which must still review the agreement's text once released, adds a further layer of uncertainty: lawmakers uneasy about proliferation risk in the region could complicate ratification independent of anything Trump says about Israel.

What to watch

The agreement's full text has not yet been made public and is due to be transmitted to Congress for review — that filing, whenever it lands, will show whether the enrichment prohibition and any Israel-related language are actually written into the deal or remain confined to Trump's public statements. Riyadh's near-term response, or lack of one, will signal whether the kingdom treats the ultimatum as a real deadline or something to wait out. And with Gulf diplomacy already strained by the Iran war's expansion, any Saudi move — toward Washington's terms or away from them — will be read across the region as a signal of where Gulf states are willing to plant their flags next.
