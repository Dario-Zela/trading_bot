Burnham unveils 20% business rates cut for English pubs

Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street as prime minister, days before unveiling his first economic policy. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0 / OGL)
In one sentence
Burnham's first policy as PM cuts business rates 20% for English pubs, clubs and music venues — but excludes restaurants and hotels, and critics call it too small.

Andy Burnham's first act of economic policy as prime minister was not a budget, a bond announcement or a growth strategy. It was a pint.

On Thursday, less than a week after entering Downing Street, Burnham confirmed a 20% cut to business rates for pubs, clubs and live music venues in England, starting next April. The government says it will benefit almost 32,000 venues and save the typical pub an estimated £1,100 over the next financial year — small beer, in cash terms, against the scale of the problems the sector says it's facing.

The £100m annual cost is being funded, Downing Street says, by trimming business rate relief currently enjoyed by vape shops and by a crackdown on online marketplace sellers who dodge VAT — a pairing that reads less like fiscal strategy than like a press release designed to avoid the words "who pays for this." Pubs and live music venues were already due a 15% discount for the 2026/27 billing year under a scheme inherited from the last government, with a real-terms freeze pencilled in for the two years after. Burnham's cut builds on top of that rather than starting from scratch.

A narrow round

The politics of the announcement were tidier than the economics. A trip to a pub makes for better pictures on day one than a spreadsheet on public borrowing, and hospitality — battered by energy costs, wage rises and a slow bleed of closures — is an easy sector to be seen backing. But the relief pointedly excludes restaurants and hotels, and that omission has produced the loudest reaction of the week.

UK Hospitality, the trade body covering the wider sector, warned that restaurants and hotels face many of the same cost pressures and called for the support to be widened. The boss of the Leon restaurant chain went further, arguing the policy "creates a divide" by helping pubs while leaving comparable businesses to face higher bills regardless. Chris Tulloch, managing director of the pub operator Blind Tiger Inns, was blunter still about what pubs themselves are getting: not "a rescue package nor a lifeline," he said, more a case of "we were going to shoot you and now we're not... it's not making things better, it just means things won't go quite as bad quite as quickly." Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch's response was shorter — "Is that it?"

What it signals

The irony is that Burnham has previously backed the industry's own preferred fix: cutting VAT on hospitality to 10%, a change trade groups argue would do more for margins than rates relief ever could. Thursday's announcement stopped well short of that, which is the real tell. A rates cut is cheap, popular and reversible; a VAT cut is none of those things, and this government is already fielding questions from bond investors about how loose its fiscal hand is going to be. For a market watching Burnham's fiscal instincts more broadly, the pub cut reads as a signal in itself — generous with headlines, careful with the Treasury's numbers. The next test comes when the wider spending review lands and hospitality finds out whether Thursday was a down payment or the whole tab.
