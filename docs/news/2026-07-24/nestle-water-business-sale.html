Nestlé explores water-business sale amid European carve-out wave

Perrier is one of more than 30 brands moving into the new Nestlé-Platinum Equity water venture, Peranel. — Donmike10 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Nestlé and Platinum Equity confirmed a €4.9bn joint venture for Perrier, S.Pellegrino and Pure Life, the latest of Europe's conglomerate carve-outs.

Nestlé confirmed on July 23rd what dealmakers had been signalling since January: it is giving up outright control of its water business. The Swiss food group and the private equity firm Platinum Equity will create a 50:50 joint venture, provisionally named Peranel, housing more than 30 water and premium-beverage brands — Perrier, S.Pellegrino, Acqua Panna and the mass-market Pure Life line among them — sold across 120 countries.

The numbers are concrete, which is more than could be said for this story a few months ago. Peranel carries an enterprise value of €4.9bn ($5.6bn), and Nestlé expects to walk away with roughly €3bn in cash once the deal closes, which the companies are targeting for the first half of 2027 pending regulatory clearance and staff consultations. Paris will host the new company's headquarters, run by Muriel Lienau, who already oversees Nestlé's waters unit.

Not a unit in decline

The standard telling of these carve-outs is that a conglomerate is cutting loose a laggard. That is not quite this story. Nestlé's waters and premium beverages arm posted 5.1% organic sales growth in the first half of the year — by some measures its second-fastest-growing category — and the group's overall quarterly organic growth of 3.7% beat analyst forecasts of 3.6%. Chief executive Philipp Navratil, who took over in September after Nestlé dismissed Laurent Freixe over an undisclosed relationship with a subordinate, framed the deal instead as a bet that water needs a specialist operator: partnering with Platinum Equity, he said, gives Peranel "enhanced agility" to pursue its own growth strategy outside a company now concentrating on coffee, pet care, nutrition and confectionery.

The unit hasn't been trouble-free. French investigators found in 2024 that Nestlé Waters had used banned filtration and UV treatment on water sold as untreated "natural mineral water," prompting a €2.2m fine, a French Senate inquiry that accused the government of slow-walking the case, and fraud authorities estimating the scale of the deception at over €3bn. A French court has since declined to force a product recall, but the scandal left a cloud over the Perrier name that a listed, independent Peranel will now have to manage on its own.

Part of a wider unwinding

Nestlé joins a queue of European consumer giants shedding divisions rather than running them. Unilever demerged its ice-cream business, including Magnum, onto Euronext Amsterdam, the London Stock Exchange and the NYSE in December, and Diageo is weighing a sale or spin-off of Guinness, potentially worth around $10bn, as part of a broader portfolio review that also touches its Moët Hennessy stake. European large-cap spin-off volume topped $22bn in 2025, ahead of the prior year — boards increasingly betting that a simpler equity story, not a broader one, is what wins back investors.

What happens next is mostly procedural: employee consultations across Peranel's markets, antitrust sign-off in the jurisdictions where Nestlé and Platinum Equity's other holdings overlap, and a closing Nestlé has pencilled in for the first half of 2027. Whether Peranel can out-execute its old parent in a roughly $300bn global bottled-water market is the question private equity, and Nestlé's remaining shareholders, will be watching for well before then.
