France evacuates 20,000 as wildfires rage near Bordeaux

In one sentence
A wildfire suspected of starting in a malfunctioning brush-clearing machine forced 20,000 evacuations near Bordeaux, as France's fire season runs four times ahead of its historical average.

The fire that emptied out a corner of France's Atlantic coast this week did not begin with lightning, or a discarded cigarette, or an arsonist's match. By the account of local officials, it began with a machine sent out to prevent exactly this kind of disaster — a brush-clearing tractor working the scrubland near the village of Saumos, in the Gironde department, that malfunctioned and threw a spark into tinder-dry pine forest.

Within three hours the fire had grown sixfold. By Thursday it had burned across more than 31 square kilometres of the Landes de Gascogne, Europe's largest man-made forest, and forced the evacuation of more than 20,000 people — residents of the town of Le Porge, campers on the Lège-Cap-Ferret peninsula, and holidaymakers pulled from eight campgrounds along one of France's most visited stretches of coast, 50 kilometres west of Bordeaux.

A machine meant to prevent fire, suspected of starting one

Judicial police have impounded the brush-clearing tractor and opened an inquiry into whether the ignition was mechanical failure or manufacturing defect. The mayor of Le Porge has described the working theory as accidental, and the local prefect responded within a day by banning all forestry work involving combustion engines across the department — an acknowledgment that the very equipment used to reduce fire risk, by clearing the undergrowth that fuels it, had become a source of it.

That irony sits at the centre of the story. Brush-clearing is standard fire prevention in a region that lost roughly 30,000 hectares of the same forest in 2022, one of the worst fire seasons in French history. The machines used to do the clearing run on the same hot, dry ground they are meant to protect, at the same time of year the ground is most flammable — a narrow operating window that leaves little margin for mechanical failure.

Eight hundred firefighters, three borrowed planes

About 800 firefighters, backed by hundreds of gendarmes and soldiers, have been deployed against the blaze, joined by water-bombing aircraft that closed to within 300 metres of the first homes in Lège before crews managed to hold the line. France asked the European Union for help, and Brussels sent three additional water-bombing planes — a reminder that even a country with its own dedicated Canadair fleet and civil-defence corps runs short of aerial capacity once several fires demand it simultaneously.

Three houses have been scorched and a caravan destroyed, but as of Thursday no injuries had been recorded from the Gironde fire itself. The same cannot be said of the wider season: two firefighters died two days earlier when their truck was overrun by flames near Bordeaux-Mérignac airport, in a separate blaze — a grim marker for what officials are already calling one of France's deadliest fire seasons in years.

A season running four times ahead of normal

Gironde's fire arrived amid France's third heatwave since May, with the mayor of Le Porge's own neighbouring commune reporting temperatures above 30°C and winds swinging unhelpfully to the east and northeast. Nationally, more than 41,000 hectares have burned so far in 2026 — over four times the seasonal average and already ahead of 2022's record pace, according to the EU's Copernicus monitoring service. Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu has said plainly that climate change is pulling the fire season earlier into the calendar, a claim the data increasingly supports: peak fire risk in southwestern France used to arrive in August, not late July.

The insurance industry has started to treat this as a structural shift rather than a bad run of summers. At Munich Re's wildfire roundtable in April, chief climate scientist Tobias Grimm told attendees that wildfire is now an established natural-catastrophe peril in Europe, with its frequency, intensity and geographic spread all changing — including into regions, like the pine-forested but historically low-risk stretches of the Atlantic coast, that underwriters had not priced for. Gironde, which sits at the edge of France's largest wine-producing region by volume, illustrates the exposure: even fires that stay out of the vineyards themselves can taint grapes with smoke, and insurers covering agricultural and tourism infrastructure across the department are only beginning to model that combined risk properly.

The immediate test is containment — the fire had not been fully controlled as of Thursday evening, and forecasters see little relief in the heat before the weekend. The longer test is what the impounded tractor's inquiry finds, and whether it prompts tighter rules for mechanical fire-prevention work during red-alert conditions, or is treated as an isolated malfunction in a season that will keep generating them regardless.
