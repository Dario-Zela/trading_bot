ECB's Nagel says central bank in 'good position', holds fire on rates

Joachim Nagel, president of the Deutsche Bundesbank and ECB governing council member. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bundesbank chief Nagel defended the ECB's rate hold by citing a 'fluid' Middle East situation, without saying which risks — or which direction September's decision might go.

Joachim Nagel, the Bundesbank president and a member of the European Central Bank's governing council, said the ECB is in a "good position" after holding its three key interest rates steady last Thursday — a decision he tied explicitly to the unresolved conflict in the Middle East rather than to any turn in the underlying inflation data.

The ECB left the deposit facility rate at 2.25%, the main refinancing rate at 2.4% and the marginal lending rate at 2.65%, unchanged since the quarter-point hike it delivered in June — its first increase since 2023, and a response to oil-driven inflation that pushed the eurozone rate to 3% for 2026 in the bank's own forecast. By June, headline inflation had already eased to 2.8% from May's 3.2%, with core inflation at 2.4%. On the numbers alone, a case for holding was straightforward.

Nagel's justification leaned elsewhere. "The renewed outbreak of military conflict in the Middle East and the fresh rise in oil prices underscore that the situation remains extremely volatile and the uncertainty is similarly high," he told Reuters. "It remains advisable to react with caution, but to act decisively if necessary."

What 'fluid' is doing here

That phrasing does a lot of work without saying much. Oil had fallen to roughly $72 a barrel after a short-lived peace agreement in June, then jumped back above $90 once US-Iran hostilities resumed — and has since pushed past $100 as Houthi attacks threaten shipping through the Bab al-Mandeb strait. Nagel did not specify which of those threads — a full Strait of Hormuz disruption, a broader regional escalation, or something narrower — would tip the ECB toward its next move. Nor did he say which direction that move would be: the ECB's own June projections still pencil in inflation cooling to 2.3% next year and 2% in 2028, a path consistent with holding, but economists surveyed after Thursday's decision largely expect a second hike in September rather than a cut, once fresh staff projections are in hand.

He is not alone in the hedge. Governing council colleague Bruno Le Maire-successor Denis-Emmanuel Moulin used near-identical language — "good position" — after the June hike, suggesting a coordinated line rather than a personal assessment. When several policymakers converge on the same vague formulation ahead of a meeting, it usually means the committee itself hasn't settled the argument, not that the argument is closed.

For now, the practical read is simple: no ECB move is coming before September 11, and Frankfurt wants the extra six weeks of data — on energy pass-through, wage settlements, and how durable the oil shock proves — before committing either way. The euro's near-term path from ECB surprise is accordingly limited. What isn't priced yet is which way September breaks, and Nagel's refusal to specify the Middle East risks he's actually watching leaves that an open question rather than a settled one.
