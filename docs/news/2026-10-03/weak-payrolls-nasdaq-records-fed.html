Weak US payrolls push Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia to records as Fed hike bets fade

In one sentence
A September payrolls miss cut Fed-hike odds to about 16% and lifted the Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia to records, even as Goolsbee said inflation still outranks jobs.

Equities were handed a weak US jobs report on Friday and responded by climbing. The Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia both closed at record highs after September payrolls missed consensus forecasts. Prediction-market odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike fell to about 16%. The market read the miss as a reason for the Fed to hold off, not as a sign that the economy was cooling.

That is the old "bad news is good news" trade. It works only while the market is more afraid of the central bank than of the economy.

The trade

The mechanics are simple. A softer labour market removes pressure on the Fed to tighten. Lower expected policy rates raise the present value of long-dated earnings, which helps the most richly valued part of the market. The Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia, its most prominent constituent, are the obvious beneficiaries.

The move suggests investors are weighing the rate path above the employment data itself. If the market were worried mainly about a slowing economy, a payrolls miss would more plausibly have hit earnings-sensitive stocks. Instead the index with the longest-duration cash flows hit a record. Nvidia reaching a high on the same day points the same way. Its fortunes depend on corporate capital spending and not on the monthly hiring count.

The 16% figure is a prediction-market price and not a Fed projection. It is still a useful gauge of how quickly sentiment moved. The data did not need to be disastrous. It only needed to be soft enough to take hikes off the table.

The Fed has not conceded

The policymakers themselves are not signalling the same relief. Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed, said after the soft print that the inflation mandate now outweighs concern for a steady labour market. He indicated that a rate hike, or a pause, remains on the table.

That sits awkwardly with the market consensus, which had been pricing in cuts by year-end. A hike or a pause is a different proposition from the easing that some equity positioning appears to assume. Traders who bought the payrolls miss are betting that the Fed will weigh the jobs data more heavily than Goolsbee says it will.

The political side adds a complication. Kevin Hassett, director of the White House National Economic Council, told Bloomberg TV he was satisfied with recent payroll growth, a more upbeat reading than the market's. He also said interest payments on the national debt are "too high". The comment points to the administration's own interest in lower rates. It is not a forecast of what the Fed will do, and the Fed sets rates independently. But the White House wants cheaper borrowing, the equity market is pricing it in, and the Chicago Fed president is not endorsing it.

Why the footing is narrow

The rally rests on a sequence that has to hold in order. Labour data must be weak enough to keep the Fed from hiking. It must also not be so weak that it changes the earnings outlook. If employment stabilises, the case for a pause strengthens and the rationale for the move weakens. If it deteriorates sharply, the premise shifts from lower rates to lower growth.

There are two further points for systematic traders. First, a rally built on a recessionary signal is unstable by construction, because the thing that supports it is the thing that could break it. Second, the gains are concentrated in the Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia. A move driven by a rate narrative and not by fundamentals is the kind that can reverse into sector rotation if the narrative changes.

The next test is the next inflation reading and any further Fed commentary. With Goolsbee already framing inflation as the binding constraint, a firm price print would pit the equity market's view of the Fed against the Fed's own.
