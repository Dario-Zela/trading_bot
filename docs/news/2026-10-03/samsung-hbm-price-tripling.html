Samsung to triple HBM prices next year, Korean press reports

In one sentence
Samsung is reportedly seeking over three times current HBM3E pricing for HBM4 in 2027, which would lift memory-maker earnings and squeeze AI chip designers' margins.

Samsung Electronics has reportedly told customers it wants more than three times the price for its next-generation high-bandwidth memory next year, according to Korean press reports. Samsung has not confirmed this. If the figure holds, the company that makes a critical component of AI accelerators would be capturing more of the money flowing into AI data centres, at the expense of the chip designers and cloud operators that buy it.

The headline number needs a qualifier. The reports describe a proposal for HBM4 priced at more than three times the roughly $1.50-per-gigabit level of today's HBM3E. They do not describe a tripling of existing contracts. One estimate cited in the coverage puts a 12-layer, 36GB HBM4 stack at about $600 this year and about $1,300 next year. That is a rise of roughly 120%, or a bit over double. The gap between "triple" and "double" mostly reflects a generation change and an opening bid, which is not the same as a price increase on an identical part.

What is being asked

HBM stacks several DRAM dies vertically beside the processor. This lets an AI accelerator read data far faster than it could from ordinary memory. Each Nvidia Rubin package is expected to carry eight HBM4 stacks, for 288GB of capacity and about 22 TB/s of bandwidth. Memory is therefore a large share of an accelerator's bill of materials, and the share grows with each generation.

Other forecasters point in the same direction, though less steeply. KB Securities expects HBM selling prices to rise more than 100% next year. TrendForce projects the blended average HBM selling price in 2027 will climb 121% from a year earlier. Samsung's own expectations fit the picture: the company has said HBM will exceed one-third of global DRAM wafer capacity by 2027, and that its HBM4 revenue share should rise from about 40% in 2026 to about 80% in 2027.

The spot market is already tight. TrendForce reported in September that HBM3E spot prices were four to five times long-term contract levels, with Samsung reportedly locking up 70% of its capacity through 2031. A contract proposal at three times current levels looks less extreme against a spot market that already clears at multiples of contract prices.

Why Samsung has leverage now

For most of the AI build-out, Samsung was the laggard. SK hynix supplied the bulk of Nvidia's HBM, with estimates of its share running from 62% to about 70% depending on the quarter and the source. Nvidia accounts for roughly 90% of SK hynix's HBM supply. Samsung has been closing the gap: Seoul Economic Daily put its HBM market share at 33% in September, roughly double the earlier level.

That recovery matters for pricing. A supplier with a third of the market and sold-out capacity can ask for more. Micron, with a share of roughly 21% to 24%, has also said its HBM supply is sold out through 2027. With all three qualified suppliers short of capacity, buyers have little room to play one against another. HBM wafers also use more silicon per bit than conventional DRAM, so each one diverted to HBM tightens supply of ordinary memory too.

The earnings effect is already visible. Korean media report Samsung heading for a record third-quarter operating profit near 100 trillion won, with HBM demand offsetting the drag from a stronger won. A tripling of HBM4 pricing, even partly realised, would extend that.

Who pays

The cost lands first on accelerator makers. Nvidia and AMD have few alternatives. Their products are only as good as the memory attached to them, and the dominant supplier qualification cycles run for months. Nvidia's gross margins are high enough to absorb part of an increase, and its record-setting share price this week suggests investors are not worried about demand (see Weak US payrolls push Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia to records). Smaller designers and custom-silicon programmes at the hyperscalers have thinner cushions and less purchasing weight.

There are three ways the cost could travel from there:

  • Absorbed by chip designers. Margins compress but list prices hold. This is plausible for Nvidia, less so for AMD.
  • Passed to hyperscalers. Accelerator prices rise, and capital expenditure budgets buy fewer chips. That would slow deployment at the margin without stopping it.
  • Passed to end users. Cloud GPU rental rates and AI service prices rise. This depends on whether customers will pay more, which is the question behind the valuation worries the Bank of England's Andrew Bailey raised this week (see Bailey cautions that stretched AI valuations pose a stability risk).

The analysis has limits. Contract prices are negotiated, and a first proposal is rarely the settled one. Buyers can also respond by qualifying more suppliers, trimming memory per chip, or committing to volumes over several years in exchange for discounts. The long-term agreements reported in September suggest suppliers are already pushing buyers in that direction.

What to watch

Samsung's third-quarter earnings, due in the coming weeks, should show how much of the HBM price increase is already in revenue and whether management comments on 2027 contract terms. SK hynix and Micron will report after that, and their language on pricing will show whether the Samsung ask is an outlier or the new market. The firmest data point will be Nvidia's guidance on gross margin for the Rubin ramp. Memory costs sit inside that figure, and a visible squeeze there would show who ended up paying.
