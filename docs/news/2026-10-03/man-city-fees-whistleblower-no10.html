Man City case: where the 'inflated' fees went, as the whistleblower loses protection and No 10 distances itself from Burnham

In one sentence
BBC Sport has traced the clubs that received City's alleged 'inflated' fees, as the case's whistleblower loses protection and No 10 says City are 'not above the rules'.

Manchester City's long-running dispute with the Premier League over its finances has produced three developments in a single day. BBC Sport has traced which clubs received the alleged 'inflated' payments. A whistleblower central to the case is set to lose witness protection. And Downing Street has stepped away from Andy Burnham's remarks on the matter, saying City are 'not above the rules'.

None of these resolves anything. Together they show how much of the case now rests on following money through deals that look ordinary on paper.

Following the fees

The BBC Sport reporting identifies recipient clubs that took payments the investigation characterises as inflated. The transactions were structured as transfer deals, with the figures overvalued rather than invented. That is why they are hard to police. A transfer fee is whatever two clubs agree it is, and no market price exists to test it against. A fee that is too high for the player can pass for a bargain, or a misjudgement, until someone looks at who benefits at the other end.

The reporting is a tracing exercise. It does not establish wrongdoing, and the case remains active. City have consistently denied breaching the league's rules, and the allegations remain allegations.

For anyone assessing a sports-linked asset, the mechanism matters more than the club. Related-party arrangements and distorted transfer pricing can move value between entities without changing the group's cash position. They blur the true size of an asset or liability, in football as in any other sector.

A witness loses cover

The second thread is the more fragile. The whistleblower at the centre of the investigation faces losing witness protection, according to BBC Sport. Cases built on complex transfers lean heavily on insiders who can explain what a deal was actually for, because the paperwork alone seldom says. Removing the protection could put that testimony at risk. The reporting does not say what would replace it.

Politics arrives

The third thread is political. Burnham's comments on City drew a backlash, and No 10 then said the club were 'not above the rules' and distanced itself from his remarks. The argument turns on the Premier League's Profit and Sustainability rules, which cap how much clubs can lose.

The government's line is a cautious one. It backs the league's right to enforce its own rules without taking a position on the case itself.

What to watch

The test is whether the money trail can be turned into findings that stand up. That depends on the investigation's progress and on whether the whistleblower's evidence stays available. The league's rules were written to stop clubs spending beyond their means. They are less well suited to checking whether a fee paid to another club was a fair one.
