Why Gen Z are opting out of pensions

In one sentence
Young workers are leaving workplace pension schemes to ease cost-of-living strain, with industry figures putting under-30 opt-outs near one in ten and raising retirement-shortfall fears.

A trainee doctor in England stopped paying into his NHS pension in September. Hassan Nassar, 26, had been putting in about £430 a month. He says he needed the money for a sick family member, a first-home deposit, rent and student loan repayments. He reckons the decision could cost him between £5,000 and £10,000 in future retirement income. "I need the money now," is the gist of his argument, and it is one a growing number of young workers are making.

A pension pause

Auto-enrolment was designed on a simple bet: put people into a workplace scheme by default and inertia will do the saving for them. For most of the past decade the bet paid off. Opt-out rates stayed low, and millions who had never saved began to.

The youngest workers are now the weak point. Industry research cited by Financial Planning Today puts the opt-out rate for under-30s at 9.3%, roughly one in ten of those eligible. Among 18- to 24-year-olds it is higher, at about 17%, the highest of any age group. Across all ages, the same research suggests around 500,000 people a year leave their scheme. These figures come from industry research rather than official data, and they do not show how much of the rise is new. The pattern does fit what workers describe: rent, food and loan repayments are crowding out a contribution that does not show up for decades.

The arithmetic of waiting

The trade-off looks small month to month. Stopping a contribution means a bigger take-home figure straight away. It also means giving up the employer's matching contribution, which is part of pay, and years of compound growth.

Advisers frame the cost in blunt terms. One estimate in the coverage suggests that delaying saving from 22 to 27 alone could cost about £40,000 in lost retirement funds. Penfold, a pension provider, calculates that four in ten Gen Z workers are on course for retirement poverty if they stick to minimum auto-enrolment contributions. An average earner relying on those minimums would retire on around £13,000 a year, it says, which is short of what basic living costs.

That last point matters for the policy debate. Minimum contributions were never calibrated to deliver a comfortable retirement, so a generation that saves the minimum is already behind. A generation that pauses even that is further back.

Why it matters beyond the individual

The numbers are modest in absolute terms, and the data do not yet show a collapse in participation. But the signal is worth reading. Opting out of a scheme that costs little and comes with free employer money is a decision people make only when budgets are tight. Pension opt-outs work as a rough gauge of household strain, and they point to pressure that is now strong enough to override long-term planning.

There is a funding-model risk too. Auto-enrolment relies on steady contributions from the young, and on those workers staying in the habit. If a cohort drifts out and does not return, the shortfall arrives later, as pressure on the state pension and on a political system already arguing over its cost. The debate over the pension triple lock is one place where that pressure will show up.

What to watch

The next test is the official data. The Department for Work and Pensions publishes its auto-enrolment evaluation and the Pensions Regulator publishes its scheme data, and both should show whether opt-outs among under-30s are a spike or a trend. Any move to raise minimum contributions or widen eligibility will also be judged against the same question: whether workers can afford to stay in.
