Christa Pike on ventilator after failed execution, lawyers say

In one sentence
Tennessee's attempt to execute Christa Pike failed and she is unconscious on a ventilator, her lawyers say, prompting a year-end pause on executions and an outside protocol review.

Tennessee's attempt to execute Christa Pike on Wednesday failed, and the state's only woman on death row is now unconscious on a ventilator in a hospital. Her lawyers said so in a court filing on Friday, two days after the attempt at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution in Nashville.

Pike, 50, was sentenced to death in 1996 for her part in the torture murder of Colleen Slemmer. Both were teenagers at the time. Her execution date had been set by the Tennessee courts in the autumn, and the case had drawn attention because she would have been the first woman executed in the state in two centuries. Her lawyers had already sued over the state's execution protocol and warned that her veins could complicate a lethal injection.

What the filing alleges

According to the filing, by Pike's attorneys Stephen Ferrell and Luke Ihnen of Federal Defender Services of Eastern Tennessee, the execution team struggled for roughly an hour to establish intravenous access. They say at least seven needles were used, one of which came out bent at 90 degrees. The filing says two doses of pentobarbital were then administered. Pike, it says, could be heard "crying, whimpering, and breathing loudly" and said her arm "feels like it's about to burst open."

The lawyers' reading is that the IV lines were misplaced or the veins had blown, so the drug entered her body only in part, or entered the surrounding tissue. When she reached the hospital, they say, both arms were "swollen, burned, and blistered." She is described as critically ill. These are the account of one side, set out in a filing. The state has not publicly disputed the broad outline of events, but it has not endorsed the detail either.

The state's response

The Tennessee Department of Correction said it "followed every step of the State's lawful, established execution protocol approved by the Attorney General's Office." That framing matters. If the protocol was followed and the outcome was still a failed execution and a hospitalised prisoner, the dispute shifts from staff conduct to the protocol itself.

Governor Bill Lee appears to have reached a similar view. He announced that executions in Tennessee will be halted through the end of the year so that an outside review of the protocol can be carried out. It is the second such review of his tenure.

Pike's lawyers have also asked the court to order that all evidence from the attempted execution be preserved, while asking that her privacy be protected during her hospitalisation.

The constitutional question

The legal significance runs through the Eighth Amendment. Courts have held that the Constitution does not guarantee a painless death, but it does bar methods that carry a substantial risk of severe pain when feasible alternatives exist. Challengers usually have to show both. A botched attempt is the kind of evidence that is hard to produce in advance. It lets lawyers argue that the risk was not hypothetical, and it raises a further question: whether the state may try again on a prisoner who has already gone through a partial administration of the drug.

That second question is likely to reach the courts first. Pike's condition, and whether she recovers, will shape any filing about whether a second attempt is permissible. The governor's pause gives Tennessee until at least January to complete its review. The preservation request is the first concrete step, and a judge's response to it will show how much of what happened in the execution chamber becomes public.
