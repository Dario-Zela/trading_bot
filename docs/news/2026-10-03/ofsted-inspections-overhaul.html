England school inspections to be overhauled again after barrage of complaints

In one sentence
Ofsted is to rewrite its inspection framework for the second time in about a year after heads' unions said the current system is more stressful than the one it replaced.

England's school inspectors are heading back to the drawing board. The education secretary, Lucy Powell, is to tell Ofsted to rework its inspection regime for the second time in about a year. The reason is a run of complaints that the new system is making school leadership more stressful, not less.

The previous round of changes was overseen by her predecessor, Bridget Phillipson. Those changes were meant to be the fix. Powell has become concerned that they are creating fresh problems for headteachers, and the regulator is being asked to think again.

The complaint file

The sharpest criticism has come from the National Association of Head Teachers. Its survey of more than 5,000 school leaders found that nearly three-quarters felt the direction of travel on inspection had worsened over the past year. The union's general secretary went further, saying the framework was "even more stressful than the one that preceded it". They added that it "poses a real and present danger to the well-being and mental health of school leaders and teachers, possibly even a risk to life."

The NAHT has also called for a halt to graded inspections. It wants ministers to swap the "high-stakes, low-trust" grades for a short narrative report setting out a school's strengths and areas for development. A separate education union has said the overhaul has not eased staff workload.

Churn as a policy

The awkward part for Whitehall is the arithmetic. Inspection reform in England has been under way for years, since the death of the headteacher Ruth Perry put the regulator's methods under scrutiny. The one-word headline judgments were dropped, and a more detailed report-card approach was introduced in their place. Each step was billed as a correction to the one before.

That pattern matters beyond the politics. A school builds its improvement plans, staff training and even its recruitment pitch around what the inspector will look for. When the yardstick moves every year or so, that planning is hard to do. Heads facing an unfamiliar framework also have one more reason to leave the job early, and retention is already a worry in the sector.

Two readings are available, and the evidence so far does not settle between them. One is that the regulator and the department have misjudged the problem and keep reaching for the wrong lever. The other is that political pressure from unions, the press and backbenchers keeps overriding the time a framework needs to bed in. Either way, credibility is the casualty. An inspector whose rules change this often struggles to be seen as a stable referee.

What to watch

No timetable, cost or scope has been published for the new framework. The first test is whether the redesign keeps grades at all, or moves toward the narrative reports the NAHT is urging. The second is whether schools get a settled period afterwards. Without one, the next round of complaints is likely to follow the next framework.
