G7 agrees to release up to 100m barrels of oil and diesel after US pressure, as UK diesel passes £2 a litre

In one sentence
The G7 agreed to release up to 100m barrels of oil and diesel after US pressure, sending diesel lower on the day UK pump prices passed £2 a litre for the first time.

The Group of Seven has agreed to release up to 100 million barrels of oil and diesel from emergency stocks. The decision followed pressure from Washington, including threats from President Donald Trump directed at allies. Diesel prices fell sharply on the announcement. On the same day, the RAC said the average price of diesel at UK forecourts had passed £2 a litre for the first time.

The two developments are linked. The release is aimed at the product that has become the most expensive part of the fuel complex. The UK number shows how far retail prices had moved before the policy response arrived.

What was agreed

The headline figure is a ceiling, not a delivery schedule. "Up to 100 million barrels" covers crude and diesel, and the reports so far do not say how the volume divides between the two, which countries supply it, or over what period it would reach the market. Those details determine the physical effect. A release spread over many months removes less near-term tightness than one front-loaded into a few weeks.

The market did not wait for the detail. Diesel dropped on the announcement itself, which fits a pattern familiar from earlier coordinated releases: the signal that governments will sell stock moves futures before any barrel leaves a cavern or tank farm. The size of the move, and whether it holds, depends on whether traders conclude the supply is real and sustained.

The angle that matters for the long run is how the decision was reached. Strategic stocks exist as insurance against physical interruption. Using them under political pressure to lower pump prices is a different purpose. By that reading, the G7 has traded some of its supply discipline for near-term market deflation. The reserves are lower afterwards, and the next shock will meet a thinner buffer.

Europe's share is unsettled

The European contribution is where the picture is least clear. The EU has been weighing a release of roughly 50 million barrels of diesel stocks, about half the headline total. No timeline or member-state allocation has been agreed, and the key terms remain under negotiation.

That matters because European emergency stocks are held nationally, under rules that set minimum cover for each member. Any coordinated release needs governments to agree who draws down, by how much, and when. Countries with comfortable cover have little reason to resist. Countries close to their minimums have every reason to. Until those splits are settled, the European half of the 100 million barrels is an intention rather than a supply.

Traders therefore have a large number and few operational facts. A market can price the headline on day one. By day ten it wants delivery dates, loading terminals and volumes, and the information on offer so far does not supply them.

The European debate is also a response to outside pressure. Several European governments are being asked to act on a timetable set in Washington. This is the fracture the story points to: major economies manage strategic reserves under different legal regimes and political incentives, and a push from the US reveals how difficult it is to line them up.

The pump price lags the barrel

The RAC figure shows the cost of that lag. Diesel above £2 a litre is a record for the UK, and it comes as the release is announced rather than after it has had any effect. Retail fuel follows wholesale prices with a delay, because forecourts sell stock bought earlier and adjust gradually. A fall in wholesale diesel on 3 October will therefore take some time to reach the pumps, and the £2 mark is likely to stay in the headlines for a while.

Diesel is more than a motorist's concern. It powers road freight, farm machinery, construction and much of the distribution network, so its price passes through into the cost of goods. That is why diesel is watched as a leading indicator for inflation expectations. A sustained move lower would ease that channel. A brief dip that reverses once the market judges the release too small or too slow would leave it intact.

The test for policymakers is whether the release changes retail prices at all. The announcement effect is real but short-lived. A release only lowers prices lastingly if it offsets a physical shortfall, and nothing in the reports so far says how large that shortfall is.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is a durable intervention or a one-day market event.

  • Allocation. Whether the EU settles the national split of its roughly 50 million barrels, and on what timeline. Without that, the headline total is incomplete.
  • Product mix. How much of the 100 million barrels is diesel, where the physical squeeze is, and how much is crude.
  • Producer response. The release changes the supply side of the calculation for the major producers. The delayed OPEC+ capacity assessment, now pushed to mid-November, leaves the market without a clear read on spare capacity just as consuming countries draw on their stocks.

If diesel futures hold their fall and the allocation is settled quickly, the G7 will have bought a cheaper winter at the price of lower reserves. If the fall fades, the stocks will have been spent and the pressure on prices will remain. The next RAC weekly fuel readings will show which of the two is happening.
