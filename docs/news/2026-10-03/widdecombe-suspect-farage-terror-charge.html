Widdecombe murder suspect charged with planning terror act against Nigel Farage

In one sentence
Joshua Kerry, accused of murdering Ann Widdecombe, now also faces a charge of preparing terrorist acts, with Nigel Farage named as an alleged target.

The man accused of murdering Ann Widdecombe has been charged with preparing acts of terrorism, with Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, named as an alleged target. Counter Terrorism Policing announced the new charge on 2 October. It adds a second, separate count to a case that had so far been handled as a murder inquiry.

The charge

Joshua Kerry, 28, is charged with one count of preparation of terrorist acts. The charge covers alleged activity between 31 May 2024 and 8 July 2026. The end date is the day Widdecombe died. He was already accused of her murder.

Widdecombe was 78, a former Conservative minister and a Reform UK spokeswoman. She was found dead at her home in Devon on 8 July. She had been struck on the head with a hammer 21 times. Kerry was arrested on suspicion of murder two days later.

The charge sheet, as reported, runs to a single count and does not set out the alleged plan. It is not yet public what Kerry is said to have done in the two years the charge covers, or how the alleged preparations related to Farage as opposed to Widdecombe. Those details are likely to emerge only in court.

Deputy Assistant Commissioner Vicki Evans said the investigation had involved "multiple lines of enquiry, which has resulted in this very serious charge." The Crown Prosecution Service and the Home Secretary, Shabana Mahmood, also commented. Mahmood's remarks concerned the security of public figures.

A reopened file

The charge arrives against the background of an earlier episode at Farage's home in south London. An incendiary device was pushed through the letterbox and burned itself out in the porch. Farage was away at the time. The incident was first investigated as an attempted burglary, no arrests were made, and the file was closed.

In August, Counter Terrorism Policing reopened the inquiry. The reports reviewed for this article do not say that Kerry is accused of involvement in that incident, and police have not said so. The sequence is notable, but it should not be read as a link the authorities have confirmed.

What the pairing signals

It is unusual for a terrorism-preparation count to be added to an existing murder charge. The terrorism offence covers conduct before and around the killing. A murder charge alone looks only at the killing itself. By bringing both, prosecutors appear to be saying that the evidence points to a wider intent than the single act. Whether the court accepts that depends on material that has not been made public.

The evidence behind the terrorism count also matters for a different reason. If the allegation is that Farage was a target, the case becomes one about the protection of party leaders and campaigners as well as one about a single killing. That is the context for the Home Secretary's comments on security for public figures.

What comes next

Kerry is due at Westminster Magistrates' Court on Wednesday 7 October. That hearing is likely to be procedural, and the case would then move to the Old Bailey. The first public account of what prosecutors say Kerry prepared, and against whom, is the thing to watch for. Equally important is whether police say publicly that the reopened Farage firebomb inquiry is connected to him.

In the meantime, a defendant is presumed innocent, and the terrorism allegation has been made but not tested.
