Riot police clash with students as education protests rage in France

In one sentence
Student protests over school conditions have disrupted more than 560 French establishments and turned violent, but there is no evidence yet of wider political or fiscal consequences.

French high-school students lit fires outside their schools and faced riot police in cities across the country on Friday. The movement began over classroom conditions and funding, and it has now closed or disrupted more than 560 establishments. Police counted roughly 900 demonstrations in a single day. Nothing yet suggests the protests are fading.

What the students want

The grievances are practical. Students say the school day is too long, that classrooms overheat, that buildings are dilapidated and that too many teachers and staff are absent. None of this is a programme in the way a pension or budget reform is. The complaints amount to a list of everyday failures, which makes them hard for a government to answer with a single concession.

The disorder has been visible and widespread. Tear gas hung over the streets of Strasbourg in the east. Fireworks were aimed at police lines in Marseille, and rubbish bins were set alight in Rennes in the west. The education ministry says at least 65 staff members have been injured.

The policing question

The protesters are mostly minors, which sharpens the argument over how they are being policed. Students and rights groups accuse officers of heavy-handed tactics, including batons, tear gas and non-lethal flash-ball launchers. On Friday the interior ministry said it had opened three investigations into police violence against protesters.

That step matters politically. A government that is trying to contain street unrest, and is also seen to be investigating its own officers, is conceding that some of the footage is difficult to defend. It also gives the movement a second grievance beyond the classroom.

What is and is not known

The wider claims made about this story need caution. Early coverage framed the unrest as feeding French political instability and fiscal uncertainty, but the reporting available so far does not show that. The government's response has been to contain the protests and open inquiries. No coalition split has been reported, no minister has resigned, and no change to the timetable for fiscal reform has been announced. The link to Macron's budget plans is an inference, not a documented fact.

History offers a guide to the risks. Education protests in France have sometimes grown into broader confrontations when unions, parents or other workers join in. They have also faded within weeks, particularly once the school calendar or a government concession takes the heat out. Which of these paths this one takes is not yet clear. High-school protests are loosely organised and harder to negotiate with than union-led ones, because there is no obvious interlocutor to bargain with.

What to watch

Three things will show whether Friday was a peak. The first is whether the number of closed or disrupted schools keeps rising from the 560-plus recorded so far. The second is whether teachers' unions or parents' groups formally back the movement, which would turn a student protest into a wider dispute. The third is the outcome of the three police-violence investigations, since any finding against officers would add fuel. For now the unrest is a serious public-order problem for the government. There is no evidence yet that it threatens anything beyond that.
