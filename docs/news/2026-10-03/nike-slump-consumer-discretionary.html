What has gone wrong at Nike, as shares are in focus after jobs-day trading

In one sentence
Nike's revenue miss and high-single-digit sales-decline guidance sent shares down 8–10%, but flat moves at On and Lululemon point to a company problem, not a consumer one.

Nike has told investors that its sales will shrink again this year, and the market marked the shares down by roughly 8% to 10% on Friday, 2 October, as they traded near 52-week lows. The sportswear maker reported a first-quarter revenue miss on Thursday and said it would cut jobs. Its trading update landed on the same morning as a soft US payrolls report that lifted most of the market. Nike went the other way.

The numbers

Revenue for the fiscal first quarter came in at about $11.2bn, down roughly 4% on the year, against a consensus near $11.3bn–$11.35bn. Sales in Greater China fell; North America grew. Net income was $712m, against $727m a year earlier. Diluted earnings of $0.48 a share beat expectations, helped by a wider gross margin, so the quarter was not a rout. The guidance was the problem.

Nike now expects fiscal 2027 revenue to fall by a high-single-digit percentage. It guided to adjusted earnings of $1.15–$1.35 a share, below a $1.61 consensus, according to Bloomberg's account of the results. Management also said pressure in Greater China, Nike Sportswear and the Jordan Brand could extend into fiscal 2028. The performance business, which covers running and training, is growing at a high-single-digit rate. The lifestyle franchises that once carried the group are shrinking at a low-double-digit rate.

The shares changed hands around $32 in morning trading on Friday, according to 24/7 Wall St. That is about 48% lower this year and, on that outlet's count, roughly 80% below the peak.

Restructuring, with a long fuse

Chief executive Elliott Hill announced an operating model called Pace. It targets $2.5bn of cumulative savings through fiscal 2031 and carries about $1bn of pre-tax charges, mostly employee-related. Decisions on affected roles begin in calendar 2027.

"This work will result in fewer roles across Nike, and I acknowledge that creates uncertainty."

The long timetable matters. A programme that saves money over five years does little for the next four quarters, and the guidance says as much.

Bellwether or special case?

The broader question is whether Nike says anything about household spending. The evidence from Friday points mostly to a company-specific problem. Lululemon and On Holding were essentially flat on the day. On reported net sales growth of 13.5% for the quarter, so a premium footwear name is still growing quickly. The consumer-discretionary ETF was unchanged. Had Nike's miss reflected a pullback across affluent households, rivals would likely have sold off in sympathy.

The causes Nike cites are narrow: China, tariffs, a direct-to-consumer channel that is still declining, and brand lines that have lost their pull. These look like errors of product and positioning, not of purchasing power. The stock is also not a clean read on big-ticket spending. Trainers are a small, repeat purchase, unlike cars or furniture.

The macro backdrop is still worth watching. September payrolls grew by less than forecast, which pushed Treasury yields lower and cut expectations of a Federal Reserve rate rise this month. Slower hiring is the usual precursor to softer discretionary budgets. A weaker labour market and a weak Nike on the same day will invite a causal story, but nothing in Thursday's figures supports it directly.

What to watch

The next tests come from other retailers. A guidance cut from a second branded apparel or footwear group, or a decline in On's or Lululemon's growth, would make the consumer reading harder to dismiss. For Nike itself, the second-quarter report in December will show whether North America growth can offset China, and whether the margin gains survive the tariff costs and the promotions needed to clear stock.
