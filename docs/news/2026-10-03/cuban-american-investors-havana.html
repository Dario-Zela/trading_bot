BEYOND THE TAPE · CUBA · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-10-03 · 3 min read

The wealthy Cuban Americans ready and waiting for Havana to fall

In one sentence
Wealthy Cuban exiles are reportedly preparing capital for a post-regime Havana, but with few named deals or sums, it remains a hedge on hope more than a visible market trade.

In Miami's Brickell district, the talk at certain dinner tables has a recurring subject: what to buy, and when, if the government in Havana falls. Some exiles and their children, according to reporting by BBC Business, are already acting on it, lining up capital, advisers and local contacts for a post-regime Cuba.

The story is more atmosphere than ledger. The reporting, as summarised, offers little in the way of named funds, disclosed sums or confirmed positions, and the claims here are limited accordingly. What it does describe is a familiar kind of bet, made by people with an unusual mix of motives.

Hope with a spreadsheet attached

Cuban Americans number in the millions, and a wealthy slice of them left property, businesses and family behind in 1959 and after. For that group, a transition is not an abstract macro scenario. It is a question of whether a house in Vedado, a sugar-era estate or a hotel site becomes recoverable, tradeable or worthless.

That gives the positioning two layers. One is sentiment: a hope that the system ends, expressed as readiness. The other is a hedge, in the sense a trader would use the word. If change comes, early movers hold relationships, legal groundwork and perhaps claims. If it does not, the cost is mostly fees, time and patience. Option-like payoffs, in other words, bought cheaply in a market that does not formally exist for them.

The legal wall

The obstacles are large, and they explain why so much of this activity stays quiet. The US embargo still restricts most American persons from dealing in Cuba. The 1996 Helms-Burton Act, which codified the embargo, also created a right for US nationals to sue over property confiscated by the Cuban state. Any serious plan has to be built around those rules, not through them, which pushes the preparation toward legal work, claims research and non-binding understandings rather than money visibly deployed.

There is also the matter of the island itself. Cuba's economy has been strained by shortages, blackouts and emigration, and a sudden opening would arrive in a country with weak institutions, no deep capital market and a currency system that has been repeatedly reshuffled. Frontier investors elsewhere know the pattern: the first wave of money after a political break is often the most exposed, and sometimes the most regretted.

What the case study shows

The comparison is with other diaspora flows into closed or unstable economies, such as remittances, diaspora bonds and the pre-positioning that followed earlier regime changes. Those are well documented. What would make the Cuban version news for markets, rather than colour, is evidence of new scale: named vehicles, disclosed amounts, a dedicated fund. That evidence has not yet surfaced.

Until then, the more interesting point may be behavioural. A community that has waited more than sixty years for a political outcome has learned to keep a financial plan alongside the hope, and to keep it quiet.

What to watch is less a date than a trigger: any formal change in US sanctions policy, or the first filing of a dedicated Cuba-focused investment vehicle, would be the sign that preparation had become capital.
