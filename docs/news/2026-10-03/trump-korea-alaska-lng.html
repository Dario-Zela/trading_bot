Trump threatens South Korea over Alaska LNG; Seoul says the $8.4bn project is not part of the deal

In one sentence
Trump wants South Korea to fold the $8.4bn Alaska LNG project into its trade deal; Seoul says it was never included, and no tariff threat has been specified.

President Donald Trump has pressed South Korea to fold the $8.4bn Alaska LNG project into its existing trade arrangement with Washington. Seoul says the project was never part of the deal. So far the dispute is a disagreement over scope, and nothing in the reporting points to a new tariff or a deadline.

What is being argued over

Alaska LNG is a proposed liquefied natural gas scheme. Washington has been looking for Asian buyers and financiers for it, and Bloomberg and Benzinga report that Trump wants Korea to commit to it as part of its trade terms. Korean officials reject that characterisation. Their position is that the existing agreement did not cover the project and that they do not accept it being added now.

The gap between the two accounts is the story. Trade deals struck quickly and announced in broad strokes tend to leave room for each side to describe them differently. Here the White House appears to be treating an energy purchase or investment as an implied term, while Seoul reads the text, and its own understanding of the talks, as silent on it.

Why Korea is a sensitive counterpart

South Korea is, by the triage figures, the world's second-largest chip exporter to the US, and it is a core supply-chain ally for memory and other semiconductors. That is why a quarrel over gas has a market dimension at all. Samsung and SK Hynix sit at the centre of the AI hardware build-out, and Korean press is already reporting steep price increases for high-bandwidth memory; see Samsung to triple HBM prices next year. Any friction that touched chip shipments or pricing would feed straight into that trade.

Nothing so far suggests it will. No tariff rate has been named in the reporting, and no date has been set for one. The argument is rhetorical and over scope, not a measured action against Korean goods.

Reading the signal

The pattern matters more than the single episode. Reopening the boundaries of an agreement after it is struck raises the cost of relying on any agreement's written terms. For allies that have already made concessions, the question is whether the finished deal is the end of the negotiation or only a floor for the next demand. Korea's public refusal suggests it has decided that conceding on scope here would invite more of the same.

Energy is the other thread. Long-term LNG commitments are priced over decades, and a government-brokered purchase of a project that has struggled to find buyers would be a political decision as much as a commercial one. Markets are also dealing with tight fuel supply elsewhere, with G7 members agreeing to release up to 100m barrels of oil and diesel (G7 stock release). That backdrop makes Washington's interest in securing gas customers easier to understand, though it does not make Seoul's agreement any likelier.

The broader market tone this week has been risk-on, with the Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia at records after weak payrolls (payrolls and the Fed). A diplomatic spat of this kind has not been enough to dent that.

What to watch

The test is whether rhetoric turns into paper. A formal tariff notice aimed at Korean goods, any change to the treatment of semiconductors, or a statement from Seoul on revised talks would each move this from noise to a trading input. Until one of those appears, the sensible reading is a negotiating position being contested in public, with no evidence yet of consequences for chip or energy prices.
