OpenAI fires workers for 'mishandling sensitive information'

In one sentence
OpenAI fired three safety researchers for mishandling sensitive information shared with an outside AI evaluator, but has given no detail on what the information was.

OpenAI has dismissed three researchers for what it calls mishandling sensitive company information. The company says the case is a breach of its own rules. The three say it is an internal-security matter, not a dispute over safety. The BBC reported the firings first. Other outlets, including RTÉ, Forbes and Japan Today, have since added detail.

The facts are thin. OpenAI has not said what the information was, how much of it left the building, or whether any customer data or model internals were involved. It has not said whether this was a single episode or the end of a longer investigation. The questions below are set by what the company has said, and by what it has not.

What is known

The RTÉ report names the three as Jasmine Wang, Tomek Korbak and Mikita Balesni. All were researchers, and at least two worked on safety and alignment. According to the reports, the conduct involved work with an outside organisation that evaluates AI models. Reports describe the issue as sharing data with that group. OpenAI has not named the group.

The company's statement was blunt. "Our investigation confirmed that these individuals mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures, violating our policies and breaking the trust essential to our work," it said. A spokesperson added that OpenAI had "parted ways with three individuals for violating our policies on accessing and handling sensitive company information."

The firings were disclosed on Friday 2 October. OpenAI says they were not a response to anyone raising safety concerns.

The awkward timing

The optics are harder than the facts. In recent weeks all three had posted publicly about AI risk. On 10 September Balesni wrote: "i am at OpenAI and i think AI is >10% likely to kill all humans." The next day Korbak wrote that he was "quite unhappy with much of what OpenAI does." Wang had commented on how dangerous it is to speed towards recursive self-improvement.

That overlap does not show the dismissals were retaliation. Employees who criticise their employer can still break data rules, and a company is entitled to enforce them. But the overlap is the reason the case is drawing attention. Outsiders cannot tell the two explanations apart, because OpenAI has released no details and the three have not set out their side in the reports reviewed here.

The external evaluator also matters. Independent testing of frontier models is something regulators and labs say they want more of. It depends on labs giving outside groups controlled access to models, data and results. If a lab fires staff over how that access was handled, evaluators and the labs' own safety teams may read it as a signal. They may conclude that informal channels are risky. They may also conclude that formal ones need to be better defined. The reports do not say which of these is true here.

Why markets are watching

OpenAI is private, but it is large enough that its governance is a market question. It raises money at valuations that depend on investors trusting it to guard its most valuable assets: model weights, training methods and evaluation results. Any suggestion that those assets are loosely controlled is relevant to that trust.

One firing episode does not prove a pattern. A company that investigates, concludes and acts is arguably showing a working control, not a failed one. The risk is in the opacity. Without even a rough description of what was shared and with whom, investors, partners and regulators have to guess. The Bank of England's governor has already warned that stretched AI valuations are a stability risk (see Bailey's warning on AI valuations). Governance questions at the sector's best-known firm add to that scrutiny.

The case also arrives as Washington decides who oversees AI. Reports say Jay Clayton is expected to be named AI czar (see Clayton as AI czar). Reports also say several technology executives have signed a voluntary safety pledge at the White House. Voluntary commitments are only as credible as the internal controls behind them.

What to watch

Three things would settle much of this. The first is whether OpenAI says what category of information was involved and who the outside evaluator was. The second is whether any of the three responds publicly or challenges the dismissals. The third is whether the evaluation group says anything about its own handling of the material. Until then, the plain reading is a company enforcing a data policy against employees who were also vocal critics. Whether that was the whole story remains unestablished.
