Sánchez loses key housing vote after eviction of 87-year-old woman

In one sentence
Spain's minority government lost two housing-decree votes, 178–172 and 184–166, after Junts joined the right, exposing the fragility of Sánchez's majority ahead of 2027 elections.

Spain's minority government lost two votes in the Congress of Deputies on Friday. They were emergency decrees meant to calm a housing dispute that had turned into street protest. The first was rejected by 178 votes to 172 and the second by 184 to 166 in the 350-seat chamber. Pedro Sánchez's centre-left coalition needed the support of parties it does not control, and did not get it.

The defeats matter more for what they say about the government's room for manoeuvre than for the housing measures themselves. Elections are due in 2027.

A eviction that became a symbol

The decrees were a response to a single, televised event. On 23 September, María del Carmen Abascal, 87, known across Spain as Maricarmen, was evicted from her flat in central Madrid. She could not pay a rent increase imposed by Urbagestión, the company that owned the property. According to Al Jazeera, her rent had risen from €500 to €2,650 a month, against a pension of about €1,350.

The case landed on a country already angry about rents. Al Jazeera puts the average price per square metre at €15.10 this year, against €8.20 in 2016. France 24 reports an average monthly rent of €1,176, equal to 98.7% of the average young person's salary. A tent camp remains in Madrid's Puerta del Sol, and hundreds of demonstrators gathered outside Congress during the debate.

What was on the table

The first decree aimed to prevent evictions of vulnerable tenants, curb real-estate speculation, tighten rules on short-term lets and offer tax incentives for building rental housing. The second would have made leases renew automatically and indefinitely, unless the landlord paid the tenant 12 months' rent to end the contract.

The Popular Party and the far-right Vox voted against, as expected. The deciding vote came from Junts, the Catalan separatist party, whose support Sánchez has repeatedly had to negotiate. Its spokesperson, Miriam Nogueras, argued that the decrees would still let speculators buy property and evict residents. Junts's wider objection, as both outlets describe it, was that the measures would squeeze small landlords and push owners to pull flats off the rental market.

That objection is a substantive economic argument, not only a tactical one. Rent controls and indefinite leases are contested in economics, and a party whose votes the government needs can fairly cite it. The practical result is the same. The government cannot pass a flagship social measure when its parliamentary arithmetic depends on a party that sits to the right of it on property.

Reaction

After the results, protesters outside Congress chanted "shame on you" and "your profits, our misery". Housing Minister Pablo Bustinduy said the opposition had made "a huge mistake" and would "pay for this at the polls". The Spanish Tenants' Union has called for a general strike, and fresh demonstrations are planned across the country.

The government has set out no immediate next step. Neither report identifies any plan to bring back a revised text.

What it means for markets

The vote does not change Spanish fiscal rules or debt issuance. It does bear on the question investors ask of any minority government: whether it can pass legislation that matters to its own voters, and so whether it can pass budgets and reforms that matter to them. A single defeat on a decree is not a verdict on that. Minority governments lose votes, and Spain has had one in various forms for years.

The pattern is the thing to watch. A string of lost votes would raise the political-risk premium on Spanish sovereign debt and equities, particularly with 2027 elections approaching and Junts holding the balance. The next tests are the 2027 budget and any attempt to revive the housing measures in a form Junts can accept. Whether the street protests grow, or the general strike materialises, will show how much pressure the government faces to try again.
