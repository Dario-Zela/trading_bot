Half of UK wants to rejoin EU, megapoll finds

In one sentence
A large poll puts UK support for rejoining the EU at about 50%, but Labour has shown no sign of turning that sentiment into campaign positioning.

Britain's relationship with the European Union has been a settled matter of policy for more than five years and an unsettled one of opinion for nearly as long. A large new poll, reported by the Guardian, puts support for rejoining the bloc at about 50% of respondents. That is not a new high so much as a confirmation: sentiment has hovered around that level for some time, and the interesting question is why almost nothing in Westminster has changed as a result.

What the poll says

The headline figure is roughly half of those surveyed favouring a return to EU membership. Polls of this kind tend to be most useful for what they show over time rather than in any single release, and the stability of the number matters more than the number itself. Rejoin support has not collapsed, as some Brexit supporters predicted it would once the novelty of the argument wore off. Nor has it surged into the commanding majority that would force a political reckoning.

A caution is warranted. A reading of about 50% in favour of rejoining is a statement of preference, not of priority. Respondents who say they would back membership in the abstract are not necessarily voters who would change their ballot over it, and the poll does not tell us how many would rank the issue above the cost of living, energy bills or the state of public services. The suggestion that a pro-EU stance could pay electorally for Labour is an inference from the numbers, not something the numbers demonstrate.

The third rail

The more telling fact is on the supply side. There is no evidence that Labour has committed to a pro-rejoin strategy, and the evidence points the other way. Rejoining remains a third rail for both major parties: the Conservatives are bound to the Brexit settlement by their own history, and Labour's leadership has long preferred to speak of resetting relations within the existing framework rather than reopening the question of membership.

The logic is easy to reconstruct. Labour's electoral coalition includes seats that voted Leave in 2016 and that the party won back only recently. A rejoin pledge would risk alienating those voters in exchange for gains among people who, for the most part, already lean towards Labour or the Liberal Democrats. Public appetite and political risk-appetite are different quantities, and the gap between them is the real subject of this poll.

There is also a practical constraint. UK trade and foreign policy continue to operate within the post-Brexit architecture, and any government seeking closer alignment would need a negotiating partner in Brussels with its own views on timing, terms and the opt-outs Britain once held. Warmer sentiment at home does not change that.

What it means for markets

For readers who trade on policy shifts, the distinction is between sentiment and signal. Stable rejoin support limits how far any future government can drift from closer European ties, which shapes long-run optionality on trade, regulation and sterling's risk premium. But it moves markets only if a government actually changes course. Nothing in this poll suggests one is about to. Today's attention is on oil stocks and diesel prices and the Fed outlook, where policy is moving.

The next real test would come from party positioning rather than polling: whether Labour's conference season rhetoric, manifesto drafting or any approach to Brussels on trade and customs arrangements shows movement beyond a reset. Until then, a steady 50% remains a number waiting for someone willing to act on it.
