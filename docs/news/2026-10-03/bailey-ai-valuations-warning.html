Bailey cautions that stretched AI valuations pose a stability risk

In one sentence
Andrew Bailey warned that stretched AI valuations threaten financial stability, tying the risk to FTSE and gilts, but announced no new policy or analysis beyond earlier central-bank cautions.

The governor of the Bank of England has added his voice to a familiar warning: that the market's enthusiasm for artificial intelligence has run ahead of itself, and that the gap could become a problem for financial stability. Andrew Bailey tied the concern to UK portfolios, pointing to FTSE equities and gilt yields. He did not announce any change in policy.

A familiar message

The substance of the warning is not new. Central banks and financial regulators have been flagging stretched AI-related valuations for roughly 18 months. The argument runs in a consistent line. Prices for a narrow group of companies have risen on expectations of future earnings that have yet to arrive in full. If those expectations were marked down sharply, the losses would not stay inside the technology sector. They would travel through index funds, pension schemes and the collateral that underpins lending.

Bailey's remarks follow that template. Nothing in the triage material points to fresh analysis, a new stress-test scenario or a change in the Bank's own settings. That limits what the comments can tell investors. A governor repeating a known risk is a different signal from a governor changing the capital buffers or the liquidity rules that sit behind it.

Why the UK angle matters, and where it is thin

The link to the FTSE and to gilts is the one part of the message that is specific to Britain. It is also the part that invites the most questions.

The FTSE 100 is not an AI index. It is weighted towards banks, energy, miners, pharmaceuticals and consumer staples, and its direct exposure to the AI trade is modest next to that of the Nasdaq 100. The more plausible channel is indirect. A sharp repricing in US technology stocks would hit global risk appetite, move sterling and shift the demand for government bonds. That is the route by which gilt yields could be pulled around by a US equity correction.

That is a reasonable description of how a shock might spread. It is an inference about the mechanism, though, and the available material does not show Bailey quantifying it.

The market backdrop

The timing sits awkwardly against the tape. US equities have been setting records, with weak payrolls data pushing the Nasdaq 100 and Nvidia to new highs as bets on a Fed rate rise faded. Supply-chain reporting adds to the picture of heavy spending, including Korean press reports that Samsung will triple HBM prices next year. Rising component costs of that kind feed the capital-expenditure bills that the valuations assume will pay off.

A warning from a regulator is not a call on timing. Regulators have cautioned about valuations through a long rally without being proved wrong or right on a particular date. Market participants have so far treated such statements as background rather than as a trigger.

What to watch

The next substantive markers are institutional rather than rhetorical. The Bank's Financial Policy Committee publishes its record and Financial Stability Report on a regular schedule, and any move from general language to a named vulnerability, a scenario with numbers attached or a change in buffers would carry more weight than a speech. Until then, the comments are best read as confirmation that the risk is on the regulators' list, not as a sign that any action is imminent.
