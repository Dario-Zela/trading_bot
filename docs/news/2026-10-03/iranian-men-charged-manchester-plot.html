Two Iranian men charged over alleged plot to target Manchester's Jewish community

In one sentence
Two Iranian men are charged over an alleged Yom Kippur-eve bomb plot in Manchester, with a possible Iran-based contact that will test how far London escalates.

Two Iranian men have been charged with preparing acts of terrorism over an alleged plot against Manchester's Jewish community. Police say the plot was disrupted on the eve of Yom Kippur. Counter-terrorism officers say the pair were in contact with someone overseas who may be in Iran. That is not the same as showing the Iranian state directed the plot, and the distinction will shape what London does next.

The charges

Rahman Salehi, 35, and Salam Ahmadyan, 36, were arrested in Manchester's Northern Quarter on 20 September, hours before the holiest day in the Jewish calendar. They are charged with engaging in conduct in preparation for terrorist acts, or assisting another person to commit them. Both are due at Westminster Magistrates' Court on Saturday.

Police allege the men tried to acquire components and equipment for an improvised explosive device and carried out reconnaissance of potential targets. They also allege contact with an overseas third party who "might be in Iran". Officials have not publicly said who that person is, or whether the contact was with an official, an intermediary or a criminal go-between.

The Home Office says both men arrived by small boat from Europe, Ahmadyan in 2020 and Salehi in 2023. One was granted asylum and the other has a live claim. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has drawn attention to that, so the case will feed the debate over small-boat arrivals as well as the one over Iran.

The timing carries weight. The arrests came a year after an attack at Manchester's Heaton Park synagogue in which two worshippers were killed. The community has had little time to feel secure since.

A pattern, not a one-off

Vicki Evans, the senior national coordinator for counter-terrorism policing, said the Manchester case is unrelated to other recent incidents. She added that "this week also brought into sharp focus some of the wider threats being projected into the UK from Iran."

She was referring to the arrests of five British nationals near RAF Fairford, a US Air Force base in Gloucestershire, after suspicious vehicles approached the site on Sunday. A dual UK-Iranian national was arrested on Thursday in connection with that incident. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said there are "strong indications that Tehran played a part". Iran has denied involvement in both cases.

British authorities have also described Iranian use of criminal proxies and hired intermediaries in earlier cases, including surveillance of Jewish targets and of Iranian dissidents in London. Using proxies gives Tehran deniability. It also makes attribution slower and more contested in court.

What it means for UK-Iran relations

The policy question is how far the evidence can be pushed in public. Prosecutors will have to prove the charges to a criminal standard, and ministers are constrained in what they can say while proceedings are live. Contempt rules limit commentary on the defendants. Intelligence assessments of state involvement can be made without being proved in a courtroom, but acting on them has usually followed a slower path.

Three routes are open to the government:

  • Sanctions. Designations of individuals and entities tied to Iran's security apparatus can be made quickly and need no conviction.
  • Diplomatic measures. These range from summoning the ambassador to expelling staff or downgrading the mission.
  • Legal tools. Pressure has been building for a state-threats proscription regime for hostile actors, which would sit alongside the terrorism powers already used in these cases.

Any of these would harden a relationship that is already strained. The Fairford incident and the Manchester charges within the same week give ministers a political case for escalation. Against that, London has reasons to keep a channel to Tehran open, including regional security, shipping and consular cases involving detained dual nationals.

For markets, the direct exposure is modest, since UK-Iran trade is minimal. The channel is indirect: a tighter sanctions posture feeds into the geopolitical risk premium on energy and into defence-sector sentiment. That matters at a time when G7 governments are releasing emergency oil and diesel stocks.

What to watch

The first hearing at Westminster Magistrates' Court will set out the allegations in more detail and send the case up the court system. The government's next statements on Fairford and Manchester are the better guide. If ministers move from "indications" to a formal attribution of state responsibility, sanctions or diplomatic action would be likely to follow in days rather than months. If they hold back, that would suggest the evidence on the overseas contact is thinner than the political rhetoric implies.
