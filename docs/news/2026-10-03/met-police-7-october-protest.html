Met Police urge students to move 7 October protest

In one sentence
The Met has asked student organisers to move a demonstration planned for the 7 October anniversary, citing public-order risk, while talks over venue and timing continue.

The Metropolitan Police have asked the organisers of a student-led demonstration to move it, arguing that holding it on 7 October risks disorder. The date is the anniversary of the 2023 Hamas attack on Israel, and the request has put a familiar question back on the table: how far the police can go in steering a protest before steering becomes restricting.

The force's concern, as relayed by the BBC, is public order. The details of the venue and the timing are still being worked through between the two sides. Organisers and police have been talking, and that is the part worth noting. This is a request, not a ban.

A request, not an order

In English law the police have two broad levers over a march or assembly. One is persuasion: an officer or liaison team asks organisers to change the route, the hour or the place, and organisers can say yes, no or "let's talk". The other is formal conditions, or a prohibition, under the Public Order Act 1986. Those powers are tied to specific tests, such as serious disorder, serious disruption to the life of the community, or intimidation. They are also open to challenge.

A public request to move sits between the two. It signals what the force may do if talks fail, and it puts on record that the police tried the lighter touch first. It also puts the organisers in a spot. Agree, and they may be accused of giving ground on a date they chose on purpose. Refuse, and any conditions that follow arrive with a paper trail.

Why the date matters

The organisers chose 7 October deliberately, and the police are reacting to that choice. For some people the day is one of mourning for the Israelis killed in the 2023 attack. For others it is the start of a war whose toll on Gaza has become the main subject of campus politics. Both groups can claim the calendar, and a single street on that date can hold both.

That is the practical worry behind "public-order concerns". Police planning for contested anniversaries usually weighs the risk of rival gatherings meeting, the effect on nearby communities, and how many officers the day can spare. The force has not, in the reporting available, published the specific intelligence behind its request. Without it, outsiders cannot judge whether the risk is as large as the force says.

What to watch

The first marker is whether organisers agree to relocate or reschedule before the day. If they hold firm, the Met would have to decide whether to impose formal conditions. Any such conditions could be challenged by organisers, and criticised by civil-liberties groups and by those who think the force has been too permissive. The date is fixed and the days are few. The talks need to settle by 7 October.

There is no direct market angle here. It is a story about who gets to mark a painful date, and where.
