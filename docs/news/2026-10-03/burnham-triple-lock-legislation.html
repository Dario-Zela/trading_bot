Burnham to legislate to scrap the pension triple lock before the next election

In one sentence
Burnham will legislate before the next election to curb the pension triple lock, saving a reported £15bn a year by the late 2030s, but the replacement formula is unpublished.

The government intends to legislate to change the state pension triple lock before the next general election. The move would end a guarantee that has been politically untouchable for a decade. Prime Minister Andy Burnham announced it in his conference speech. The lock stays in place until 2030, in line with Labour's manifesto pledge, so no pensioner would see a smaller rise before then.

The lock raises the state pension each year by the highest of earnings growth, inflation or 2.5%. Burnham's plan, as reported, would not abolish it outright. The earnings element would be adjusted to prevent unusually large increases when wages jump. The detail is thin. No replacement indexation formula has been published, and nothing has been said about the timing of a bill beyond "during the current parliament".

The numbers

The government's own estimate, as reported, is that the adjustment would reduce state pension spending by about £15bn a year by the end of the 2030s and by about £50bn a year by 2050. The gain is back-loaded. Little of it arrives inside the forecast window that the Office for Budget Responsibility uses to judge whether fiscal rules are met.

The back-loading explains why this matters more for the long-run debt trajectory than for next year's gilt auctions. State pension spending is among the largest and fastest-growing lines in the budget. A rule that ratchets it up faster than the economy grows compounds the pressure on long-dated gilt supply. A credible change would trim the long-run path at the margin. It would not alter the near-term borrowing figures.

The politics

The political cost is immediate, and the fiscal benefit comes later. Pensioners vote at higher rates than any other age group. Sharon Graham, general secretary of Unite, called ending the triple lock "electoral suicide". Critics have also noted the tension with the earlier promise to leave the mechanism alone until 2030.

The legislative route is deliberate. By requiring MPs to vote before the election, the government forces every party to say where it stands. Reports say the parties would then go into the election setting out in their manifestos whether to keep the new rules or repeal them. That is a way of sharing the political risk. It also means the policy is only durable if a future government declines to reverse it, and an opposition keen on the pensioner vote has an obvious reason to promise a reversal.

What gilt investors will read

Investors will separate signal from substance. The signal is positive: a government willing to touch the most protected item in the budget is showing some appetite for consolidation. The substance is not yet there, because a savings figure for the 2040s is not a deficit-reduction plan for this parliament. Its credibility depends on three things: the text of the bill, the formula that replaces the earnings link, and whether the OBR scores the savings in its forecasts.

The next checkpoints are the publication of draft legislation and the autumn fiscal event, where the OBR's long-term treatment of pension spending will show how much weight the market should give the plan. Until a bill is tabled and survives a Commons vote, the announcement remains an intention rather than a policy.
