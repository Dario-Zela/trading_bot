BMW triples motor-finance mis-selling provision to £612m as automakers challenge FCA redress scheme

BMW's Munich headquarters, where the group's British finance arm reports into. — Saptarshi Pal / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BMW's UK provision for car-loan mis-selling claims nearly tripled to £612m as Mercedes-Benz, VW and Crédit Agricole fight the FCA's redress scheme in the Upper Tribunal.

BMW's British finance arm has nearly tripled the money it has set aside to cover motor finance mis-selling claims, to £612m, in newly filed accounts that tipped the division into a pre-tax loss of £139m for 2025 — a sharp reversal from the £39m profit it posted the year before.

The provision, up from £206.9m in 2024 and just £70.3m the year before that, is BMW Financial Services (GB)'s best guess at what it will owe under the Financial Conduct Authority's industry-wide redress scheme for historic car loans. But BMW is hedging even that number. In the accounts, the company warns the final bill could be "materially different" — in either direction — because the scheme itself is now under sustained legal attack from rival lenders who think the regulator has overreached.

A £9bn scheme built on discretionary commission

The scandal traces back to discretionary commission arrangements, or DCAs, under which car dealers could raise the interest rate a customer paid on a loan in exchange for a bigger cut from the lender — a structure the FCA banned in 2021 but which had operated largely undisclosed for over a decade beforehand. After the Supreme Court narrowed the scope of lender liability in a ruling last summer, the regulator pressed ahead anyway, publishing its policy statement, PS26/3, on 30 March. It estimates around 12.1 million agreements are eligible, with total redress of roughly £7.5bn and an all-in sector cost, including administration, of £9.1bn. The average payout works out to about £830 per agreement, with interest added at the Bank of England base rate plus one percentage point, floored at 3%.

Set against that backdrop, BMW's £612m looks proportionate rather than alarmist — it sits behind Lloyds Banking Group, the UK's largest car lender through Black Horse, which has now provisioned £1.95bn, but ahead of Santander (£295m) and Close Brothers (around £300m). Collectively, the numbers being booked by lenders are converging on the FCA's own estimate of the total damage.

Lenders take the fight to the Upper Tribunal

What has changed since the spring is that a bloc of lenders no longer accepts the scheme is inevitable. Volkswagen Financial Services and the French bank Crédit Agricole Auto Finance filed the first challenge, with Mercedes-Benz Financial Services and the claims group Consumer Voice joining separately. Their argument, put to the Upper Tribunal by Lord Keen of Elie KC for Mercedes-Benz, is that the FCA exceeded its statutory authority and that the scheme's method — treating an unspecified commission structure as an automatic breach requiring redress — interferes disproportionately with lenders' property rights under the European Convention on Human Rights. Lawyers for Volkswagen told the tribunal the interest-rate reductions used to calculate redress "are not reasonable proxies for loss and are not evidentially supported."

The FCA has pushed back hard, telling the tribunal its approach is the fastest and cheapest route to certainty for both consumers and firms, and that any alternative would be "slower and much more costly." It has not been enough to keep the scheme moving on schedule. On 2 July, the tribunal suspended the parts of the scheme requiring lenders to calculate and pay compensation, on terms agreed with the four challengers, while leaving other rules — record-keeping, complaint-handling — in force. A full hearing is now pencilled in for 14–18 December, with a fallback window in February 2027 if that slot slips, and a judgment is not expected until some months after that.

Why BMW is provisioning anyway

That a scheme worth £9bn to the industry is suspended pending a human-rights challenge might argue for lenders sitting tight rather than booking bigger numbers. BMW's decision to nearly triple its provision regardless suggests the finance arm reads the challenge as a fight over the mechanics of redress — how commission-linked loss gets calculated, how much interest accrues — rather than a serious bid to kill the scheme outright. Accounting rules also compel provisioning against a probable liability whether or not litigation is pending, and BMW's UK book of DCA-era loans is large enough that even a partial win for the challengers would only trim, not erase, the exposure.

The next marker is the December tribunal hearing, or its February fallback if the calendar slips again. Until a judgment lands, redress payments to consumers under the suspended limbs of the scheme stay frozen — leaving BMW, Lloyds and the rest of the sector holding provisions that may prove either too cautious or not nearly cautious enough, depending on what four judges in the Rolls Building decide about the limits of the FCA's power to make lenders pay.
