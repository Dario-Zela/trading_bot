Netflix hit with second analyst downgrade in a week on YouTube threat

In one sentence
HSBC cut Netflix to Hold and its target to $76 from $96 on YouTube's rising TV time share, but the two metrics may not be measuring the same kind of viewing.

Netflix has now been downgraded twice in five days, and both notes point at the same culprit: YouTube. HSBC cut the stock to Hold from Buy on Tuesday, slashing its price target by 21% to $76 from $96, and pinned the call on a single, striking data point — Netflix's share of US TV viewing time fell to 7.8% in July, a multi-year low, while YouTube climbed to a record 14.2%.

That follows Wells Fargo's move eight days earlier, on September 18, cutting Netflix to Underweight and its target to $57 from $80 — a 24% reduction — on what analyst Steven Cahall titled simply "Engagement Risk." Netflix shares closed at $71.82 on Tuesday, down 2.1% on the day and more than 20% for the year, putting the stock on track for its worst year since 2022.

The case for concern

HSBC's Mohammed Khallouf built his downgrade around Nielsen's monthly Gauge report, which tracks how Americans split their television time across broadcast, cable and streaming platforms. YouTube's climb to 14.2% extended its lead over every other distributor by five full percentage points, while Netflix's 7.8% marked a level not seen in years. Khallouf wrote that "near-term recovery in engagement looks unlikely," citing a cooling reception for Netflix's original slate.

Wells Fargo's note added a harder number to the same worry: viewing time per subscriber per day fell 8% in the first half of 2026 compared with the same period in 2023, and the bank's base case assumes a 21% year-over-year decline in hours watched across Netflix's own top 100 original titles in the back half of the year. Both banks also flagged the spending gap — HSBC estimates YouTube will pay creators roughly $23 billion in 2026, against Netflix's approximately $20 billion in cash content spend, a reversal from the era when Netflix comfortably outspent every rival on programming.

The category question

What the downgrades gloss over is what, exactly, is being compared. Nielsen's Gauge measures total minutes of television-screen time, and it does not distinguish between someone watching a 90-minute film on Netflix and someone watching a string of five-minute product reviews or gaming clips on YouTube through a connected TV. YouTube's growth has been driven substantially by that kind of casual, algorithm-fed viewing rather than scripted, appointment programming — the category Netflix was built to dominate.

That distinction matters for the trade. A time-share chart makes for a clean downgrade thesis, but it treats a Marvel-style franchise series and a fifteen-minute unboxing video as equivalent units of attention. eMarketer estimates put US adults spending only about three more minutes per day with YouTube than with Netflix in 2026 — a much narrower gap than the Nielsen share numbers imply, and one where YouTube's edge comes disproportionately from short-form and creator content rather than from prestige drama or film poaching Netflix subscribers directly.

What's actually moving

The more defensible read is narrower than "YouTube is eating Netflix": YouTube has become a genuine competitor for a slice of connected-TV attention that didn't used to have alternatives, and Netflix's own content output has softened enough that even its flagship titles are drawing smaller audiences than in past years — the 21% projected decline in Wells Fargo's top-100 hours estimate is a Netflix-specific problem, not a YouTube one. Both things can be true without validating the framing that Netflix and YouTube are now fighting over the same viewer in the same moment.

Wall Street, notably, has not swung wholesale behind the bearish view. Of 52 analysts tracked by LSEG, 38 still rate Netflix a buy or strong buy, meaning two downgrades in a week are a shift in tone, not a consensus break. HSBC's own $76 target sits barely 3% above Tuesday's close — a hold call in substance as well as in name.

The next real test lands with Netflix's third-quarter earnings in October, when the company reports engagement metrics and subscriber additions against a comparison period that both banks now consider weak. If viewing hours stabilize, this week's downgrades look like an overreaction to a noisy chart. If they don't, the YouTube framing will matter less than the plainer explanation: Netflix's own content slate needs to get better.
