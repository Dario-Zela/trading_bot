UK to help refuel Saudi jets countering Houthi attacks in Red Sea

An RAF Voyager tanker refuels Typhoon jets during a Middle East sortie flown from RAF Akrotiri — the same base from which Britain's Saudi refuelling mission will operate. — Cpl Nathan Edwards / UK MOD, Open Government Licence
In one sentence
Britain will refuel Saudi jets defending against Houthi attacks after the group seized the Bab al-Mandab strait and struck near Riyadh.

Britain has agreed to help refuel Saudi Arabian fighter jets defending the kingdom against Houthi missile and drone attacks, a modest-sounding commitment that nonetheless marks Britain's deepest military involvement yet in a war reshaping one of the world's busiest shipping corridors.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham approved the request as he flew to New York for the UN General Assembly on Monday. "We have received a request from the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia for military support, and on the advice of the defence secretary and the foreign secretary, last night I agreed to that request for defensive air-to-air refuelling," he said. A single RAF Voyager tanker, operating out of RAF Akrotiri in Cyprus, will begin topping up Saudi aircraft in the coming days. British officials were careful to stress the limits: the Voyager will support jets defending Saudi airspace from incoming drones and missiles, not aircraft striking targets inside Yemen. The deployment is expected to run a matter of weeks but will be kept under review — the kind of formula that tends to stretch.

A fast-moving front

The request came after a Houthi missile aimed at Riyadh was intercepted on Saturday, the first strike to target the Saudi capital in years. It followed a punishing few weeks in which the Iran-aligned group pushed south along Yemen's coast, seizing the port of Mocha and the Hanish and Perim islands to complete control of the Bab al-Mandab strait — the narrow gateway between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil and container traffic normally passes. Earlier this month, Houthi missiles and drones struck Abha, Jizan, Najran and Khamis Mushait in southern Saudi Arabia, wounding 73 people and setting fire to oil and utility facilities. Fighting near the coast has killed more than 300 people, part of a wider conflict tracing back to Iran's confrontation with the United States and its regional allies.

The stakes for global markets are already visible in crude prices, which have climbed roughly $25 a barrel since early July as traders price in the risk to a corridor that handles a large slice of global trade. Burnham tied the refuelling decision directly to that arithmetic, saying Britain needed to "secure the interests of Britain and the wider region" and keep oil-shipping routes open, warning that further disruption would feed through to pump prices at home.

What London has not done is commit to any offensive role, and officials are keen to draw a bright line between refuelling defensive sorties and joining the fight over Yemen's coastline. Whether that line holds will depend on how the Houthis respond to a war they are currently winning, and whether Riyadh's request for help stays a request for fuel rather than firepower. The next marker is whether Saudi Arabia asks for more.
