Ionis Pharmaceuticals faces FDA decision on first targeted Alexander disease treatment

FDA headquarters at the White Oak campus in Silver Spring, Maryland, where the agency approved Zanvastro ahead of its scheduled decision date. — U.S. Food and Drug Administration / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The FDA approved Ionis's zilganersen (Zanvastro) as the first treatment for ultra-rare Alexander disease, but peak sales guidance of just over $100 million makes it a minor financial catalyst.

The Food and Drug Administration approved Ionis Pharmaceuticals' zilganersen on September 3rd, nearly three weeks ahead of its September 22nd target decision date, making it the first disease-modifying treatment for Alexander disease — an ultra-rare, usually fatal brain disorder that has had no approved therapy until now.

The drug, sold under the brand name Zanvastro, is an antisense oligonucleotide: a short synthetic strand of genetic material designed to reduce production of a specific protein rather than treat symptoms after the fact. In Alexander disease, mutations cause astrocytes — support cells in the brain — to overproduce glial fibrillary acidic protein, or GFAP, which clumps into structures called Rosenthal fibers and progressively damages the brain's white matter. Zilganersen is engineered to suppress GFAP output at the mRNA stage, before the protein accumulates.

Alexander disease is rare enough that most neurologists will never encounter a case. Ionis estimates roughly 300 diagnosed patients in the United States, and global estimates cluster around 2,000 to 3,000, though the company and outside researchers both suspect the disease is underdiagnosed. The infantile form, the most common presentation, brings seizures and enlarged head circumference within the first two years of life; most children with it do not survive past six. Juvenile and adult forms progress more slowly but still carry a median survival of 14 to 25 years from diagnosis, according to case-series data cited by MedLink Neurology.

What the trial showed

The pivotal study enrolled 53 to 54 patients aged roughly 2 to 53, randomized 2-to-1 to zilganersen or a control arm and dosed every 12 weeks for a 60-to-61-week double-blind period. In patients five and older, the drug met its primary endpoint: gait speed on the 10-meter walk test showed a statistically significant 33.3 percent difference versus control at week 61 (p=0.041) — read as stabilization of a measure that typically declines in untreated patients, rather than an outright reversal. In children aged 2 to 4, a secondary gross-motor-function measure showed a 22.9-point difference (nominal p=0.034). Holly Kordasiewicz, Ionis's head of development, described a flat line in a degenerative disease as "really remarkable"; the FDA's Emily Freilich called the approval a "landmark moment" for the small patient community, according to trade press covering the announcement.

A modest catalyst, priced accordingly

Ionis has set Zanvastro's list price at $285,000 per dose, or roughly $1.14 million a year at the quarterly dosing schedule — before rebates, insurance negotiation or patient-assistance discounts, which for ultra-rare disease drugs typically absorb a substantial share of list price. Even so, the company itself guides to only "more than $100 million" in peak annual sales; William Blair's published model puts peak U.S. sales nearer $160 million by 2040. Both figures are trivial against a company whose shares have fallen more than 40 percent this year after phase 3 misses for pelacarsen and eplontersen, and whose second-quarter revenue dropped 40.7 percent year-over-year to $268 million with a $102 million net loss. Zanvastro matters less for its own revenue than as evidence Ionis's antisense platform can still clear the FDA and support an independent commercial launch — a signal analysts appear to be weighing more heavily than the ultra-rare addressable market would otherwise justify. The next test of that thesis arrives with regulatory decisions on Ionis's Angelman syndrome candidate, obudanersen.
