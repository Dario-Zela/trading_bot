Burnham's approval holds at +15%, six years after Starmer's collapse

Andy Burnham's official portrait, taken at 10 Downing Street shortly after he became prime minister in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Burnham's net approval holds at +15%, but Labour's edge over a resurgent Reform UK and Conservatives is narrow enough to vanish in one bad month.

Six years of prime ministers arriving and leaving faster than they could unpack their boxes had left Britain's political class braced for another wobble. Instead, ten weeks after Andy Burnham replaced Keir Starmer, his numbers are, for now, holding.

BritPolls, which averages YouGov, Ipsos and Savanta tracking, puts Burnham's net approval at +15.2% this month — a rating that would have seemed fantastical to Starmer, who left office at −23.8% net after a year in which his government cut winter fuel payments for pensioners, raised taxes in an autumn budget, then reversed course on welfare reform under backbench pressure. It was the lowest final rating Ipsos has recorded for a departing prime minister since it began tracking in 1977. Ipsos's own favourability series tells a similar story of recovery: Burnham rose from net +1% in his first days in office to net +13% by late August, helped by a 50-29% lead over Nigel Farage when voters are asked to choose directly between a Burnham government and a Farage one.

The bounce, cooling

That honeymoon has begun to fray. Ipsos polling conducted 10-15 September found only 31% of Britons satisfied with Burnham's performance as prime minister against 44% dissatisfied — a net of −13% on that specific measure, and a 14-point jump in dissatisfaction over the month. Pollsters caution the shift looks more like undecided voters forming opinions than a reversal: the "don't know" share fell 15 points over the same period. Still, the gap between a resilient personal favourability rating and a souring verdict on his government's record is now the story — Burnham remains comfortably the best-liked senior figure in British politics, but liking him and approving of his government are no longer quite the same question.

A three-way squeeze, not a realignment

Vote intention shows why Labour is not celebrating. BritPolls' September average has Labour at 26.8%, Reform UK at 23%, and the Conservatives — written off after their 2024 wipeout — recovering to 20%. Ipsos's own poll in the same window found a similar four-point Labour lead over Reform, down from earlier in the year when Reform briefly led outright. None of this looks like realignment so much as a stalemate: three parties bunched within striking distance of each other, none commanding a lead wide enough to settle the argument.

That stalemate is precisely Downing Street's dilemma. Burnham has resisted pressure to seek an early dissolution while Labour holds even a narrow edge — a bet that trades away his best current position for years of governing without a fresh personal mandate. The next general election is not legally required until August 2029, but with Reform and the Conservatives both a normal-sized swing away from overtaking Labour, every monthly poll between now and then will be read as a referendum on whether the bounce was ever more than relief that Starmer was gone.
