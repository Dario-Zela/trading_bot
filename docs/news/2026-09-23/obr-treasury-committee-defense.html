MPs tell colleagues to stop 'shooting the messenger' on the OBR ahead of October Budget

Dame Meg Hillier, chair of the Commons Treasury Committee, which defended the OBR's independence ahead of the October Budget. — Roger Harris / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
MPs on the Treasury Committee defended the OBR against attacks from left and right, five weeks before its forecasts underpin Healey's 28 October Budget.

Britain's fiscal watchdog has spent the past year fending off attacks from almost every direction — from Conservatives who say its forecasts are chronically wrong, from Labour backbenchers who say they're treated as gospel, and from a leak scandal that cost its chairman his job. On Tuesday, the cross-party MPs who oversee it decided enough was enough.

The Commons Treasury Committee published a report dismissing what it called "siren calls to fundamentally reform" the Office for Budget Responsibility, five weeks before Chancellor John Healey delivers a Budget on 28 October that will lean heavily on the OBR's numbers to justify whatever mix of tax rises and spending restraint he proposes. The message to critics, in the words of committee chair Dame Meg Hillier: stop shooting the messenger.

"A wielder of dark powers"

Hillier's committee — a body that includes MPs from all the major parties — said the OBR had increasingly become a proxy target for politicians unhappy with the constraints of the public finances rather than with the forecaster's methodology. "Certain policymakers and commentators like to characterise the OBR as a wielder of dark powers with a stranglehold on Treasury ministers and officials," Hillier said. "It's simply not true."

The committee's underlying argument is one familiar to anyone who has watched fiscal policy in London for the past fifteen years: the OBR does not set the rules, it measures compliance with them. "The fiscal rules, and therefore the OBR's forecasts, become more important when government spending operates on razor-thin margins," the report noted — an observation with particular bite this year, since gilt-market moves have already done more damage to Healey's headroom than any OBR judgment call. Thirty-year gilt yields touched their highest level since 1998 in September, and independent estimates suggest the roughly £26bn of fiscal headroom pencilled in at the last forecast could be closer to £14bn by the time the OBR publishes its next one alongside the Budget.

Attacks from both flanks

What makes the committee's intervention notable is that the criticism it is pushing back against comes from opposite ends of the political spectrum, for opposite reasons.

On the right, senior Conservatives — Kemi Badenoch among them — have long argued the OBR's growth and borrowing forecasts are simply unreliable, pointing to a track record of misjudging productivity trends and inflation shocks. That critique hardened during the Truss government's brief, doomed attempt to sideline the watchdog altogether in 2022, and it has resurfaced whenever OBR numbers force uncomfortable choices on the government of the day.

On the left of Labour, the complaint is almost the inverse: not that the OBR is wrong, but that ministers treat a forecast as though it were a fact. Dozens of Red Wall-aligned Labour MPs, led by Bassetlaw's Jo White and joined by figures such as Andy MacNae, have urged the Prime Minister to stop letting twice-yearly OBR headroom numbers dictate the pace of spending decisions. "The OBR has never claimed to have a crystal ball," MacNae has argued, "and we shouldn't treat it as if it does." White wants the government to acknowledge the OBR's "limitations" more openly rather than treating its central forecast as a fixed ceiling.

The Treasury Committee's report tries to answer both flanks in one move — proposing that ministers ask the OBR to produce a new 10-year forecast to sit alongside its existing five- and fifty-year projections, giving policymakers a longer lens without touching the five-year forecast against which the fiscal rules are actually judged.

A watchdog still recovering

The committee's defence lands against an unusually raw backdrop. The OBR's previous chair, Richard Hughes, resigned in December 2025 after the office's own investigation found that its pre-Budget economic and fiscal outlook had leaked — reportedly because parts of the document sat at a predictable web address — in what the OBR itself called the worst failure in its history. The leak briefly rattled markets on the day it happened and left the institution's internal controls, not just its forecasting judgment, under scrutiny just as the row over its independence was intensifying.

That history matters for how markets should read this month's committee report. An OBR under sustained political pressure, still rebuilding credibility after an operational failure, needs both operational competence and institutional independence intact if its October numbers are going to do the job markets rely on them for: giving traders, ratings agencies and gilt investors a broadly trusted read on how the government's fiscal rules stack up against reality. A forecaster seen as either compromised by politics or prone to leaks is one whose numbers get discounted — precisely the outcome a committee of MPs from all parties is now trying to head off.

What's next

Healey's Budget on 28 October will be the first real test of whether the committee's intervention holds. The OBR will publish a fresh Economic and Fiscal Outlook alongside it, and with headroom already squeezed by higher gilt yields, the numbers are likely to force genuine trade-offs rather than presentational ones. Whether Labour's backbenchers accept those trade-offs without renewing pressure on the watchdog — and whether the Conservatives use any forecast miss to revive calls for deeper reform — will show whether Tuesday's cross-party statement was a genuine truce or simply a marker laid down before the argument resumes.
