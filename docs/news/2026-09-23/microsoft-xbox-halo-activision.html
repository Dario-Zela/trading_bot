Microsoft cuts 268 more Xbox jobs, hands next Halo game to Activision

Microsoft's Redmond, Washington campus, home to Xbox and Halo Studios — Jiaqian AirplaneFan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Xbox cut 268 more jobs and handed the next Halo game to Activision, deepening a reset that has already shed thousands of gaming roles since 2023.

Microsoft cut another 268 jobs across its Xbox game studios on Monday, and buried inside the announcement was a bigger signal than the headcount number: the next mainline Halo game will not be made by the team that has owned the franchise for nearly two decades. Activision — the Call of Duty publisher Microsoft bought for $69 billion in 2023 — is taking over.

The cuts hit Halo Studios (the outfit formerly known as 343 Industries) along with other first-party teams and the Xbox Game Studios management layer. Xbox chief content officer Matt Booty framed it as sharpening focus: "strengthen our franchises and games by operating fewer business units." In practice, it means Halo Studios keeps a small crew to patch existing titles and run the esports scene, while a newly assembled Activision team — separate from the Call of Duty group — builds whatever comes next in the series.

A franchise changes hands

Halo has been Microsoft's console flagship since 2001, and 343 Industries was built in 2007 specifically to keep it alive after Bungie split off. Handing the next entry to Activision's people — even under the Microsoft umbrella — is a real break from that lineage, not a reshuffle of org chart boxes. Activision president Rob Kostich said the studio has "already begun assembling a purpose-built team" with the goal of making "the greatest Halo game ever, worthy of its universe and legacy." Standard launch-day language, but the subtext is that Microsoft now trusts its shooter specialists more than its own in-house studio to deliver one.

Activision isn't just getting Halo. It also absorbs Rare (Sea of Thieves) and World's Edge (Age of Empires), pulling more of Xbox's biggest franchises under the publisher that used to be a rival before the acquisition.

Part of a longer reset

Monday's cuts extend a restructuring Xbox began in July, when it announced roughly 3,200 job losses through the current fiscal year and spun off four studios — Ninja Theory, Undead Labs, Compulsion Games and Double Fine — either independently or into new ownership. That earlier round is now about three-quarters complete. Undead Labs has since gone fully independent, with State of Decay 3 still slated for a Game Pass day-one release. Ninja Theory's situation looks worse: two separate deals to sell the studio have fallen through, and closure is now the likelier outcome. Obsidian Entertainment is being folded into Bethesda, Turn 10 Studios is merging with Playground Games, and Microsoft's casual-games unit is moving under King.

Zoom out further and the pattern is longer than one bad year. Since closing the Activision Blizzard deal in October 2023, Microsoft has now cut gaming staff in waves — 1,900 jobs in January 2024, a further 650 that September, and thousands more in this year's reset. Xbox employed roughly 22,000 people at the time of the acquisition; a meaningful share of that workforce is gone.

Why hand off the crown jewel

Moving Halo to Activision reads less like a vote of no confidence in any individual and more like Microsoft admitting that maintaining a large, standalone first-party studio around a single franchise is expensive relative to what it returns. Xbox has spent the past two years pushing hardware and exclusivity into the background in favor of Game Pass subscriptions and multiplatform releases — Halo titles and other former exclusives already ship on PlayStation. Consolidating development into Activision's existing shooter infrastructure fits that same logic: fewer standalone units, shared pipelines, lower fixed cost, and games funneled toward the subscription service rather than console-exclusive bragging rights.

For a company whose capital is increasingly pointed at AI infrastructure and Azure, a gaming division that runs leaner — even at the cost of a storied studio's independence — is the trade Microsoft appears willing to make.

What happens to Ninja Theory is the next marker to watch, along with whatever Activision's "purpose-built" Halo team actually ships — no release window has been given, and Microsoft's fiscal year runs through June 2027, when the remainder of the July cuts are due to complete.
