Reform-linked thinktank calls for abolition of the state pension and £75bn of tax cuts

Nigel Farage's Reform UK has pledged to protect the pensions triple lock, even as an allied think tank calls for the state pension to be scrapped entirely. — Official portrait, UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Reform-linked think tank wants to scrap the UK state pension and cut £75bn in taxes — but Reform itself has pledged to keep the pensions triple lock.

A think tank with close ties to Reform UK wants Nigel Farage's party to scrap the state pension entirely — and Farage's party wants nothing to do with the idea, at least not yet.

The Centre for a Better Britain, founded last year by Jonathan Brown, Reform's former chief operating officer, will formally launch a 183-page report called "Boosting Britain" at a private City event this week. Its centrepiece: replace the state pension with mandatory "lifetime investment accounts," seeded with a £1,000 payment at birth in the mould of Donald Trump's child savings accounts in the US, topped up with contributions of up to 15% of earnings. A means-tested safety net would remain for those who fall short. Brown's justification is blunt. "The issue with the state pension is that it's essentially a Ponzi scheme," he has told reporters, arguing that today's workers are propping up retirees with no claim on what they pay in.

The pension plan sits inside a wider £75 billion package of tax cuts: abolition of capital gains tax and inheritance tax, scrapping stamp duty on property and share trades, the end of the digital services tax and air passenger duty, and a cut in corporation tax from 25% to 15% phased over eight years. The report also calls for looser capital rules for UK banks, a sliding scale for the bank levy, closing public-sector pension schemes to new teachers and civil servants, folding much of the Treasury into a new "Department for Economic Growth," and tying Bank of England leadership pay to inflation performance.

A think tank, not (officially) the party

The report lands awkwardly for Reform. Farage has repeatedly said the party would keep the pensions triple lock and has ruled out means-testing, telling audiences the party debated the matter and settled on protecting it. Reform's official line is that it is "not associated with any think-tanks" — a claim that sits uneasily alongside CFABB sharing office space with the party at Millbank Tower and Brown's reported direct contact with Farage. The think tank is backed by investors Mark Thompson and David Lilley and says it is aiming to raise £25 million; its advisory board is chaired by academic James Orr, a prominent figure in the national-conservative movement.

For markets, the report is a straw in the wind rather than a policy signal with teeth — there is no bill, no costed manifesto commitment, and Reform is not in government. But it lands in a week when the Office for Budget Responsibility's independence is already being defended by MPs ahead of October's Budget and when the IMF is warning advanced economies to get debt under control. A credible opposition-adjacent proposal to abolish the pension and cut £75 billion in taxes without saying how the gap gets filled is the kind of thing gilt investors file away rather than react to immediately — but it does firm up a sense of where UK fiscal policy could head if Reform's polling lead holds into the next election. The next test of that appetite comes with the Budget itself, and with whatever position Reform is forced to take once journalists start asking it to reconcile Brown's report with its own triple-lock pledge.
