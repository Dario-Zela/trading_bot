Nasdaq 100 hits fresh record as chip rally extends to sixth day, oil slides on Iran diplomacy

The Nasdaq MarketSite in Times Square, where the Nasdaq 100 closed at a record high for the first time since June. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Chip stocks rallied a sixth day to send the Nasdaq 100 to a record while Brent crude fell to $99.25 on hopes of a US-Iran deal.

The Nasdaq 100 closed at a record high on Tuesday, its first since June, as a rally in semiconductor stocks stretched into a sixth straight session and crude oil kept sliding on hopes that Washington and Tehran might finally strike a deal. The tech-heavy index rose 0.8%, while the broader Nasdaq Composite notched its own record close. The S&P 500 was roughly flat and the Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.4%, dragged down by a selloff in bank shares. The split — chips up, banks down — was itself a small data point about where investors think the next leg of growth sits.

Brent crude told the other half of the story, falling 1% to settle at $99.25 a barrel, its fifth straight losing session and lowest level in two weeks. West Texas Intermediate shed 1.2% to $94.59. The catalyst was diplomatic rather than economic: American and Iranian officials held roughly three hours of talks on the sidelines of the UN General Assembly in New York, which President Trump described as "a very good meeting."

Six days and counting for chips

The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index jumped 4.3% on the day, its best single session since early August, closing above both a falling trendline and its 50-day moving average — technical signals traders read as the end of a consolidation period. Individual moves were sharper still. Advanced Micro Devices surged almost 10%, pushing its market capitalization above $1 trillion for the first time and making it the fourth US chipmaker to cross that threshold, alongside Nvidia, Broadcom and Taiwan Semiconductor. Intel gained 12.2%, Arm Holdings climbed 17%, and memory makers Micron and SK Hynix's US-listed shares each rose more than 3%.

The proximate trigger wasn't a new data-center contract or chip architecture but a consumer app. Meta's AI assistant Muse reached the top of the US App Store's free downloads chart, and investors read that as evidence that AI "agents" — software that runs continuously in the background rather than answering one prompt at a time — lean heavily on general-purpose CPUs and memory to orchestrate work, not just the GPUs that dominated the last two years of AI capital spending. Meta shares themselves gained more than 11% on the read-through, adding roughly $25 billion to Mark Zuckerberg's net worth, even as the same rally coincided with a selloff in bank stocks tied to Meta's push into consumer finance products. The rotation suggests investors are widening their definition of who benefits from AI infrastructure spending, from a handful of GPU makers to a broader swath of the chip supply chain.

"Leadership has rotated decisively back toward semiconductors and hyperscalers, giving the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 enough to keep pushing higher without signs of an outright upside chase," said Michael Ball, a macro strategist at Markets Live. He added that a more durable rally would need broader participation beyond the tech-heavy names currently carrying the indexes.

Oil eases as Iran talks resume

The oil market has spent weeks pricing geopolitical risk in both directions. Brent traded above $100 for stretches of September as the US and Iran remained at odds, with Trump telling the UN General Assembly he faced a "big decision" between negotiating a deal or moving to "annihilate the Islamic Republic." But he tempered that in the same appearance, saying he expected a deal "right after the election because it doesn't make sense for them not to." Iran, for its part, has signaled it would reopen the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of global oil supply passes — within seven days if the US eased its blockade of Iranian ports.

That combination of rhetorical brinkmanship and substantive talks has left crude drifting lower on net, even as headline risk stays elevated. Falling oil also does double duty for equity investors: it dampens inflation expectations and eases the case for the Federal Reserve to hold rates higher for longer, which matters disproportionately for growth stocks like the chipmakers now leading the market.

What the rotation says

Taken together, Tuesday's session reads less like an isolated tech pop than a market willing to underwrite two related bets at once — that AI infrastructure demand is broadening beyond GPUs, and that the Iran standoff resolves short of supply disruption. Both are, for now, more hope than certainty. The semiconductor rally has run six days without a pause, a streak that historically invites profit-taking, and Trump's own language at the UN shows the annihilate-or-negotiate framing hasn't disappeared, only receded from the front pages.

The next tests arrive on two separate clocks. On oil, watch whether the US-Iran channel produces anything concrete before Trump's stated post-midterm timeline, and whether Iran follows through on Hormuz access if a blockade eases. On chips, AMD's next earnings report and any follow-through — or reversal — in Muse's usage numbers will show whether the CPU-and-orchestration thesis behind this week's gains has legs, or whether it was a one-week reshuffling of an AI trade that has moved fast in both directions all year.
