Australia's proposed algorithm opt-out law draws US criticism as 'censorship'

Australian Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, whose government wants to introduce the Digital Duty of Care bill to parliament in October. — David Foote / AUSPIC, Department of Parliamentary Service, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
The US called Australia's plan to let users opt out of algorithmic feeds 'censorship'; Canberra says it's just giving people back control.

Australia wants to let social media users switch off the algorithm. Washington says that amounts to censorship — and it wants Canberra to leave American companies out of it.

The flashpoint is a Labor government bill called the Digital Duty of Care, unveiled earlier this month under the banner "My Feed, My Way." It would force platforms — think Meta, TikTok, X, YouTube — to notify users aged 16 and over and let them choose: keep the algorithm-curated feed, or switch to one showing only posts from accounts they actually follow. Companies that don't comply could face fines up to A$109.2 million (about US$78.6 million). Prime Minister Anthony Albanese says he wants to introduce the legislation to parliament in October.

Washington's objection

The US response, filed as part of Australia's public consultation and published by the US Embassy in Canberra, didn't hold back. It warned the bill risks "extraterritorial censorship of protected speech" and said Washington had "serious concerns" about how Australia intends to define "foreseeable harm" — the legal trigger that would require platforms to act.

The embassy's argument has two prongs. First, that forcing platforms to redesign their recommendation systems for the Australian market would ripple outward, changing "how users everywhere, including Americans, experience these platforms" — because it's cheaper to build one global product than separate ones per jurisdiction. Second, that vague harm standards push companies toward the safest possible compliance path: demoting anything remotely sensitive or controversial, which in practice narrows the reach of legitimate journalism and commentary along with the content regulators actually had in mind. The submission noted that the "large majority" of platforms the law would touch are US-headquartered, and asked Canberra to exclude American companies from the algorithmic opt-out requirement altogether.

Canberra pushes back — quietly

Albanese, in New York for the UN General Assembly this week, rejected the framing: the bill, he said, "is not about giving government control, it's about giving people back control over what they receive on their devices." Communications Minister Anika Wells was more dismissive still, calling the US warning "broad" and noting she hadn't been approached directly by any American representative about it. Both her office and the US Embassy declined to elaborate further.

That measured response fits a pattern. Australia's eSafety Commissioner, Julie Inman Grant, has already been summoned to testify before a US congressional committee over the country's separate under-16 social media ban and broader content-moderation push — a sign this is not a one-off spat but an accumulating disagreement between the two governments over who gets to set the rules for platforms with global user bases.

Why the disagreement runs deep

At bottom, this is a clash of first principles rather than a dispute over technical detail. Australian regulators treat the algorithm as a product feature — something a platform chooses to build, and which users should therefore be able to decline, in the same spirit as opting out of cookies or targeted ads. US free-speech doctrine, by contrast, treats a platform's curation choices as closer to editorial judgment, meaning government mandates about how that curation works brush up against speech protections that don't bend easily for foreign regulators, however well-intentioned.

Neither side is obviously wrong on its own terms, which is what makes the standoff durable rather than a quick diplomatic fix. For platforms, it's a preview of a more expensive future: building compliance not for one global product, but for a patchwork of regimes that increasingly define "harm," "censorship" and "user control" in incompatible ways. The next marker is October, when Albanese's government says it will formally introduce the bill to parliament — and when the gap between Canberra's consultation process and Washington's objections either narrows or hardens into something platforms have to design around.
