Sri Lanka court convicts 15 men over 2019 Easter Sunday bombings

St. Anthony's Shrine in Kochchikade, Colombo, one of the churches targeted in the 2019 Easter Sunday bombings — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Sri Lankan court convicted 14 men on all charges and one partially over the 2019 Easter bombings, but acquitted nine others after a five-year trial.

Seven years after Sri Lanka's Easter Sunday, a courtroom in Colombo finally delivered its verdict. On Tuesday, a three-judge panel of the High Court found 14 men guilty on every charge brought against them over the 2019 bombings that killed more than 260 people at churches and hotels across the island — murder, conspiracy to commit terrorism, and possession of firearms and explosives under the Prevention of Terrorism Act. A 15th man was convicted on some but not all counts. Nine others, among the 24 originally charged, walked free.

The man prosecutors identified as the plot's architect, Naufer Moulavi, was among those convicted on every count. He had spent the trial disputing the confession at the heart of the prosecution's case, telling the court in August that investigators had shown him a document to sign that did not match what he had actually said — "everything in that confession, except the mention of my name, is false," he told the bench. The court had already ruled, in June, that the confession was given voluntarily and admissible.

The scale of the case is what has made it Sri Lanka's longest-running terrorism trial. Prosecutors filed more than 23,000 individual charges, the most ever brought in a single Sri Lankan case, and called over 2,300 witnesses across roughly 2,076 pieces of evidence since the trial opened in November 2021. The 15 convicted men received sentences of up to 220 years, with courts also ordering the confiscation of their property and other assets.

For families who buried relatives in April 2019 — worshippers at St. Anthony's Shrine in Kochchikade, St. Sebastian's Church in Negombo, and guests at three luxury hotels including the Shangri-La and Cinnamon Grand — the verdict landed as long-delayed vindication rather than surprise. "Finally, we have justice," the Catholic priest Jude Fernando told the AFP news agency outside court. "It has taken a long time to get to this point." Among those present was Teresa Fernando, a survivor who suffered head injuries in the blasts and had waited years to see the case reach a close.

Acquittals reopen the accountability question

The nine acquittals complicate the sense of closure. Sri Lankan investigators have long faced criticism that the bombings — carried out by a local cell inspired by the Islamic State group — succeeded despite specific intelligence warnings that reached senior officials weeks in advance. That failure has produced a parallel track of accountability entirely separate from Tuesday's verdict: Sri Lanka's Supreme Court ruled in 2023 that then-President Maithripala Sirisena and four other officials had failed to act on prior warnings, ordering Sirisena to pay 100 million rupees in compensation to victims. In August, a former police chief and a former defence ministry secretary were sentenced to death on criminal negligence charges over the same lapses. And in February, a retired major-general who once headed military intelligence was arrested on conspiracy and abetment charges, after a 2023 Channel 4 documentary alleged he had met the bombers beforehand — a claim that prompted Colombo's Cardinal Malcolm Ranjith to say publicly that the attacks may have been "part of a grand political plot."

None of that is resolved by this week's convictions. Appeals from the 15 men are expected, and the negligence and conspiracy cases against former officials continue to work through separate courts. For a country whose deadliest post-war atrocity has never produced a single, tidy account of who knew what and when, Tuesday's verdict closes one file while leaving several others open.
