Lib Dems pledge income tax cuts funded by rejoining the EU single market

Sir Ed Davey, Liberal Democrat leader, whose party conference pledge ties income tax cuts to EU single market re-entry. — Chris McAndrew / UK Parliament (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Ed Davey pledged to raise the 40p tax threshold to £56,000, funded entirely by growth assumed from rejoining the EU single market — a plan the IFS says is underpriced.

Sir Ed Davey closed the Liberal Democrat conference in Brighton on Monday with the kind of pledge that only a third party, insulated from the arithmetic of actually forming a government, can make with a straight face: cut income tax for millions, and pay for it by rejoining the EU single market.

The mechanics are specific enough to be worth taking seriously as a policy document, even if the politics make it improbable as a near-term market event. Davey would raise the personal tax-free allowance from £12,570 to £15,000 and lift the threshold at which earners start paying the 40p higher rate from £50,270 to £56,000. The party says that second change alone would keep about 760,000 people out of the higher band, and that most taxpayers would eventually be roughly £680 a year better off. The total package is costed at £17bn.

Growth as the funding line

What distinguishes this from a standard opposition tax pledge is the financing. There is no VAT rise, no wealth levy, no departmental squeeze in the small print — the entire £17bn is assumed to arrive as a dividend from re-entering the EU single market and a customs union, on modelling commissioned from Frontier Economics. Davey framed it as reversing the drag of Brexit-era trade friction: cut the paperwork at the border, the argument runs, and the resulting boost to trade and investment funds the tax cuts on its own.

It is a clean pitch, and also a fragile one. The plan does not treat single-market re-entry as a one-line ambition; the tax cuts are not scheduled to land until the fifth year of a Lib Dem government, which by the party's own timeline could mean as late as 2034. That reflects the underlying assumption baked into the modelling — that negotiating a new UK-EU relationship takes years, and that the growth uplift compounds only after the deal is struck and trade actually adjusts. Davey has separately floated agreeing at least a customs union within twelve months of taking office, a timetable that assumes EU willingness on a scale not yet tested, and one that would get harder, not easier, if Eurosceptic or populist governments gain ground elsewhere in the bloc between now and then.

The costing problem

Independent scrutiny arrived quickly. Stuart Adam of the Institute for Fiscal Studies told BBC Verify the package would in practice cost "much more" than the £17bn Davey has quoted — a familiar critique of tax plans that lean on a single, externally modelled growth number rather than a conventional static costing. The core vulnerability is sequencing risk: the tax cuts are promised on a fixed calendar, but the growth that is supposed to pay for them depends on a negotiation with a counterparty — the EU27 — that the Lib Dems do not control and cannot compel to move on any particular schedule. If the trade deal slips, or delivers less growth than Frontier Economics projects, the funding gap has to be closed by borrowing, other taxes, or a broken promise. None of those options is costed in the £17bn figure.

The comparison worth drawing is with Reform UK's own tax platform, which pairs a higher personal allowance with proposed cuts of some £75bn funded chiefly by abolishing the state pension — a plan set out by a Reform-aligned think tank at a similar remove from office. Both parties are now running the same play: a headline tax cut, funded by a structural change (EU re-entry on one side, welfare state retrenchment on the other) that neither is remotely close to being able to enact. It says less about fiscal seriousness than about where opposition parties currently think the political space is.

Why it's on the desk

For sterling and gilts, none of this is a today trade. The Lib Dems hold a fraction of Commons seats, are not in government, and the pledge carries no legislative mandate — there is no vote, no bill, no near-term mechanism for it to move a curve. But it belongs on the watch list for a narrower reason: it is a data point on how far British politics has shifted on Europe since 2016. A Westminster party is now campaigning, in public, on rejoining the single market as its central fiscal offer, at the same conference season in which the Bank of England has just halted long-end gilt sales and the OBR faces political pressure ahead of the October Budget. None of these threads are causally linked, but they describe the same underlying story — a UK fiscal and trade position still being renegotiated in the open, six years after the original exit.

The next marker is the October Budget, where the government's own tax and borrowing choices will be judged against a fiscal backdrop the Lib Dem plan implicitly criticises. Whether re-entry into the single market ever becomes a mainstream manifesto commitment, rather than a third-party marker, is the longer question — one that won't be settled before the next general election campaign properly begins.
