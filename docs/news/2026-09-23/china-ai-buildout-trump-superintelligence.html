China surges ahead in AI buildout as Trump pushes 'superintelligence' rebrand

A Chinese data centre — the kind of facility multiplying across China's rural west under Beijing's 'East Data, West Computing' strategy — Charlie fong / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China is pouring $295bn into state-planned AI data centres while Washington's biggest AI move this week was renaming the technology 'super intelligence.'

In the grasslands of Ulanqab, in China's Inner Mongolia region, farmers now grow potatoes next to windowless concrete halls the size of small towns. In the hills of Guizhou province, Huawei has built its largest data centre inside a cluster of buildings styled like a European village, clock tower included, humming around the clock. The BBC, reporting from inside both sites, describes construction moving at what one worker called "China speed" — hundreds of facilities going up in provinces most foreign visitors have never heard of.

On the same week that reporting landed, Donald Trump stood before the UN General Assembly in New York and announced that the United States would no longer call the technology "artificial intelligence." From now on, he said, US government documents will refer to it as "super intelligence" — because, in his words, "artificial" makes it "sound fake and it is not fake." He used the same speech to reject any international effort to regulate the technology, framing it as an existential race with Beijing: "whoever wins super intelligence wins."

In one sentence: China is pouring hundreds of billions into physical AI infrastructure while Washington's headline AI move this week was renaming it.

China's build-out, by the numbers

Beijing calls its strategy "East Data, West Computing" — a deliberate push, dating to 2021, to shift energy-hungry AI infrastructure away from crowded eastern cities into the sparsely populated west and north, where land is cheap, the climate is cooler, and wind and solar power are abundant. China has mandated that new data centres draw 80% of their power from renewables by the end of the decade, a target the rural sites are built to hit.

The scale of money behind it is now concrete. Beijing has outlined plans to spend roughly 2 trillion yuan — about $295bn — on data centres nationwide over five years. Separately, Goldman Sachs estimates that China's four biggest AI hyperscalers — Alibaba, Tencent, ByteDance and Baidu — will lift combined AI capital spending from $57bn in 2025 to $102bn this year and $123bn in 2027, alongside a roughly 25% jump in the power demand those facilities create. Ulanqab alone has reached 172,000 petaflops of installed computing capacity, according to local officials, with intelligent-computing workloads now accounting for more than 95% of it.

None of that means China has overtaken the US on raw scale — not yet. Stanford's 2026 AI Index counted 5,427 data centres in the US against 449 in China as of last year, a figure Trump himself cited at the UN (he put China's count at 376). Goldman puts China's hyperscaler capex at roughly an eighth of its US counterparts'. The four biggest American cloud firms — Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta — are on track to spend around $725bn on capex in 2026 alone, more than double China's five-year state data-centre plan compressed into a single year. The gap in money is still enormous. What has narrowed is the gap in direction: China's spending is centrally planned, tied to explicit industrial targets, and visibly under construction in places journalists can walk into. Washington's most visible AI move this week was a word.

A rebrand instead of a budget line

Trump's UN address did include one financial figure: a $20bn data-centre investment he said would become available "over a very short period of time," plus a suggestion that the Gulf developer DAMAC might double that commitment. Set against $725bn in hyperscaler capex already flowing from the private sector, and against China's $295bn state-directed plan, the number reads as modest — more a talking point than a policy shift. The substantive US AI infrastructure push remains the private sector's, driven by Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta chasing data-centre capacity and power contracts largely without the kind of top-down siting strategy Beijing has run since 2021.

The rebrand lands against a domestic backdrop that makes the word choice more legible: mounting local protests over noisy, water-hungry data centres in US communities, and warnings from researchers at Anthropic and OpenAI that AI capability is advancing faster than anyone fully understands. Reframing the technology as "super intelligence" while rejecting external oversight — Trump told delegates the US would "encourage it, not rein it in" — reads as an attempt to control the narrative around a build-out that is, in dollar terms, still overwhelmingly American, even as the visible, government-choreographed momentum sits with Beijing.

What traders should watch

The practical read for markets is a divergence between spending and story. US capex commitments remain the larger number by far, which is why chip and cloud stocks have kept rallying on American demand. But China's state-backed build-out is a signal of where Beijing expects AI workloads to concentrate domestically — relevant to firms selling into China's supply chain, and to the credibility of any China-US AI safety dialogue ahead of the two countries' planned summit. The next marker is whether that dialogue produces anything more binding than talking points, and whether Washington's $20bn figure grows into an actual federal infrastructure commitment or stays a rounding error next to private capex.
