Trump threatens to 'annihilate' Iran at UN as US and Iranian officials hold direct talks

Trump at the UN General Assembly podium — a familiar setting for a starker warning on September 22, 2026. — Official White House Photo / Daniel Torok
In one sentence
Trump told the UN he could 'annihilate' Iran, hours after Kushner and Witkoff held previously unannounced direct talks with Iranian envoys in New York.

From the same podium where he has twice now threatened the country with obliteration, and one floor down in a room that does not appear on any public schedule, the Trump administration sent Iran two messages on Tuesday. Neither was subtle. Only one was new.

Addressing the UN General Assembly, President Trump told delegates he faces what he called a big decision: strike a deal that lets Iran "rebuild and create a far greater country than it ever was before," or "annihilate the Islamic Republic, and do it quickly, never giving them a chance to kill and destroy people and countries again." He said he expects the former — but not before American voters go to the polls in November. "I believe we'll make a deal, right after the election," he said, "because it doesn't make sense for them not to."

While Trump was delivering that ultimatum inside the General Assembly hall, his son-in-law Jared Kushner and special envoy Steve Witkoff were three hours into a meeting with Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, elsewhere in the UN complex. Qatar's prime minister, Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, sat in for most of it as mediator. The talks were not on any published itinerary. Trump confirmed them only after the fact, telling reporters Witkoff and Kushner "had a very productive meeting today with mediators of Iran."

A war that has not stopped for diplomacy

The backdrop is eighteen months of escalation that diplomacy has repeatedly failed to arrest. Talks that opened in Muscat and Rome in April 2025 collapsed by June, when Israel struck Iranian nuclear sites and the US followed with its own strikes. A second round, begun in February 2026, deteriorated into open war within weeks. A framework brokered in Islamabad in June — the Islamabad Memorandum, with a 60-day ceasefire window — never fully held.

Iran's closure of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade normally passes, has been the conflict's most consequential economic weapon. The shutdown of the East-West Crude Oil Pipeline on September 11 compounded it, and by one estimate the combined disruptions have touched 39% of global trade. Brent crude climbed toward $100 a barrel in early September as tankers avoided the Gulf and Iran-aligned Houthi forces seized a port on Yemen's west coast, adding pressure in the Bab al-Mandab strait as well.

It is against that backdrop that Tuesday's meeting mattered more than its lack of a signed outcome would suggest. Iranian officials came to New York with three specific conditions for reopening Hormuz: an end to the US naval blockade, release of Iran's frozen assets, and a halt to hostilities across what Tehran calls its "resistance" fronts. Iran had separately floated reopening the strait within seven days if Washington eased pressure — an offer that sent oil prices down roughly 3% on hopes of a breakthrough, even before the UN meeting was confirmed.

Annihilate, then negotiate

No agreement came out of the three hours in New York. Officials on both sides described it as a first structured, direct encounter of this phase of the conflict rather than a breakthrough, and another session is expected within days. But the fact that the administration let both tracks run in public on the same day — apocalyptic rhetoric from the podium, quiet engagement through envoys — fits a pattern that has recurred through Trump's foreign policy: maximal public pressure paired with private channels that do the actual negotiating. It was the shape of his approach to North Korea in his first term and, more recently, to Russia over Ukraine.

Whether it produces the same result here is a separate question. Araghchi, in remarks after the speech, accused Trump of squandering an opening. Iran's president, Masoud Pezeshkian, had signaled willingness to de-escalate provided neighboring states' territory was not used to strike Iran, Araghchi said — an offer he claimed "was almost immediately killed by President Trump's misinterpretation of our capabilities, determination and intent." He warned that further escalation "is precisely what our powerful armed forces have long been prepared for, and what he will get," and put the cost of the war to the US at roughly $100 billion.

Pezeshkian arrived in New York saying Iran no longer fully trusts the negotiating process, and planned to use his own address to the Assembly, delivered Wednesday, to detail civilian harm from the war rather than extend an olive branch. Yet his delegation still sat across the table from Kushner and Witkoff for three hours. That contradiction — declared distrust alongside sustained engagement — is arguably the more informative signal than anything said at the podium.

What the market is pricing

Trump's own framing — a deal timed to land after the midterms, not before — tells its own story. It suggests the administration sees more value in Iran negotiating from a position where Washington's leverage looks fixed rather than contingent on an election outcome, and it gives both sides months to test whether the Hormuz conditions can be narrowed through further Witkoff-Kushner sessions rather than resolved in one sitting. Oil markets have already begun trading the possibility rather than waiting for confirmation, with Tuesday's price move showing how sensitive crude remains to any signal — public or private — out of New York.

The next marker is the follow-up meeting between Witkoff, Kushner and Araghchi's team, expected within days, and whether Iran's seven-day Hormuz offer survives contact with Washington's insistence on dismantling enrichment capacity rather than merely freezing it. Pezeshkian's own UN address, and any Israeli response to the week's diplomacy, are the other things worth watching before the midterms give Trump's stated timeline its first real test.
