Counter-terror police disrupt suspected plot targeting Manchester's Jewish community

Heaton Park Hebrew Congregation synagogue in Crumpsall, Manchester, site of the October 2025 Yom Kippur attack — Geograph / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK counter-terror police arrested two men over a 'fairly imminent' plot against Manchester's Jewish community on the eve of Yom Kippur, a year after the Heaton Park synagogue attack.

Counter-terrorism police in Manchester said on Wednesday that they had disrupted what they believe was a plot to attack the city's Jewish community, arresting two men in their 30s on the eve of Yom Kippur, the holiest day in the Jewish calendar.

The men were detained in central Manchester under Section 41 of the Terrorism Act 2000, which allows arrest without warrant on reasonable suspicion of involvement in terrorism. Searches were also carried out at addresses in Salford and Liverpool. Both suspects can be held without charge until 27 September while officers examine devices, communications and material seized in the raids.

Deputy Assistant Commissioner Vicki Evans, the senior national coordinator for Counter Terrorism Policing, said the arrests followed "a long-running proactive investigation which has led to the disruption of what we believe to be a plot to target the Jewish community in the Manchester area." Officers do not believe there is any further threat linked to the case, she said, though patrols around synagogues and Jewish community sites have been stepped up for the High Holy Day period, which runs from Rosh Hashanah through Yom Kippur and into Sukkot. Sources close to the investigation told the BBC the plot was assessed as "fairly imminent" and in its later planning stages before the arrests were made.

A year after Heaton Park

The timing lands almost exactly on the anniversary of an attack that is still raw in Manchester's Jewish community. On 2 October 2025, Jihad al-Shamie drove a car into pedestrians outside the Heaton Park Hebrew Congregation synagogue in Crumpsall during Yom Kippur services, then stabbed worshippers before armed police shot him dead. Adrian Daulby, 53, and Melvin Cravitz, 66, were killed; three others were injured. Al-Shamie had shouted a reference to Gaza as he tried to breach the synagogue doors, which the congregation's rabbi had locked to protect those inside.

The Community Security Trust, which monitors threats to British Jews, called this week's disrupted plot "utterly chilling coming so close to the anniversary" of that attack. The Campaign Against Antisemitism was more pointed, arguing that "essentially nothing has changed in law enforcement policy since this time last year" and accusing the government of prioritising foreign-policy statements over confronting extremism at home. Israel's charge d'affaires in the UK, Christian Cantor, thanked police for their vigilance but pointed to a "wider climate" in which anti-Israel rhetoric, he said, emboldens people to act against Jews.

Evans acknowledged the strain the news would place on a community already on edge. "We are acutely aware this news will cause further concern to the Jewish community, as well as the wider public, especially given the significant High Holiday period," she said, adding that Counter Terrorism Policing was working closely with Greater Manchester Police to keep people safe.

No charges have yet been brought. The detention clock runs out on 27 September, by which point prosecutors will need to decide whether to charge, release or seek a further extension — the next concrete marker in a case that, for now, underlines how closely diaspora communities are being watched over the holiday season rather than how the threat itself will ultimately be resolved.
