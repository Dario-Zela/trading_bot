IMF's Georgieva warns advanced economies must cut debt as borrowing costs spiral

IMF Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva, who told the BBC that advanced-economy debt is rising 'like a staircase' with no action taken on service costs. — Grant Ellis / World Bank Group (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
IMF chief Kristalina Georgieva says rich-nation debt is climbing unchecked, and the resulting rise in borrowing costs is now squeezing emerging markets too.

Kristalina Georgieva does not often reach for staircase metaphors, but the one she used this week was pointed. Debt in the world's advanced economies, the IMF managing director told the BBC, has been rising "like a staircase not to heaven" — climbing steadily, with no landing in sight — while governments have taken "no action to contain that service cost." She named the UK and the US specifically. Neither, she said, was in a materially different position from the other advanced economies now facing the same math.

The math is this: when debt is large and interest rates are high, the cost of simply rolling over existing borrowing starts to crowd out everything else a government might want to do. The IMF's own Fiscal Monitor, published in April and updated this month, puts global public debt at just under 94% of GDP in 2025, on course to cross 100% by 2029 — a year earlier than the Fund projected only months ago. Under a plausible downside scenario, put at a 5% probability, debt could hit 124% of GDP by then, a level unseen since 1948. Canada, China, France, Italy, Japan, the UK and the US — seven of the G20's biggest economies — are already above or heading above the 100% threshold.

The staircase, in numbers

Georgieva's complaint is not simply that debt is high — it has been high before — but that the interest bill attached to it is no longer being treated as a policy problem requiring a policy response. In the UK, government borrowing hit £18.3bn in August, up nearly 20% on the same month last year, according to figures cited around the interview. In the US, total federal debt has passed $40 trillion, roughly double where it stood a decade ago, and interest payments on that debt now exceed the entire US military budget — a threshold crossed for the first time and one that leaves progressively less fiscal room for anything else, from defence to healthcare to the next recession's stimulus.

None of this is happening in a vacuum. Bond yields have been pushed up by a mix of forces: persistent inflation linked to the disruption of oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz amid the Iran standoff, heavy government issuance generally, and — Georgieva noted, with a caveat — competition for capital from the wave of debt financing behind the AI infrastructure buildout. She said the IMF does not yet see AI-related borrowing crowding out sovereign issuance in a material way, but added: "it's possible this becomes a problem, so we have to watch it."

Her prescription for advanced economies was blunt and, by IMF standards, undiplomatic: bring debt levels down, make fiscal consolidation a priority, and let central banks do their job on price stability without political interference. "These are politically tough steps to take, but necessary steps to take," she said, adding that rich countries "don't have the cash" left to buy growth through spending and will instead have to find it through supply-side reform — planning, housing, that sort of thing rather than another round of stimulus.

Where it lands: emerging markets

What elevates this from a rich-country fiscal scold to something closer to a systemic warning is the second half of Georgieva's argument, made in a separate briefing earlier this month: the consequences of rich-world debt do not stay in the rich world. As advanced-economy yields rise, capital that might otherwise flow to developing economies gets redirected — either because investors can now get comparable returns from safer sovereign debt, or because the borrowing costs of every issuer, including emerging markets with no direct connection to Washington's or Westminster's balance sheets, get repriced upward alongside the benchmark.

"It's the punishment for someone else's sins that emerging markets have to bear," Georgieva said, describing a group of countries that had, in many cases, done the hard fiscal work — cutting deficits, building credibility, compressing the spread investors demand over US Treasuries — only to watch those gains erode as the benchmark itself moves against them. For low-income countries in particular, the mechanism is direct: debt-service costs rise, refinancing gets harder, and money that would otherwise go to infrastructure, health or education gets diverted to interest payments instead.

This is the knock-on squeeze Georgieva flagged: not a discrete shock but a slow redistribution of who gets to borrow cheaply, running from the countries with the deepest capital markets down to the ones with the least room to absorb the hit. She warned the dynamic risks becoming self-reinforcing — higher advanced-economy yields tightening financial conditions everywhere, which in turn makes the underlying inflation problem harder to solve, which keeps rates higher for longer.

What happens if nothing changes

Georgieva's language extended past the balance sheet and into the street. Persistently high borrowing costs feeding into inflation and squeezed public spending, she suggested, carry a political cost too: "We should prepare for people being more unhappy, maybe on the street." It is a notably undiplomatic thing for an IMF managing director to say about the electorates of the Fund's largest shareholders, and it underscores how far the debt conversation has moved from technical concern to something approaching a stability warning.

The test of whether anyone is listening comes soon. The UK's autumn Budget is due in the coming weeks, with the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecasts already the subject of political sniping in Westminster ahead of it. In Washington, there is no equivalent single event — American fiscal policy moves by omission as much as design — but the next Treasury refunding announcement and any further deterioration in the deficit trajectory will be the numbers markets watch. The IMF's next full Fiscal Monitor update, typically released around the October Annual Meetings, will show whether the staircase Georgieva described has gained or lost a step.
