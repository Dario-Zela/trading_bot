Barclays staff demand pay for commuting and childcare as RTO mandate tightens

Barclays' headquarters at One Churchill Place, Canary Wharf, London. — Bajajvikram / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Unite is demanding Barclays pay staff for £96-a-week commuting and childcare costs as a stricter three-day office mandate begins in October.

Barclays is about to find out what its return-to-office policy actually costs — not in real estate, but in payroll.

From October, roughly half of the bank's 45,000 UK staff will be required to work from the office three days a week, up from two, with managing directors and other senior leaders expected in four days. The change, announced in July, hits operations and technology teams hardest — the parts of the bank least likely to be near a trading floor and most likely to have swapped a season ticket for a home-broadband bill over the past few years.

Unite, which represents around 36,000 Barclays employees, wants the bank to pay for the privilege. In an open letter to management, the union is demanding a one-off payment by March 2027 to offset higher costs, along with childcare vouchers, subsidised onsite meals and travel allowances or discounted fares. It also wants exemptions from the mandate for staff whose commute exceeds 40 minutes or 35 miles each way, a one-day-a-week cap for anyone with caregiving duties lasting more than three months, and flexible start times for parents managing school runs.

"Our members think that Barclays is trying to fix a problem that doesn't exist," said Rick Coyle, Unite's national officer, in comments reported by the Financial Times.

The arithmetic behind the anger

The union's case rests on a specific number: £96.40 a week in average UK transport costs, layered on top of childcare that runs £47.57 a week for under-fives and £18 for school-age children nationally — figures that climb sharply in London, where part-time nursery care averages £210 a week, 29% above the rest of England. Add it up and the union's open letter frames the extra office day as roughly £410 a month in additional household expense, squarely into a cost-of-living argument rather than a workplace-culture one.

That framing matters. Barclays isn't proposing anything unusual by banking-sector standards — JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs have pushed staff back to five-day weeks, and most large UK lenders now sit at three or four days. What's different here is that Unite has translated the policy into a household budget line and asked the bank to write a cheque against it, rather than simply objecting to the principle of in-office work.

Barclays holds the line, for now

The bank's public response has stayed procedural. A spokesperson said minimum office-attendance requirements "vary by business area, reflecting the nature of the work and the needs of the business," while acknowledging the need to balance flexibility with "collaboration, decision-making and leadership visibility." There's no indication yet that Barclays plans to negotiate on payments or exemptions before the October start date.

That leaves a narrow window. Unite's letter sets no strike threat, but the union has previously shown it can mobilise thousands of signatories quickly on RTO grievances, and a March 2027 deadline for the one-off payment gives both sides several months to negotiate before any escalation. For a bank managing its own cost base amid rate cuts and margin pressure, the more interesting number may not be £96 a week per employee, but what a sustained retention fight in operations and tech — the functions most exposed to this mandate — would cost to unwind.

The next marker is the October start date itself: whether Barclays enforces the three-day requirement uniformly, or quietly grants the exemptions Unite is asking for on a case-by-case basis rather than as policy.
