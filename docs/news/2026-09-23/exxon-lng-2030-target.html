Exxon sets 2030 target of 50 million tons of annual LNG sales

Chiksan loading arms and tugboats assist inbound LNG tankers at the Sabine Pass export terminal, home to Exxon's Golden Pass joint venture — roy.luck / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Exxon raised its 2030 LNG sales target by 25% to 50 million tons a year, betting fossil-fuel demand holds up even as it talks up its climate credentials.

ExxonMobil told investors this week it now expects to sell 50 million tons of liquefied natural gas a year by 2030, up from a prior target of 40 million tons — a 25% upgrade to a growth plan the company had only recently set out. The revision, disclosed by Peter Clarke, Exxon's senior vice president for LNG, came without an accompanying announcement of new projects, which is itself a signal: Exxon is betting it can squeeze more out of the assets and options it already holds rather than commit fresh capital to greenfield developments.

The math behind the number

Exxon currently sells around 25 million tons of LNG annually, meaning the new target requires doubling output in four years. The company points to global demand climbing from roughly 422 million tons in 2025 to about 500 million tons by 2030, and further doubling by 2050 on Exxon's own projections — with Asia accounting for an expected 70% of that demand by mid-century. Hit the 50-million-ton mark and Exxon's share of the global LNG trade rises from about 6% today to roughly 10%, a meaningful jump in a market where the largest players jockey over single-digit share points.

The near-term engine is Golden Pass, the Texas export terminal Exxon operates with QatarEnergy (Exxon holds 30%, Qatar 70%). Golden Pass shipped its first cargo in April after starting production in March, and management expects the facility to reach full run-rate capacity — 18 million tons a year across its three trains — by the end of 2027. Layered on top is the North Field expansion in Qatar, already under construction, while proposed projects in Papua New Guinea and Mozambique sit further out and are not expected to contribute meaningfully before 2030.

Reading the signal

Exxon frames LNG as one of three core upstream growth engines, alongside the Permian Basin and Guyana, and executives have said the more ambitious 2030 targets — which also include $25 billion in incremental earnings growth and $35 billion in cash flow growth companywide — can be hit without raising capital spending. That claim matters for how traders read the announcement: rather than signaling a fresh multibillion-dollar commitment, it signals confidence that assets already sanctioned will run harder and sell into a tighter market than the company previously assumed.

That confidence sits awkwardly next to Exxon's own emissions rhetoric. The company has pledged more than $15 billion toward lower-carbon initiatives such as carbon capture, and CEO Darren Woods has argued net-zero transition costs are being underestimated industry-wide. But Woods has also said Exxon's internal outlook sees oil and natural gas supplying more than half of global energy demand in 2050 — a view that puts LNG expansion, not contraction, at the center of the company's next decade. A 50-million-ton target by 2030 is Exxon's capital allocation answering that question before the rhetoric does.

What to watch

Golden Pass reaching full three-train capacity by end-2027 is the nearest checkpoint against the new target; a slip there would push the 2030 math onto Qatar's North Field expansion and any acquired capacity Exxon has yet to name. Rival majors are making similar bets — Chevron laid out its own expanded LNG ambitions the same week — which will keep pressure on Exxon to show, project by project, how it plans to double sales rather than simply raise the target and leave the arithmetic to investors.
