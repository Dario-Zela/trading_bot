US and China agree AI safety dialogue ahead of Trump-Xi summit

Trump greets Xi Jinping ahead of their October 2025 Busan meeting — the two leaders meet again in Washington this week. — Daniel Torok / The White House
In one sentence
Bessent and China's He Lifeng agreed to an AI incident hotline and advanced tariff-cut talks ahead of the Trump-Xi summit, but the Nov. 10 truce deadline remains unresolved.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng spent several hours in New York over the weekend talking about artificial intelligence and tariffs, and by Sunday evening Bessent was calling it "very successful." The substance is thinner than the adjective suggests, but for a market that has spent 2026 flinching at every US-China headline, thin can still move prices — the Nasdaq Composite closed at a fresh record on Tuesday with traders citing the talks alongside the ongoing chip rally.

The two sides agreed to open a dedicated AI dialogue and, separately, to keep pushing forward a mechanism for cutting tariffs on non-strategic goods. Both were framed explicitly as groundwork for Wednesday's arrival of Xi Jinping in Washington — his first state visit to the US capital in eleven years — ahead of a formal summit with President Trump.

A hotline, not a treaty

Strip away the diplomatic packaging and the AI "notification mechanism" Bessent proposed to He Lifeng is closer to a phone line than a framework. Bessent described it to reporters as "a communications line, an incident line, so that we have constant communications, especially in the event of some kind of an incident" — a channel meant to flag AI-related developments that rise to the level of a national-security concern before they escalate into something harder to walk back.

That modesty is deliberate. Washington and Beijing are nowhere near agreeing on rules for AI development, let alone enforcement of them; what they can agree on is that a runaway incident — a rogue model, a compromised system, an AI-enabled cyber event — is more dangerous if the two governments' first conversation about it happens in public. The mechanism gives both sides a name and a line to call. It will go to Trump and Xi for sign-off at the summit, but it is a confidence-building gesture rather than a governance regime, and markets should read it as such: a risk-reduction measure for the diplomatic relationship, not a new constraint on the AI buildout itself.

The tariff math gets a delivery date

The more consequential thread is the one with an actual number attached. Trump and Xi agreed in May, at their Beijing summit, to set up a joint "Board of Trade" to identify non-strategic goods eligible for tariff relief on both sides. The US Trade Representative's office spent the summer collecting public comment on which products might qualify — USTR chief Jamieson Greer has described the goal as a "relatively small subset" of non-sensitive American and Chinese goods traded in a more balanced way. Beijing, according to people briefed on the talks, is expecting the eventual list to cover something in the region of $30 billion of trade.

That is a modest slice of roughly $500 billion in annual two-way goods trade, and it does nothing about the tariffs that actually matter for markets: the US average tariff rate on Chinese imports stood at 36.5% as of July, per the Congressional Research Service — down sharply from the 145% peak during last year's escalation, but still a structural tax on the relationship. Washington has also opted to delay a separate round of planned tariffs targeting Chinese industrial overcapacity until after the summit, preserving them as leverage rather than taking them off the table.

The clock that actually matters

The deadline that will determine whether any of this holds is November 10 — the date the current truce expires, along with Beijing's parallel suspension of rare-earth export controls first imposed last October. The US has floated a three-to-six-month extension; the September 20 preparatory session in New York, by the account of trade publications tracking the talks, did not produce an agreement on that extension, leaving it as unfinished business for the leaders themselves rather than their deputies.

That sequencing is the tell for how to read this week. Officials are stacking the summit with the easy wins — an AI hotline, a curated tariff list, likely agriculture and aircraft purchase commitments, with Boeing floated as a possible beneficiary — while leaving the truce extension, the item with the most direct read-through to rare-earth supply chains and the broader tariff base, for the principals to settle face to face.

What's next

Xi and Peng Liyuan land at Joint Base Andrews later Wednesday, with a state dinner that evening and a formal arrival ceremony at the White House on Thursday morning. The market-moving question is whether Trump and Xi extend the truce past November 10 and, if so, by how much — an outcome that will matter far more to rare-earth-dependent industrials and semiconductor supply chains than the AI channel or the Board of Trade list. Any signing ceremonies this week, on agriculture, aircraft or otherwise, will be worth reading against that November deadline rather than in isolation.
