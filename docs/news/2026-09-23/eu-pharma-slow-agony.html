Nine of Europe's biggest drugmakers warn continent risks pharma 'slow agony'

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters — one of nine European drugmakers whose chairs jointly warned governments over falling R&D competitiveness — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nine European pharma chairs jointly warned the EU's R&D share has fallen from 43% to 31% since 1990, urging faster trials and more funding or face a 'slow agony' of decline.

Nine chief executives of Europe's largest drugmakers do not, as a rule, put their names to the same piece of paper. Rivals for capital, talent and shelf space, they tend to make their arguments to regulators separately, through trade bodies, in private. On Monday, the chairs of AstraZeneca, GSK, Novartis, Roche, Sanofi, Novo Nordisk, Boehringer Ingelheim, Chiesi and Ipsen broke that pattern, publishing a joint open letter warning that Europe's pharmaceutical industry faces what one of the companies called a "slow agony" of decline — borrowing the phrase Mario Draghi used in his 2024 report on European competitiveness.

The headline number is stark. Europe's share of global pharmaceutical research and development has fallen from 43% in 1990 to 31% today, and is still falling. Over the same period the region's share of commercial clinical trials has roughly halved to 9%, with China overtaking Europe on trials, patents and new medicines brought to market. The letter, addressed to EU institutions and national governments, is not a lobbying footnote — it is nine competitors agreeing, in public, that the ground is moving under all of them at once.

The numbers behind the alarm

The letter's arithmetic is designed to be uncomfortable for policymakers. The United States and China have between them announced more than $600bn in pharmaceutical investment over the past two years, dwarfing anything on the table in Brussels or national capitals. Europe spends roughly 1% of GDP on pharmaceuticals against 2% in the US and 1.8% in China — a gap the signatories argue compounds year on year as capital chases the faster, cheaper, more predictable path to market.

The patient-facing consequence, according to the letter, is that around 40% of newly approved therapies never reach European markets at all, and those that do arrive after an average wait of nearly 600 days — the gap between a drug's first global approval and its availability to a European patient. Developing a single medicine still takes more than ten years and roughly $4bn, with only one or two candidates succeeding out of every 10,000 that enter the laboratory. Every year of regulatory delay on top of that economics is, in the signatories' framing, a year in which the next tranche of investment defaults to Boston, New Jersey or Shanghai instead of Basel, Cambridge or Leverkusen.

The letter is signed by the chairs rather than chief executives — AstraZeneca's Michel Demaré, GSK's Jonathan Symonds, Roche's Severin Schwan and Sanofi's Frédéric Oudéa among them — a detail that matters. Chairs speak for boards and long-run capital allocation, not quarterly guidance, which is part of why the letter reads less like a complaint about this year's pricing round and more like a warning about where balance sheets are pointed over the next decade.

What the nine are asking for

The asks are specific rather than aspirational: accelerate clinical-trial assessment timelines, strengthen intellectual-property protections, adopt "sensible" digital and data policies, and give national governments fiscal flexibility to treat medicines as strategic infrastructure rather than a line item to be squeezed for savings. The letter puts a number on the upside too — closing the clinical-trial competitiveness gap alone, the companies argue, could unlock €53bn in economic activity and 82,000 jobs.

Whether governments respond is an open question, and one with a poor recent track record — the Draghi report itself, published over a year ago, made a similar case with limited follow-through in binding policy. But the coordination this time is new. Trade associations lobby routinely and are, to a degree, discounted by the officials who hear from them constantly. Nine chairs signing one letter is a different register — closer to a warning that individual firms are already quietly weighing where the next research centre or trial gets sited, and that the decision is being made now, not hypothetically.

Reading through to positioning

For investors, the letter is best read as a signal rather than a catalyst in itself — European pharma equities did not move sharply on the news, and nothing in it changes a company's near-term earnings. But coordinated public pressure of this kind tends to precede one of two things: a policy response, which is slow and uncertain in the EU's fragmented regulatory landscape, or capital reallocation by the companies themselves, which is faster and already underway in smaller ways — trial sites, manufacturing investment and early-stage licensing increasingly favouring the US market, where the regulatory and reimbursement path is quicker even before accounting for pricing.

AstraZeneca is a useful bellwether here. The company's shares have been flagged by some analysts as meaningfully undervalued even as its pipeline — Enhertu's expanding approvals chief among them — continues to deliver, a divergence that partly reflects exactly the discount European-domiciled pharma names carry relative to US peers on execution speed and market access. If the letter marks the start of a more overt drift of investment and trial activity toward the US and China, that discount is more likely to widen than close, and the sector's next moves — on manufacturing footprint, on M&A, on where the next mega-trial gets sited — are the concrete things to watch rather than the letter's language itself.

The next data points will come from the EU side: whether the European Commission's pharmaceutical legislation review, still working through the Council and Parliament, picks up any of the letter's specific asks on trial timelines and IP protection. Budget season in several member states this autumn will also show whether "fiscal flexibility for health investment" gets funded or stays rhetorical.
