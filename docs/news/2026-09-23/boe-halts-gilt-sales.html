BoE holds at 3.75%, halts long-end gilt sales; bonds rally

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which halted sales of its longest-dated gilt holdings on September 17th. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
BoE held rates 3.75% on a 6-3 hawkish-leaning split but unanimously halted long-dated gilt sales, sending 10-year yields to 5.24% and 30-year yields down from a 1998 high.

The Bank of England held its base rate at 3.75% on September 17th, as expected. The more consequential decision came alongside it: the Bank will stop selling the longest-dated gilts it holds from quantitative easing, pausing all bond sales until April while it works out a new way to unwind its £488bn balance sheet. Gilts rallied on the news. The ten-year yield fell six basis points to 5.243%, and the thirty-year — which had touched 5.96% earlier in the week, its highest since 1998 — dropped twelve basis points to 5.74%, its biggest one-day fall since May.

The rate vote itself split the Monetary Policy Committee 6-3, but not in the direction the gilt-market relief might suggest. The three dissenters — chief economist Huw Pill, Catherine Mann and Megan Greene — voted to raise rates a quarter point, to 4%, not to cut them. UK inflation rose to 3.1% in August, and the Committee flagged that a prolonged conflict in the Middle East has pushed crude and refined energy prices higher and more volatile, raising the risk of second-round effects on wages and prices. The majority held on the view that tight financial conditions are still doing enough of the disinflationary work. Governor Andrew Bailey summarised the balance as one requiring patience rather than resolution: the minutes noted that if the energy shock persists, "it is likely that policy may have to tighten." This was a committee arguing about which way to move next, with the hawks in the ascendant for now — a genuinely different story from a Bank edging toward cuts.

Untangling rates from the balance sheet

The gilt-sales decision was unanimous and is best read as a separate, structural fix rather than a dovish signal on rates. Quantitative tightening — the run-off of gilts bought under QE — has added an estimated 200 basis points to term premia on long bonds since the programme began in February 2022, according to the Bank's own minutes. With thirty-year yields at levels last seen when John Major was prime minister, and a global bond selloff still working through markets, the calculus on selling duration the market didn't want had turned unfavourable.

Under the new framework, the Bank will hold £222bn of gilts maturing by 2034 to maturity rather than sell them, permanently retain £120bn of the longest-dated bonds to back banknote issuance, and sell the remaining £146bn maturing between 2035 and 2049 at roughly £20bn a year. Combined with maturities, that implies average annual balance-sheet shrinkage of £46bn through 2034 — slower than the pace of the past year and below the roughly £50bn markets had pencilled in. The Bank also said it will consult the government on selling gilts directly to the Debt Management Office at market prices, rather than through its own auctions, a mechanism that would let the Treasury absorb the paper without forcing it through the open market at moments of stress.

"Today we provided clarity over the future of our quantitative tightening policy," Bailey said, framing the move as removing an unnecessary source of volatility rather than loosening policy outright.

For traders, the practical upshot is a lighter supply overhang at the long end of the curve just as the government prepares to test that same market. Chancellor Rachel Healey delivers the Autumn Budget on October 28th, alongside a fresh OBR forecast that will incorporate the latest gilt yields — yields that have already trimmed his fiscal headroom by roughly half over the summer. A calmer long end buys the Treasury some room, but the MPC's hawkish undertone, and the still-live risk of a Middle East-driven inflation shock, mean the next rate move is no foregone conclusion. The November 5th MPC decision, and whatever the Budget does to gilt supply expectations, are the next tests of whether Thursday's calm holds.
