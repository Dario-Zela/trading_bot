CLIMATE · EL NIÑO · By S. Whitfield · 2026-09-23 · 4 min read

El Niño intensifies toward strongest on record, set to peak near November

Sea surface temperature anomalies during the 1997–98 El Niño — the previous benchmark this year's event is forecast to surpass. — NASA / Charles Thompson, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
NOAA and IRI's 22-model consensus puts El Niño peaking near +4°C in November, with 75% odds it becomes the strongest since 1950.

The tropical Pacific is running a fever, and the world's climate models are no longer hedging about how high it will go. The Niño 3.4 index — the strip of ocean between the Date Line and South America that meteorologists treat as El Niño's vital sign — hit 3.05°C above the 1991–2020 average on September 21, already among the warmest readings ever logged outside a full seasonal peak. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center and Columbia University's International Research Institute for Climate and Society (IRI) now agree the event is still climbing, with an ensemble of 22 dynamical and statistical models converging on a seasonal-average anomaly near +3.6°C and a November peak that could touch +4°C.

That is not a subtle revision. The 1997–98 El Niño, the benchmark against which every subsequent event is measured, topped out around 2.5°C above average by some accounts and reshaped global grain and coffee markets for two years. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center puts the odds of this event becoming the strongest since consistent records began in 1950 at 75%, and separately gives it better than 90% odds of remaining "very strong" through the Northern Hemisphere winter of 2026–27.

Why the models agree this time

El Niño forecasting has historically struggled through a "spring predictability barrier" — a stretch each year when models disagree sharply about how a developing event will evolve. This year that barrier came and went with unusual consensus. By September, IRI's multi-model plume showed nearly every dynamical model, and most statistical ones, clustered above the +3.0°C threshold that defines a "very strong" event through fall and into winter, rather than spread across the moderate-to-strong range typical of most years.

The daily Niño 3.4 reading of 3.0°C recorded in September puts this event in the same company as 2015–16, the only other episode since 1950 to reach that daily threshold. Sea surface temperatures in the eastern tropical Pacific touched 21.11°C in a single August reading — a number that matters less for its precision than for what it confirms: the ocean-atmosphere coupling that defines El Niño, trade winds weakening, warm water piling up in the east, is locked in, not still forming. NOAA's own July assessment put the odds of the event persisting through early spring 2027 at 97%.

What a November peak means for positioning

For traders, the value of this forecast lies less in the record-breaking framing than in its timing. A peak "near November" gives risk managers a specific window rather than a seasonal shrug, and El Niño's downstream effects are well enough studied to price against.

On the agricultural side, strong El Niño events have reduced cocoa yields in every instance over the past 55 years, as warm Pacific water shifts wind patterns that bring hot, dry conditions to West Africa — still the source of roughly two-thirds of the world's cocoa. Vietnam and Indonesia's robusta coffee crops face the same heat-and-drought exposure, and Southeast Asian rice production has historically seen yield reductions in the high single digits during comparable events. Analysts covering the softs complex have flagged agricultural commodity prices already climbing 7% in a single month this year, with cocoa, coffee and wheat leading the move. Argentina is the partial counterweight: El Niño years tend to bring above-average rainfall that supports its soy, corn and wheat output, though traders note that offset rarely covers what Asia and West Africa lose.

Energy markets are pricing a different, quieter effect. A strong El Niño typically pushes the U.S. winter storm track north and warms much of the northern hemisphere's population centers, denting heating demand. Natural gas forward prices for the winter 2026–27 strip have already been sliding on that expectation, compounding a three-year downtrend in the contract. Hurricane risk in the Gulf tends to fall in El Niño years too, removing a second source of supply-side volatility that gas traders usually have to hedge through autumn.

What comes next

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center issues its next monthly ENSO update in mid-October, which should show whether the Niño 3.4 index is tracking the model consensus toward +4°C or beginning to plateau early — a divergence that would itself be informative, since none of the 22 models currently in agreement forecast an early peak. The November reading is the one to watch most closely: it will either confirm the strongest El Niño since 1950 or reveal fresh uncertainty in a forecast that has, so far, been unusually unanimous. Either way, the event's second half — the retreat through spring 2027, which historically brings its own La Niña-adjacent volatility — is likely to matter to commodity and currency desks for longer than the peak itself.
