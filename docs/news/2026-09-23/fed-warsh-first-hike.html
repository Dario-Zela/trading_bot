Fed hikes to 3.75-4% in Warsh's first meeting as chair

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, delivered his first rate increase in September. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Fed raised rates a quarter point to 3.75-4%, with Chair Kevin Warsh citing Iran-war-driven oil prices, not economic overheating, as the inflation threat.

The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark interest rate a quarter point on Wednesday, to a target range of 3.75% to 4%, the first increase in borrowing costs in more than three years. It was the third meeting of Kevin Warsh's chairmanship, and the first at which he had a hike to deliver — one the White House had spent two weeks publicly demanding he avoid.

The vote was unanimous, 12-0, a detail the Fed was keen to underline. "The plain fact is that inflation is too high, and has been for too long," Warsh told reporters afterward. "This summer's inflation readings do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved."

What makes this hiking cycle unusual is where the inflation is coming from. Warsh's two predecessors, Jerome Powell and Janet Yellen, tightened policy against a backdrop of an overheating economy — too much demand chasing too few goods, or a labor market running hot. Warsh is tightening into a supply shock. Since the Iran war escalated this year and fighting cut into shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, Brent crude has climbed above $107 a barrel and WTI above $102, up more than 18% in September alone. The International Energy Agency has called the resulting disruption the largest to hit the global oil market on record. That has pushed gasoline and energy costs higher across the economy, feeding directly into headline inflation without any accompanying surge in consumer spending or wage growth.

A hike Trump didn't want

The decision came over explicit political objection. In the run-up to the meeting, Trump, the vice president, the Treasury secretary and senior economic advisers all lobbied publicly for the Fed to hold off, with Trump separately saying he wanted rates cut to "1% or less." Warsh, who Trump nominated in January and who was confirmed by the Senate in a 54-45 party-line vote in May, didn't budge. "Part of the independence of the Federal Reserve is we stay in our lane," he told Congress in July. "Independence is a two-way street."

The Fed's updated projections, released alongside the decision, show why officials aren't finished. The median forecast for core PCE inflation this year rose to 3.7%, and 16 of 19 committee members now expect at least one more rate increase before year-end, which would put the funds rate at roughly 4.1% by December — a level the median projection holds through 2027. Growth and employment forecasts were revised up slightly alongside the inflation number, which is precisely the combination that makes a supply-driven price shock harder to manage than a demand-driven one: the usual fix for inflation, cooling the economy, does nothing to unblock a shipping lane.

The next test comes at the Fed's October 27-28 meeting, where the path of the Iran conflict — and with it, oil prices — will matter more to the rate decision than any domestic data release between now and then.
