Evergrande founder Xu Jiayin sentenced to life in prison for fraud

Evergrande's headquarters tower, a symbol of the debt-fuelled expansion that preceded the developer's 2021 default — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Shenzhen court gave Evergrande founder Xu Jiayin life in prison and seized his assets, closing the legal chapter on China's property crisis five years after its collapse.

The man who built China's most indebted company, and then broke it, will not build anything again. On Thursday a court in Shenzhen sentenced Xu Jiayin — the Evergrande Group founder better known by his Cantonese name, Hui Ka-yan — to life in prison, stripped him of his political rights permanently, and ordered every asset he owns seized. It is the final, and heaviest, punishment to fall on any figure from China's property crash, a reckoning that took five years to arrive and closes, at least in the courtroom, one of the most consequential corporate collapses in modern Chinese history.

The Shenzhen Intermediate People's Court found Xu and Evergrande Group guilty of a sprawling list of offences: illegally absorbing public deposits, fundraising fraud, unlawful loan issuance, fraudulent securities issuance, failing to disclose material information, embezzlement and corporate bribery. Evergrande Group was fined 8.82 billion yuan (about $1.3 billion), and its onshore development arm, Hengda Real Estate, was fined a further 7 billion yuan — a combined penalty north of $2 billion. Two of Xu's sons, Xu Zhijian and Xu Tenghe, were also convicted and given sentences ranging from 22 months to 18 years.

A guilty plea, then a verdict

Xu pleaded guilty in April to eight charges, sparing the court a contested trial over facts that, by then, were largely a matter of record. Prosecutors laid out how pre-sale funds — money paid by ordinary Chinese families for apartments that did not yet exist — were siphoned from the projects they were meant to finance and redirected into new developments, leaving hundreds of housing projects half-built. That diversion sits at the center of why Evergrande's failure did more damage than a typical corporate bankruptcy: the debt was one problem, but the empty concrete shells scattered across dozens of cities, and the buyers who had paid for homes they might never receive, was the more politically dangerous one.

It was not Xu's first brush with regulators. In March 2024, China's securities watchdog fined him personally $6.5 million and imposed a lifetime capital-markets ban after finding Evergrande had overstated revenue by roughly $78 billion in 2019 and 2020 — inflating the books even as the company's debts were already becoming unmanageable. Thursday's sentence folds that episode into a far larger judgment: the court's finding covers systemic fraud running from 2016 to 2021, the years in which Evergrande grew from large to unmanageable.

From China's richest man to a life sentence

Xu founded Evergrande in 1997 and rode two decades of credit-fuelled property expansion to become, at his 2017 peak, China's richest person, with a fortune Forbes estimated at $45.3 billion. The company he built ballooned into the world's most indebted developer, carrying liabilities that topped $300 billion by the time it defaulted on its offshore bonds in late 2021 — a default that rattled global credit markets and became shorthand for the risks embedded in China's property-driven growth model.

The unwinding that followed was slow and largely fruitless for creditors. A Hong Kong court ordered Evergrande into liquidation in January 2024 after the company failed to produce a workable restructuring plan for $23 billion in offshore debt; the stock was delisted from the Hong Kong exchange in August 2025 after a decline of more than 99% from its highs. More than 90% of Evergrande's assets sit onshore in mainland China, largely beyond the reach of the Hong Kong liquidators — which is part of why Thursday's mainland court judgment, with its asset confiscation and criminal sentence, matters more than the offshore process ever could.

What the verdict resolves, and what it doesn't

A life sentence and a $2 billion fine settle the question of accountability; they do not rebuild anything. Independent estimates have put the number of unfinished, pre-sold Evergrande homes at around 800,000, and Nomura has estimated roughly 20 million unfinished units across China's property sector broadly, with a funding gap to complete them approaching $446 billion. Real estate and related activity once accounted for close to a third of China's GDP and a major share of local-government revenue; the sector's contraction since 2021 has been a persistent drag on growth that no verdict against Xu Jiayin will reverse.

What the sentencing does signal is Beijing's appetite for making an example of the crisis's central figure, years after the acute phase of the crash has passed and market attention has moved on. For a governing apparatus that has spent years managing the property slowdown quietly — extending credit lines to weaker developers, leaning on state-backed buyers to absorb unsold inventory — a maximal criminal penalty against the man most associated with the excess offers a different kind of message: that individual culpability, not just systemic risk, will be pursued to its conclusion. Whether that hardens investor caution toward other credit-exposed developers, or simply draws a line under a chapter markets had already priced in, will show up in how China's remaining property names trade in the weeks ahead — and in whether prosecutors move with similar force against other developers still working through debt restructurings.
