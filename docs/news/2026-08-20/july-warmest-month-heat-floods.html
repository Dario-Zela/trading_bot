CLIMATE · HEAT · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-20 · 6 min read

July ranks among warmest months on record as heat and floods hit Europe and Asia

Copernicus Climate Change Service data showing July 2026's temperature anomalies, with western Europe running far above average. — Copernicus Climate Change Service / ECMWF
In one sentence
July 2026 tied as the world's second-hottest month on record, and the storms, floods and cloudbursts it fed are starting to show up in how central banks price risk.

The world just lived through one of the hottest months ever measured, and for the second summer running, the heat did not stay an abstraction. It came down as tennis-ball hail on cars in northern Germany, as floodwater in the streets of Mumbai, and as cloudbursts that have killed at least 31 people in Jammu and Kashmir since June.

According to the EU's Copernicus Climate Change Service, July 2026 was the joint-second warmest July in the 147-year instrumental record, tied with July 2024 to within a hundredth of a degree and trailing only July 2023. Global average surface temperature reached 16.90°C — 1.47°C above the pre-industrial baseline and a whisker off the threshold governments pledged, under the Paris Agreement, to avoid breaching on a sustained basis. The World Meteorological Organization put it more bluntly. "This is becoming too hot to handle," said Secretary-General Celeste Saulo, noting that the ten warmest Julys on record have all occurred since 2016, with 2023 through 2026 occupying the top four spots outright.

A summer of extremes, not just heat

The headline number obscures how uneven the damage was. Western Europe had its warmest June–July stretch on record, at 22.48°C — 2.53°C above the 1991–2020 average — with France posting its hottest July ever and Spain tying its own record. Samantha Burgess, Copernicus's strategic lead for climate, tied the extremity directly to a feedback loop: "As soils dry out, they lose their ability to provide natural cooling, allowing heat to build more readily," she said, describing how drought and heat are increasingly reinforcing each other across the continent.

That same heat energy also fed instability. In mid-July, severe thunderstorms tore through northern Germany, dumping hailstones the size of tennis balls on Heidenau, south of Hamburg, smashing car windows and stripping roofs; further storms swept Central Europe from July 18 to 22, bringing damaging winds and flash flooding to Poland and Austria as well. Munich Re's global tally of insured catastrophe losses for the first half of 2026 came to roughly $44 billion, with severe convective storms — the hail-and-thunderstorm category that hit Germany — a growing share of that bill, a pattern the reinsurer has flagged for several consecutive years.

Meanwhile monsoon season turned lethal across South Asia. Mumbai's Santacruz weather station recorded 238.2mm of rain in 24 hours in early August, the city's highest single-day August total since 2020, prompting India's weather agency to issue its highest-level red alert, ground flights, halt trains and shut schools. Nationally, floods and landslides have killed more than 100 people since July. In Jammu and Kashmir, the toll has been driven by an unusually persistent pattern of cloudbursts — 35 of them so far this year, killing 31 people, concentrated in Poonch, Rajouri, Kathua, Doda and Kishtwar, with further clusters in Shopian and Kupwara. Local meteorologists describe the frequency, not any single event, as the anomaly: cloudbursts that were once rare have become close to routine.

Where the money starts to move

Heat records and flood tolls, taken alone, rarely move markets. What has started to change is whether the institutions that set the price of risk are treating this pattern as recurring rather than exceptional — and there are early signs they are.

The European Central Bank has spent 2026 building climate exposure directly into its plumbing. In June it introduced a "climate factor" into its collateral framework, discounting the value of corporate bonds pledged by banks according to their transition-risk exposure; in July it extended that factor to bank loans and credit claims, which make up 29% of collateral pledged to the Eurosystem, against 2% for the bonds originally covered. The ECB has also warned separately that physical disasters — the kind of flooding and storms seen this summer — could trigger repricing of loans and securities concentrated in exposed regions, a mechanism distinct from, but complementary to, its transition-risk collateral work.

The Network for Greening the Financial System, the 144-member coalition of central banks and supervisors that includes the ECB, the Bank of England and the Federal Reserve's international counterparts, published short-term climate scenarios this year modelling exactly the kind of summer just recorded. Its "Disasters and Policy Stagnation" scenario assumes a sequence starting with heatwaves, droughts and wildfires in 2026, followed by floods and storms in 2027 — and finds the compounding effect could cut euro-area GDP by up to 4.7% by 2030, with France alone losing an estimated 7.4% under a severe-weather cluster. The scenario was built as a hypothetical. This summer's data track its opening chapter closely enough that NGFS members are likely to face questions about whether the assumption still counts as a tail case.

The insurance gap is the fiscal risk

The more immediate transmission channel runs through insurance, or the lack of it. Only around a quarter of climate-related catastrophe losses in the EU are insured, and in some member states coverage sits below 5%, according to figures cited by ratings agency Fitch. Weather and climate extremes cost the EU an estimated €822 billion between 1980 and 2024, with a quarter of that total incurred in just the last four years — a compression that analysts say is turning disaster relief from an occasional budget line into a recurring one. Germany's 2021 floods alone required €30 billion in public reconstruction funds precisely because private insurance covered so little of the damage. Franklin Templeton's David Zahn estimated the annual fiscal drag from climate costs could reach 1–2% of GDP in some countries — meaningful against euro-area deficits already averaging around 3% of GDP.

Portugal is moving toward mandatory home insurance backed by a disaster fund; Greece is expanding coverage requirements; Brussels has floated a joint EU catastrophe reinsurance pool with a loan-based backstop of €10–65 billion. None of this is a response to July's heat specifically — it predates the month. But it is the policy machinery that a summer like this one feeds into, turning a climate bulletin into a line item for finance ministries.

What comes next

Copernicus and the WMO will publish August data in early September, by which point the Northern Hemisphere's wildfire and drought season will be largely resolved one way or another. The more consequential date for markets is likely to be the NGFS's next full scenario update and any accompanying statements from the ECB on how the climate collateral factor is performing in practice — the first real-world test of whether a central bank will let physical and transition risk move the price of money, rather than just the price of insurance.
