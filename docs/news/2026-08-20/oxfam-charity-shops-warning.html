Oxfam warns it 'can't guarantee future' of UK charity shops

The site of Oxfam's first permanent charity shop, which opened in Oxford in December 1947. — Chris McAuley / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Oxfam says it 'can't guarantee' the future of its 500-plus UK shops after retail losses topped £2m and donations kept falling.

There is a version of this story that has played out on British high streets for a decade: the charity shop as fixture, its window a jumble of paperbacks and mismatched mugs, its rail of donated coats a small act of civic recycling nobody thinks much about. Oxfam wants to make clear that version cannot be taken for granted anymore.

The charity has confirmed it is reviewing its three UK textile-recycling warehouses — in Batley, Bicester and Milton Keynes — and will not rule out closing some of its 500-plus shops, after its retail arm posted losses of more than £2m in the year to March 2025, on top of a similar loss the year before. Sales fell 7% over the same period, driven largely by a collapse in income from recycling.

"As is standard practice, we keep all parts of our retail operations under review," said Lorna Fallon, Oxfam GB's chief supporter officer, adding that the charity "cannot give guarantees in this difficult economic climate." That is a notably blunter line than the usual charity-sector reassurance, and it has been read by insiders as a signal that Batley — home to the main recycling plant, whose lease is up for renewal — is the site most at risk. The three warehouses together employ around 90 people and process some 12,000 tonnes of donated textiles a year.

Squeezed from both ends

Oxfam's retail business is being pinched from two directions at once. On the supply side, the price of "rag" — the unsellable or damaged clothing once baled and sold on for recycling — has fallen sharply in recent years, hollowing out a revenue stream the warehouses depended on. On the demand side, resale apps such as Vinted and eBay have given people a direct, cash-in-hand alternative to dropping a bag of old clothes at the local Oxfam, diverting both donations and the second-hand shoppers charity shops rely on to move stock.

Oxfam is not alone in feeling the squeeze. The British Heart Foundation has closed around 150 shops, citing rising costs and the shift to online shopping, with about a quarter of its high-street locations judged commercially unsustainable. Cancer Research UK is set to close 90 shops in October. Across the sector, the charity-shop model built for a pre-Vinted Britain is being re-costed in real time.

A source close to Oxfam said the charity was weighing closures of up to 100 of its outlets where sales no longer covered costs — a fifth of its network, though nothing has been decided. Oxfam Activities, the trading arm that runs the shops, warehouses and festival stewarding contracts, still handed over a net £6.3m to the charity's humanitarian work last year, underscoring what is at stake if the model keeps eroding.

The soft signal

None of this moves markets on its own — Oxfam is a charity, not a listed retailer, and its troubles are substantially about a structural shift in how people offload old clothes, not about household budgets tightening. But it sits alongside a broader pattern worth noting: discretionary and "non-essential" spending categories, of which charitable giving and impulse browsing are archetypal examples, tend to be among the first to soften when consumers feel stretched. Oxfam's own diagnosis leans more on Vinted than on inflation, which is worth taking at face value — but a donations pipeline that keeps thinning is, at minimum, a small tell about how much slack households have for giving things away rather than selling them.

Oxfam says it has no immediate plans to close any of the three warehouses and points to newer bets, including a Manchester superstore and this year's Second Hand September campaign, as signs it is still investing in retail rather than simply retreating from it. The review of Batley's lease is expected to conclude in the coming months, and that decision — more than any shop-by-shop closure list — will be the clearest indication of how much of Oxfam's physical retail footprint survives the shift already under way.
