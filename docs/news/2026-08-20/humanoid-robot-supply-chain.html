Investors chase humanoid-robot 'picks-and-shovels' plays in the supply chain

Unitree's G1 humanoid robot. The company's Shanghai-listed shares surged as much as 629% on their trading debut this week. — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Analysts are steering money toward humanoid-robot component suppliers — actuators, sensors, motion control — over flashier robot makers like Unitree and Figure AI.

Yesterday in Shanghai, shares in Unitree Robotics — the Chinese maker of backflipping humanoid and quadruped robots — closed up 542% on their trading debut, at one point spiking as much as 629%. The IPO was oversubscribed more than 8,000 times, a record for the city's STAR market, and the closing price valued the company at roughly 342 billion yuan (about $48 billion). It is, by any measure, the loudest signal yet that investors want in on humanoid robotics.

But a growing number of analysts are telling clients to look past the robot itself. The thesis, borrowed from 19th-century mining booms, is that the safer money sits with whoever sells the tools everyone in the gold rush has to buy — not with the prospectors. In humanoid robotics, that means the makers of actuators, joint motors, sensors and gearing: unglamorous industrial suppliers that collect a fee from Tesla's Optimus, Figure AI's humanoids and Unitree's machines alike, regardless of which one eventually wins.

Why the parts matter more than the robot

Actuators — the motorised joints that let a robot bend an elbow or take a step — account for an estimated 40 to 60% of a humanoid's total bill of materials, according to McKinsey and Roland Berger. A single robot needs 28 to 44 of them. That concentration creates real bottlenecks: fewer than five companies manufacture precision harmonic-drive gears at scale worldwide, and the global supply of force-torque sensors for dexterous robot hands runs through a handful of factories.

The order books back this up. Sanhua Intelligent Controls, a Chinese thermal-management and actuator maker, took a $685 million order from Tesla last October for linear actuators — enough components for roughly 180,000 Optimus units. Goldman Sachs expects Sanhua's robotics business to grow revenue at a 19% annual clip through 2030 and to reach a quarter of total sales; JPMorgan has started coverage with an Overweight rating. In the US, Regal Rexnord — which integrates motors, actuators and micro-gearing into humanoid joints — reported automation and motion-control orders up 34% year-on-year, and carries nine Buy ratings against zero Sells. Schaeffler launched dedicated planetary-gear actuators for humanoids at CES in January. Micron is pitched as a quieter beneficiary too: humanoids reportedly need roughly ten times the memory of a self-driving car, and only three firms make DRAM at meaningful scale.

The valuation gap

The contrast with headline robot makers is stark. Figure AI was valued at $39 billion in a private round last September, before shipping meaningful revenue. Unitree now trades at a valuation ahead of several recently listed Chinese chipmakers. Component suppliers, by comparison, are still largely priced as ordinary industrial businesses — several trade in the high single-digit to mid-teens price-to-earnings range, with little to no premium yet built in for robotics.

The catch is durability, not existence, of that gap. Vertical integration is the obvious risk: Tesla and others could eventually bring actuator production in-house, as automakers did with parts suppliers decades ago. The next test comes with Regal Rexnord's and Sanhua's third-quarter results, plus any follow-on disclosures of robot-maker component orders — the clearest read on whether suppliers are capturing durable margin or just riding a temporary shortage.
