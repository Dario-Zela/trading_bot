Treasury doubles long-bond buybacks as yields tumble, dollar slides and gold nears $4,500

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C., whose debt-management office more than doubled its long-bond buyback capacity this week. — Sealy j / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Treasury more than doubled long-bond buybacks to at least $4bn after a buyers' strike pushed 30-year yields to 19-year highs; yields fell, the dollar slid to a 3-month low and gold neared $4,500.

The US Treasury is buying back its own long-dated debt at more than double the pace it was a week ago, and the market's answer to why is not flattering: because almost nobody else wants to.

On Wednesday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's department said it would raise the maximum size of its buyback operations in 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year securities to at least $4bn per operation, up from $2bn, effective September 9 through November 4. The 30-year yield, which had touched its highest level since 2007 just two days earlier, fell more than 10 basis points to 5.184%. The 10-year dropped 6 basis points to 4.637%. The dollar slid to its weakest in three months, and gold — which had been drifting below $4,400 as yields climbed — jumped toward $4,480 an ounce, within striking distance of $4,500.

That is a lot of movement to hang on a technical adjustment to a debt-management tool most people have never heard of. The reason it moved markets is that the buyback program exists, in blunt terms, because the ordinary buyers of long US debt have been going on strike.

A strike at the long end

Since late June, demand for the 10-to-30-year part of the Treasury curve has been visibly thin. Five of the last seven 20-year bond auctions have tailed — Treasury-speak for pricing at a higher yield than the market indicated just before the sale, a sign the government had to pay up to clear the deal. This week's 20-year auction priced at 5.204%, the second-highest yield on record for the tenor, with a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.53 — down from 2.64 in July and 2.75 in June, the weakest since February.

The causes are not mysterious, even if they are compounding. The federal government crossed $40 trillion in total debt this week, having doubled that figure in under a decade, and is on pace to borrow more than $2 trillion in the current fiscal year. At the same time, corporate America — led by hyperscalers financing an AI infrastructure build-out — has been issuing long-dated bonds of its own at a clip that competes directly with Treasury for the same pool of duration-hungry capital. Add persistent inflation concerns and a market still working out where the Federal Reserve's rate path actually goes, and the long end has been a difficult sell. Bessent's own buyback boost was explicitly aimed at keeping this week's 20-year sale from clearing above the record set in October 2023.

The buyback mechanism itself is not new — Treasury has run these operations since 2024 as a liquidity tool, repurchasing off-the-run securities to keep the market functioning smoothly rather than as an instrument of monetary policy. What changed Wednesday is the scale. Doubling the ceiling is Treasury effectively agreeing to absorb more of the paper that private investors are declining to hold, at the exact maturities where the strike has been most visible.

Gold, the dollar, and a market repricing duration

The currency and precious-metals reaction followed a fairly conventional script once the yield move is taken as given. Lower long yields reduce the opportunity cost of holding a non-interest-bearing asset like gold, which had already rallied some 10% in August — its best month since January — as soft jobs, CPI and PPI prints pulled down expectations for where policy rates settle. Wednesday's move added a fresh leg: gold's push toward $4,500 came specifically alongside the dollar's slide to a three-month low, as the buyback was read by currency traders as confirmation that Washington will lean on financial engineering to keep a lid on borrowing costs rather than wait for demand to recover on its own.

Equities, notably, did not treat this as a haven signal. The S&P 500 rose 0.43% and snapped a three-day losing streak, with the Dow up 0.25% and the Nasdaq up 0.4%; six of eleven sectors advanced, led by healthcare. That is not really a contradiction once the yield channel is accounted for — falling long-term borrowing costs are unambiguously good for equity valuations and corporate refinancing, independent of whatever the same move implies for the dollar or for gold as a store of value. Stocks, currency and metals were all pricing the same underlying fact — lower long yields, engineered rather than earned — through three different lenses.

Whose problem this becomes

The awkward part of Wednesday's move is what it implies about the alternative. If Treasury has to nearly double its own buying to keep long yields from ratcheting higher, it is fair to ask what happens to the buyback program's next renewal, or the one after that, if the underlying demand gap does not close. Critics were quick to frame the move in political terms — RSM's chief economist described Bessent as acting on a short-term timetable tied to the electoral calendar rather than to price stability — and there is a structural tension worth taking at face value regardless of motive: a Treasury absorbing more of its own long-dated issuance is, functionally, doing part of the job markets usually expect the Federal Reserve to do through its own balance sheet. That leaves Fed Chair Kevin Warsh in an odd position, managing a policy rate path that now has to account for a Treasury that is actively suppressing the long end by other means.

None of this resolves the underlying question, which is whether private demand for 10-to-30-year Treasuries recovers once the AI-driven corporate issuance wave and the deficit trajectory become clearer, or whether buybacks of this size become a recurring fixture. The next test arrives quickly: the buyback expansion runs through November 4, and the 20- and 30-year auction calendar between now and then — alongside whatever the Fed signals about its own rate path — will show whether Wednesday's relief was a turning point in long-bond demand or just a well-timed subsidy.
