Doctors warn vapes could cause lasting health harm in children

A disposable e-cigarette of the type UK doctors and regulators have targeted for restriction. — Vaping360 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
RCPCH review of 81 studies links youth vaping to breathing problems and later smoking, but no flavour ban is yet planned — only a consultation closing October 2.

Nearly one in five 11- to 17-year-olds in Britain has now tried vaping, and the country's leading children's doctors say the habit is being sold to families as harmless when the evidence increasingly says otherwise. The Royal College of Paediatrics and Child Health (RCPCH) on Wednesday published a systematic review of 81 studies on vaping's effects on children, concluding that the devices are linked to wheezing, coughing, sleep problems, oral damage and — most pointedly — a higher likelihood of taking up cigarettes later on.[^1]

"Vaping should not be seen as a harmless part of growing up," said Professor Will Carroll, one of the review's authors, citing "clear links with respiratory symptoms, nicotine dependence and subsequent cigarette smoking." Dr Mike McKean, RCPCH vice-president for policy, went further: "A clear and concerning picture is emerging" about the long-term effects on young lungs, hearts and brains — organs, the college notes, that are still developing through adolescence.

The review also flagged evidence of harm in babies born to mothers who vaped during pregnancy, including abnormal reflexes and slower motor development, and pointed to associations with anxiety and depression, though the authors are careful to describe these as correlations rather than proven causes.

Caveats attached

Not every expert is convinced the evidence is as solid as the RCPCH's language suggests. Dr Nicola Lindson, a smoking-cessation researcher, told the Science Media Centre that much of the underlying data comes from surveys — a weaker form of evidence for establishing cause and effect — and that the review does not distinguish clearly between higher- and lower-quality studies, nor between regulated and unregulated vapes. She also noted the review should be read alongside separate evidence that nicotine vaping helps adult smokers quit, and that cigarettes remain far more harmful than vaping.

What regulators are actually doing

The RCPCH is pressing ministers to go beyond current plans and restrict vape flavours themselves, not just their names and packaging. For now, the government isn't there. The Tobacco and Vapes Act, which became law in April, bans free samples and sales to under-18s from October 29, and a single-use disposable vape ban takes effect October 1 alongside a new vaping products duty. An advertising and sponsorship ban follows in June 2027. A separate consultation on child-appealing flavour names and plain packaging — not an outright flavour ban — closes October 2.

That gap between medical advocacy and legislative reality is the story here for now: real warnings, a real review, but no flavour ban on the table and no sign one is imminent. The October 2 consultation deadline is the next point at which that could change.

[^1]: Published in Archives of Disease in Childhood.
