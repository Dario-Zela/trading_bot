Russia launches fresh missile strikes on Kyiv region, killing at least eight

The aftermath of a Russian missile strike on a residential building in Kyiv, part of the intensified bombardment campaign this year. — Rasal Hague / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Overnight Russian strikes killed at least eight in Kyiv as Ukraine says a Patriot interceptor shortfall — worsened by the U.S.-Iran conflict — is leaving its capital undefended.

Explosions rolled across Kyiv shortly after midnight on Thursday as Russia launched one of its now-familiar overnight barrages against the Ukrainian capital — ballistic missiles followed by waves of drones, hitting residential blocks, a children's hospital and warehouses in the Sviatoshynskyi, Solomianskyi and Shevchenkivskyi districts. By morning, officials put the toll at eight dead in the capital, with a ninth killed in the surrounding Brovary region, and at least 33 wounded. A nine-storey residential building collapsed and burned in Solomianskyi; rescuers pulled at least two people from the rubble as Mayor Vitali Klitschko warned that others remained trapped.

It was not an aberration. It was the fourth mass strike on Kyiv in three weeks — attacks on August 1, 4, 5 and now 20 have together killed more than fifty people in the capital region alone. What distinguishes this phase of the war from the grinding trench attrition of the past two years is what Ukrainian officials say is happening in the sky above the city: increasingly, nothing is shooting the missiles down.

A building falls, a hospital goes dark

The overnight assault combined Iskander-type ballistic missiles with cruise missiles and attack drones — Russia's now-standard formula for saturating Ukrainian air defenses, which must intercept ballistic threats in seconds and can only do so with Patriot batteries. Windows were blown out and vehicles set alight on the grounds of a children's hospital in the capital; power was cut across several districts as substations were damaged. Klitschko described "massive" damage and prolonged rescue operations at more than a dozen sites. The pattern echoes the May and July strikes documented on Kyiv's Spaska and Mezhyhirska streets — this is now a recurring geography of destruction, not a single night's tragedy.

The interceptor math

The more consequential story is arithmetic, not geography. President Volodymyr Zelensky said Ukraine received only a third as many air-defense interceptors this year as over the same period in 2025 — a shortfall he has traced directly to the U.S.-Iran-Israel conflict that erupted in February and consumed a large share of the Patriot PAC-3 stockpile the Pentagon had earmarked for Kyiv. The Center for Strategic and International Studies estimates U.S. Patriot PAC-3 MSE inventories fell from more than 2,300 to roughly 800 during that campaign. Lockheed Martin, the sole manufacturer, produced around 620 interceptors in all of 2025 — not much more than 1.7 a day, against a Russian ballistic-missile launch rate now running at twice its prior monthly pace, according to Zelensky.

In an August 13 interview, Zelensky put the gap starkly: Ukraine currently receives about 1% of the interceptor volume it needs from Washington. "If they sell us 5%, we will go through the winter and save people's lives," he said. "If they can sell us 10%, we will destroy all the Russians' ballistic missiles." Days earlier, after Russia fired 27 ballistic missiles at Kyiv and Ukraine intercepted just one, he was blunter still: there were, he said, "simply no interceptors for the Patriot system." In one mid-August strike, Ukrainian defenses failed to down a single one of 28 incoming ballistic missiles.

From attrition to exhaustion

For most of the war, Russia's aerial campaign has aimed at breaking morale and grinding down infrastructure through volume — missiles and drones launched faster than Ukraine's economy could rebuild what they destroyed. The shift now underway is narrower and, in some ways, more calculating: Moscow appears to be timing its heaviest ballistic salvos to periods when it knows Western interceptor stocks are thinnest, effectively testing how much of Kyiv's sky it can leave uncovered. Zelensky has framed this partly as coercion aimed past Ukraine, at NATO capitals — "pressure on NATO countries to look at the reaction," as he put it — a signal that Moscow is probing not just Ukrainian defenses but Western resolve to keep replenishing them.

The timing carries its own political charge at home. The strike lands as Zelensky faces domestic pressure over long-delayed elections, with a former defense minister publicly pushing for a wartime vote and parliament this week confirming a new one by a 312-2 margin — a reminder that the war's toll on Ukraine's institutions runs alongside its toll on Ukrainian lives.

For now, Washington has floated a joint U.S.-Ukrainian upgrade of Soviet-era S-300 systems as a partial stopgap, though officials do not expect it to ease the shortage before late 2026. The next data points to watch are the size of the U.S. Patriot allocation Kyiv receives before winter — Zelensky's 5% threshold — and whether Russia keeps concentrating ballistic strikes on nights it judges Ukraine's magazines to be emptiest. Neither answer will come from a single briefing; both will show up, as this week did, in the casualty counts.
