US tightens China AI curbs ahead of Trump-Xi September summit

President Trump arrives for a bilateral meeting with Xi Jinping at Gimhae International Airport, Busan, October 2025 — the last Trump-Xi meeting before Xi's planned September 24 Washington visit. — The White House / Daniel Torok
In one sentence
The US is tightening AI and chip curbs on China even as it courts Beijing's help on North Korea, ahead of Xi's September 24 White House visit.

Xi Jinping is due at the White House on September 24 — the return leg of a diplomatic pairing that began with Donald Trump's trip to Beijing in May. On paper, the visit is meant to bank the "constructive strategic stability" the two leaders claimed after that meeting and after a follow-up sit-down in South Korea in October. In practice, Washington has spent the weeks before Xi's arrival doing two things that sit awkwardly together: squeezing China's access to advanced computing, and quietly working the phones for Beijing's help in restarting talks with North Korea.

The result is a negotiating posture that looks less like a single coherent China policy and more like two tracks running in parallel, each with its own logic, that are now colliding just weeks before the two leaders meet again.

The tightening track

The clearest signal came from the State Department, which has drafted letters to 35 countries — allies and partners who signed on to a US-led "AI Opportunity Statement" in June — warning them that they cannot simultaneously belong to Washington's semiconductor and AI supply-chain initiative, known as Pax Silica, and Xi's rival World AI Cooperation Organization (WAICO), launched in July with Russia and other partners. "To be part of everything is to be part of nothing," the draft reportedly says, telling capitals to "choose deliberately." The demand followed a scare in Washington after Kazakhstan — a major supplier of the rare minerals advanced chips depend on — joined both blocs at once.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has separately blocked Apple from using Chinese-made memory chips — supplied by CXMT and YMTC — as a cheaper alternative to domestic parts, saying it is "not desirable for great American companies to use Chinese-made memory chips." Seven senators, including Republican Jim Banks and Democrat Chuck Schumer, have given Apple chief executive Tim Cook until August 21 to say whether Chinese chips will be used in Apple products sold outside China and whether any technical data leaked during supplier verification.

The sharpest edge, though, is a sanctions threat that has nothing to do with chip exports and everything to do with software. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in July the administration was examining whether Chinese AI firms had engaged in unauthorized "distillation" — training smaller models by harvesting outputs from more advanced American systems — and suggested sanctions were on the table if the evidence held up. Beijing's response was immediate and pointed: foreign ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun called the allegations "entirely baseless," and the Ministry of Commerce accused Washington of running a "smear campaign" designed to hobble a rival industry rather than police genuine theft. China had already sanctioned six US entities and tightened drone-export controls in retaliation for an earlier US move barring imports from 43 Chinese companies over Uyghur forced-labor concerns — a tit-for-tat that CNN and others have flagged as a real risk to the September meeting itself.

Amid all this, Nvidia chips remain the pressure valve Washington controls most directly: Beijing has approved ByteDance and Tencent to import roughly 10,000 H200 processors each this year, a fraction of the 100,000-per-company ceiling originally floated, underscoring how tightly the administration is metering even the sales it does permit.

The courtship track

Set against that is a second, quieter effort. Trump has told aides he wants to pursue a summit with Kim Jong Un as early as this autumn, and has instructed Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to "sharply reduce the scale" of joint military exercises with South Korea — a concession aimed at making a Kim meeting more palatable in Pyongyang. One option under discussion would tie a Trump-Kim summit to the APEC gathering in Shenzhen on November 18–19, which would put it on Chinese soil.

That is where Xi's leverage comes in. China's foreign minister, Wang Yi, spent August 19 and 20 in Seoul, meeting South Korean counterpart Cho Hyun and President Lee Jae Myung, with the stated aim of discussing "the political settlement of the Korean Peninsula issue." Wang's visit followed Xi's own trip to Pyongyang in June for a summit with Kim, and came as Lee has floated multilateral talks among the original parties to the 1950-53 Korean War. Beijing's foreign ministry spokesperson Lin Jian framed it in cooperative terms: "Maintaining peace and stability on the Korean Peninsula and promoting the political settlement of the Korean Peninsula issue is in the common interests of all parties."

None of this happens by accident weeks before Xi lands in Washington. China has real reasons of its own to want a calmer peninsula — an unpredictable North Korea complicates Beijing's own regional standing — but the timing gives Xi a card to play at the White House: help on North Korea, offered or withheld, as leverage against the AI and chip restrictions bearing down on Chinese firms.

What it signals

Taken together, the two tracks tell traders and policymakers something about priorities. Washington is not softening on technology to smooth the summit; if anything, the run-up has brought a sanctions threat, an ultimatum to 35 allied governments, and a corporate crackdown on Apple's supply chain. But it is simultaneously willing to make a real security concession — scaling back joint exercises with a treaty ally — to keep a North Korea opening alive, and is prepared to lean on Beijing to help deliver it.

That asymmetry matters for how the September summit should be read. If the administration is willing to trade military posture on the Korean Peninsula for Xi's cooperation, but treats AI and semiconductor controls as non-negotiable, then chip export rules, Pax Silica membership and the distillation-sanctions threat are the more durable variables for pricing US-China tech risk — not whatever conciliatory language emerges from the summit itself. Xi, for his part, arrives with Wang Yi's Seoul groundwork already laid, giving him something concrete to offer in exchange for relief on the technology front.

What's next

Apple's board has until August 21 to answer the Senate letter on Chinese memory chips — a small but immediate test of how far the crackdown extends into consumer hardware. The State Department's Pax Silica ultimatum to 35 countries is still in draft form; how many capitals push back, and how Kazakhstan resolves its dual membership, will show whether the bloc-forming strategy actually holds. And the clearest test of the North Korea trade comes at the November 18–19 APEC summit in Shenzhen, the venue floated for a possible Trump-Kim meeting — which would make the location, on Chinese soil, itself a marker of how much Xi's help was worth.
