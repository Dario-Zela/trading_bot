US national debt passes $40 trillion, more than doubling in under a decade

The U.S. Treasury Building in Washington, D.C., days before Treasury data confirmed gross federal debt had crossed $40 trillion. — MeanieHyaena / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Federal debt hit $40.05tn on August 18, with interest payments now exceeding defense spending and CBO projecting $63tn in debt by 2036.

The Treasury Department's daily statement crossed a line on August 18 that had been visible on the horizon for months: gross federal debt closed the day at $40.05 trillion. It is a round number and, in the way of round numbers, mostly symbolic — the debt was $39.99 trillion the day before and will be $40.1 trillion soon enough. But the pace behind it is the real story. Washington passed $30 trillion in 2022 and $38 trillion last October. It reached $39 trillion in March. Each trillion is now arriving faster than the last.

A decade ago the debt stood at roughly $19.4 trillion. It has since more than doubled, a milestone reached not through one crisis but a sequence of them — the 2008 financial crash, the pandemic-era stimulus, and now a run of ordinary peacetime deficits that shows no sign of narrowing. About a third of the post-2016 increase landed in the two years around the COVID-19 declaration alone. Under President Trump's two terms combined, debt has grown by roughly $11.6 trillion; under President Biden's single term, by $8.4 trillion. Since Mr Trump returned to office in January 2025, another $3.8 trillion has been added, partly because revenue that budget forecasters had counted on from tariffs failed to materialize after several were struck down in court — a shortfall that pulled the $40 trillion crossing forward by months.

The monthly arithmetic

The run-up shows in the monthly figures as much as the headline total. July's deficit came in at $432.3 billion, the highest for that month since March 2021 and the fourth-largest of any month on record, pushing the year-to-date shortfall toward $1.8 trillion. Twenty-six consecutive years of budget deficits sit behind the total, compounded by two decades of tax cuts that reduced revenue relative to spending, an aging population drawing more from Social Security and Medicare, and — this year — the fiscal package known as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, projected by the Congressional Budget Office to add $4.2 trillion to deficits through 2034.

On a per-person basis, the $40 trillion works out to roughly $117,000 for every American, or about $297,000 per household. Those are abstractions until they show up in a specific line of the federal ledger, which is where the more consequential shift is happening.

Interest overtakes the Pentagon

Net interest payments on the debt have climbed to roughly $1.1–1.2 trillion over the past year, making debt service the second-largest item in the federal budget behind Social Security — larger, now, than Medicare, and larger than the Pentagon. Interest costs first exceeded national defense spending in fiscal 2025 and have stayed ahead of it since; through the first ten months of fiscal 2026 they also passed Medicare. Interest now accounts for roughly 14-15% of all federal spending, a share the CBO projects will keep climbing: net interest is set to rise from about $1.0 trillion this year to $2.1 trillion in 2036, a 106% increase over the decade — faster growth than any other major budget category, including Medicare and Social Security.

The CBO's February baseline has gross federal debt reaching $63 trillion by 2036, with annual deficits widening from about $1.9 trillion this year to $3.1 trillion. Debt held by the public — the narrower, more market-relevant measure — is projected to hit 108% of GDP by 2030, which would surpass the 106% record set in 1946, when the debt reflected the cost of winning a world war rather than the ordinary drift of a peacetime budget.

Michael Peterson, chief executive of the Peter G. Peterson Foundation, described the mechanism plainly: "It's typically called crowding out when the interest costs represent such a big chunk of the budget that it puts downward pressure on every other program across the budget, and puts upward pressure on taxes because we need more money in the system to cover these interest costs." Higher Treasury yields feed directly into private borrowing costs, he noted — mortgages, auto loans, credit cards — making the debt's effects felt well outside Washington. Margaret Spellings, CEO of the Bipartisan Policy Center, put it more bluntly: "Federal debt is already raising the cost of living and choking out other spending and investment."

The trade-off ahead

What makes this milestone different from the $30 trillion or $38 trillion markers is the shape of the choice it forces. Interest is not a discretionary line item Congress can trim in a spending bill — it is a fixed obligation set by the size of past deficits and the rate the market demands to hold them. As it swallows a larger share of the budget, the money has to come from somewhere: deeper cuts to defense and non-defense discretionary programs, restraint on entitlements that neither party has been willing to touch, or higher revenue. None of those options has an obvious political constituency behind it, which is why the interest bill has grown for a decade largely unaddressed.

The next marker to watch is less the debt total than the rate the Treasury pays to roll it over. The department's shift toward heavier long-bond buybacks this month was aimed partly at holding yields down even as issuance grows; the September Federal Reserve meeting, and any further signal on the pace of rate cuts, will matter more to the trajectory of interest costs than any single debt milestone. The CBO updates its baseline again early next year — the number to watch there is not the debt total, but whether the deficit projection widens again.
