Monzo suffers UK-wide outage, activates backup system for first time

Monzo's current wordmark, adopted in 2022. — Monzo Bank Limited / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Monzo's independent backup bank kept card payments and transfers running through a near-four-and-a-half-hour outage — its second live activation in seven months.

Monzo customers spent the middle of Wednesday afternoon staring at a holding screen reading "we're experiencing issues — we'll let you know when they're resolved." Behind that message, the digital bank was doing something it has only had to do once before in a live emergency: handing the whole country's worth of card payments and transfers to a second, entirely separate bank it built for exactly this moment.

The fault surfaced shortly before midday and knocked out money transfers, investment access and in-app discounts, with balances and live transaction feeds also affected. Downdetector reports spiked to around 3,195 shortly after 1pm. Monzo posted on X that it had "identified some issues affecting Monzo" and activated Monzo Stand-in — its "fully independent backup bank" — so that customers could keep making card payments, withdrawing cash, freezing cards and sending transfers while engineers worked on the underlying problem. The outage ran nearly four and a half hours before Monzo said, in a statement to the BBC, that services were back to normal.

What Stand-in actually is

Stand-in is not a failover server sitting idle in a cupboard. It's a second bank, in the plainest sense: a replica set of 18 independent services running on Google Cloud Platform, written in different code to Monzo's roughly 3,000-service Primary Platform on Amazon Web Services, so a bug or outage in one has no way of propagating into the other. It handles only the essentials — Mastercard card payments, Faster Payments transfers, Bacs direct debits — reconciling what it processes back to the main system afterwards via what Monzo calls "advice" messages, once the primary platform is healthy again. Monzo has said the whole setup costs roughly 1% of what the Primary Platform does to run, precisely because it does so much less. It went into production in March 2025 after roughly two years of build and shadow-testing against live traffic.

Not actually its first outing

That's the part worth being precise about. Stand-in has already had a live outing this year: a two-hour outage on 13 January saw Monzo switch customers onto the backup bank so they could keep moving money while engineers chased the fault. And Monzo customers were caught up in a third incident just three weeks before this one, when a Faster Payments System fault on 28 July disrupted transfers at Lloyds, Barclays, HSBC, Halifax and Monzo simultaneously — an outage traced back to Lloyds' own infrastructure, not Monzo's, a reminder that even banks with their own backups still sit on shared national payment rails they don't control.

So the system worked, again. What's less reassuring is the frequency: three incidents touching a 15.2-million-customer bank inside seven months, two of them serious enough to need the spare bank switched on. Monzo has not said what caused Wednesday's fault, and its status updates — as with January's — offered no root cause.

What's next

Regulators are already tightening the screws on exactly this kind of disclosure gap. The FCA, PRA and Bank of England published a joint policy statement in March setting new rules on operational incident and third-party reporting, prompted partly by cloud-provider outages that rippled across the sector last year. Firms have until 18 March 2027 to comply. Whether Monzo eventually publishes a fuller post-mortem on Wednesday's fault — as some peers have done after high-profile incidents — will be one early test of how seriously that new disclosure culture is being taken before the rules formally bite.
