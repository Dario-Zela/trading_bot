UK inflation holds at four-month high of 2.9% as BoE weighs energy shock

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK inflation held at a four-month high of 2.9% in July as a 13% jump in the energy price cap, tied to Middle East oil disruption, complicates the BoE's next move.

UK inflation held at its highest level in four months in July, as a jump in household energy bills fed through to the headline number and left the Bank of England no closer to resolving its central dilemma: cut rates into an economy running above target, or hold and risk choking off a recovery that is already fragile.

The Office for National Statistics said the Consumer Prices Index rose 2.9% in the year to July, up from 2.6% in June and comfortably above the Bank's 2% target. CPIH, which includes owner-occupier housing costs, rose to 3.1% from 2.8%. Core inflation, which strips out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, held at 2.6% — evidence that the pressure is concentrated in one place rather than spreading broadly through the economy.

"The increase was the largest rise in gas prices since October 2022," the ONS said, pointing to the driver behind July's move.

The energy channel

The arithmetic is straightforward. Ofgem raised its quarterly price cap by 13% from 1 July, lifting the typical household's annual gas and electricity bill to £1,862 from £1,641 — an increase of £221. Gas accounted for most of the rise, up 24% on the quarter against roughly 5% for electricity, and gas prices overall rose 14.7% year-on-year. Ofgem attributed the increase to wholesale gas costs, which doubled in early March after conflict broke out in the Middle East and the Strait of Hormuz — a channel through which roughly a fifth of global LNG cargoes normally pass — became largely impassable. Crude has since settled around $91 a barrel, about 25% above pre-conflict levels.

Elsewhere the picture was more benign. Transport inflation slowed to 3.6% from 5.7% as motor fuel price growth eased to 15.5% from 21.3%, and food inflation fell to 1.3% — its lowest contribution since October 2021. Strip out energy and July's report reads as a continuation of the disinflation trend rather than a break from it. Include it, and the headline number is stuck well above where the Bank would like it.

The Bank's bind

Governor Andrew Bailey has been candid about the limits of what interest rates can do here. "A big rebound in energy prices would certainly change things," HSBC economist Elizabeth Martins said of the risk facing the Monetary Policy Committee — and Bailey himself has repeatedly framed the shock as one monetary policy cannot fix at the source. Speaking after the Bank's July decision, he said policymakers have "time to judge the pass-through of higher energy prices" before deciding whether they warrant a response, while acknowledging the Bank faces the "most difficult combination" of pressures: inflation running hot even as growth stays weak.

That combination is splitting the nine-member MPC. Three members voted for an immediate rate rise to 4% at the July meeting, up from two the previous month, while the Bank held its Bank Rate at 3.75%. The Bank's own projections now see inflation surpassing 3% later this year. Reuters polling of 64 economists found 56 expecting no change through year-end, six expecting a rise, and just two a cut — though a narrow majority still expect at least one cut by the middle of next year, once the energy shock is judged to have passed through rather than embedded itself in wages and prices more broadly.

That last point is doing a lot of work in the Bank's thinking. Wage growth has stayed within tolerable bounds and the labour market retains enough slack, on the Bank's reading, to stop an energy-driven price spike from becoming a second-round inflation problem the way it did in 2022. If that holds, July's reading is a one-off distortion the Bank can look through. If gas prices climb further, or wage-setters start pricing in the higher cost of living, the calculus changes fast.

What's next

The next Ofgem price cap review, covering October to December, lands in late August and will show whether energy costs are still climbing or beginning to ease as the market adjusts to reduced Hormuz throughput. The MPC's September meeting — where no economist currently expects a move — will be the first chance to see how directly Bailey's committee lets July's number shape its guidance. Markets, for now, are pricing a possible quarter-point hike before year-end rather than the cut that seemed plausible a few months ago.
