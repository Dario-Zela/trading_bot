HMRC's crypto 'nudge letters' rise 25% as DeFi scrutiny widens

HM Revenue & Customs' headquarters at 100 Parliament Street, Westminster. — Carlos Delgado / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
HMRC's crypto 'nudge letter' campaign rose 25% to 81,000 in a year, now explicitly targeting DeFi income and token swaps, not just cashed-out gains.

HM Revenue & Customs sent 81,000 warning letters to UK crypto holders in the twelve months to August 2026, up 25% from roughly 65,000 the year before, according to figures obtained by accountancy firm UHY Hacker Young. It is the fourth consecutive year of growth in a campaign that sent just 27,700 letters in 2024 — a near-trebling in two years.

The letters, known as 'nudge letters,' are not enforcement notices. They carry no penalty on arrival and accuse no one of wrongdoing. Instead they tell a recipient that HMRC holds data suggesting they may have underreported gains or income from cryptoassets, and invite them to review their tax position — typically through HMRC's digital disclosure service — before the department decides whether to open a formal inquiry.

From capital gains to the harder cases

Earlier waves of the campaign, which HMRC began in 2020, focused on straightforward capital gains: crypto sold for sterling, gains left undeclared on a self-assessment return. The letters landing this year go further, flagging token-to-token swaps, income from staking and lending, and activity routed through decentralised protocols.

Token swaps are, by HMRC's own description, a particular blind spot. Someone who traded ether for a smaller altcoin in 2024, rather than cashing out to pounds, may not have realised the trade itself crystallised a taxable gain on the ether position — no bank transfer, no obvious paper trail, but a disposal under UK tax rules all the same. Income earned through mining, staking, airdrops or DeFi yield is treated separately again, taxed as income rather than as a capital gain, which changes both the rate applied and the record-keeping required to defend a return.

"Once HMRC has this data, then tax investigations into cryptocurrency investors will be like shooting fish in a barrel," said Neela Chauhan, a partner at UHY Hacker Young, describing the department's growing ability to cross-reference exchange records against self-assessment filings.

Where the data comes from

The expansion in scope tracks an expansion in what HMRC can see. UK-registered exchanges are already required to hand over customer transaction data, and bilateral tax treaties extend that reach to some offshore platforms. From January 2026, that data pipeline widens further under the OECD's Crypto-Asset Reporting Framework, adopted by roughly 70 jurisdictions, which will require exchanges to report user and transaction data directly to national tax authorities. The first CARF submissions are due by 31 May 2027, with international information exchange following later that year. A further wave of nudge letters is already scheduled to run from July 2026 through March 2027, suggesting the current total is a staging post rather than a peak.

HMRC estimates around 7 million UK adults hold cryptoassets worth a combined £12.9 billion. Ignoring a nudge letter carries no immediate consequence, but a subsequent formal investigation that finds unreported gains can bring penalties of up to 100% of the tax owed, and in the most serious cases criminal prosecution.

For traders running DeFi strategies — yield farming, liquidity provision, wrapped-token swaps — the practical shift is that record-keeping now needs to cover trades that never touched a bank account. The next marker to watch is less the letters themselves than the CARF reporting deadline in May 2027, when HMRC's picture of offshore and DeFi activity moves from patchy to largely automatic.
