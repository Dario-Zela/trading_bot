India's beauty market booms as Gen Z spending surges

A Forest Essentials boutique in Mumbai — Estée Lauder took full ownership of the Ayurvedic beauty brand in March 2026. — Anthropologialist / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
India's beauty market is racing toward $40bn by 2030 as Gen Z spending surges, drawing buyouts from Estée Lauder, L'Oréal and Unilever.

India's beauty and personal-care market was worth roughly $23 billion in 2025. By the end of the decade, industry estimates put it near $40 billion — growing at roughly twice the pace of India's GDP and its broader retail sector. The engine behind that arithmetic is demographic: India counts 1.45 billion people, and 900 million of them are under 35. Add 950 million smartphone owners discovering products through Instagram and short-form video rather than department-store counters, and the result is a beauty market being built and shopped for almost entirely on Gen Z's terms.

The numbers on spending power are stark. Gen Z's share of India's beauty spending is projected to rise from 32% in 2024 to 50% by 2030, and per-head, that generation already spends roughly double what millennials did on skincare and personal care at the same age. Retail platform Flipkart says 56% of its beauty and personal-care shoppers are Gen Z, and two out of three beauty searches on the site now come from outside India's largest metro areas — evidence that the boom is not confined to Mumbai and Delhi boutiques. E-commerce's share of beauty spending is expected to hit 35% by 2030, up from just 8% five years ago.

Global brands buy their way in

Rather than build from scratch, multinationals are acquiring the Indian brands that got there first. Estée Lauder completed a full buyout of Forest Essentials in March, taking outright ownership of an Ayurvedic skincare house it had held a minority stake in since 2008. Founder Mira Kulkarni built the brand from a home operation with two employees into a business valued near $1 billion, and stays on to run it. In June, L'Oréal followed with a majority stake in Innovist, the digital-first personal-care group behind Bare Anatomy and Chemist at Play — financial terms undisclosed, but the founding team remains in place as minority shareholders. Unilever, meanwhile, has made at least four beauty investments in India through its venture-capital arm over the past two years, a quieter but broader bet on the same trend.

The pattern is consistent: global groups are paying for distribution, formulation know-how and an existing Gen Z following rather than trying to out-market local challengers. Redseer partner Kushal Bhatnagar frames the shift in structural terms — "the internet broke these barriers, with brands leveraging the power of social media platforms and influencers to reach consumers directly" — which is another way of saying the old advantage of owning shelf space in a mall has eroded.

What it means for the pipeline

Industry trackers now expect 10 to 15 Indian beauty companies to cross $200 million in annual revenue within three to five years, a threshold that typically triggers a fresh wave of acquisition interest and, eventually, IPOs. For multinational consumer-goods investors, the read is less about any single deal and more about positioning: Estée Lauder, L'Oréal and Unilever are each establishing footholds before the market matures, at valuations lower than they would pay once India's Gen Z cohort — and its disposable income — fully arrives. Domestic brands are not being pushed aside so much as absorbed, with founders retained to keep the growth engine intact.

The near-term catalyst to watch is the crop of $200 million-revenue Indian beauty firms working toward that threshold — the next acquisition or IPO filing from that group will show whether global buyers keep paying up front for access, or wait for public markets to set the price.
