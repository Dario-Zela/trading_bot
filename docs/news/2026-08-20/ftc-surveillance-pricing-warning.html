FTC warns retailers over algorithmic 'surveillance pricing'

FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, who said retailers must disclose when prices are set using consumers' personal data. — Federal Trade Commission / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The FTC proposed requiring retailers to disclose personalized, data-driven pricing, opening a 30-day comment window and a Section 5 enforcement path.

The Federal Trade Commission moved this week from studying algorithmic pricing to threatening to police it. On Wednesday the agency released a proposed enforcement policy statement declaring that retailers who set individualized prices using consumers' personal data — without telling them — may be breaking federal law.

"When consumers see a listed price, they expect it to be the same price that everyone else sees, not the retailer's estimate of how much they are willing to pay based on their personal data," FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson said in announcing the proposal. Under the policy, a business engaged in what the agency calls "surveillance pricing" — using location, browsing history, abandoned-cart data or demographic profiling to vary a price by customer — must make a "clear and conspicuous" disclosure: that the price is personalized, what it's based on, and what data feeds it. Skipping that disclosure would count as an unfair or deceptive practice under Section 5 of the FTC Act, the same statute the agency uses to police false advertising.

The proposal follows a two-year paper trail. A 2024 order compelled eight firms — Mastercard, JPMorgan Chase, Accenture, McKinsey, PROS, Bloomreach, Revionics and Task Software — to hand over records on their pricing tools; a January 2025 staff report found at least 250 retail, grocery and hospitality clients had adopted some form of the practice. Examples in the FTC's file include cosmetics promotions steered by a shopper's skin tone and higher-priced baby thermometers surfaced to profiles flagged as new parents.

The compliance clock

Once the policy statement is published in the Federal Register, the public gets 30 days to comment before it can be finalized — a formal rule or enforcement sweep would follow, not precede, that window. The National Retail Federation, whose members include Walmart, Target and Macy's, is already pushing to carve out loyalty and rewards programs, which rely on much of the same personal data. The grocery trade group FMI argues electronic shelf labels — the digital tags increasingly used for real-time repricing — don't by themselves adjust prices per shopper. Consumer Reports wants the FTC to go further and ban the practice outright rather than settle for disclosure.

States are already ahead of Washington. Maryland and Connecticut have banned surveillance pricing for groceries, and New Jersey joined them on July 23 with penalties up to $50,000 per violation, treble damages and a private right of action; it also imposed a one-year moratorium on electronic shelf labels. California and New York are weighing their own bills.

For retailers and the pricing-technology vendors that serve them — PROS Holdings (NYSE: PRO) chief among the publicly traded names — the immediate stakes are compliance cost and disclosure friction, not an outright ban. But a 30-day comment period is a short runway, and a final policy statement would give the FTC a ready-made theory to bring cases against named companies rather than merely gather data. The next marker is the Federal Register filing date, which starts that clock.
