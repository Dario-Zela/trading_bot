Meta's landmark child-safety trial opens in Oakland

In one sentence
A seven-week Oakland trial pits 29 states against Meta over claims it engineered Instagram and Facebook to addict children, with damages theories ranging up to $1.4 trillion.

A federal courtroom in Oakland became the venue this week for the biggest legal test Meta has ever faced over how it treats children — a trial that could, in theory, cost the company more than it is worth.

On Tuesday, lawyers for a coalition of 29 US states opened their case against Meta Platforms, accusing the company of deliberately engineering Facebook and Instagram to hook young users, then lying about the harm it knew the apps were causing. A separate claim alleges Meta violated the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) by collecting data from millions of children under 13 without parental consent. The trial, before US District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers, is expected to run six to eight weeks, with Mark Zuckerberg and Instagram chief Adam Mosseri both due to testify.

Hook, hold, harvest, hide

The states' opening argument, delivered by California Deputy Attorney General Megan O'Neill, compressed the whole case into four words. Meta's business model, she told the eight-person advisory jury, could be summed up as "hook the users, hold them for as long as they can, harvest their data, and then hide the truth from the public" — a strategy she said worked "especially well for kids." California Attorney General Rob Bonta has framed the case less as a damages fight than what he called a matter of "restitution and disgorgement": forcing Meta to give back what it gained by allegedly designing products it knew were harming young users while telling the public otherwise.

The case, filed in 2023 and consolidated into a multidistrict litigation, is the first of that group to reach trial — with California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey arguing on behalf of the full 29-state coalition. A whistleblower testified this week that Meta altered how it labeled and measured "addiction" internally in ways that undercounted the scale of the problem, according to court testimony reported this week — the kind of internal-documents evidence the states are leaning on heavily, in the mold of the 2021 Frances Haugen disclosures that first put Meta's internal research on teen harm into public view.

Meta has denied the allegations outright. In a statement, the company called the states' claims "unsubstantiated" and pointed to more than 30 teen-safety features rolled out since 2024, including Teen Accounts with default content restrictions, parental controls, enhanced privacy settings, and a one-hour usage reminder on Instagram. In court, Meta's lawyers argued the states were cherry-picking internal documents out of context, and that the scientific literature does not establish a clear causal link between social media use and reduced adolescent well-being.

The number that concentrates minds

Meta's own lawyers have told the court the states' theory of damages could, in the most extreme reading, run as high as $1.4 trillion — a figure that would exceed the company's roughly $1.5 trillion market value. The states' own lawyers have called that framing exaggerated, putting a more realistic figure closer to $200 billion if they prevail. Either way, the range gives some sense of why Meta fought hard, and unsuccessfully, to get the case dismissed before trial — Bonta's office secured a ruling last week largely denying that attempt.

Money is only part of what is on the table. California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey are also asking the court to order Meta to overhaul core product mechanics: eliminating features like the visible "like" count and infinite scroll that the states argue are specifically engineered to maximize time-on-app among minors, along with mandatory age verification and time limits for younger users. That is a request aimed not at Meta's balance sheet but at the underlying architecture of the product — the algorithmic feed, the notification design, the engagement loop — that has become the default operating model for nearly every major consumer app built in the last fifteen years.

Why the jury is only advisory — and why that matters anyway

One procedural detail is easy to miss and important to understand: the eight-person jury hearing opening statements in Oakland is advisory only. Judge Gonzalez Rogers, who previously presided over the Epic Games v. Apple antitrust trial, will issue the binding verdict herself. That structure keeps the case in her hands as a bench decision, but it does not lower the stakes — a written finding from a federal judge that platform design choices constitute an actionable, quantifiable harm to children would be a legal template other state attorneys general, and other platforms, would have to reckon with immediately.

That is the wider significance traders and executives elsewhere in the industry are watching for. TikTok, Snap and YouTube all face their own strands of the same multidistrict litigation, and all run on some version of the same engagement-optimized feed architecture this trial is putting on trial. A finding against Meta on the design-liability theory — as opposed to a narrower loss confined to specific misleading statements — would hand plaintiffs' lawyers a roadmap for the rest of the industry. A finding for Meta would, just as clearly, blunt years of regulatory and litigation momentum built since the 2021 Haugen leaks first made "Big Tech and teen mental health" a mainstream policy fight rather than an academic one.

The trial is scheduled to run into October, with Zuckerberg's and Mosseri's testimony likely to be the next major inflection point in press coverage and, potentially, in Meta's stock. Judge Gonzalez Rogers is not expected to rule until sometime after closing arguments — meaning the market will be pricing this uncertainty in real time for weeks before anyone gets a verdict.
