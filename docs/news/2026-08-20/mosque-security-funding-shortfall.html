Fewer than 200 of 1,800 UK mosques approved for post-riot security funding

Southport Mosque and Islamic Cultural Centre, which was besieged during the 2024 riots that prompted the government's mosque security scheme. — Hassocks5489 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A post-riot scheme meant to secure UK mosques has approved fewer than 200 of 510 applications, with 311 still stuck in a two-year backlog.

The government scheme set up to protect British mosques after last year's riots has approved fewer than 200 of the 510 applications submitted to it, according to data the Home Office released only after a nine-month freedom-of-information fight and an order from the UK's data regulator.

Figures obtained by the outlet Hyphen show that of 510 applications made to the Protective Security for Mosques Scheme between July 2024 and 3 November 2025, just 199 were approved. A further 311 — well over half — remained stuck in the pipeline, neither approved nor formally rejected. The Home Office did not turn down a single application outright during that period; it simply left most of them pending, some for close to two years.

A scheme born of crisis

The Protective Security for Mosques Scheme grew directly out of the disorder that followed the Southport stabbings in the summer of 2024, when false rumours about the attacker's identity spread online and far-right crowds besieged mosques in Southport, Hull, Sunderland and elsewhere. The Southport mosque itself came under siege within days of the killings, its windows smashed and worshippers trapped inside as police were attacked outside. The Home Office responded with a promise of rapid security funding — CCTV, alarms, fencing, reinforced doors, guarding — and has since folded the mosque-specific scheme into a wider £40 million annual pot for places of worship, run for the department by a single approved contractor, Esotec.

That scale of ambition is what makes the delivery numbers land badly. Across England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland there are roughly 1,800 mosques; on Hyphen's figures, fewer than 200 have actually received sign-off through the scheme built in response to attacks on them.

Backlog, not rejection

What distinguishes this failure from an ordinary funding shortfall is the shape of it. The Home Office has not been telling mosques no — it has mostly not been telling them anything. Of the 311 pending applications, some have reportedly been waiting the better part of two years, according to the Muslim Council of Britain (MCB), which represents more than 500 affiliate mosques and other institutions. The department resisted disclosing the figures at all, telling the Information Commissioner's Office that publication risked "undermining national security" and "stoking community tensions" — an argument the regulator rejected in July, ordering the data released.

Dr Naomi Green, the MCB's deputy secretary general, said the numbers were "yet more evidence that this scheme is not fit for purpose and in desperate need of overhaul," and the organisation is now calling for an independent review of how applications are assessed and processed. Labour MP Bell Ribeiro-Addy, who has raised the issue in Parliament, put the underlying complaint more bluntly: "Protection for places of worship should not be a favour — it should be a basic duty."

The MCB's own briefing papers point to structural causes beyond simple under-resourcing: applicants have not been able to choose their own security contractor since 2020, a restriction the council has asked twice, in 2019 and again this year, to have reversed, and unsuccessful or stalled bids are often let down by unclear guidance on what counts as sufficient evidence of vulnerability to hate crime. The Home Office's response to Hyphen was that it is "working to process all applications as quickly as possible."

What to watch

The MCB's call for an independent review is the immediate pressure point, and the government has not yet said whether it will grant one. With the backlog now public and the ICO having forced the issue into daylight, the next marker will be whether the Home Office publishes updated approval figures — and whether the 311 pending cases move before the second anniversary of the riots next summer.
