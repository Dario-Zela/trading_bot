Whales move into Greenland waters as Arctic sea ice disappears

A humpback whale breaches — one of three baleen species now gathering in unprecedented numbers off East Greenland as sea ice retreats. — Brigitte Werner / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Roughly 16,000 baleen whales now summer in East Greenland's newly ice-free waters, a shift researchers say squeezes ice-dependent Arctic species and hints at wider fishing and shipping disruption.

Off the east coast of Greenland, in waters that were locked under pack ice for most of the twentieth century, marine biologists are now counting whales by the thousand. A study published in Frontiers in Marine Science estimates that roughly 16,000 baleen whales — about 4,000 humpbacks, 6,000 fin whales and a minke population that has doubled to around 6,500 since 2015 — now spend their summers feeding along the East Greenland coast. Before 1950, researchers say, these species were rarely seen there at all. Humpback sightings alone climbed from seven in 2007 to more than 150 by 2024.

The draw is capelin. The small, oily fish that whales feed on in vast quantities has shifted its range northward as sea-surface temperatures east of Greenland have risen by roughly 1-2°C since the 1990s, following a marked shift in the region's ice regime around the year 2000. Summer pack ice that once sealed off the coast has receded sharply, particularly through the Fram Strait, opening water that whales can now reach — and follow their prey into for as long as four months a year. The whales researchers tracked are estimated to consume between 800,000 and 1.3 million tonnes of capelin and krill annually during that stretch, a volume that itself reshapes the local food web.

Winners and losers

"For some species, climate change is not necessarily a problem — it opens up new opportunities," said Mads Peter Heide-Jørgensen of the Greenland Institute of Natural Resources, who led the research with colleagues from Aarhus University, the University of Copenhagen and Iceland's Marine and Freshwater Research Institute. "But other species are losing their habitat."

That second half of the sentence is the one worth sitting with. Narwhals, belugas and bowhead whales evolved around the protection sea ice provided — a refuge from predators and a platform tied to their feeding and breeding cycles. As humpbacks, fins and minkes move in to compete for the same fish stocks, researchers describe ice-dependent species as being squeezed on two fronts: vanishing habitat and new competition for food, arriving at once.

Why it travels beyond biology

The reshuffling is not confined to whales. Arctic sea ice extent in September has fallen by more than a third since 1980, and vessel traffic into Polar Code Arctic waters rose by roughly 37% between 2013 and 2023 as routes that were once ice-locked became navigable for more of the year. More ships mean more overlap with whale migration corridors and a rising risk of strikes, while capelin's northward shift raises harder questions for the commercial fisheries in Iceland, Norway and Greenland that depend on it. Both threads point the same direction: an Arctic marine economy — fishing quotas, shipping insurance, port access — built around where ice used to be, now having to be redrawn around where it no longer is.

The next marker to watch is Arctic sea ice minimum, typically reached in September; this year's figure, expected within weeks, will show whether the retreat that opened Greenland's waters to whales continues at pace or slows.
