'Housefishing': when AI-enhanced property photos become misleading

In one sentence
Buyers say AI-edited listing photos mislead them, and reported labelling rules at portals like Rightmove appear patchily followed, but regulatory action is unconfirmed.

Estate agents have long known that a wide-angle lens and a tidy sofa sell a house. What has changed is how much software can now do to a listing photo. Paint can be recoloured, furniture conjured, a patchy lawn replaced with a green one, a grey afternoon turned into golden dusk. Buyers have begun calling the practice "housefishing", after catfishing, and a BBC report published on 7 November, as summarised by secondary outlets, put it in front of a wider audience.

The BBC article itself could not be read for this piece. What follows relies on those summaries and on a separate industry survey, and the detail should be treated accordingly.

What buyers are reporting

According to summaries of the BBC report, property hunters in Sussex described AI-generated furnishings in online listings as "false advertising" that wastes everyone's time. Some listings carried watermarks such as "Virtually staged" or "Artificially enhanced". Many others, the BBC reportedly found, carried no disclosure at all.

Rightmove, the UK's largest property portal, is reported to allow AI imagery provided it is clearly labelled. The reported gap between that rule and what the BBC found on the site is the heart of the matter. A policy that depends on agents labelling their own pictures is only as strong as the checking behind it, and no detail of that checking has been verified here.

Agents, for their part, defend virtual staging mainly on cost. Dressing an empty room digitally is far cheaper than hiring furniture and a photographer's time. A staged living room that is labelled as staged is not obviously deceptive. A photo with a repainted wall and no label is a different thing.

The numbers so far

The best figures come from a survey commissioned by House Buyer Bureau, a property-buying company, of 906 UK people who had bought a home in the previous year. It is an interested party's research, and the polling firm was not named in the coverage found. Its findings:

  • 20% said they had viewed a property whose listing photos had been digitally enhanced.
  • Of those, the commonest edits were making rooms look larger (25%) and brighter (21%). 16% believed the home's condition had been improved digitally.
  • 53% said the images persuaded them to book a viewing they might not otherwise have made.
  • 54% ruled the property out immediately on seeing it in person. 34% still made an offer.
  • 38% lowered their maximum price because the presentation felt misleading.
  • 82% did not complain about the images. 15% raised it with the agent.

The company's managing director, Chris Hodgkinson, said: "Once a buyer feels they've been misled, it's incredibly difficult to rebuild that confidence." The figures cut two ways. Enhancement does get people through the door, but it also appears to cost sellers goodwill and, in some cases, price.

Where the line sits

The consumer-protection question is where legitimate touch-up ends. Brightening a dim photo or straightening verticals is routine. Adding a view, removing a pylon, enlarging a room or hiding damp changes what a buyer is being told about the property. UK law already bars misleading advertising, and the usual enforcers are the Advertising Standards Authority, Trading Standards and the Competition and Markets Authority. None of the coverage found establishes whether any of them has acted on AI-edited listings, so that remains an open question rather than a finding.

The low complaint rate matters too. If four in five affected buyers say nothing, regulators will see little of the problem unless they go looking.

What to watch

The near-term test is whether portals tighten enforcement of their own labelling rules, and whether any regulator issues guidance on AI-altered listing images. Neither has been confirmed. Until then, the evidence is a mix of anecdote and one commissioned survey, enough to show a real complaint but not yet to size it.
