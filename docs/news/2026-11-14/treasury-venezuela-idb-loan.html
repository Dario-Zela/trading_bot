US Treasury weighs short-term loan to help Venezuela clear IDB debt

In one sentence
The US Treasury is reportedly weighing a short-term loan so Venezuela can clear about $2.3bn of IDB arrears, but nothing is decided and no terms are public.

The US Treasury is considering a short-term loan to Venezuela so that Caracas can clear what it owes the Inter-American Development Bank, according to Bloomberg, which reported the idea on 13 November citing people familiar with the matter. Nothing has been decided. No amount, terms or timetable have been made public.

The point of the exercise is access. The IDB's rules bar new lending to a member that is in arrears, and Venezuela owed the bank about $2.3bn in overdue principal and interest as of 30 September. A bridge from Washington would, in principle, pay that off and let the bank start lending again.

Why the arrears matter

The IDB board unanimously approved a country engagement note for Venezuela on 13 November, the same day as the Bloomberg report. The note is a framework for re-engagement. It is not a loan, and it does not clear the arrears. Until the arrears are settled, the bank cannot turn the framework into financing.

That leaves a sequencing problem. Venezuela needs the money to unlock the lending, and the lending is what would eventually put it in a position to repay.

Treasury, or Qatar

The Treasury idea is not the first bridge proposed. In October, Reuters reported that Venezuelan authorities were weighing a bridge loan from Qatar to clear the same arrears, with US support. The Bloomberg report suggests Washington may now be considering using its own balance sheet instead, or as well.

The Spanish-language outlet La Patilla, summarising the story, says an operation of this kind could draw political debate in the US because it might involve the Exchange Stabilization Fund. That is a Treasury tool lawmakers have already questioned in Venezuela-related proposals. A January Semafor report likewise said the fund had been floated as a short-term bridge towards an IMF programme. Which vehicle Treasury would use, if any, has not been confirmed. The figures in circulation are scenarios rather than authorised disbursements.

What it would signal

A loan from Treasury would be a different kind of signal from a Gulf lender stepping in. It would show US policy using public money to reopen a multilateral channel to Caracas, rather than simply easing sanctions and leaving others to finance the gap. For sovereign-debt investors, clearing IDB arrears would not touch Venezuela's defaulted bonds. It would, though, mark a step in how the country's creditor relationships are being rebuilt, with the multilateral lender paid first.

The signal is weaker if the idea stays a proposal. Treasury has not publicly commented in the reports reviewed, and the Bloomberg story rests on unnamed sources.

What to watch

Three things would settle it: a confirmation of the amount and the vehicle from Treasury, any congressional reaction if the Exchange Stabilization Fund is involved, and whether the Qatari option is pursued instead. The IDB's next move on a lending programme is the final test. It cannot begin until the roughly $2.3bn is cleared.
