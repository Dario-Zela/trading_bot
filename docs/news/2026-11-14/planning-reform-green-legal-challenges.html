Lawyers warn planning reforms threaten the right to bring green legal challenges

In one sentence
UK lawyers warn planning reforms could narrow environmental judicial review, reopening a dispute over who can challenge major infrastructure and housing schemes in court.

A familiar argument is being rerun in Britain's planning system: how easily should a citizen or a charity be able to ask a court whether a major scheme was lawfully approved? Lawyers are now warning that planning reforms could narrow the route for environmental judicial review, the mechanism green groups use to challenge infrastructure and housing decisions. The warning matters to developers, councils and anyone counting on new building to square with climate and nature commitments.

The detail of the latest warning is not yet established. The report behind it, from the Guardian's politics desk, is summarised only briefly, and this article has not been able to confirm the provisions at issue, the lawyers involved or the timetable. What can be set out is the legal terrain on which the argument is being fought.

What judicial review does

Judicial review is not an appeal on the merits. A court does not decide whether a road, a runway or an estate should be built. It asks whether the decision-maker acted lawfully: whether it followed the right procedure, applied the law correctly, and considered what it was required to consider. In environmental cases that often means whether an assessment of effects on habitats, emissions or water was properly carried out.

That narrow function is why both sides care about it. Campaigners see it as the main practical check on a government that also writes the rules. Developers and ministers see it as a source of delay and cost, particularly for schemes the government has designated as national priorities.

A fight already under way

The current dispute follows earlier ones over the Planning and Infrastructure Bill, now an Act. In 2025 the Guardian reported that a nature group had threatened judicial review of the bill, arguing it would weaken protections built up over decades. Wild Justice, the campaign group, went on to file a High Court application. Its target was a statement made in Parliament and in official guidance by the then Deputy Prime Minister, Angela Rayner, in April 2025, that the bill would not reduce the level of environmental protection in law.

The group relied on an opinion from planning barristers saying the bill would in fact weaken existing protections. The case was therefore not a direct attack on the policy. It tested whether ministers had described its effect accurately.

Since the Act passed, lawyers' commentary has turned to its practical consequences. A June 2026 blog from Garden Court Chambers on climate litigation after the Act records critics' argument that the judicial review changes risk raising costs, reducing early judicial filtering, and setting a precedent for wider limits on public-law accountability. That is a claim by critics, and the same reforms have defenders who say they will speed decisions.

The wider debate

Ireland has had a parallel row. Proposals there would have required groups seeking judicial review of a development to have existed for three years, have at least 100 members, and be "personally and substantially" affected. One lawyer called the plans "an attack on the rule of law", while a Law Society Gazette report argued the effect on judicial review had been overstated. The comparison is imperfect, since the legal frameworks differ, but it shows the shape of the disagreement: critics read procedural tests as barriers to access, supporters read them as filters for weak or tactical claims.

Three questions will decide whether the British version is as significant as the lawyers fear. Is there a concrete proposal, or is this a warning about one still being drafted? Would it change who may bring a claim, what costs they risk, or how much time they have? And would it apply to every scheme or only those designated as nationally significant?

What to watch

The next markers are the text of any amendments or regulations and the government's response. If ministers publish provisions on standing, costs protection or time limits, the practical effect on groups and developers can be measured against how many challenges are brought and how many succeed. Until then the dispute is over who holds developers to account, and how quickly.
