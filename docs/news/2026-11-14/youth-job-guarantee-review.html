Review author urges a legal guarantee of a job or training for young people

In one sentence
The author of a review on youth unemployment is urging that a guaranteed job or training place for young people be made a legal entitlement, tied to the government's NEET agenda.

The author of the government-commissioned review into youth unemployment is pressing ministers to go further than policy and write a guarantee into law. The proposal, reported by BBC UK, is that every young person would be entitled by statute to a job or a place in training. It would turn what is now a government aim into a right that could be enforced.

The review is led by Alan Milburn, the former Labour health secretary, who was asked to examine why so many young people in Britain are NEET: not in education, employment or training. Close to a million young people fall into that group. The article's own detail on the new recommendation is thin, and the precise wording of the proposed entitlement and its timetable could not be independently confirmed.

What the review has already said

The interim report, published in the summer, set out the scale of the problem. It put the annual cost of youth worklessness at about £125bn and described a 'broken system' that, in Milburn's telling, left Britain more exposed than almost any other country in Europe. Final recommendations were due in the autumn, which is the point the country has now reached.

A statutory guarantee would sit at the ambitious end of what such a review could propose. The government already has a 'youth guarantee' of its own, backed by about £2.5bn, which includes work experience, job placements and a £3,000 grant for employers who hire young people. But that is a policy commitment, funded and adjustable by ministers from year to year. Milburn has previously said the government 'must go further'.

Policy versus right

The difference between the two is practical. A policy can be scaled back in a spending review. A legal entitlement creates a duty on someone, whether central government, local authorities or Jobcentre Plus, to deliver an offer, and gives a young person a route to complain if none arrives.

The European Union's Youth Guarantee, the model most often cited, shows the alternative. It is a Council recommendation, not binding law, and member states have varied widely in how fully they have delivered it. Campaigners in Britain have also looked at statute: a Policy Connect report, Earning or Learning, called for NEET prevention to become a core statutory duty by amending Section 507B of the Education Act 1996. That is a third-party recommendation, not government policy.

The welfare politics

The proposal lands in a politically charged setting. The youth jobs question is tied to the government's welfare agenda, and to the worry among Labour MPs that further benefit changes could revive a backbench rebellion. A guarantee that pairs support with an expectation to take it up could be presented as the constructive half of a tougher welfare message. The unions, which urged an expanded jobs guarantee in their own submission, are likely to favour it.

The obstacles are cost and delivery. A legal right to a job or training place requires places to exist, and a duty to provide them has to be paid for. Ministers have so far pointed to existing schemes rather than new legal obligations.

What to watch

The question now is whether the government accepts the statutory element when it responds to the final report. A commitment to legislate, in a bill or in a King's Speech, would signal that the guarantee is meant to be enforceable. A reply that welcomes the recommendation but promises only to 'consider' it would suggest the existing, non-statutory youth guarantee will remain the limit.
