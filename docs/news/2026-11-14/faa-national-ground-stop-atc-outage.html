US imposes longest nationwide ground stop since 9/11 as air traffic radar and radio systems fail

In one sentence
The FAA halted all US departures for over four hours after radar and radio instability; officials blame an overloaded legacy network, say it was not a cyberattack, and the cause is unconfirmed.

The United States stopped every departing commercial flight on Friday afternoon after the systems that let controllers see and talk to aircraft began failing across the country. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) ordered a nationwide ground stop at 3:54pm Eastern. It was lifted at about 8:15pm, after more than four hours, which was the longest such order since the aftermath of 9/11. Officials said no one was endangered and that the cause was not an attack. By Saturday the question had shifted to why a system this central could be knocked over by what officials describe as an overload.

What happened

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the trouble began shortly after 3:30pm, when the FAA saw "instability for our radar and communications" at its Air Route Traffic Control Centers and at the Terminal Radar Approach Control facilities that handle aircraft near airports. The agency's notice cited a "telecommunications outage" and "extraordinary circumstances." Within about 25 minutes it halted departures nationwide. Duffy said roughly 3,500 aircraft were airborne at the time. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said about 3,000 landed safely after the order.

The disruption outlasted the stop itself. Cirium counted at least 827 diversions by 7:05pm, of which 670 were flights returning to their departure airports. About 11,000 flights carrying more than 1.5 million passengers had been due to leave after 4pm. FlightRadar24 put Friday's total at more than 4,700 cancellations and about 10,000 delays, with a further 700 flights already cancelled for Saturday. Counts varied through the evening. Figures in the region of 6,500 to 7,000 delayed or cancelled flights were being cited in the afternoon, before the totals climbed.

The previous nationwide stop came in January 2023, when a failure of the Notice to Air Missions (NOTAM) system, which feeds pre-flight safety information to pilots, halted departures for about two hours. Friday's was a different kind of failure. NOTAM is a database. This was the live connection between controllers and aircraft.

The official explanation

Duffy said the cause was old equipment meeting new demands. "Our switches, our routers, actually got overloaded with data," he said, which produced inconsistent data and led the FAA to shut the system down rather than trust it. He described an "incredibly old air traffic control system" running on analog telecom, and said: "We have old switches, old routers." He also said radar and communications were never lost entirely, and that the problem was intermittent. Officials acknowledged that new technology being added to the network had overloaded older components.

That account has two features worth noting. First, it is a description of a change interacting badly with a legacy system, not of a component simply wearing out. Reports that the FBI is examining whether a software update lay behind the outage fit that pattern. I could not confirm an FBI inquiry or a specific update in the official statements, so that remains a reported line of inquiry and not an established cause. Second, the explanation arrived within hours. Duffy has a clear interest in tying the failure to the age of the system, since he is asking Congress for billions of dollars for what he calls a "complete overhaul." He warned that more outages could happen and that flying would be "less safe" without the money.

There is independent support for the general diagnosis. A September 2026 report from the Government Accountability Office warned of ageing copper cabling, slow modernisation, and poorly scheduled changes that often overlapped and overwhelmed the system. The report is documentary evidence that the weakness was known in advance. It does not prove that this particular failure came from it.

Not an attack, though not yet fully certain

Mullin said there was no sign of a cyberattack. "It doesn't appear that way," he said, while adding that "there's nothing completely certain right now." Earlier in the evening he had said authorities were ruling nothing out. He also said the event was not "another 9/11."

One side question was dealt with quickly. AirSpace Intel, the contractor behind an AI tool called SMART, said on X that the ground stop was "not based on or a result of the SMART system." Duffy agreed it could not have been involved. Mullin said officials were still looking at it.

The distinction between attack and accident matters for what follows. An attack would raise questions about adversaries and defences. An accident points at the unglamorous disciplines of testing, staging and rollback: how a change is trialled, how much load the old components can bear when it goes live, and whether a failing system can be isolated before it drags others down. Aviation experts said backups should have contained this. "The whole aviation community is based on redundancy," said Ron Bartsch, a professor of international aviation law. Tony Stanton, formerly of Australia's Civil Aviation Safety Authority, said: "These are things that can be foreseen, can be anticipated."

Political fallout and recovery

Congressional Democrats moved quickly. House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries called it an "unprecedented breakdown." Senator Tammy Duckworth, the ranking Democrat on the Aviation Subcommittee, said she would demand answers from the FAA, writing that "the safety of our skies is built on redundancies." Senator Maria Cantwell, the ranking Democrat on the Commerce Committee, said the committee would review technology issues involving primary and backup communication frequencies.

The practical recovery is slower than the stop was. Aircraft and crews are out of position, and analysts said it would take more than a day, possibly several, to reschedule. "It's going to be slow," Duffy said, adding that it would take time "to get back to capacity." He said he expected operations to be fully up before the heavy Thanksgiving travel period, now less than two weeks away.

What to watch

Three things will settle the story. The first is the FAA's formal root-cause finding, which should say whether the trigger was a software change, a hardware overload or something else, and whether the change was tested under realistic load. The second is the hearings Duckworth and Cantwell have signalled. They will test whether Duffy's request for a full rebuild gets traction, or whether lawmakers focus on the FAA's change management instead. The third is the Thanksgiving week schedule, the first real stress test of a network whose weak points were exposed on a Friday afternoon.
