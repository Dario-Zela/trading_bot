Probation officers in England and Wales vote to strike over pay and early-release work

In one sentence
Probation staff represented by Napo have backed strike action in a ballot, putting a staffing dispute alongside the early releases meant to ease prison capacity.

Probation officers in England and Wales are moving towards strike action in a dispute that touches the part of the justice system now under most strain: supervising people who leave prison early.

The most recent figures I could confirm come from the union Napo. In an indicative ballot, 90.76% of the members who took part backed strike action, according to the union's own account. That ballot was reported in August, and it was not a formal legal ballot. No strike dates appear in the reports I found. The reporting does not give a turnout figure, so the size of the mandate cannot yet be judged.

What the dispute is about

The headline issue in the coverage I could check is workload rather than a pay claim. Napo says the Probation Service is in a deepening crisis, with caseloads too heavy for staff to supervise people safely. Pay and the early-release work are part of the surrounding grievance, but the reports I found do not set out a pay figure or a specific pay demand. Those details need confirming against the latest BBC and Guardian reports before they are treated as settled.

The early-release link is real but indirect. Napo has criticised the government's early-release plans as rushed. It has said they would see "thousands of domestic abuse perpetrators and stalkers" freed early. Its general secretary has said probation officers are crucial to any such scheme, and the union wants the money to go towards improving conditions for probation staff.

Why the pipeline is exposed

Probation is the final stage of the release process. Officers assess risk, prepare release plans, and supervise people once they are outside. A scheme that moves prisoners out sooner does not shrink the system's workload. It moves part of it from prison wings to probation caseloads.

That is why a dispute among probation staff matters beyond the workplace. If releases are being used to ease prison capacity, any slowdown in processing or supervision could push pressure back towards prisons. It could also leave more people under thinner supervision.

The early-release policy itself is politically fragile. Prime Minister Andy Burnham paused the scheme last month after a public outcry over the possible early release of the killers of PC Andrew Harper. He has since ordered Justice Secretary Alex Norris to find a way to stop those releases. The Ministry of Justice said it was disappointed by the Napo vote and would continue to engage with the union.

What to watch

The next step is whether Napo moves from an indicative result to a formal statutory ballot. Under trade-union law, that requires a minimum turnout, and it would give the union legal cover to name strike dates. The second test is whether the government's response to the dispute includes funding for probation staffing, rather than only a revised release policy. Without that, a paused or reshaped scheme would still leave the service with the caseloads that prompted the ballot.
