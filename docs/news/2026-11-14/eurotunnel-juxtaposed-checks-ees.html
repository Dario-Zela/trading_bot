UK and EU agree airport-style checks at Eurotunnel to avoid border queues

In one sentence
A UK-France arrangement for single-booth, airport-style checks at Eurotunnel aims to stop the EU's biometric entry system from causing queues at Folkestone and Coquelles.

Britain and France are working on a way to stop the European Union's new biometric border system from turning the Channel Tunnel crossing into a queue. According to reporting by the Guardian, the plan is to run airport-style checks at the Eurotunnel terminals, with a single juxtaposed booth handling the passport formalities for Le Shuttle passengers. The detail of the arrangement has not been published, and the two governments have not set out a full text. The outline matters because Folkestone and Coquelles are among the places where a slow check is felt most quickly.

Why the crossing is vulnerable

The EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) replaced passport stamping for non-EU travellers with a digital record. A first-time visitor has fingerprints and a facial image taken, and later crossings are matched against that record. The system began operating on 12 October 2025 and was meant to reach full operation after about six months, in April 2026.

The Channel crossing is awkward because of how the border is arranged. Under the Le Touquet and Sangatte arrangements, which have applied since the early 2000s, one country's officers carry out checks on the other's soil. French police check passengers at Folkestone and Dover. British officers do the same at Coquelles and in Calais. Le Shuttle carries cars and coaches between Folkestone and Coquelles, so every vehicle passes through one site, and any extra seconds per vehicle accumulate quickly.

Getlink, Eurotunnel's operator, has said in written evidence to a parliamentary committee that the new system risks disruption at terminals that are already short of space. It has also said it is investing more than €80 million at Folkestone and Coquelles to prepare. Eurotunnel has built a checks area at Folkestone with 106 booths and has argued that the system should add only a few minutes per crossing. Those figures come from the company's own statements and from regional press reports, and they are estimates rather than measured outcomes.

A rollout that has been phased in

The practical rollout at the Channel has been gradual. In July the Local reported that EES passport checks would not be fully implemented at Dover and Eurotunnel over the summer. Coach passengers and freight drivers were already being checked under the full system. Cars were not, and in some cases enrolment was done manually by border officers because the automated kiosks installed at St Pancras, Folkestone and Coquelles were not being used. A separate trade report said the UK government had pressed French authorities to relax checks at peak times, and that MPs had been warned of freight queues of up to 12 hours. Dover has asked for the checks to be suspended at busy times. The UK government has also announced £20 million to add passport booths at the Port of Dover.

This is the setting for the proposed deal. A phased start lets operators manage queues by switching checks on and off. It does not remove the underlying problem that a biometric check takes longer than a passport glance, and that it has to happen somewhere. A single combined booth, with the UK and French formalities done in one stop, tries to answer that by taking away a second stop.

What the deal tests

The politics are as significant as the logistics. Post-Brexit friction at the Channel is a recurring grievance for hauliers, holidaymakers and the Kent economy, and each side has reasons to avoid a visible failure. Britain wants to avoid the pictures of lorries backed up along the M20. France and the EU want the system to work without being seen to exempt a major crossing.

The harder question is whether the arrangement can be made smooth without reopening the wider border settlement. Juxtaposed controls are bilateral treaty arrangements, not part of the EU-UK trade agreement. That gives the two governments room to adjust procedures at the terminals without renegotiating the rest. It also means that any change needs to be consistent with EU rules on how EES data is collected and held. Whether a combined booth can satisfy both sets of rules is the open point.

Further details are still to come: the date from which the combined checks would apply, who staffs the booth, and how queue figures would be reported. The first test will be the first heavy travel period after any change takes effect. The next holiday peak will show whether the average of a few minutes per crossing holds at the busiest times.
