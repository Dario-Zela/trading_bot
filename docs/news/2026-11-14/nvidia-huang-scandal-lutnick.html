Nvidia CEO scandal spreads to Washington, with pressure on Lutnick

In one sentence
After Jensen Huang's resignation over reported affairs, including one tied to Singapore customer Megaspeed, senators are pressing Howard Lutnick on Nvidia chip export oversight.

The scandal that cost Nvidia its chief executive has moved from the boardroom to Capitol Hill. Jensen Huang resigned on Thursday after reports of affairs, one of them with an executive at a Singapore-based customer. Senators are now asking whether the Commerce Department, under Howard Lutnick, did enough to police the chip sales that customer was making.

The account below rests on CNBC's reporting from 13 November. The BBC pieces named at triage could not be located, and the White House has not been quoted. The allegations are reported, not adjudicated.

What is reported

CNBC says Huang acknowledged an affair with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company. The report describes that executive as an alleged Chinese spy. A separate affair with a senior Nvidia executive has also been reported. Megaspeed has bought more than $4.8bn of advanced Nvidia AI chips over the past two years, a figure that turns a personal story into an export-control one. Huang resigned on Thursday. A search summary of other coverage says the chief financial officer, Colette Kress, is serving as interim chief executive. That detail comes from a secondary summary and is not confirmed here.

Why Washington

Singapore matters because it is a known transit point for the question regulators keep asking: whether restricted chips can reach Chinese buyers through third countries. Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott summoned Lutnick over whether Nvidia could have supplied advanced chips to Chinese companies. He asked Huang to appear on 3 December. Both letters went out on Thursday evening. Lutnick's letter cited "recent public reports" raising national security concerns.

Lutnick has agreed to testify voluntarily, with NBC giving the date as 10 December. A spokesperson said he "proactively agreed to appear voluntarily" to answer lawmakers' questions. Lutnick has been a friend of President Trump for more than three decades and is seen as a close adviser. That makes any pressure on him a pressure on the White House by extension.

The political reaction

The criticism comes from both parties, and its target is licensing policy rather than the affairs themselves:

  • Sen. Jim Banks said Lutnick "appears to have used loopholes" to allow some chip sales.
  • Sen. Elizabeth Warren, who is due to chair the committee from January, asked Lutnick and Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler why export-control loopholes remain open. She and Sen. Chris Coons also demanded that all documents on Nvidia exports to China be preserved.
  • Sen. Ron Wyden and Rep. Richard Neal are examining approvals of sales to firms linked to the Chinese government, military or sanctioned entities.
  • Rep. Brian Mast called for a congressional look at the Commerce Department's role but said he has confidence in Lutnick.

Some of this builds on earlier reporting. Reuters said in June that the administration did not add more than 100 companies, including DeepSeek and CXMT, to a trade blacklist despite national security concerns.

What to watch

Two gaps limit what can be concluded. CNBC reports no White House response and no Nvidia share-price move, and neither is established here. Nothing reported shows that Lutnick or the Commerce Department approved a sale to Megaspeed, or that any chips reached China. The hearings are the test: Huang on 3 December, if he attends, and Lutnick a week later. The questions will be about Megaspeed's licences and how the department handled them. Nvidia's chip sales to Asia, and the successor who inherits them, will be judged by the same record.
