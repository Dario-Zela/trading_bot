Heat pumps: how much heat does your home really need?

In one sentence
A room-by-room heat-loss calculation, not the old boiler's output, should set heat pump size, and getting it wrong costs money and efficiency.

A heat pump that is too small struggles on the coldest days and leans on its backup electric heater. One that is too large cycles on and off, which wears the compressor and wastes electricity. Both mistakes are avoidable. Under the UK's installer rules, the size of the unit is meant to come from a calculation of how fast the house loses heat, not from a guess.

Not a like-for-like swap

The habit of matching the new unit to the old boiler is a poor guide. Boilers are routinely oversized, because they can fire hard and cut out, and because a larger boiler was a cheap hedge against complaints. A heat pump works differently. It delivers heat steadily at a lower water temperature, typically 45–55°C against roughly 70°C for a gas boiler, according to installer guides. The best-performing systems are sized closely to the building's actual needs.

What the calculation does

The method in use for certified UK installs is a room-by-room heat-loss calculation, based on the European standard BS EN 12831-1. Installer guides describe it as a requirement for certification under the Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS). The surveyor measures each room's walls, windows, floors and roof. They record the insulation in each and an assumed rate of air leakage. They then set a design outdoor temperature for the region and an indoor target for each room.

The result is a figure in kilowatts for each room and for the whole house. The whole-house figure sets the capacity of the heat pump. The room figures set the size of the radiators or the layout of underfloor heating. This second step matters because lower flow temperatures mean some existing radiators are too small and need replacing.

Why the numbers can mislead

The calculation is only as good as its inputs. A government-published field study found that calculated heat loss was often well off the measured figure. In one set of results, a calculation tool matched measurement in only about 30% of cases. Several sources suggest pairing the desk calculation with measured data on how quickly a home actually loses heat. Insulation work done before the install lowers the figure, and can allow a smaller, cheaper unit.

Homeowners can ask for the room-by-room results and check them. The assumed room temperatures, the design outdoor temperature and the listed wall and glazing types should match the house. A quote that gives a size without showing a calculation is a warning sign.

The policy backdrop

In England and Wales the Boiler Upgrade Scheme offers £7,500 towards an air-to-water heat pump. Official notices list £2,500 for an air-to-air unit. Reports indicate a higher grant of £9,000 for homes heated by oil or LPG from July 2026, but this has not been checked against the official notice. Sources disagree on the scheme's end date, with the most recent pointing to 2030. Anyone relying on the grant should check GOV.UK or Ofgem.

The government's 2021 goal of 600,000 installations a year by 2028 looks far off. Government statistics recorded 51,886 retrofit installations in 2025, up 7% on 2024. The Heat Pump Association, in February 2026, cited a target of 450,000 a year by 2030. The change in the goal has not been explained in any official source found. Factory-gate sales were higher, at about 125,000, but they count units sold, not homes retrofitted.

With volumes still small against the ambition, sizing quality matters. Each poorly specified install risks a high bill and a sceptical neighbour. The next data point is the government's quarterly deployment statistics, which will show whether the 2026 quarters recover from reported early-year weakness.
