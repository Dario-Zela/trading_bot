Putin hardens demands for peace with Ukraine

In one sentence
Reports say Moscow's peace terms now extend beyond Donbas to a ban on foreign troops and limits on NATO's eastern flank, but much rests on leaks and no text has emerged.

Russia's terms for ending the war in Ukraine have grown wider, not narrower, according to reporting this week, and the question for Europe is how much of that is new. Part of it looks like old demands restated more bluntly. Part, if the reports hold, reaches well beyond Ukraine's own borders. This account rests on press reports and leaks. The BBC report that prompted it could not be opened for this article, so its specific claims are not reproduced here.

What is reported to have changed

The long-standing core is familiar. Moscow wants Ukraine to hand over the whole of Donbas, to stay out of NATO, and to accept limits on its armed forces. None of that is new.

The reported additions are more consequential. At a Kremlin meeting on 12 November with the US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner, Putin reportedly rejected any foreign troop presence in Ukraine after a settlement. He is said to have called any Western force a "legitimate target", which would mean the war resuming. He is also reported to have said that "there is only one line of demarcation: the line of the state border". Analysts read that as leaving open Russian claims beyond Donbas, including parts of Kharkiv and Sumy.

The German newspaper BILD has gone further. It reports an informal Russian plan that would demilitarise Ukrainian Black Sea ports, including around Mykolaiv and Odesa. It would also curb NATO infrastructure on the alliance's eastern flank, and covers Moldova and Belarus. The Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov has denied one reported element, a demand that Finland leave NATO. The rest rests largely on BILD's account and is unconfirmed.

Why Europe is affected

If the wider list is real, it changes who the negotiation is with. A demand that Kyiv cede land is a bargain between Russia and Ukraine. A demand to remove Alliance bases from the Baltic states or rule out European peacekeepers is a bargain with Europe, which has not been at the table. Europe's security planning has leaned on the idea of a post-war reassurance force. A Russian veto on foreign troops would take that idea away.

The energy picture is murkier. Reports around a 5 November meeting between Kirill Dmitriev, Putin's investment envoy, and Donald Trump cited a possible energy truce lasting until the end of 2026. That would be a limited, temporary measure, and the claim comes from a single account of Dmitriev's briefing. It does not amount to a settlement. A tough winter for European gas, already visible in rising LNG prices, makes any such pause matter to buyers. A hardening of terms makes a lasting end to attacks on energy infrastructure less likely.

What the talks have produced

The talks in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November ended with an agreement to prepare a large prisoner exchange. They produced no movement on territory or on ending the fighting. There has been no public sign that Moscow is ready to concede anything that would bring a deal closer.

The test of whether this is substance or posture is whether the maximal demands appear in a written Russian text, or only in leaks and statements. Watch for any formal Russian paper that includes the troop ban or the eastern-flank provisions, and for how Washington answers it. Also watch whether the energy truce is formalised before year's end. If it is, that would be the one concrete sign of give.
