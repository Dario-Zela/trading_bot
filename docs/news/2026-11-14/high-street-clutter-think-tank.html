Think tank: rip out ugly clutter to revive UK high streets

In one sentence
A think tank argues street clutter is holding back UK high streets, reviving a planning-powers debate, though the report's own figures remain unverified.

Britain's high streets have a long list of troubles, from online shopping to business rates to empty units. A think tank now wants to add a cheaper, more visible culprit: the clutter on the pavement. Its proposals on street furniture and town-centre regeneration, reported by the Guardian, argue that redundant signs, bollards, bins and disused kiosks are part of what keeps people away. The reporting reaching The Bot Tribune did not include the report's own figures or its full list of recommendations. Those need checking against the original before they are quoted.

An old argument, newly framed

The case has a track record. In October 2023 the Centre for London published Reducing Street Clutter in Central London. It said that obstacles such as A-boards, disused phone boxes and stray rubbish bags make streets harder to navigate and less welcoming, and that they can deter shoppers and hurt local traders. Its case study of three streets counted 122 items of clutter along a 400-metre stretch, with Goodge Street the worst. Its recommendations leaned on central government: fund clutter assessments, and give councils clearer powers to remove street furniture that has outlived its use.

In 2024 the think tank Create Streets argued that old 1990s phone boxes were damaging high streets. It proposed amending the Town and Country Planning Act 1990 so they could be removed more easily. A charity survey of 69 UK towns has also put numbers on the problem, counting 109 obstructions on Camden High Street and 105 in Colchester, though Camden disputed the findings.

Whether the latest proposals build on these or break new ground is not yet clear from the material available.

Why a design question becomes a planning fight

Clutter looks like a matter of taste, but removing it is a matter of law and money. Pavement furniture is scattered across owners: highway authorities, utilities, telecoms firms, advertisers and shopkeepers. A council that wants a bollard or a kiosk gone often has to work out who owns it and whether any statute lets it act. That is why earlier proposals have asked for new powers rather than new designs.

Retailers sit on both sides. A-boards are a cheap way for a small shop to catch passers-by, and a ban would be felt by exactly the independents that regeneration plans say they want to save. Disability groups, for their part, have long argued that clutter blocks wheelchair users and blind pedestrians from shops altogether.

There is also a limit on what tidying can achieve. No study cited so far shows that clearing furniture raises footfall or sales by a measurable amount; the evidence is mostly about access, appearance and visitor perception. A think-tank recommendation rarely turns into policy on its own, and clutter would be a modest line in any regeneration budget.

What to watch

The test is whether the proposals reach ministers or the planning reforms now working through Parliament. A new council power to remove redundant street furniture would be a concrete, cheap change. A call for another round of guidance would not. Until the report's text is checked, its figures and named recommendations should be treated as unconfirmed.
