Setback for SpaceX Starship double-catch test

In one sentence
A planned simultaneous catch of a Starship booster and ship reportedly did not go to plan, but the cause and outcome are unconfirmed.

SpaceX's planned attempt to catch both halves of a Starship, the Super Heavy booster and the ship, did not go to plan, according to an early report from BBC World. No date, flight number or account of what failed has been confirmed. Searches run for this article found no reporting that describes the outcome, and this piece does not guess at it.

What is and isn't known

The only source is the BBC World item, which says the dual catch was planned and that it did not go as intended. "Did not go to plan" covers a lot of ground. It could mean a called-off attempt, a catch that was tried and missed, or the loss of a vehicle. Nothing available distinguishes between these, and nothing says whether either stage was recovered. Until SpaceX or a named outlet gives specifics, the headline claim is a setback and no more.

Why the catch matters

Starship is built around reuse. The idea is that a booster and a ship can each fly again within days of landing, with no ocean recovery and no long refurbishment, which is the only route to the very low per-launch cost SpaceX says it needs. The method is to bring each stage back to the launch tower and have its mechanical arms, nicknamed chopsticks, grab it out of the air.

The record so far shows how hard that is. On Flight 6 in November 2024, SpaceX called off the booster catch mid-flight. Automated checks on the launch and catch tower triggered the abort, and the booster diverted to the Gulf of Mexico. On Flight 7 in January 2025, the booster was caught, but SpaceX lost contact with the ship about 8.5 minutes into the flight after an apparent anomaly. Those reports date from 2024 and 2025 and say nothing about the latest attempt.

A simultaneous catch of both stages would be a harder test than either alone. It needs the pad, the tower and two returning vehicles to work together, and a failure of one piece can cost the other its chance.

What it means for the economics

The stakes are financial as much as technical. Rapid reuse of both stages underpins Starship's cost targets, and those targets shape the outlook for rival launch providers and for investors in the wider space sector. One failed attempt does not settle that question, since test flights exist to find problems. But the size of the setback depends on the cause. A tower-side fault can be fixed quickly. A fault in the returning vehicles could push reuse timelines back by months.

What to watch

The next marker is SpaceX's own post-flight statement and any review of the flight by the US Federal Aviation Administration, which has in the past required investigations after Starship mishaps. Those should say which stage failed, whether any hardware was recovered, and when the next flight might happen. This article will need updating once they appear.
