Bristol aims to become the UK's first 'wetland city' to cut flood risk

In one sentence
Bristol, backed by WWT and Aviva, is pursuing UK-first wetland city status with a five-year, 200-plus project programme, but no funding total or flood-reduction target has been published.

Bristol is trying to become the UK's first accredited "wetland city", a bid that would put ponds, restored rivers and small rainwater-catching wetlands at the centre of how it handles water, heat and flooding. The programme was announced in September. It is led by the wetland charity WWT and backed by the insurer Aviva, Bristol City Council and the West of England Combined Authority.

The aim is accreditation under the Ramsar Convention's Wetland City scheme. There are 74 accredited cities in 27 countries and none in the UK, according to WWT.

What is planned

The programme runs for five years and envisages more than 200 wetland projects across the city. They range from restoring urban ponds and rivers to building small wetlands that hold back rainwater before it reaches drains and watercourses.

Flooding is only part of the pitch. WWT and Aviva also frame the work as a response to heat, citing European Commission Joint Research Centre findings that city centres can run up to 6°C hotter than their surroundings in heatwaves, and as a response to drought. The city has more than 100 miles of waterways and sits beside the Severn Estuary, which has the world's second-largest tidal range.

The flood problem

Aviva's Building Future Communities report found almost one in three Bristol homes were at risk of the most common forms of flooding in 2025. The council's own figures are more specific. It says about 1,100 properties near the city centre and 200 in neighbouring communities face severe river or tidal flood risk. It projects that almost 4,500 existing properties could be at risk by the end of the century without action. Council leader Tony Dyer has warned that 3,000 homes are at risk if defences are not upgraded.

The wetland programme sits alongside, not in place of, that engineering work. The long-term Bristol Avon Flood Strategy remains the main plan for the tidal and river risk. Separately, flood defences at Avonmouth and Severnside include about 80 hectares of new wetland habitat.

What is not yet known

The published material gives no funding total, no target for how much flood risk the projects would remove, and no interim milestones beyond the five-year span. That matters because small urban wetlands are generally good at slowing runoff from heavy local rain. Their capacity to cut the risk from a tidal surge or a swollen Avon is a different question, and one that embankments and barriers are built to answer.

The backers present it as a shift in approach. Sarah Fowler, WWT's chief executive, said: "Solutions exist where we make space for nature, cooling our cities and making urban areas less vulnerable to drought." Claudine Blamey, Aviva's chief sustainability officer, said the approach can help communities "reduce risk as well as insure it." Dyer said Bristol "has always been shaped by its relationship with water."

What to watch

The test will be whether the programme publishes project-level targets and a delivery schedule, and whether the Ramsar application is lodged and accepted. Other flood-exposed UK cities will be looking for evidence that the approach reduces claims and damage, not only that it adds habitat.

Planning policy is a related pressure point. Lawyers have warned that planning reforms could narrow the right to bring green legal challenges.
