Chip rally unwinds: HSBC cuts Nvidia, AMD falls 10.5% and the Nasdaq 100 drops 1.7%

In one sentence
HSBC cut Nvidia to Hold with a $170 target, and AMD's 10.5% fall helped drag the Nasdaq 100 down 1.7% in what looks like a crowded-positioning unwind, though figures are unverified.

A single analyst downgrade and one very bad day for a second-tier chipmaker were enough on Friday to undo a rally in semiconductors. HSBC cut Nvidia to Hold with a $170 price target. AMD fell 10.5%. The Nasdaq 100 closed down 1.7%.

The figures come from aggregator reports (Motley Fool, TIKR, Benzinga) and have not been checked against exchange data or the HSBC note itself. The HSBC note's date and Nvidia's previous rating were not available. Those caveats apply throughout.

What moved

The arithmetic points away from Nvidia. A downgrade to Hold with a $170 target is a change of view on valuation, not a profit warning. The headline move was AMD's 10.5% fall, the largest single-stock move in the story. A drop of that size in a large-cap chip name is the likelier driver of a 1.7% fall in the Nasdaq 100, because semiconductors carry a heavy weighting in that index. How much Nvidia itself fell was not available in the reporting reviewed, and it is the number a reader would most want.

Some reports tie the downgrade to uncertainty over Nvidia's chief executive. The one-line summary of the HSBC call does not support that explanation, and no primary source has been located. It should be treated as unconfirmed. Separately, Nvidia's chief executive is the subject of a developing story in Washington, covered elsewhere in this edition, but that story does not establish why HSBC acted.

A crowded trade

The case for reading Friday as a positioning event rests on a second data point. Benzinga reports that non-dealer net long positioning in US equity futures stands at about $330bn, the second-highest level on record. Neither the date of that reading nor the record level was given. The underlying figures come from CFTC data, which should be checked before the "second-highest" claim is relied on.

If the figure is right, the mechanism is straightforward. When speculative longs are near a record, a modest trigger can set off selling that is out of proportion to the news. A downgrade of the sector's bellwether, followed by a double-digit fall in its main rival, is the sort of trigger that tests that. Nothing in the available figures shows how much of Friday's selling was forced liquidation, so the link is an inference from positioning levels, not an observation.

Beyond the chipmakers

The selling reached the companies that sell computing capacity as well as those that make the chips. Benzinga reported that shares in IREN, a former bitcoin miner that has moved into AI computing capacity, fell as AI-infrastructure names sold off. No percentage move was given, and the item is a single source. One company's fall says little alone. It would carry weight only if repeated across the group over several sessions.

What to watch

The test of whether Friday was a broad reversal or a concentrated one is whether other chip names fell by anything like AMD's margin, or whether the index decline came mainly from a few heavyweights. The first check is closing prices for the major semiconductor stocks and the sector's weights in the Nasdaq 100. The second is the next CFTC positioning release, which would show whether the $330bn long position shrank after Friday's fall. Until then, the evidence supports a sharp one-session unwinding of a rally in a crowded sector. It does not yet support a change in the trend.
