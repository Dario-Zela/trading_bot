Police investigate David Walliams over reports alleging serious sexual offences

In one sentence
The Met says it is investigating alleged serious sexual offences involving David Walliams, who denies wrongdoing and has not been charged.

Police in London are investigating a series of alleged serious sexual offences involving David Walliams, the comedian and best-selling children's author. Walliams denies any wrongdoing. He has not been charged, and nothing described below has been tested in court.

The Metropolitan Police announced the investigation on Friday. It said it had received non-recent allegations "in recent days" and that they were "separate from those previously reported by ITV News and The Guardian". Officers are also reviewing that earlier reporting "to establish whether there is any evidence of offences". Detective Superintendent Mike Cagney, who leads the inquiry, urged anyone with information to come forward, whenever the alleged incident took place.

The force has not said how many offences have been reported, what Walliams is accused of, or when and where anything is said to have happened.

How it reached this point

The police announcement follows a three-year joint investigation by ITV News and The Guardian, published on Tuesday after Walliams lost a High Court attempt to stop it. The reports allege that he preyed on teenage girls, some as young as 15, and on young women, with allegations dating back to 2003. Mr Justice Nicklin also refused his bid for an injunction to stop media outlets identifying him.

The sequence matters. Earlier in the week the Met said it had received no related reports of offences. It had contacted both outlets only so that potential victims would know how to report to police. Friday's statement marks a change: complaints have now arrived, and they are not the ones the journalists described.

Walliams has not responded to the published reports. In a letter reportedly sent to journalists some months ago, he denied being a "sexual predator" and said he had "behaved thoughtlessly" at times, while maintaining that he had not broken the law.

The institutions move first

The consequences for his career have come faster than any legal process could. The BBC has begun removing his programmes, including Little Britain, from iPlayer. His talent agency, Independent Talent Group, has dropped him as a client, and the Labour Party has suspended his membership. HarperCollins ended its relationship with him last year, after an internal inquiry into alleged harassment of junior staff, which is not a police matter.

He was a judge on Britain's Got Talent from 2012 to 2022, and sources have alleged inappropriate behaviour towards women on that show.

These decisions sit awkwardly beside the principle that an investigation is not a finding. Broadcasters and publishers act on their own standards and risk assessments, which are not the criminal standard of proof. A police inquiry of this kind can run for months and end without charge, or with one.

What to watch

The next markers are procedural. The Met may say whether any suspect has been interviewed, and the Crown Prosecution Service decides on charge only once a file reaches it. Walliams may also respond to the reporting directly. Until then the public record is a police statement that says little, a set of journalistic allegations, and a denial.
