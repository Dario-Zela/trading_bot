UK inflation and borrowing hit one-year highs, putting gilts and the fiscal rules in focus before the Budget

In one sentence
UK inflation rose to 3.5% and September borrowing of £24.5bn beat forecast, thinning the Chancellor's headroom and sharpening gilt-market scrutiny before the 28 October Budget.

Two numbers landed from the Office for National Statistics this week, and both point the same way. Consumer price inflation rose to 3.5% in the year to September, the highest since October 2025. Government borrowing for the month was £24.5bn, above the official forecast. Both arrive six days before the Budget on 28 October, and both bear on the gilt market, which sets the price of the government's debt.

The inflation print

CPI inflation was 3.5% in September, up from 3.1% in August. Economists had expected about 3.4%, according to FXStreet's summary of the consensus. The monthly rise was 0.4%, against a forecast of 0.3%.

The ONS attributes the largest upward push to transport, and specifically motor fuels, where prices were 29.8% higher than a year earlier. That makes the headline partly an energy story. The underlying measures also moved up, though:

  • Core CPI, which strips out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, rose to 2.8% from 2.6%.
  • Services inflation, which the Bank of England watches closely as a gauge of domestic price pressure, rose to 3.7% from 3.4%.
  • Goods inflation rose to 3.2% from 2.7%, its highest since September 2023.

The rise is a move back to where inflation stood a year ago, not a new peak. It does change the Bank's task. Market commentary in recent weeks has been about whether the Bank's next move is a rise, not a cut. Trading Economics reports that investors largely expect a 25 basis point increase to Bank Rate, currently 3.75%, at the 5 November meeting, though those bets have eased slightly. The brief for this piece framed the risk as delayed rate cuts. On current market pricing the debate has moved past that.

Borrowing above forecast

Public sector net borrowing was £24.5bn in September, £1.8bn (7.7%) more than a year earlier and £1.9bn above the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecast. Economists had pencilled in about £24bn. The ONS says it was the second-highest September on record, behind 2020, before adjusting for inflation.

The overshoot came mainly from debt interest. Central government debt interest was £10.9bn, up 16.6% on last year and £2.0bn above forecast. It was the highest September figure since monthly records began in 1997. Of the total, £4.0bn reflected the uplift on index-linked gilts, whose payouts follow RPI inflation. So higher inflation feeds into the interest bill directly, on top of the effect through yields.

For the first half of the financial year, borrowing totals £97.8bn. That is £4.4bn below the same period last year, so the trend is not uniformly worse. It is £6.0bn above the OBR's forecast of £91.8bn. Public sector net debt passed £3 trillion for the first time, at a provisional £3,005.0bn. As a share of GDP, at 94.0%, it is 0.7 percentage points lower than a year ago and 1.2 points below the OBR's projection. Debt is large in cash terms but not running ahead of the official path as a share of output.

Why gilts are the transmission channel

The Chancellor, John Healey, is measured against a fiscal rule that is judged on the OBR's forecast, which will be published alongside the Budget. The margin by which he meets that rule is known as headroom. At the spring forecast it stood at £24bn. Pantheon Macroeconomics now puts it at about £13bn, and other commentators give a range of £10bn to £15bn.

Yields explain most of the erosion. The OBR's own sensitivity analysis suggests a sustained one percentage point rise in gilt yields costs about £8.4bn of headroom by 2030. Yields have been elevated. The 10-year gilt yield reached about 5.38% in late September, and City AM reported the 30-year approaching 6%, a level last seen in 1998. Bloomberg reports the 30-year has since eased to about 5.8%, from a 6.04% peak earlier in October. That relief is welcome at the Treasury but leaves yields high by recent standards.

The September data add to this in two ways. Borrowing running above forecast means the starting point for the OBR's projections is slightly worse. Higher inflation, if the OBR judges it persistent, also raises debt interest on index-linked stock. Handelsbanken has said higher yields "add to the likelihood that fresh tax increases will be announced on 28 October".

That leaves the Chancellor with a familiar set of choices: raise taxes, cut planned spending, or alter the fiscal rule. Gilt investors tend to respond most to the last of those. A rule loosened to make room would probably be priced as a bet on more issuance. A package of tax rises that rebuilds headroom would probably be read as supportive of the gilt market, even if it is politically costly.

What to watch

Two dates matter. The first is 28 October, when the Budget and the OBR's forecast arrive together; the headroom figure, and whether it is rebuilt or accepted as thinner, is the number the market will fix on. The second is 5 November, when the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee meets. A September inflation rate above consensus, a services rate at 3.7% and a fiscal statement that adds to gilt supply would all push in the same direction. The Budget will show whether the Treasury intends to push the other way.
