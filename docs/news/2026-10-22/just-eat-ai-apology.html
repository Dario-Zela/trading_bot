Just Eat apologises for AI food mistakes after online ridicule

In one sentence
Just Eat apologised after errors in its AI-generated food content were mocked online, a brand-reputation lesson more than a technology story.

Just Eat has apologised after AI-generated food content on its platform drew ridicule online. The company's mistakes were spotted and mocked in public, and the apology followed. The episode says less about what the technology can do than about how little room a consumer brand has when it publishes machine-made material without close checking.

The account rests on a single report, from BBC Technology. That report is thin on detail. It does not make clear which content was affected, how many errors there were, or what Just Eat has changed since. Those gaps limit what can responsibly be said here, and nothing below goes beyond what has been reported.

What happened

The sequence is simple. Just Eat put out AI-generated food content, and it contained errors. People noticed and shared examples on social media, where they were widely laughed at. The company then apologised.

The speed matters more than the specifics. A mistake in a brochure or a printed menu might sit unnoticed for weeks. A mistake on a platform used by millions can be screenshotted, shared and mocked within hours. The correction, in this case, came from the public rather than from internal review. The apology came after the ridicule, not before it.

A brand problem, not a breakthrough

It is tempting to file this under AI failure. That framing is too generous to the story and too harsh on the technology. Generative systems are known to produce confident errors, and any company using them for customer-facing material knows this. Food is an especially unforgiving subject: customers can see at a glance when a picture or a description doesn't match a dish they recognise.

The risk lies in the process around the tool. If the content went live without a human checking it, the failure was one of review, not of software. The reporting does not say whether that was the case, so that point remains open. What can be said is that the cost fell on the brand, in the form of public embarrassment and an apology it would have preferred not to issue.

What it signals

For companies deploying generative AI in customer-facing content, this is a small but clear data point. Errors will be found by customers, quickly and in public, and the response is expected to be fast. The saving from producing content cheaply can be wiped out by a single viral example.

It also raises the bar for what an apology needs to contain. A statement that says sorry but names no fix invites the next round of mockery. The reported coverage does not yet say what Just Eat will do differently, whether that means more human checking, narrower use of the tools, or removal of the content in question.

What to watch

The test is whether Just Eat explains what went wrong and what it has changed, and whether further errors surface in its content afterwards. Other delivery and retail platforms using AI-generated listings and images will be watching how this plays out. If the company publishes details, the BBC report is where they are most likely to appear first.
