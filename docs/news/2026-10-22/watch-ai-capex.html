AI capex: the trade returns as Fink ties US growth to data centres

In one sentence
BlackRock's Larry Fink says halting data-centre building could cause a US recession, while a Trump power pledge nudges operators to self-supply but remains largely unenforced.

The argument that artificial-intelligence spending is holding up the US economy now has a prominent champion. BlackRock chief executive Larry Fink said on 14 October, after the asset manager's third-quarter results, that halting data-centre construction could push the country into recession. Washington, meanwhile, has been steering the industry towards supplying its own electricity. Together the two points make the build-out look less like a sector story and more like a macro exposure.

Fink's warning

Speaking to CNBC, Fink tied the recession risk to the scale of capital expenditure now under way. "So much of our economy is based on the buildout of this capex," he said, according to coverage of the interview. He described opposition to data centres as "misinformation" and said "data centers are not the problem."

The remark is aimed at a political fight as much as a forecast. Brookings has counted at least 100 localities approving data-centre moratoriums as of July, and New York has paused certain permits for up to a year. Fink's framing, that a freeze is a growth risk and not merely a planning dispute, is the counter-argument. No figures for the share of GDP growth attributable to the build-out accompanied the remarks, so the claim is best read as a judgement by an interested party — BlackRock has large infrastructure and AI-related investments — rather than a measured estimate.

The power question

The power-supply thread is older than the triage summary suggests, and less binding. Trump announced a "Ratepayer Protection Pledge" in his February State of the Union and signed it on 4 March. Under it, major technology companies agreed to develop their own generation, may sell surplus power to utilities, and are to make backup capacity available in emergencies. Trump framed it as protection for households: companies can build their own power plants "as part of their factory," he said, so that no one's prices rise.

Two caveats matter. Early reporting said no companies were named and no enforcement mechanism described, and the White House later declined to say which firms had signed. Experts also doubt it is enforceable federally, since electricity supply is largely regulated by states and managed regionally. The Benzinga summary describing an order that data centres "must" generate their own power therefore overstates what is documented; this desk could not locate an order text. If self-supply does become the norm, the cost lands on operators and shifts demand towards new on-site generation rather than the existing grid.

The tape

Markets have not waited for clarity. The Nasdaq 100 rose on the session and cyclical sectors led defensives, which Benzinga attributed to the AI trade returning; no index level or sector figures were supplied. In Europe, ASML was the focus as chip names followed the AI theme, with SAP's third-quarter beat as the read-across. One session does not establish a rotation, and the evidence offered is sentiment rather than earnings.

What to watch

The link Fink draws runs in both directions: if capex is load-bearing, any hyperscaler guidance cut becomes a macro event. The next test is the run of big-tech earnings, including Meta's, where capital-expenditure guidance will show whether the spending path is intact. On power, the signal to look for is a named list of signatories and any state-level rules that give the pledge teeth.
