Orangutans prefer food linked to the sound of laughter, study suggests

In one sentence
A Biology Letters study finds orangutans prefer materials they searched while hearing laughter, hinting that play sounds lift mood and raise the value of rewards; the sample is small.

Play a recording of laughter to an orangutan while it roots through a bowl for food, and it will later favour the bowl's contents. That, in outline, is the finding of a new study of captive apes. It suggests that the sound of play does more than signal fun. It may tilt how an animal values the things around it.

The work, published on 21 October in Biology Letters under the title "Play vocalizations induce affective bias in reward learning and memory in orangutans", was led by Daan Laméris, a postdoctoral researcher at Indiana University, with Erica Cartmill, a professor at IU Bloomington. It was first reported by the Guardian.

How the test worked

The experiment ran over five consecutive days. Orangutans dug through materials in two bowls to find food. On the first and third days, food always lay under one material, wood chips, and never under another, paper towels. On the second and fourth days, the unrewarded material was paired with a new one, yarn, that did cover food.

The key manipulation was sound. While the animals searched in one material, recordings of orangutan laughter played. While they searched in the other, a control sound played. On day five the researchers offered a choice between the two previously rewarded materials. The orangutans preferred the one they had searched through while hearing laughter.

The researchers read this as an "affective bias". Laughter, they argue, puts the animals in a more positive mood. That mood, in turn, raises the value placed on a reward encountered at the time. The effect is familiar from human psychology, where a good mood during an experience can colour how it is remembered.

Why laughter, and why orangutans

Laughter-like play vocalisations are well documented in great apes, and a bonobo study reported in Scientific Reports found that animals hearing laughter were more willing to chase uncertain rewards. Orangutans are a less obvious subject. "It is surprising to find these effects with orangutans, because they are the least social of all the great apes," Laméris said.

Cartmill framed the wider point more broadly: "What we've shown in this study is that we can measure the value of laughter."

What is not yet clear

There are reasons for restraint. The university's press release does not state how many orangutans took part. Secondary coverage of the Guardian report describes six animals from two zoos, of which four showed the preference, but that detail could not be checked against the paper itself. Samples of that size are common in primate work, where subjects are scarce, but they limit how far a result can be generalised. The study also shows a preference after a pairing. It does not show what the animals felt, which is inferred from behaviour.

The sound of laughter is also not isolated from other things, such as novelty or arousal, unless the control sound is well matched. The paper's methods will show how closely it was.

What to watch

The obvious next steps are replication with larger groups and other ape species, and tests of whether the effect depends on laughter specifically or on any positive social sound. For now it stands as a small, tidy signal that the line between emotion, memory and valuation runs through the great apes too.
