US to tap diesel reserve as inventories draw and midterms near

In one sentence
Washington will lend up to 315,000 barrels from the heating-oil reserve just before the midterms, while EIA data show crude and gasoline stocks drawing by about 3m barrels each.

Washington is reaching into its emergency fuel stocks again, and the timing is hard to miss. The Department of Energy is offering to lend up to 315,000 barrels of ultra-low-sulfur distillate from the Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve. The offer lands 12 days before the 3 November midterms and a week after the US government's own weekly data showed commercial crude stocks at their lowest in four decades.

What is being released

The headline question is which reserve. The answer is the diesel-type one, and a small one. The Northeast Home Heating Oil Reserve holds 1m barrels across sites in New Jersey, Connecticut, Massachusetts and Maine. The offer covers roughly a third of that. Each site can lend no more than 150,000 barrels, with a 25,000-barrel minimum bid. Bids close on 27 October and deliveries must be completed by 13 November.

It is a loan, not a sale. Borrowers must return the same volume plus a premium before the 2027-28 heating season. Energy Secretary Chris Wright called it "a temporary measure to solve a temporary supply disruption."

The scale matters. At 315,000 barrels the release is a rounding error against US distillate stocks of 104.3m barrels. It is a signal more than a supply fix. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve is a separate matter. A 40m-barrel crude exchange from it was offered in late September, with bids due on 6 October. That reserve is also returning barrels later with interest, and it stood at 282.7m barrels in the latest week, its lowest since October 1982.

The political clock

The heating-oil move followed a letter from Senators Susan Collins and Angus King. Collins faces a difficult re-election race in Maine. Heating oil costs are up about 70% on a year earlier, and energy inflation is a live issue. AAA data put regular gasoline at $4.26 a gallon and diesel at $6.32, against $3.05 and $3.62 a year ago.

The repayment schedule is the awkward detail. Loans fall due from now through 2029, which means the same reserves will have less cushion after the election than before it. The releases buy a few weeks of relief for pump prices. They do so by borrowing against future supply.

The weekly data

The Energy Information Administration's report for the week ended 16 October did not argue against a tight market:

  • Commercial crude fell 3.06m barrels to 412.5m, the third straight weekly decline and the lowest since the early 1980s. Analysts polled by Reuters had expected a draw of about 820,000 barrels.
  • Gasoline stocks fell about 2.95m barrels to roughly 205.3m, around 5% under the five-year average. (Some outlets round the draw to 3m.)
  • Distillate stocks rose 1.68m barrels to 104.3m, still about 9% below the seasonal norm.

The distillate build is the one soft spot, and it is modest. A single week's rise leaves diesel well short of normal, which is why the administration is looking at it.

What to watch

The next EIA report, due on 28 October, will show whether the crude draw runs to a fourth week. The heating-oil bids close the day before. After that, the midterms on 3 November end the electoral rationale for the releases. The question then is whether the inventory deficit has closed or whether the reserves have been spent to hide it.
