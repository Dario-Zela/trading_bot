Offenders who skip sentencing to face solitary confinement or loss of visits

In one sentence
The UK government is bringing in sanctions, including solitary confinement and loss of visits, for offenders who refuse to attend their own sentencing hearings.

The UK government is bringing into force powers to punish defendants who refuse to attend their own sentencing hearings. Offenders who stay in their cells rather than face the court can now expect to be held in solitary confinement or to lose social visits. The reported sanctions treat non-attendance as a matter for the prison regime, not only for the court.

The detail of the start date should be read with care. The reporting available to this desk is mostly from the bill stage in 2025, and it does not confirm the date on which the sanctions begin to be applied in practice.

Why the law changed

The measure grew out of a run of cases in which convicted offenders declined to come up from the cells. The best known is Lucy Letby, who refused to attend even by video link while bereaved families read their victim personal statements at Manchester Crown Court. The judge passed a whole life order in her absence. Thomas Cashman and Koci Selamaj also stayed away from their sentencings.

Victims' families said the absence denied them the chance to address the offender and see the sentence delivered. Ministers took the view that a defendant should not be able to dodge that moment by simply declining to leave their cell.

What the Victims and Courts Act provides

The provisions are in the Victims and Courts Act 2026, which was introduced as a bill in 2025. The first section inserts a new chapter into the Sentencing Code. It lets the Crown Court order a convicted person held in custody to attend sentencing. Failing to comply "without reasonable excuse" can be treated as criminal contempt.

The Law Gazette reported that the court can also impose "prison sanctions" modelled on existing prison disciplinary measures. These include loss of privileges, deductions from earnings and solitary confinement. Other coverage mentioned removal of access to the gym. Prison staff may use "reasonable force" on adult offenders to get them to the dock or the video link.

When the bill was announced, coverage also said judges could add up to two years to a sentence for refusing to attend. Sources disagreed on the scope. Some said the extra jail time applied only where the maximum sentence is life imprisonment. Others said the measure could apply to any Crown Court case. The loss of social visits, as described in this week's reporting, was not mentioned in the bill-stage material I reviewed.

The objections

The Prison Reform Trust has called the measures disproportionate and unnecessary, arguing that existing law and policy already cover the problem. It warned of unintended consequences for prison safety, and it was particularly concerned about people who arrive in custody with sanctions already attached, especially in their first days. Practitioners writing in the Law Gazette have also argued that forced attendance is "no panacea".

There is a practical question underneath. Isolation and the loss of family contact are among the harshest tools a prison has. Using them to compel a court appearance moves them from managing behaviour inside the prison to enforcing a duty owed to the court.

What to watch

The first test will be how often judges use the sanctions and how long they last. Whether any are challenged on proportionality grounds will also matter. Ministry of Justice guidance to prisons on applying them is the next document to look for.
