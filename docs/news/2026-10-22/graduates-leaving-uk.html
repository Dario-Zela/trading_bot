The graduates leaving the UK for a fresh start abroad

In one sentence
More than one in ten UK final-year students now plan to work abroad, up from 7.8% two years ago, as graduate jobs draw about 140 applicants each.

Britain's newest graduates are doing the sums on a UK career and, in growing numbers, deciding the answer lies elsewhere. A survey of 15,000 final-year students found that more than one in ten now plan to move abroad after finishing their degrees, up from 7.8% two years earlier. The BBC reported the trend this week.

The survey is the sturdiest figure in the story, and it measures intent, not departures. Nobody can say from it how many will actually board the plane.

A crowded queue

The push is easy to see. The Institute of Student Employers puts the average at 140 applications for every graduate vacancy, up 14% on 2025 and against roughly 30 twenty years ago. Separate data from the job board Adzuna showed graduate vacancies down about 46% year on year in July, to 8,383 from 15,397. Adzuna is a single commercial platform, not an official count, so it shows direction better than scale. Official figures point the same way: the ONS has put youth unemployment well into double digits this year.

The ONS also gives the demographic backdrop. About two-thirds of British emigrants are aged 16 to 34, and in 2025 some 75,000 more people in that age group left the UK than arrived.

Three people, three routes

The BBC's examples are a useful reminder that "abroad" is not one place.

Rachel James, 26, from Surrey, left a corporate job in London in 2025 and went to Sydney with no job lined up. She now works in marketing for Fitness First. Her pay fell from £42,000 to £37,000, and she pays about £1,000 a month to share a two-bedroom flat, roughly what she paid in London. She said she "went for a fresh start".

Charlie West, 25, from Cheshire, took a different path: a funded six-year PhD at Yale after an Oxford master's. His stipend is $51,000 (£37,770), against about £21,000 for funded doctoral stipends in the UK.

Owein Iveson, 24, from Sunderland, is finishing a design master's at Aalto University in Finland and lives in Espoo, 15 minutes by metro from Helsinki. Finland's job market is soft, he said, but IT demand is high, and he has no plans to come back soon.

Australia is the most popular destination for British adults overall, helped by a working holiday visa open to UK passport holders aged 18 to 35 for up to three years. The US is second; the EU remains attractive, if harder to enter since Brexit.

Rational, not romantic

John Kiely, an immigration lawyer at Howard Kennedy, told the BBC that student debt, high housing costs, weak wage growth and job insecurity make leaving a rational choice for many graduates. The pay evidence in the profiles is mixed on that score: Ms James took a cut, while Mr West gained a pay premium.

There is a policy angle too. Graduates who leave take their training with them, a worry Whitehall has voiced before, including during the post-2008 slump. How many return, and when, is the number that would settle whether this is a gap year or a drain. No official series tracks it directly. The next ONS migration estimates and the next High Fliers student survey are the data points to watch.
