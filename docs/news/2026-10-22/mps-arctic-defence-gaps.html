MPs warn Royal Navy cannot counter Russia in the high north

In one sentence
A Commons Defence Committee report says shortfalls in Royal Navy submarines and frigates leave the UK unable to counter Russia in the high north, putting defence priorities under scrutiny.

A House of Commons committee has concluded that the Royal Navy does not have the ships and submarines to deter Russia in the high north, the waters stretching from the North Atlantic towards the Arctic. In a report published on 22 October, MPs on the Defence Committee said the gaps leave the UK exposed to Russian attack and risk failing to deter future aggression.

The finding lands in a long-running argument over whether a shrinking surface and submarine fleet can cover both the Atlantic approaches and commitments further afield. Parliament has raised the Arctic before. A 2018 defence sub-committee report, On Thin Ice, warned of rising Russian activity in the region and called for a comprehensive strategy. The new report says the capability to act on that concern is still short.

What the committee found

The central complaint is availability. The committee described a "shockingly low availability" of submarines and pointed to reports that all five of the navy's Astute-class attack submarines were in dock at one point this summer. It also noted a temporary loss of the ability to operate submarines under ice. According to press reports of the document, the Astute boats had not yet been certified for under-ice operations.

Surface ships fare little better in the MPs' account. The navy has five frigates available for hunting Russian submarines, against a target of eight. The committee cited declining frigate numbers and the slow deployment of HMS Dragon to Cyprus as evidence of a fleet stretched thin.

The chair, Tan Dhesi, said the UK "does not currently have all the capabilities to operate successfully in the High North".

Cables, pipelines and allies

The committee's concern is not only a shooting war. It warned that failure to defend the region could leave undersea cables and pipelines carrying data and energy to the UK exposed to sabotage in peacetime. Those routes run through exactly the waters where Russian submarine activity is closely watched.

On allies, the report suggested ministers consider whether the British-led Joint Expeditionary Force, a grouping of northern European states, should concentrate more on the high north. It framed this against uncertainty over how far the US will continue to underwrite NATO's northern flank.

The Ministry of Defence said it was "clear-eyed about the threat Russia posed" and was investing heavily to protect the UK's undersea cables.

Why it matters for spending

A cross-party committee finding that capacity is short adds to pressure on the defence budget, and on the shipbuilding and submarine programmes that would close the gaps. The report as covered so far points to numbers of boats and hulls and their availability rather than a costed funding request. It is therefore less a bill than an argument about priorities. The coverage reviewed here did not establish a figure for what closing the gaps would cost, and that figure would decide how much weight the report carries in budget talks.

The government is due to respond to the committee, and its reply will show whether it accepts the capability shortfalls or disputes them. That response, and any commitment on frigate numbers or Astute availability, is the next point of evidence.
