Russia and Ukraine back US-brokered trilateral talks as missiles hit Kyiv

Vladimir Putin greets US envoy Steve Witkoff before their talks in St Petersburg on 21 October 2026. — Gavriil Grigorov / Sputnik pool via AFP
In one sentence
Putin and Zelenskyy both back resuming US-brokered trilateral talks, possibly in Abu Dhabi around 10 November, but strikes on Kyiv and unchanged demands leave it unclear whether this is more than a pause.

Russia and Ukraine have each said they will return to three-way talks with the United States, and Abu Dhabi has been floated as the venue. Missiles were hitting Kyiv as they said it. The signals came within 24 hours of each other. Volodymyr Zelenskyy met American and European officials in Oslo on Tuesday. On Wednesday Vladimir Putin received Donald Trump's envoy, Steve Witkoff, in St Petersburg. Neither meeting produced a date or a delegation list. What they did produce is the first public agreement in months that a negotiating channel should be reopened.

That channel is not new. Some of the framing around this week's announcements implies a first-ever format, and that is wrong. Trilateral rounds took place in Abu Dhabi in January and February and in Geneva in February, and they produced little beyond prisoner exchanges. What is being proposed now is a resumption after a pause of roughly eight months, not a debut. The question is whether anything has changed since the last round stalled.

Two capitals, one message

In Oslo on Tuesday, Zelenskyy met Witkoff alongside the prime ministers of Norway and Finland and representatives of the EU and NATO. Witkoff called the talks productive and spoke of "some degree of momentum". Norway's prime minister, Jonas Gahr Støre, suggested a possible "new phase". Zelenskyy said Kyiv wants a trilateral meeting "no later than early November", at the level of negotiating teams, and that Russia supports that format. He listed the agenda as security guarantees, occupied territory, reconstruction and a possible ceasefire. He called a freeze along the current front line the "minimum" Moscow must accept, and has said only a meeting of leaders can break the deeper impasse.

The next day Witkoff, on his ninth in-person meeting with Putin according to Al Jazeera, spent about three hours with the Russian leader at the Constantine Palace. Jared Kushner joined by video link. The Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov said Russia is "in principle" ready to resume trilateral talks, with a date "sometime in the middle of November, around November 10". PBS reported Ushakov as saying the talks would turn on postwar security arrangements. Ushakov was lukewarm about what the Europeans and Kyiv had brought to Oslo. He said they showed "some new aspects" but that "there wasn't really much substance in them".

The visit came with sweeteners. Russia released Stephen Hubbard, a retired American teacher jailed on mercenary charges, which Witkoff called a goodwill gesture. He also thanked Putin for lifting a ban on Russian diesel exports. Putin said he would consider restoring US-Russia economic ties and offered to discuss the American-led conflict with Iran.

Missiles over Kyiv

The strikes did not wait. Overnight attacks on Kyiv were reported to have killed two to three people, with about a dozen wounded and around ten residential buildings damaged, according to Ukrainian officials. Reports differ on the toll. Al Jazeera cited two dead in a high-rise in the Sviatoshynskyi district, while PBS reported three dead and 12 wounded in the capital. Ukraine's prime minister, Serhii Koretskyi, said energy facilities in Kyiv and three other cities were hit. The Russian defence ministry said it struck a warehouse, a substation and a bulk carrier bound for Odesa. By its count it also shot down 373 Ukrainian drones overnight. Further missile strikes hit Kyiv on Wednesday evening, hours after Moscow's "ready in principle" line, and the city's mayor, Vitali Klitschko, reported injuries and damaged homes.

Neither side is holding fire. Ukraine hit an oil export terminal and depot in St Petersburg on Sunday, as part of a drone launch of more than 1,000 aircraft that was described as one of the largest aerial attacks of the war. A drone attack in Russia's Bryansk region killed at least one person, according to its governor.

This pattern is familiar. A strike on Kyiv's energy infrastructure landed on the second day of the January Abu Dhabi round, and Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, said then that Putin had ordered it "right while delegations are meeting". Trump said on 11 October that the two sides had agreed to an energy-infrastructure ceasefire. Attacks on energy sites have continued regardless.

What would make the channel real

The gaps are the same ones that ended the earlier rounds. Moscow has demanded that Ukraine withdraw from parts of the Donbas it claims but has not fully taken. Kyiv refuses any unilateral pullback and wants the line frozen. Russia also wants sanctions eased, American ones above all, and Washington has already temporarily allowed sales of Russian diesel. Relations between Washington and Kyiv have frayed as a result. Zelenskyy has accused the US of slowing Ukraine's access to the Starshield satellite internet service. He said American intelligence sharing, which had been cut off, has been restored and is "very, very important", and that Kyiv is again receiving early warnings of incoming missiles after a call with Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Trump, for his part, has pressed Kyiv to "make a deal". He told Zelenskyy the US had received "absolutely nothing back" and said failure would be "a very bad thing". That pressure matters for how the offer to talk is read. Agreement to a meeting costs Moscow little, and it can show the White House willingness while the front-line and territorial demands stay unchanged. It also matches Ushakov's cool assessment of the European and Ukrainian contribution. The evidence that the channel is more than a holding pattern would be concrete: a fixed date and venue, named delegations with authority to commit, and some restraint on energy strikes. None of the three exists yet.

The Gulf dimension is practical. Abu Dhabi hosted the first two rounds, and the US-led conflict with Iran has taken up diplomatic bandwidth in the region since the last session. A US official's own conditions point the same way, since the January round was meant to be followed by further trilateral work before any Putin-Zelenskyy meeting. For Western capitals the stakes are aid and sanctions. A functioning channel gives Washington a reason to ask for sanctions relief and to press Kyiv, while Europeans worry about being asked to underwrite a settlement they did not shape.

What to watch

The first marker is whether a date around 10 November is formally announced, with Abu Dhabi confirmed, and whether Witkoff's planned return to Oslo takes place first. Zelenskyy wants the meeting earlier than Moscow does. The second is whether either side's delegations carry any mandate beyond what the January and February rounds had, given that the Kremlin has described the Oslo contributions as thin. The third is the strikes. If energy infrastructure keeps burning in Kyiv and in Russian ports through the weeks before the meeting, the talks will have been announced in a war that has not changed its terms.
