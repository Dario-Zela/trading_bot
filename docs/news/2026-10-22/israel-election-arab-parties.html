Arab parties could hold the balance of power in Israel's election

In one sentence
With neither Netanyahu's bloc nor the opposition near 61 seats, Arab parties polling 10 to 13 could decide whether any Israeli government forms, despite both sides' public rejection.

Israel votes on Tuesday 27 October, the date set by law four years after the November 2022 election. The arithmetic is as old as the state's coalition politics: the Knesset has 120 seats, and a government needs 61. This time, neither of the two main blocs looks likely to get there with Jewish-led parties alone, and that puts a set of parties that both sides have long kept at arm's length in a position to decide the outcome.

A stalemate on paper

Polling averages cited in late October put Binyamin Netanyahu's bloc and the opposition each at about 51 seats, with a few seats' difference depending on the survey and the window. One outlier poll gave the Netanyahu camp 62. The opposition is led, in most polls, by Gadi Eisenkot, the former army chief, whose new Yashar party is running roughly level with Likud. Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid are running together on a joint list that polls well behind both.

The outgoing Knesset shows how close the margin is. Netanyahu's coalition held 60 seats when parliament dissolved, one short of a majority. The opposition held 59, plus one unaffiliated seat. Neither side has been able to find the extra seat.

That leaves roughly 18 seats outside the two blocs in the polling averages. Most belong to the Arab parties, which have generally been polling between about 10 and 13 seats in total, and the remainder to small lists hovering near the 3.25 per cent threshold. Votes for any party below that line are discarded, which can move the final arithmetic by several seats. A bloc's gain from a rival's wasted votes can matter as much as any swing in support.

What the Arab parties hold

In 2022 the Arab parties won 10 seats between them: five for Ra'am and five for the Hadash-Ta'al alliance. That was down from 15 for the Joint List in 2020, when the four parties then in that alliance ran together. The fall followed a split, and many Arab voters stayed home. Turnout in Arab communities is the main variable this year. When it dips, the Arab parties lose seats, and the arithmetic shifts toward whichever bloc the Arab vote was never going to help.

Hadash and Ta'al have re-formed the Joint List with Balad for this election, while Ra'am, the Islamist party led by Mansour Abbas, runs separately. Ra'am has generally polled between four and six seats, close to its 2022 result.

The campaign has also tested whether they would be allowed to run at all. In September the Central Elections Committee, a body made up of party representatives, voted 15-8 to bar the Joint List after a petition from Likud, and voted to exclude Ra'am as well. It also blocked Sami Abu Shehadeh, Balad's chair. The petition relied on Basic Law provisions that bar parties backing armed struggle against Israel or negating its existence as a Jewish and democratic state. The committee's chair, Noam Sohlberg, a deputy president of the Supreme Court, and Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara opposed excluding the parties. On 2 October the Supreme Court unanimously overturned the bans on the parties, finding that the evidence did not meet the legal threshold for exclusion. Abu Shehadeh withdrew after the court signalled that a majority of its judges would uphold his disqualification.

The 2021 precedent

The question of whether an Arab party can sit in government is not hypothetical. In 2021 Ra'am, with four seats, joined the coalition of Bennett and Lapid, the first time an Arab party had been part of an Israeli government. The coalition, which spanned the right, centre and left, lasted about a year before collapsing in 2022. Its brief life is cited by both sides: by supporters as proof that the arrangement can work, and by Netanyahu's camp as proof that it cannot hold.

The present positions leave little room. Most Jewish-led opposition leaders have ruled out joining forces with Arab parties, and reporting this month says both Netanyahu and Bennett have publicly rejected governments that depend on Arab party support. Four leading opposition parties have signed a declaration not to attack each other before the vote and to cooperate afterwards. It does not extend to the Arab parties.

There is a distinction in Israeli coalition arithmetic that campaign rhetoric tends to blur. A party can be in a government, or it can merely deny the other side a majority by holding seats that cannot be counted on. If the Arab parties win 10 or more seats and the Jewish-led opposition falls short of 61 without them, an opposition government would need either their formal entry, their outside support in confidence votes, or their abstention. Their seats also shrink the pool the right needs, so Netanyahu's route to 61 is narrower the larger the Arab vote.

What to watch

The first signal will be turnout, especially in Arab towns, and whether the small lists near the threshold clear it. Final results follow within days. The president then consults party leaders before choosing who is asked to try to form a government, and that stage is where the stated rejections of Arab support will meet the arithmetic. If no one reaches 61 and the Arab parties are held at arm's length by both sides, the likelier result is not a government but another round of coalition talks, and possibly another election.
