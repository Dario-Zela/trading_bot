Are the Tories back? Polling debate over a Conservative recovery

In one sentence
A podcast-fuelled debate asks whether the Conservatives are recovering as Badenoch presses Labour on energy, but with no poll figures cited the recovery remains unproven.

Whether the Conservatives are recovering is, for now, a question being argued over rather than a fact. The Guardian's Politics podcast has put it under the title "Are the Tories back?", and the debate comes as Kemi Badenoch presses the government on energy policy. The material available so far gives no poll figures, pollster names or dates. That limits how much weight the claim can bear.

What is being claimed

The argument is about direction rather than position. A party that has spent a long stretch trailing in the polls can look like it is recovering if a few surveys tick up in succession. It can also be the ordinary scatter of polling, where individual results move a point or two without any change in opinion. Telling the two apart usually needs several pollsters moving the same way over several weeks, and a margin larger than the usual statistical noise.

None of that has been established in the reporting this piece draws on. The podcast framing poses a question, and the question mark in the title carries real weight. Treating it as a finding would be getting ahead of the evidence.

Energy as the battleground

The attribution to Badenoch is more concrete: the Conservative leader is pressing the government on energy. It is a sensible choice of ground for an opposition. Household bills, North Sea licensing and standing charges are issues where voters feel the cost directly and where the government's choices can be set against the previous administration's record. The dispute over new North Sea approvals, covered elsewhere in this edition, shows how live the argument is.

An energy attack does not by itself show a recovery. Oppositions raise cost-of-living issues in good polling periods and bad ones. What would matter is whether the pressure registers in voting intention, and that is the part still unproven.

Why the numbers matter

The wider stakes are about the right-of-centre vote. Reform UK has been the main threat to the Conservatives, drawing support that once went to them, and Labour is under pressure from both flanks. A genuine Conservative recovery would change the arithmetic for each: it would suggest the Tories can win back some defectors from Reform, and it would complicate Labour's assumption that a divided right helps it in marginal seats.

The reverse also holds. If the apparent upturn fades, the story reverts to a Conservative party fighting Reform for second place, which is a very different race.

What to watch

The test is straightforward. Whether the next round of polls from the regular pollsters shows the Conservatives holding any gain, and whether Reform's share falls as theirs rises, will say more than any podcast discussion. The Budget, with inflation and borrowing at one-year highs, offers the next big moment when voters' attention turns to the government's handling of the economy, and the first chance to see whether the energy argument carries over into the numbers.
