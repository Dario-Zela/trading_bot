Meta hit with record $25bn privacy penalty ahead of Meta and Tesla earnings

In one sentence
A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $25bn for years of 'willful' misleading on user data, the same day the company posted strong revenue but negative cash flow.

A New Mexico judge has ordered Meta to pay $25bn for what the court called years of deliberate deception about how Facebook handled user data. It is the largest fine ever imposed on the company. The ruling came on Wednesday 21 October, and the BBC's account gives no sign that it came ahead of Meta's results. On the evidence available, it landed the same day as them. The earnings and the fine belong to one story, and the fine is the part that stays open.

Two points in the original framing need correcting. The penalty is not from a federal or European regulator but from a state court, in a case brought by New Mexico's attorney general. And Meta's third-quarter report was due after the US close on Wednesday, not days later.

The ruling

Judge Francis Mathew found that Meta's conduct was "willful" and ran for more than 11 years, from 2010 to 2021. The case traces back to the Cambridge Analytica affair. Its core claim was that Meta told the public user data was not being shared with third parties without permission, when it was. A jury at a two-week trial earlier this year found the company had violated state law more than 40 million times. The jurors also found it had intentionally misled the public about hate speech on its platforms and about how its rules against it were enforced.

The arithmetic behind the number is stark. Applied violation by violation, the penalties came to about $219bn. The judge cut that to $25bn. New Mexico had asked for up to $40bn; Meta argued that anything above $3.4bn would be unconstitutional. The sum is roughly one quarter's profit for the company.

"The lies extend beyond Facebook's data practices as well," the judge wrote. Attorney General Raúl Torrez said the result "makes clear that no corporation is too powerful to be held accountable."

Meta's head lawyer, CJ Mahoney, said the ruling made it "all but impossible to do business in the state of New Mexico". He called the suit a tax on speech and an attempt to impose new rules on online expression. Meta says it will appeal and expects to win.

For scale, the figure is five times the $5bn FTC penalty of 2019, which was a separate case. It is nearly twenty times the €1.2bn ($1.3bn) fine Ireland's regulator levied in 2023 over EU–US data transfers, previously the largest GDPR penalty. Two caveats apply. This is a first-instance state judgment, and large penalties of this kind are often cut on appeal. It also falls under state consumer-protection law, so it is not obviously a template for regulators elsewhere, though it shows how large the arithmetic can get when violations are counted per user.

It is also not Meta's only recent bill in the state. New Mexico fined the company almost $1bn earlier this year over misleading claims about child safety, and Meta agreed an $18bn settlement with California on child-safety claims.

The earnings backdrop

The fine arrived against a set of results that investors were already treating as a test of Meta's AI spending. Previews had pencilled in third-quarter revenue of about $63.2bn, up more than 23%. They expected earnings per share of around $6.54, a fall of almost 10% (Zacks consensus). The reported numbers were stronger on the top line: revenue rose 26% to $64.5bn, and profit nearly tripled to $9.8bn, according to the BBC.

The cash picture is the harder part. Meta reported negative cash flow of more than $2bn, reportedly its first since it listed 14 years ago. Free cash flow was only $784m in the second quarter. It expects 2026 spending of at least $135bn, mostly on AI, and possibly up to $145bn. Spending over the past four quarters has reached nearly $90bn.

Against that, a one-off $25bn charge is large but not existential: it is roughly a quarter of earnings, and it compounds a cash position that is already tight. Chief financial officer Susan Li also announced a stock split that would create a new class of non-voting shares. The BBC piece does not report how the shares reacted.

Tesla, and what comes next

Tesla reported the same evening, and the preview coverage treated the two as a pair. Both are bets on spending that has not yet produced matching cash. Consensus expected Tesla's earnings per share to fall 12% to $0.44, on revenue down 1% to $27.78bn. Third-quarter deliveries of 486,532 vehicles beat the roughly 462,000 expected, but were still down 2% on the year. Investors were watching automotive margins, energy storage (13.7 GWh deployed) and updates on robotaxi, Cybercab and Optimus. This article does not cover Tesla's reported results.

The next data points are procedural and financial. The first is whether Meta books any of the $25bn as a charge or provision before appealing, which would show in its filings. The second is the appeal itself: the size of the penalty, set at roughly 7.5 times the maximum Meta says is constitutional, is the obvious ground. The third is the 2027 capex guidance. Analysts flagged it as the key figure to watch, and it now has to be read alongside a legal liability that may take years to resolve.
