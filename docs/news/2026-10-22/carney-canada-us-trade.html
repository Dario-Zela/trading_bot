Carney: Canada ready for a fair US trade deal, but auto and steel fixes will take time

In one sentence
Carney says Canada is ready for a fair US trade deal and the door is open, but the structural problems in autos and steel will take time to fix.

Canada is ready to strike a fair trade deal with the United States, Prime Minister Mark Carney said on Wednesday. He also cautioned that the problems in the auto and steel sectors are structural and will take time to fix. The remarks, first carried by Benzinga, tell Washington the door is open. They also suggest Ottawa does not expect a quick end to the tariffs that weigh most heavily on those two industries.

The account of the remarks comes from a single wire item, and the primary transcript was not available at the time of writing. The words below are therefore summarised, not quoted. No dates, tariff rates or negotiating deadlines were attached to the comments.

A familiar position, with the emphasis shifted

Nothing here breaks new ground on Canada's stated terms. In early September, Bloomberg reported that Carney was open to a US pact only if the terms for autos and steel kept Canadian operations competitive. He has said Canada will not sign anything that would substantially weaken those industries. Earlier this year he indicated Canada could drop its remaining retaliatory tariffs on strategic sectors, but only if Washington cut its own substantially.

What is new is the stress on time. Saying the fixes are "structural" concedes that a signature on a document would not, by itself, restore the old arrangement. Integrated North American auto supply chains, in which parts cross the border several times before a vehicle is finished, and a steel market now shaped by quotas and tariffs, cannot easily be rebuilt. Carney has himself acknowledged that an integrated continental auto industry may no longer be a priority for Washington.

Where talks stand

The backdrop is an unresolved dispute. Reports indicate that talks that had appeared close to a deal broke down in August, after which the US imposed further tariffs on Canadian goods and threatened higher duties on cars, trucks and parts from the start of 2027. Details of those measures vary between reports, and readers should check them against official US and Canadian announcements.

Ottawa has meanwhile leaned on domestic support. Canadian media reported a multibillion-dollar package for manufacturers, aimed at Ontario's steel and auto producers, and Carney has set a goal of doubling exports to markets other than the US over the next decade. Both moves point to a government that wants a deal but is preparing for a long wait.

What to watch

The open question is the timeline, not Canada's willingness to talk. The next marker is the threatened January 2027 increase in US auto tariffs. If Washington signals a date for resuming formal negotiations before then, that would suggest the "time" Carney describes is measured in months. If it does not, the structural fixes he describes may be addressed only after the next round of tariffs has taken effect.
