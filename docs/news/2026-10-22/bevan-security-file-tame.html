Aneurin Bevan's security file 'remarkably tame', National Archives shows

In one sentence
A National Archives release of Aneurin Bevan's security file finds it unremarkable, a small curiosity that adds little to the story of the NHS founder or of state surveillance.

The state kept a file on Aneurin Bevan, and the file, it turns out, is not much to read. A release from the National Archives has put the NHS founder's security file into public view. The Guardian describes it as "remarkably tame".

That verdict will disappoint anyone hoping for a Cold War thriller. Bevan was the most combustible figure in the postwar Labour government. As Minister of Health from 1945 to 1951 he built the National Health Service, which opened in July 1948. He did it over the open hostility of much of the medical profession. He was also a standing provocation to the right, and he was plainly the kind of politician about whom a security service might be expected to keep notes. The surprise is how little the notes appear to contain.

What the file does and does not say

The details of the release could not be independently confirmed for this article, so this account relies on the Guardian's characterisation. On that account, there are no revelations about surveillance of Bevan and no new light on how the state regarded him. The file does not change the picture of a politician who was watched, as many on the left were, but not obviously pursued.

Bevan's record was the sort that invited attention. He was a Welsh miner's son who rose through the South Wales coalfield and the Labour left. He was an outspoken critic of Conservative governments, and in the 1950s he led the left of his own party. Public controversy was never in short supply. Wartime and postwar records at Kew already hold much on his politics, including a 1942 War Cabinet memorandum on a newspaper article of his. A thin security file sits easily alongside all that: a record of a loud public figure whose views were hardly secret.

A curiosity, not a correction

Archive releases of this kind make good copy when they overturn something. This one does the opposite. A dull file is a small piece of evidence that the machinery of watching was less interested in Bevan than his reputation might suggest. It is not strong evidence either way. A tame file is silent on what was kept elsewhere, what was destroyed, and what was never written down.

Bevan died in 1960, aged 62. The NHS he founded is the thing he is remembered for, and a modest folder at Kew adds nothing to that.

The next thing to watch is the catalogue entry itself. The National Archives' Discovery catalogue will show exactly what the file holds and which departments created it. Historians of the period can then test the Guardian's description against the pages.
