Jackdaw approval, a Norway-style fund that never was, and gas standing charges: the North Sea fight

In one sentence
The government has approved the Jackdaw gas field but kept the ban on new licences, drawing 'climate vandalism' charges from the left and demands for the ban's reversal from the Tories.

The government has approved the Jackdaw gas field in the North Sea while leaving in place its ban on new oil and gas exploration licences. That combination is now the centre of a dispute that runs from the Green benches to the Conservative front bench. Critics call the approval "climate vandalism". Kemi Badenoch wants the licence ban overturned altogether.

The decision came on 21 October, according to Novara Media. Andy Burnham's government cleared the Shell and Equinor project. Burnham has defended the decision, arguing that it protects jobs and helps keep homes warm this winter, as Oceanographic reports. Critics dispute that it will lower bills.

An approval, not a new policy

The distinction between approving a field and licensing new exploration is the whole of the government's position. Jackdaw is an existing, already-licensed development. It was first consented under the previous Conservative government. A Scottish court later found the consents unlawful because the emissions from burning the fuel had not been assessed. Approving it again is therefore a case-by-case decision on a project with a history, not a change to the licensing regime. Labour's 2024 manifesto pledge of no new oil and gas licences remains in force on the government's account.

The pledge has been under strain for weeks. Before the decision, Burnham had complained that approval of Jackdaw and Rosebank was delayed by "government process", as City AM reported. ITV News reported the Conservatives urging him to stop "dithering". Labour is reportedly split on the matter.

The reaction from the left was swift. Scottish Greens co-leader Ross Greer called it a "devastating betrayal of our children's future", per STV News. Greenpeace and Green Party of England and Wales leader Zack Polanski were also critical, according to the Novara and Oceanographic reports. Novara noted that Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh had opposed new drilling before taking the post.

The Tory counter-offer

The Conservatives do not regard the approval as sufficient. Badenoch is pressing the government to overturn the ban on new licences, and her party also wants the Energy Profits Levy scrapped. Shadow energy secretary Andrew Bowie has accused Burnham of "dithering and delaying".

That leaves the government with critics on both sides. For the Greens and campaigners, approving Jackdaw is already too much. For the Tories, approving single fields without a licensing round is too little. Whether the arrangement is stable is the question for the sector. UK-listed producers would find it easier to plan around a regime that approves tie-backs and existing developments than around one that revisits each project. The approvals do not open up new acreage, though, and the long-run reserves outlook still depends on the licensing ban.

The fund that was never built

The argument has a counterfactual attached. A BBC piece asks what a Norway-style North Sea fund might be worth had Britain built one, and it puts the answer in the region of a trillion pounds. The BBC's methodology could not be checked for this article, and the figure should be treated as an illustrative counterfactual rather than a measured loss.

Other estimates are lower. The Institute for Public Policy Research has said that a fund built from North Sea tax receipts in the 1980s would now be worth over £500bn. It put those receipts at roughly £166bn between 1981 and 1990. The IPPR argued that the money went on cutting other taxes. Norway's own fund is now generally reported at over $2tn. The size of the gap depends heavily on the assumed revenue base and the period. The IPPR also concluded that the opportunity to capitalise a UK fund from North Sea revenue has passed.

The counterfactual feeds the licensing debate in both directions. Supporters of drilling say that remaining production should at least be taxed and used well. Opponents say the history shows that the revenue case for new fields is weaker than advertised.

Bills: the standing-charge thread

Burnham is also pushing a bills argument. The BBC reports that he has proposed a plan to cut standing charges from gas bills. Earlier reporting in July said he was considering a plan drawn up by the Nesta think tank, which suggested savings of about £130 a year for the average household. The proposal would overhaul gas standing charges, which add about 29p a day regardless of usage, and shift some levies off energy bills.

The costings in the coverage conflict. One account says the standing-charge reform alone costs taxpayers nothing, while the wider package is put at £3.2bn a year. Households using more gas would pay more under the reform, and those using less would pay less. No government response, timetable or funding mechanism for the latest proposal could be confirmed. Until one appears, it remains positioning rather than policy, and the effect on suppliers such as Centrica cannot be assessed.

What to watch

The next test is whether Rosebank follows Jackdaw. It faces the same legal history and the same Tory pressure, and a decision there would show whether case-by-case approval has become the settled approach. A legal challenge to Jackdaw would be a second marker. The earlier approvals were struck down on exactly that ground. The third is any formal Conservative commitment to reverse the licence ban before the Budget.
