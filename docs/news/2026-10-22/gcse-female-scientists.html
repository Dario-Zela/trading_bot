Only one female scientist named in GCSE specifications, study finds

In one sentence
A Cambridge study of six exam boards' specifications found one woman, Rosalind Franklin, among 86 scientists named at GCSE level, and proposes small, specific additions.

Open a GCSE science specification and look for the names of the people who made the discoveries. In a new study of the documents that set what the exams can ask, one woman turns up at GCSE level. She is Rosalind Franklin, and she appears in just one specification.

Researchers at the University of Cambridge went through the specifications of the UK's six main exam boards. These are the official documents that tell teachers what students must learn and what papers may assess. They covered biology, chemistry, environmental science, geology, physics and astronomy. The study was published on 21 October in the British Educational Research Journal, under the title "The absence of women's scientific contributions in core UK pre-university science qualifications". The authors are Dr Greg J Cooke, Skyla B White and Stephanie Buttigieg, who worked with the IncludeHer movement.

The numbers

At Key Stage 4, which covers GCSEs and equivalent qualifications, the specifications name 86 scientists. One is a woman: Franklin, whose X-ray work was central to working out the structure of DNA.

At Key Stage 5, covering A-levels and equivalents, the count is 181 scientists, of whom three are women. Franklin is joined by the geophysicist Marie Tharp and the seismologist Inge Lehmann.

The study looked at specifications, not textbooks or what teachers do in lessons. Teachers can and do add names that the documents leave out. But specifications are what exam boards and publishers build on, so a gap there tends to carry downstream.

What the authors say it means

The researchers point to what they call a "compelling" correlation between the gender of the scientists students meet at KS4 and the subjects girls choose at KS5. Girls generally outperform boys in STEM at KS4. At KS5, though, boys are far more likely to take physics, maths and further maths. The share of girls taking further maths is lower than it was in 2005.

The authors are careful about this. Women's underrepresentation in science has many causes, and the study does not show that missing names drive subject choices. The link is a correlation. Physics is the clearest case: the KS4 material is dominated by male scientists, which the authors suggest may play a part in fewer girls continuing with the subject.

A short fix list

The paper does not call for a rewrite of the curriculum. It asks for small, targeted changes to existing frameworks:

  • Include Franklin in all biology specifications that cover DNA structure.
  • Include Marie Skłodowska-Curie in KS4 chemistry, in the teaching of atomic structure and isotopes.
  • Include Lise Meitner in KS4 physics.
  • Include Dorothy Hodgkin in KS5 biology, in the study of protein structure.

White makes the same point about astronomy. Annie Jump Cannon is not mentioned where the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram is taught, although her classification of stars underpins it. "You can't tell one story without the other," she said.

"It is long overdue that we teach these inspiring stories about women," Cooke said. Buttigieg said: "When school science specifications leave out women's names, they miss a real opportunity to help girls see a future for themselves in science."

What to watch

The fixes are small and the target is easy to check, because a specification either names a scientist or it does not. The coverage seen for this article carried no response from the exam boards. Whether any board adds names at its next specification revision will be the test of whether the findings change anything.
