Trump's green card curbs test Indian tech workers

In one sentence
A US freeze on green card applications at eight tech firms, including Infosys, TCS and Microsoft, leaves Indian engineers stuck and could, if prolonged, squeeze the US firms that rely on them.

The United States has frozen one of the main routes by which skilled foreign workers become permanent residents, and the firms hit hardest are the Indian-founded IT companies that have supplied American technology with engineers for decades. The measure does not cancel anyone's visa. But it narrows the road to a green card, and that road is much of what makes a US posting attractive.

What the freeze does

On 8 October, Vice President JD Vance and Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling announced that eight technology firms would be suspended from hiring skilled international workers on a permanent basis. The list includes Microsoft and the Indian-founded Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro and HCL Technologies. Business Standard also names Cognizant, so the published lists differ, and the full roster should be checked against the official notice.

The mechanism is the Permanent Labor Certification programme, known as PERM. It is the first step in most employer-sponsored green card cases: the employer certifies that no qualified American worker is available. New and pending PERM applications from the named firms are frozen. Immigration lawyers stress that existing H-1B visas and approved green cards are untouched. The block falls on new applications.

The administration's stated reason is that employers misused employment-based immigration programmes to replace American workers with cheaper foreign labour. The reasoning is contested by the companies and by Nasscom, India's technology trade body. It has argued that Indian firms have cut their reliance on H-1B visas.

Who is exposed

The workers' position is the plainer part. An H-1B visa runs for a limited term, and the green card is the route out of that uncertainty. For Indian applicants, the permanent-residency queue is already long because of per-country caps. A freeze at the first stage adds delay to a wait measured in years, and it does so for people who cannot easily switch employers without losing their place.

There are signs that the pipeline was thinning before this. Xpheno data reported by The Economic Times show 12,700 Indian tech specialists had returned from the US by September 2026, up from 9,800 in 2024 and 7,200 in 2023. The data do not say why they left, and the freeze is too recent to account for most of that rise. It does suggest the supply was already softening.

The supply-risk argument

Analysts see little immediate financial damage to the companies. HDFC Securities says the impact would be minimal because PERM-linked roles account for a small share of revenue, and Nasscom says the number of staff moving to permanent residency through PERM is relatively limited. That is reassuring for quarterly earnings.

It is less reassuring for the longer game. Permanent residency is a retention tool as much as a benefit: engineers who expect a green card tend to stay, and those who do not can leave, or never come. If the freeze persists or widens to more employers, US firms that depend on this talent would face higher attrition and a harder time recruiting. Some work would move to India or elsewhere. That is the risk to employers, separate from the hardship to individuals.

What to watch

Two things will show whether this is a narrow administrative action or something larger. The first is whether the list of suspended firms grows beyond the eight. The second is whether the freeze is challenged in court. The results reviewed turned up no legal ruling yet. There is precedent for reversal: a 2020 Trump order blocking many green card applicants was revoked by President Biden in 2021. This time, the test is how long the suspension lasts, and whether the companies' own hiring plans change before it ends.

This article draws on reporting by the BBC, Business Standard, ThePrint and The Economic Times. The scope and company list rest on secondary accounts and have not been checked against the primary notice.
