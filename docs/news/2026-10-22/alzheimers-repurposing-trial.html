Diabetes and ADHD drugs to be tested on Alzheimer's patients in UK study

In one sentence
A UK platform trial, AD-SMART, will test metformin and atomoxetine in Alzheimer's patients; it has just begun and has no results yet.

A new UK trial will test whether two medicines already in routine use, one for type 2 diabetes and one for ADHD, can slow the progression of Alzheimer's disease. The study, called AD-SMART, has begun treating its first participant. It has no results yet, so it is an announcement of a study and not evidence that either drug works against dementia.

The drugs and the design

The first two compounds under test are metformin, a first-line treatment for type 2 diabetes, and atomoxetine, which is prescribed for ADHD. Reports of the launch say both may dampen inflammation in the brain, a process implicated in neurodegeneration. That is a hypothesis, and the trial exists to test it.

AD-SMART is described as a multi-arm study: several candidate drugs run side by side against a single shared placebo group. If one drug shows no benefit it can be dropped and replaced by another without starting a new trial from scratch. Organisers say this structure could roughly halve the time taken to evaluate treatments, with conclusions targeted for 2030. That timeline is the organisers' aim, not a forecast.

Coverage reports a target of 1,200 participants at more than 30 sites across the UK, with regular blood tests and scans to track progress. Angela Bucksey, 67, from Cambridgeshire, diagnosed in January, is reported as the first person to start treatment. The UK Dementia Research Institute and Alzheimer's Research UK are reported to lead the work, with government backing among other funding. Details such as the trial phase, dosing and the primary outcome measure were not available in the coverage reviewed, and the original BBC report could not be read directly; these figures come from secondary summaries and should be treated accordingly.

Why repurpose?

Drugs already approved for another condition have known safety profiles and manufacturing routes, which can remove years and a large share of the cost from development. Alzheimer's Research UK has estimated that roughly a third of drugs in clinical trials for Alzheimer's are already used for other conditions, so the approach is well established.

The diabetes link in particular has a long, mixed history. Observational studies have associated some diabetes drugs with lower dementia risk; one analysis of about 110,000 people with type 2 diabetes linked SGLT-2 and DPP-4 inhibitors to a 35 per cent lower risk. Such designs cannot establish causation, because people prescribed one drug differ from those prescribed another in ways that are hard to measure. Experts reacting to those findings repeatedly called for randomised trials, which is what a placebo-controlled platform provides.

There is also a caution specific to this study. Patients with Alzheimer's are typically older and often take several other medicines, so side effects and interactions will matter as much as any cognitive signal. Whether metformin, which is taken by people with diabetes, behaves the same way in participants without it is an open question the trial design will have to address.

What to watch

The first checkpoint is enrolment: whether the trial reaches its 1,200-person target and publishes its protocol and primary endpoints. Interim decisions to drop or add arms would be the earliest sign of how the platform is working. Any efficacy answer is not expected before the end of the decade.
