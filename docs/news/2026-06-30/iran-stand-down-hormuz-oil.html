US and Iran stand down: Hormuz risk premium evaporates as oil retreats to pre-conflict levels

Satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz and the Musandam Peninsula; roughly one-fifth of all global seaborne oil transits this 33-kilometre-wide chokepoint between Iran and Oman. — MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
After Iran's drone hit the Ever Lovely and CENTCOM struck back, a US-Iran stand-down has erased four months of crude risk premium, pulling WTI back below $70 for the first time since the war began.

The surprise, looking back at last weekend's flare-up in the Strait of Hormuz, is not that it happened — it is how quickly it stopped. An Iranian drone struck the Ever Lovely, a Singapore-flagged cargo vessel, on 26 June, adding a new chapter to a conflict that had seemed, tentatively, to be winding down. US Central Command responded with strikes on Iranian missile and drone storage facilities and coastal radar sites. Then, after a weekend of mutual bombardment that tested the limits of the memorandum of understanding signed on 17 June, both Washington and Tehran agreed to stand down.

Oil markets, which had spent four months pricing in the worst-case scenario, drew a quiet exhale. WTI crude settled below $70 a barrel on Friday — the first time it had closed there since 27 February, the day before the war began. Brent, the international benchmark, which had spiked to $126 a barrel in late April, fell back below $72.

The speed of the de-escalation matters. In a conflict that began on 28 February and produced what the International Energy Agency described as "the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market," the machinery of diplomatic containment appears to have held — just.

From drone to diplomacy

The Ever Lovely incident struck at the most sensitive point of the existing framework. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps fired what President Donald Trump described as "at least four one-way attack drones" at vessels transiting near the Omani coast, with one hitting the upper deck of the cargo ship. The vessel continued under its own power, but the attack was read as a deliberate challenge to the ceasefire terms that had allowed strait traffic to resume.

CENTCOM's response was calibrated rather than escalatory: military infrastructure struck, not population centres. Iran retaliated with drone attacks on Bahrain, and the standoff continued through the weekend before both sides agreed to pull back. Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Special Envoy Steve Witkoff briefed Congressional leaders in a 4pm conference call on Monday, with Witkoff also expected in Doha for talks with Qatari and Pakistani mediators on Tuesday.

The central ambiguity the Doha meeting is designed to resolve is whether the 17 June memorandum of understanding constitutes a binding ceasefire or an informal pause — and what its terms actually require of both parties inside the strait. Iranian Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei went so far as to deny that any direct US-Iran meeting would take place, even as Witkoff's aircraft was en route to Qatar. That contradiction is itself a measure of how much interpretive distance still separates the two governments.

What the market heard

Crude's retreat tells a story about the composition of the price spike as much as the de-escalation itself. Persian Gulf producers — the UAE, Kuwait, Qatar — began boosting exports as soon as the strait reopened, restoring throughput to roughly 75% of pre-war levels within weeks of the original 17 June framework. If the physical supply disruption had been total and sustained, prices would not have retreated this fast or this cleanly.

The market is confirming what sharper analysts argued in April, at the wartime peak: the bulk of the $50-plus premium above pre-conflict levels was risk sentiment, not actual barrel losses. Volumes through the strait had surged after the MOU, and Middle Eastern producers — though constrained by tanker shortages — moved quickly to capture market share.

This matters for how energy markets behave from here. The risk premium is not fully extinguished — it rarely is when two governments with unresolved nuclear talks and disputed waterway interpretations are still trading accusations across an active conflict theatre. But the tail risk of a full Hormuz closure, which would choke off roughly 20% of global seaborne oil, looks substantially reduced. Algorithmic strategies built on geopolitical-volatility signals will be unwinding risk-off positioning across energy and safe-haven assets.

Libya's underpriced option

As the Hormuz premium evaporates, a different variable is quietly coming into view. Libya — Africa's largest oil reserve holder, with approximately 48 billion barrels of proven reserves and extraction costs below $10 a barrel in its major fields — recorded a 13-year production high on 21 June: 1.43 million barrels per day, according to the National Oil Corporation.

That figure would have seemed improbable a year ago. Libya's output has swung from 200,000 to well above a million barrels per day within single quarters, dragged up and down by the political fracture between Khalifa Haftar's eastern Libyan National Army and the Tripoli-based Government of National Unity. An April budget agreement brokered with US involvement temporarily brought both governments to the same table, but structural reunification remains distant; the LNA retains military autonomy over eastern oilfields and the GNU controls the Central Bank.

The market relevance is precise. As Iran's export capacity slowly returns under a potential sanctions reprieve, Libya's production sits entirely outside OPEC+ coordination and most analyst supply models. A breakdown in Tripoli or Benghazi — of the kind the country has experienced repeatedly since 2014 — could shave 500,000 to 800,000 barrels per day from global supply within weeks, at exactly the moment markets are repricing geopolitical risk downward. It is not a prediction; it is a tail risk the current spot price is not adequately compensating for.

What holds the framework together

The durability of the stand-down hinges on whether the Doha talks can produce something more legible than the June 17 MOU's current ambiguities. Congressional scrutiny — from members who want to know whether what Rubio and Witkoff sold them is architecture or scaffolding — will intensify if Tuesday's meeting produces no concrete text.

The next pressure point is not months away. A second incident in the strait, even a minor one, would carry an amplified market effect: the market has just re-rated risk down, and any reversal would move crude sharply from a vulnerable base. The position to watch is not the spot price but the options market — specifically, what traders are paying to insure against a return to April's levels. If that premium stays compressed, the stand-down is holding. If it begins to rebuild before the Doha talks conclude, the risk premium may be returning faster than the diplomacy.
