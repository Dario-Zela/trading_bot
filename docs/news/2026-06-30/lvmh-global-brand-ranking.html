LVMH leads the global brand market-cap table — a reminder that luxury pricing power is holding

The Louis Vuitton flagship at 101 Avenue des Champs-Élysées, Paris — one of the world's largest luxury retail floors and a focal point of LVMH's brand estate — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
LVMH's $280.7bn market cap tops 1,372 apparel and luxury peers as price discipline and wealth-gated demand insulate the sector's elite from broader retail weakness.

LVMH Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton has taken the top position in a Sourcing Journal analysis of S&P Capital IQ data covering 1,372 textile, apparel and luxury companies worldwide. With a market capitalisation of $280.7 billion, the French conglomerate — whose portfolio spans Louis Vuitton, Dior, Bulgari, Tiffany and some 75 other houses — sits well clear of Hermès International at $194.4 billion and Richemont at $136 billion. The top 25 names together account for $1.14 trillion in market value, around 77 percent of the entire sector.

The ranking arrives at a moment of genuine complexity for luxury. LVMH's first-quarter 2026 revenue came in at €19.1 billion, down six percent in reported terms as the strong euro weighed on dollar and yen-denominated sales — but up one percent organically, suggesting underlying demand held. Quietly, Loro Piana, the understated cashmere house that sits at the very apex of the group's pricing pyramid, posted double-digit growth. Watches and jewellery, with Tiffany leading, grew seven percent organically.

Two markets in one

What the headline numbers do not fully capture is the structural split now running through global consumer spending. Bain & Company estimates that roughly 80 percent of luxury sector growth between 2023 and 2025 was driven by price increases rather than volume gains. In the United States, wealthy buyers — those whose wealth sits in equities and real estate — have raised their share of total luxury expenditure from 30 percent in 2019 to 47 percent today. Meanwhile the aspirational middle cohort that powered the post-pandemic surge has quietly withdrawn: approximately 30 percent of that segment has reduced or paused luxury purchases, squeezed by cumulative price rises of 54 percent since 2019 and persistently high housing costs.

This bifurcation flatters the market-cap table. The brands able to hold price — that sell, in the words of one market analysis, products people see as investments rather than temporary purchases — attract sustained investor interest and resist the discounting cycles that erode value at the mass-market tier. Brands that over-extended into aspirational price points during the boom years are finding that floor considerably more slippery.

Bain's senior partner Claudia D'Arpizio puts it this way: "Consumers are stepping forward into a new relationship with luxury — one defined by meaning, not just by product." Whether that abstraction translates into durable pricing power, or simply rationalises a difficult environment, is the question the results need to answer.

The next concrete data point is LVMH's first-half release, expected in July. A second consecutive quarter of organic growth — even thin — would reinforce the case that the top tier of luxury has genuinely decoupled from the broader consumer cycle. A miss would reopen debate about whether that market-cap premium is warranted, or merely a lag.
