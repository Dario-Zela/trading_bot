Lloyds up 48% in twelve months — analysts disagree on whether the NIM recovery is fully priced

Lloyds Banking Group headquarters at 25 Gresham Street, City of London — Carcharoth / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
LLOY closed at £1.09 after a 48% twelve-month rally, with analysts split on whether the NIM recovery is fully priced in or still offers upside.

LLOY shares closed at £1.09 on Monday, capping a twelve-month gain of 48.3% that has made the UK's largest retail bank one of the FTSE 100's more discussed recovery plays. The question confronting traders — and the algorithmic models recalibrating positions in UK financial stocks — is whether the rally has done the fundamental work or simply borrowed against it.

The engine is well-understood. Net interest margins, compressed during the ultra-low-rate era, have been recovering as the interest-rate environment shifted. In the first quarter of 2026, Lloyds reported a banking NIM of 3.17%, up 14 basis points year-on-year and a touch ahead of analyst expectations of 3.15%. Statutory pre-tax profit rose to £2.0 billion from £1.5 billion a year earlier. Management lifted full-year net interest income guidance to above £14.9 billion — from £13.6 billion in 2025 — and reiterated a return on tangible equity target above 16%.

Much of the NIM upside traces to the bank's structural hedge: a portfolio of fixed-rate assets that locks in rates as deposits roll. Lloyds now expects that hedge to generate more than £7 billion in income this year and to clear £8 billion in 2027, with the notional value of the portfolio reaching £246 billion.

The market disagrees on what that's worth

At 13.76 times earnings, Lloyds trades at a premium to both the sector average of 11.52x and its closest peers at 11.89x — a reading Simply Wall St's composite scoring system flags as a potential overvaluation signal, awarding the stock just two out of six on its valuation checks. Morningstar has similarly characterised the shares as moderately overvalued.

Bears anchor to a scenario-based fair value of £0.79, implying the stock sits at a near-30% premium to that floor, and argue the structural hedge income tailwind is already visible in the price. Bulls, by contrast, run excess-returns models and arrive at implied intrinsic values closer to £2.01 per share — which at £1.09 suggests a 45.6% discount. The consensus across 18 analysts tracked by TipRanks remains a Buy, with 13 in that camp against one Sell and four Holds; the average 12-month price target sits around 115p, approximately 5.5% above Monday's close.

The two camps are not simply arguing about margin levels — they are arguing about duration. If the Bank of England eases faster than expected, and markets have priced in two further 25-basis-point cuts this year, the structural hedge advantage moderates and competition for deposits intensifies, compressing spreads from both ends simultaneously.

Layered on top is the FCA motor-finance redress scheme. Lloyds has provisioned a further £800 million for it, and the regulator now indicates that payouts are unlikely before 2027 — a known unknown that sits on the balance sheet without a clean resolution date.

The next hard data-point arrives with Lloyds' first-half results, due in late July. If the banking NIM holds above 3.1% despite BoE easing, the bull case gains traction. A softer reading would lend weight to the argument that the 48% gain arrived ahead of its justification.
