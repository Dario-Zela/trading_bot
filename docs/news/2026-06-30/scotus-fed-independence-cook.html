SCOTUS shields the Fed — then expands presidential removal power over other agencies in the same session

The US Supreme Court building in Washington DC, where Chief Justice Roberts authored majority opinions in both landmark cases on 29 June 2026 — Duncan Lock / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
SCOTUS shielded the Fed 5-4 while overturning 91 years of independent-agency protection 6-3, carving the central bank out while putting the SEC, CFTC, and FTC under presidential control.

On the morning of June 29, Chief Justice John Roberts published the majority opinion in two cases that reached opposite verdicts about the same underlying question. One preserved the Federal Reserve's century-long independence. The other dismantled ninety-one years of protection for nearly every other independent regulatory body in Washington.

The mechanism Roberts used was identical in both cases: history. The difference was which history applied.

In Trump v. Cook, a 5-4 majority held that Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook — the first Black woman to serve on the Fed's Board of Governors — could not be removed without the due-process protections Congress attached to her appointment. Trump had purported to fire Cook in August 2025, alleging she had falsely designated a second home as a primary residence on a mortgage application filed years before she joined the Fed. Cook called it "a manufactured pretext" for a removal rooted in policy disagreement. Roberts' majority agreed, at least procedurally: the President failed to give Cook "the procedural protections to which she was entitled by statute," rendering the termination invalid as executed.

The vote broke along unusual lines. Kavanaugh joined Roberts and the three liberal justices to form the majority. Barrett, Alito, Gorsuch, and Thomas dissented, arguing that alleged mortgage fraud was statutory cause enough and that the majority was protecting Cook on procedural technicalities rather than substance.

Cook issued a statement following the ruling: "This was never about mortgage documents signed years before I became a Federal Reserve governor. Today's ruling affirms a principle that has underpinned sound economic stewardship for generations: that the Federal Reserve must make all its policy decisions guided by evidence and independent judgment, free from political interference."

The carve-out Roberts built

What saved Cook — and, by extension, the Fed's broader independence — was not the strength of her individual case but the institution she represents. Roberts wrote that the Federal Reserve "follows in the distinct historical tradition of the First and Second Banks of the United States," entities whose political insulation was deliberately encoded at their founding. That lineage, the majority held, means the presumption of at-will presidential removal simply does not apply. Independence — and, critically, "the appearance of independence" — are not incidental features of the Fed's design; they are structural requirements.

The ruling stops short of a sweeping constitutional guarantee. Roberts explicitly noted that Trump retains the option to pursue Cook's removal again, provided he follows the statutory procedure: notice of charges, an opportunity for her to respond, and a defined deadline. The President had not yet done so as of June 29. But the majority's framing matters enormously for what comes next. The Fed's carve-out rests on a documented historical tradition that the Court has now converted into binding precedent — not merely convention that an administration can choose to override.

Ninety-one years of precedent, overturned

Four hours after the Cook decision, the same Chief Justice handed down the majority opinion in Trump v. Slaughter — and reached the structurally opposite conclusion for every other independent regulatory agency.

In a 6-3 ruling, Roberts overturned Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935), the nine-decade-old case that established Congress's right to insulate multi-member commissions from at-will presidential removal. The immediate facts involved the Federal Trade Commission: Trump had fired commissioners Rebecca Kelly Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya in March 2025 without citing statutory cause, telling them their service was "inconsistent with [his] Administration's priorities." Slaughter sued; Bedoya later resigned. The Court found the FTC's for-cause removal protection unconstitutional.

Roberts wrote that "[t]he executive Power" and the president's duty to "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed" require the ability to remove subordinates who exercise executive functions — and the FTC, which enforces 80 federal statutes and files civil suits, plainly does. "The President must have the assistance of officers he can trust," the majority held.

Sotomayor, writing in dissent alongside Kagan and Jackson, argued the Court had opted for "unitary, total executive control" at the cost of a constitutional architecture specifically designed to prevent exactly that. Slaughter herself said FTC policy will "unquestionably" become more political.

The logic of the ruling extends well beyond the FTC. The SEC and CFTC — whose commissioners enforce rules that underpin daily trading and shape portfolio construction across every asset class — now sit under the same constitutional framework as any ordinary executive-branch appointee. So do the NLRB, the MSPB, and approximately two dozen other multi-member agencies that have operated under varying degrees of independence since the New Deal.

What the Fed exception means for markets

For investors, the day's net read is more nuanced than the competing headlines suggest. The Fed's independence is structurally intact — Roberts drew a tight historical argument rooted in the traditions of the First and Second Banks of the United States, a lineage that is not easily relitigated. Bond markets and the dollar both derive credibility from the assumption that rate decisions track economic data, not the Oval Office calendar. That assumption holds.

The Slaughter ruling is a different order of risk. A president who can reshape SEC or CFTC leadership at will can redirect enforcement priorities and rulemaking agendas without waiting for term expirations. The change will not be instantaneous — confirmed commissioners serve until replaced, and Senate confirmation battles impose friction — but the directional pressure is unambiguous. Financial regulation that was once insulated from election cycles now moves, at least in principle, with administrations.

For crypto markets, the CFTC angle is immediately live: jurisdiction over digital-asset spot markets has been an active regulatory dispute, and a commission more directly responsive to White House priorities could resolve that dispute differently than a fully independent one.

Roberts threaded the needle by asking which agencies carry a documented historical tradition of insulated independence that predates the New Deal. The Fed does. The FTC does not. That distinction — academic constitutional theory for decades — is now the operative dividing line. The next concrete test is whether Trump initiates a renewed, procedurally correct attempt to remove Cook. If that challenge reaches the courts, the lower courts will have to determine whether Roberts' procedural framing opens a viable path or whether the Fed's historical carve-out forecloses it regardless of process.
