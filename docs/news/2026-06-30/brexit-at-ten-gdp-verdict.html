Brexit at ten: Deutsche Bank puts the damage at 4% of GDP — and says it won't grow back

The Palace of Westminster, seen from the south bank of the Thames in 2022 — the parliament that legislated Brexit and now faces its long-term economic consequences. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Deutsche Bank's 10-year Brexit verdict: the UK economy is 4% smaller than if it had stayed in the EU, employment is down 685,000, and no policy fix closes that gap.

Ten years ago this month, a narrow majority of British voters chose to leave the European Union — 51.9% in favour of departure, on a turnout of 72%. The margin was slim, the political reverberations vast. What has been harder to pin down, in the decade since, is the economic cost: gradual enough to be masked by sterling depreciation, monetary stimulus and pandemic distortions, diffuse enough to fuel perennial arguments about counterfactual assumptions. Deutsche Bank has now attempted a definitive accounting. Its conclusion is that the UK economy is roughly 4% smaller than it would otherwise have been — and that the gap is structural, not cyclical.

The bank's report, released to coincide with the tenth anniversary of the June 23 vote, uses a synthetic control model: a statistical technique that constructs a "doppelganger" UK economy by blending the trajectories of comparable countries that remained inside the EU, then measures the UK's actual path against that composite. The divergence, Deutsche Bank finds, has not closed over time. On current policy settings, the bank sees no mechanism through which it will.

The anatomy of a 4% hole

The headline GDP figure is accompanied by secondary findings that give it texture. Employment is roughly 2% lower than the counterfactual — approximately 685,000 jobs fewer than Britain would otherwise have had. Consumer prices are about 0.7% higher, a modest but persistent uplift that compounded through years when UK inflation was already running at the top of the Western European table: consumer prices have risen 41.4% since the referendum, the highest rate among comparable economies except Austria.

The timing of these effects matters. The years immediately following the 2016 vote were cushioned by a sequence of offsetting forces — the Bank of England's emergency rate cuts, a depreciation in sterling that boosted exporters, a surge in EU workers arriving ahead of the transition deadline, and corporate stockpiling that temporarily inflated activity data. The full weight of the new arrangements became visible only after the pandemic, and in particular after the UK-EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement came into full effect in 2021. It was then that the synthetic doppelganger and the real economy parted company most visibly.

Investment's quiet retreat

If one variable captures what Brexit has cost the UK business sector, it is investment. Since mid-2016, UK business investment has grown by roughly 12% — against 23% in France and 48% in the United States over the same period. That suppression of capital formation, compounding annually, is among the principal reasons productivity has underperformed. The Office for Budget Responsibility has made a comparable assumption in its long-run fiscal modelling: Brexit, it estimates, will reduce UK productivity by around 4% through lower trade intensity alone.

The financial services sector illustrates the pattern in sharper relief. In 2015, UK financial exports exceeded the combined output of France, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands and Italy. A decade later, those five countries collectively surpassed Britain. Across twelve international finance categories, the UK lost market share in ten. The sector's output fell 27% between 2015 and 2025. Some of this reflects broader technology and regulatory shifts that would have occurred regardless of Brexit; some of it reflects the loss of passporting rights that had made London the natural hub for European capital markets activity.

The pound's trajectory reinforces the picture. Sterling has weakened approximately 10-15% against both the dollar and the euro since the referendum — a decline that inflates import costs persistently and has contributed to the higher consumer prices the Deutsche Bank report identifies.

Why the damage is structural

The core argument in the Deutsche Bank report — and in parallel assessments from Goldman Sachs, which estimates a 6% output shortfall using a broader peer group, and from the UK in a Changing Europe think-tank — is that Brexit's costs are not cyclical pressures that demand stimulus or time will repair. They reflect a permanent reduction in trade intensity, in business investment expectations, and in the UK's attractiveness as a platform for European market access. Structural gaps of this kind do not close through interest-rate cycles or fiscal boosts alone.

Adam Posen, former Bank of England rate-setter and president of the Peterson Institute for International Economics, has put the geopolitical dimension bluntly: "In a world where the U.S. is not providing global economic insurance, a UK that is neither in Europe nor fully with the U.S. is a small, open economy in a fundamentally more exposed position than it's been for decades."

Deutsche Bank does identify some gains. The UK now has greater regulatory flexibility, independent trade policy capacity, and what the bank characterises as a stronger position in AI regulation — where divergence from EU frameworks has allowed faster permissive licensing. Services exports and the current account balance have also performed better than pessimists forecast. But the bank's own figures suggest the upside from targeted improvements to the TCA — the kind of closer alignment that does not require formal EU re-entry — amounts to at most 0.4-0.8% of GDP. That recovers, at best, one-sixth of the estimated shortfall.

What investors and policymakers are watching

The report lands in a political moment that gives it unusual relevance. Andy Burnham's emergence as the leading candidate in Labour's forthcoming leadership contest — the formal race opens 9 July — is set to bring renewed debate about the UK's European posture. Burnham has been notably more open to closer EU alignment than the current leadership. Separately, polling cited by Goldman Sachs now shows 52% of Britons supporting EU membership — the first sustained majority in that direction since the referendum.

For sterling traders and investors in UK equities, Deutsche Bank's framing matters less as a historical verdict than as a forward-looking constraint. If the 4% structural shortfall is real, the UK's trend growth rate remains trapped below what monetary or fiscal policy can unlock without a genuine change in trade arrangements. The FTSE 250 has delivered a 2% real-terms loss since the Brexit vote, against inflation-adjusted gains of 13% in France and 19% in Germany. Closing that performance gap requires more than a rate cut.

The next material data point is likely to be the outcome of ongoing UK-EU sectoral talks, expected to yield a framework update before the end of the year. Deutsche Bank's estimate of a 0.4-0.8% GDP gain from TCA improvements sets a ceiling on what such a deal could realistically deliver — and underlines how far below the 4% damage figure it falls.
