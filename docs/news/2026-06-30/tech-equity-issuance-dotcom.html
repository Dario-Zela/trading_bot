Tech equity issuance hits dot-com pace — and bondholders are keeping score

Traders on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where tech credit spreads have widened to their worst relative performance in the investment-grade index year-to-date. — Scott Beale / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Tech's $160bn equity sprint — Alphabet's record $85bn offer plus SpaceX's $75bn IPO — signals more debt ahead, not less, and bond spreads are already moving.

The comparison that keeps circling fixed-income desks this June is not a recent analogue. It's 1999. Technology companies have placed roughly $160 billion of new equity into the market in a fortnight: Alphabet raised $85 billion in early June — the largest single equity offering on record — to fund a $190 billion capital-expenditure programme for the year. SpaceX followed with a $75 billion IPO on June 12, the biggest debut in US market history, leaving the company's cash position at $100.8 billion even before the secondary market had absorbed the new paper.

The volume arriving in such compressed time has not been seen since the dot-com build-out. The crucial distinction is that today's issuers are profitable, with free cash flow running to tens of billions annually. The uncomfortable similarity is the pattern that follows the equity: more debt.

Equity as signal, not shield

Bondholders have learned to treat large equity raises with caution precisely because the cash rarely reduces leverage — it underwrites the next phase of spending. "Bondholders are inclined to cheer equity-raise announcements as a signal for slower balance-sheet deterioration," said Anthony Woodside, head of multi-sector fixed income at L&G Asset Management America, "but it really means there's a lot more debt coming too — equity is not replacing debt, it's supplementing it."

The sequence played out in real time. SpaceX closed its $75 billion IPO on June 12. Ten days later it launched a $25 billion investment-grade bond offering — one of the largest debut IG deals on record. Within a week, those bonds were carrying approximate book losses of $360 million relative to Treasuries; long-dated tranches widened to spreads above 200 basis points, a pace traders described as among the sharpest post-pricing deterioration in recent memory. Alphabet's bonds also drifted weaker after its equity announcement, though the move was more contained.

The sector-level picture confirms the pressure. Tech bond spreads have widened roughly 15 basis points year-to-date relative to the broader investment-grade index — the worst sector performance in IG — with the aggregate risk premium reaching 79 basis points by mid-June, up from 74 at the end of May. Wall Street estimates approximately $300 billion in AI-related IG supply will price in 2026 alone. J.P. Morgan has raised its five-year data-centre bond-financing projection to $2.1 trillion, a 40% increase from its November estimate.

Tom Murphy of Columbia Threadneedle distilled the investor read: "It's telling us that the amount of capital expenditure that they're going to do is probably going to go up."

Rating agencies have not yet moved — current leverage among the largest tech borrowers remains well below the investment-grade average, with net debt ratios in the 0.4–0.7x range versus the IG mean of roughly 3x. But credit desks note that secondary-market absorption is beginning to strain at current issuance volumes, a condition that has historically preceded formal ratings pressure by two to three quarters. The next observable test arrives in mid-July, when Alphabet, Meta, and Microsoft report Q2 results. Any further upward revision to capex guidance would confirm the trend bondholders are already pricing — and give spread widening a concrete catalyst before the analysts can formally respond.
