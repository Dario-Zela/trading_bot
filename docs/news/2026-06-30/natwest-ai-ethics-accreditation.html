NatWest mandates AI and data ethics accreditation for all 60,000 employees

A NatWest branch on Clapham High Street, London — the bank is rolling out AI ethics training across its entire 60,000-strong workforce. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NatWest is making AI and data ethics accreditation mandatory for all 60,000 staff, built with the University of Edinburgh and rolling out by October.

NatWest Group has made AI and data ethics accreditation mandatory for all 60,000 of its employees — from tellers to senior executives — in what amounts to one of the most sweeping workforce AI governance programmes attempted by a major UK bank.

The accreditation, developed in partnership with the University of Edinburgh, requires staff to complete eight e-learning modules aligned to NatWest's internal AI Ethics Principles, followed by a half-day practical session focused on real-world banking scenarios. The full programme takes two to three months to complete. The first modules launched this month; the bank expects the entire workforce to finish by October.

"As AI becomes increasingly embedded in how we serve customers and run our bank, it's important that we equip colleagues with the skills and confidence to use it responsibly," said Dr Paul Dongha, NatWest's head of responsible AI and AI strategy. The curriculum covers AI fundamentals, data privacy, fairness, bias mitigation, and the mechanics of how training data shapes model outputs.

The programme was not built from scratch on a whiteboard. NatWest first ran a bespoke version with the University of Edinburgh across three cohorts of around 90 specialist colleagues, then refined and scaled the material. Staff who complete the accreditation receive an internal credential and are required to undertake ongoing learning as AI capabilities and regulatory standards evolve. According to the bank, more than half of staff have already opted into additional training beyond the mandatory modules.

Regulators are watching

The timing is not incidental. UK financial regulators are in the middle of a sustained push to sharpen oversight of AI in banking. The FCA launched a long-term review of how AI might reshape retail financial services in January, and is expected to publish comprehensive guidance on consumer protection and senior manager accountability under AI-driven decisions before the end of 2026. NatWest is already participating in the FCA's AI Live Testing programme, which focuses on security and ethical practices in agentic AI — systems that act autonomously across multi-step tasks.

Many banks publish AI principles. Far fewer make completion of AI ethics training a condition of employment for the whole organisation, not just compliance and technology teams. The distinction matters: principles are aspirational, accreditation is measurable. For an institution deploying AI across fraud prevention, customer-facing virtual assistants, complaints handling, and credit analysis, embedding that baseline across the workforce is also a risk management call, not just a public relations one.

The question for the rest of the sector is whether a voluntary commitment at this scale eventually becomes the de facto standard — or whether regulators decide to make it one. The FCA's forthcoming guidance will be the clearest signal yet of which way that pressure is headed.
