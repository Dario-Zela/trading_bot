Comcast breaks itself in two, separating broadband infrastructure from the content bet

Comcast Center, Philadelphia — corporate headquarters of the cable and broadband giant now pursuing a structural split — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Comcast will split into two listed companies by mid-2027, separating its profitable broadband network from NBCUniversal and Sky in a bet that focused businesses command higher valuations than the bundled conglomerate.

Comcast's board gave the green light on Sunday for a restructuring that has been telegraphed in stages for years: the company will divide itself into two independent, publicly listed businesses. One will hold the broadband cables, wireless networks, and Xfinity customer relationships that generate most of the group's cash. The other will carry NBCUniversal — NBC, Telemundo, Peacock, Universal Studios, theme parks — and the European pay-TV operator Sky. The tax-free spinoff is expected to close in roughly twelve months, subject to regulatory sign-off and final board approval.

Shares leapt 23 percent in premarket trading on June 29, before the stock settled to a gain of roughly five percent by the close. That opening gap tells a story: investors had been applying a discount to the combined entity, and they moved immediately to reprice the parts.

The arithmetic of separation

The case for splitting has been accumulating for half a decade. Comcast's shares traded near $60 in 2021; by Monday they were at approximately $25, a decline of 46 percent over five years that puts the stock at a forward price-to-earnings multiple of seven and an EV/EBITDA of four — numbers more typical of a regulated utility than a media empire with 46 million streaming subscribers.

The problem, in broad terms, is that two very different businesses have been sharing one equity certificate. The broadband and wireless operations — reaching more than 65 million homes and businesses across the United States — generate dependable cash flows and benefit from relatively limited competition in most of their footprint. NBCUniversal is a different proposition: a legacy broadcast network carrying structural linear-TV decline, a streaming service in Peacock that posted a quarterly EBITDA loss of $432 million in the first quarter of 2026, and a theme-park division that is growing but capital-intensive.

Comcast had already moved once this year to trim the portfolio. In January 2026, it completed the spinoff of Versant Media — the rump of its cable-network holdings, including CNBC, MSNBC, USA Network, Golf Channel, E!, and Syfy — into a separately listed company. Versant's shares fell 13 percent on their first day of trading, a pointed reminder that simply separating assets does not guarantee the market will like what it sees. The new NBCUniversal will not include Versant; the earlier deal was explicitly structured to clear the decks before this larger move.

With Versant gone and the full NBCUniversal split now announced, Comcast is effectively completing a two-step unwinding of the acquisition strategy it pursued after buying NBC in 2011 and taking full control in 2013.

What each half looks like

The broadband company will continue to trade as Comcast and will be led by Michael Angelakis, who returns to the company after serving as its chief financial officer. Its core proposition is straightforward: a converged network infrastructure serving residential and business customers, with wireless services layered on top via an MVNO arrangement with Verizon's network. Wells Fargo analysts estimated the standalone cable business at approximately $20 per share in a sum-of-the-parts framework.

The media company — still referred to internally as SpinCo ahead of a permanent branding decision — will be run by Mike Cavanagh, who has served as co-CEO of the parent. It will control NBC and Telemundo's broadcast networks, the Peacock streaming platform, Universal's film and television studios, three theme-park resorts, Bravo (retained specifically because of its outsized contribution to Peacock viewership), and Sky's European pay-TV operations. Comcast expects to retain up to a 19.9 percent stake in the new company for up to one year after the transaction closes, unwinding that position "in a tax-efficient manner over time."

Brian Roberts, chairman and co-CEO of the parent, will remain actively involved with both companies. The dual-class share structure that has concentrated voting power — Roberts controls roughly one-third of votes — survives the split.

Implications beyond Comcast

Comcast's move is the largest media-sector restructuring since AT&T divested WarnerMedia in 2022 and Viacom resorted to the CBS merger in 2019. Its significance extends past the company's own balance sheet.

For the broadband business, independence means the market can finally price a capital-disciplined infrastructure operator without the drag of Peacock's quarterly losses. For NBCUniversal, it means a dedicated equity currency and a standalone board — which analysts see as the precondition for a content-sector roll-up. A free-standing NBCUniversal could, in theory, pursue deals with scaled content owners that would have been complicated by a parent anchored to cable infrastructure.

The harder question is whether the separation actually repairs what ails the media side. Peacock faces the same competitive environment as an independent company that it faced inside Comcast: Netflix's subscriber base, Disney+'s content library, and a linear-advertising market in structural decline. The NBCUniversal spinoff gives the streaming service a cleaner story to tell investors, but it does not change the underlying competitive dynamics.

For the wider sector, the move will refocus attention on companies that have not yet made the structural call. Disney still combines its parks, studios, and streaming operations under one roof. Warner Bros. Discovery — created via merger, not separation — continues to carry heavy debt alongside its own streaming ambitions. If Comcast's post-split equity values do converge toward analyst sum-of-parts estimates in the $30 to $37 per share range, the pressure on conglomerate holdouts will be difficult to ignore.

The next concrete data points: regulatory filings in the coming weeks will reveal the capitalization structure and debt allocation between the two companies, and Comcast's second-quarter earnings — due in late July — will give investors the first financial look at each business on a pro-forma standalone basis.
