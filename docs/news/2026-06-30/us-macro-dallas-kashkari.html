US macro sends mixed signals: Texas factories slow sharply as Kashkari raises the rate-hike flag

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, whose monthly Texas Manufacturing Outlook Survey showed a sharp deceleration in output for June 2026. — Andreas Praefcke / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Texas factory output dropped 56% in one month to 4.1, but Kashkari's rate-hike pencil complicates the easing case traders had been banking on.

Texas factories slowed sharply in June. The Dallas Fed's monthly Texas Manufacturing Outlook Survey showed the production output index dropping to 4.1 — down from 9.4 in May, a 56% deceleration in a single month. The index remains above zero, meaning the sector hasn't tipped into contraction, but the trajectory is unmistakable: momentum is ebbing.

The reading fits a broader pattern. The ISM Manufacturing PMI came in at 54 for May — an apparently healthy print — but markets are pricing a meaningful probability of contraction when the June figure lands on July 1. Taken together, regional and national data increasingly suggest the manufacturing expansion that carried through early 2026 is losing altitude.

For rate-watchers, softer factory output narrows the Fed's patience window. Weaker activity data builds the case that monetary policy is now restrictive enough to do its work, strengthening the hand of officials who favour cutting rates before year-end. Bond markets have been pricing that story for months.

Kashkari plants a flag

Then came Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari, speaking at the Aspen Ideas Festival on June 26. "I have one rate hike penciled in for 2026," he said — a pointed shift from a man historically regarded as among the more dovish voices on the Federal Open Market Committee. His worry: inflation pressure is not merely a geopolitical artefact. "I am concerned about inflation, especially in services," Kashkari added. "Inflation move-up is not just about oil and the Middle East." He is not alone: nine of the 18 officials on the FOMC now have at least one rate hike penciled in for this year.

The juxtaposition is the story's tension. Weaker manufacturing data — like Monday's Texas survey — shortens the timeline for cuts. Kashkari's hawkish pivot, grounded in services-sector stickiness rather than energy-price spikes, argues for the opposite. Traders who came into the summer expecting a straightforward easing cycle are now pricing something more complicated: relief from slowing growth, but tightening from persistent price pressure.

That dynamic also exports across the Atlantic. The Bank of England held its benchmark rate at 3.75% at its June meeting, on a 7-2 vote, with two members preferring a hike. Policymakers there have telegraphed a summer easing path, but a Fed that raises rather than cuts would widen the transatlantic policy gap — lifting dollar strength, pressuring sterling, and complicating BoE communications just as it tries to anchor expectations ahead of what many in London still hope will be a July cut.

For now the data flow is the arbiter. The ISM's June manufacturing print due Wednesday morning will be watched for whether national numbers confirm the regional softness. A sub-50 reading would turn up the volume on the rate-cut case considerably — and force more explicit engagement with Kashkari's counter-argument.
