Britain's digital services tax keeps the UK in Trump's 100% tariff crosshairs — no carve-out in sight

10 Downing Street, where the Starmer government is walking a tightrope between defending its Digital Services Tax and managing the threat of US tariff escalation. — Sergeant Tom Robinson RLC / Crown Copyright
In one sentence
Trump's June 26 Truth Social post threatens 100% tariffs on any DST country — directly targeting Britain's 2% levy, with no legal shortcut in sight and no UK carve-out agreed.

Britain's Digital Services Tax — a 2% levy on the UK revenues of companies like Google, Meta and Amazon — has lurked in every UK-US trade discussion since its introduction in 2020. On 26 June, President Trump moved from hints to ultimatum, posting on Truth Social that any country maintaining a digital services tax would face a 100% tariff on all goods exported to the United States. The threat carried a pointed override: it "will supersede Trade Deals made with the Country, whether implemented, signed, or not."

That single clause wrote off the Starmer government's principal diplomatic buffer. The May 2025 "Economic Prosperity Deal" — through which London secured reduced car tariffs and a steel carve-out — had deferred the DST question rather than resolved it. Washington's new position treats the deferral as irrelevant.

What the UK is defending

The DST raised £800 million in 2024-25, ahead of the £515 million projected when it launched, and the OBR expects it to approach £1 billion annually by 2027. That revenue flows almost entirely from US firms — those meeting the threshold of £500 million in global digital revenues and £25 million in UK turnover. Abolishing the tax would require finding the money elsewhere or cutting spending, a problem the Treasury has not solved in any public statement.

Chancellor Rachel Reeves has not moved. "We will always set our own tax policy and our own tax rates — whether it's VAT, income tax, corporation tax, or the digital services tax," she told a podcast earlier this year. A spokesperson for Starmer's office called the levy "a hugely important tax to make sure that those businesses continue to pay their share. It is a fair and proportionate approach."

One structural caveat applies to the immediate threat. The Supreme Court's February ruling ended Trump's ability to impose tariffs via IEEPA; the administration's Section 122 authority expires on 24 July. The remaining mechanism is Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — which White House officials have identified as the intended vehicle — but Section 301 requires a formal investigation with a statutory 12-month minimum, including public submissions, a hearing, and interagency review. An "immediately imposed" 100% tariff has no clear legal path under present law.

The procedural buffer offers time, not immunity. Trump has consistently used deadline pressure as a substitute for patient legal process, and the pattern established around the EU-US talks — a 4 July deadline for initial terms — points to the same playbook. The UK is betting the gap between threat and execution is navigable.

The structural stakes extend well beyond the bilateral. The OECD's Pillar 1 project, designed to replace national DSTs with a multilateral profit-reallocation regime, has stalled since the US withdrew engagement. Without that framework, every country with a digital services tax faces the same binary the UK now does: hold the line or capitulate. If Britain folds, the signal to France, Italy, Spain — and every economy further down the list — will be unambiguous.

The sharpest clock ticks to 24 July, when the Section 122 authority lapses. That removes one of the executive's remaining short-cycle tools and could shift the dynamic. In the meantime, sterling traders and UK exporters are watching the same question as the Treasury: how much diplomatic heat can a 2% levy absorb.
