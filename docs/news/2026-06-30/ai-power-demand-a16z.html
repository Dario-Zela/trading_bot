US data-centre power demand has nearly doubled since 2023 — grid access, not algorithms, is now the competitive moat

Server racks inside a modern data centre — the physical layer that AI competition is increasingly won or lost on — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
US data-centre demand has nearly doubled to 42GW since 2023; grid access and cooling capacity, not model quality, now determine who can scale AI.

The constraint binding AI in 2026 is not intelligence — it is electricity. US data-centre power demand has risen from roughly 23 gigawatts in 2023 to an estimated 42 gigawatts today, an 82 percent jump in three years, according to a Barclays AI survey cited in a policy paper from venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz. The pace of that climb has outrun the grid's ability to absorb it, and the resulting bottleneck has reordered what counts as competitive advantage in institutional AI.

Goldman Sachs research places current US demand at 41 GW, tracking to 66 GW by 2027 — more than doubling the 2023 baseline in four years. Data centres' share of peak US summer electricity load is on course to rise from 4.1 percent this year to 8.5 percent by 2027. The occupancy rate for data-centre infrastructure is projected to breach 95 percent in late 2026 — a level that signals genuine scarcity rather than normal capacity utilisation.

Power before algorithms

The a16z paper, published in November 2025 under the title Speed-to-Power: An Energy Policy Agenda for a Thriving AI Market, makes an argument that cuts against the dominant narrative of AI as a software race. Energy, the firm warns, "could become AI's new moat, making it harder for startups to compete with hyperscalers." Hyperscalers — Amazon, Microsoft, Google — can lock in long-term power purchase agreements at preferential rates and deploy legal and lobbying resources to secure priority grid access across entire regions. Smaller operators cannot.

The physical constraints compound this asymmetry. Electrical transformer delivery times now stretch three to five years. Switchgear is sold out through 2028. Between 30 and 50 percent of large data centres scheduled to open in 2026 are delayed or cancelled outright, representing roughly 11 GW of announced capacity that has stalled. The components causing the hold-up — transformers, switchgear, circuit breakers — account for under 10 percent of total construction costs, but without them the remaining build-out cannot be energised.

Cooling is a parallel constraint. As AI training and inference clusters scale, rack power density has surged from 10–14 kilowatts to more than 100 kW, requiring fundamental redesigns of water-cooling systems and electrical distribution. A single large-scale AI training facility now requires between 100 MW and 1,000 MW of dedicated power — the electricity equivalent of 80,000 to 800,000 homes.

The implication for institutional operators is structural: firms that secured grid interconnection agreements, long-term power contracts, and site permits two or three years ago hold assets that cannot be replicated in any near-term planning cycle. The US grid interconnection queue currently holds over 2,600 GW of proposed new projects — more than double the entire installed US generating fleet — ensuring that late entrants face waits measured in years, not quarters.

The next concrete data point to watch is the mid-2026 NERC summer reliability assessment, which is expected to flag elevated risk in the Mid-Atlantic, Mid-Continent, and Northwest power markets — the three regions most exposed to data-centre load growth that has already outpaced new generation capacity.
