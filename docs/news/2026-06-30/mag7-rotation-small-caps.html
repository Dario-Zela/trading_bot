Small caps beat the S&P 500 by the widest margin in 23 years as the Mag Seven faces a crowded exit

The floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where the divergence between small-cap and mega-cap performance has become the defining trade of 2026. — Scott Beale / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Small caps are beating the S&P 500 by 11 percentage points YTD — the widest gap since 2003 — as hedge funds dump Magnificent Seven at the fastest pace since the 2022 bear market.

The Russell 2000 is running away from the S&P 500. Through June, small-cap stocks have climbed roughly 20 per cent year-to-date, against 9 per cent for the large-cap benchmark — an 11-percentage-point advantage that represents the widest annual gap between the two indices since 2003. That gap is not statistical noise. It reflects a genuine shift in where professional money is flowing, and why.

"Some professional investors are becoming less comfortable with the concentration and valuations that have defined the AI-driven rally." — Goldman Sachs Prime Services

A gap 23 years in the making

The last time small caps outpaced the S&P 500 by this margin, the economy was still climbing out of the dot-com wreckage. The parallel is uncomfortable — and deliberate. As of late June, the S&P 500 trades at roughly 23 times forward earnings, the most stretched since that era. The Magnificent Seven — Apple, Nvidia, Microsoft, Amazon, Tesla, Alphabet, and Meta — sit at closer to 30 times forward earnings, a premium of roughly 60 per cent over the rest of the index. The top ten names in the S&P 500 now account for 37–38 per cent of the entire index by weight.

The result of that concentration has been asymmetric. When the mega-caps stumble, the index feels it immediately. Microsoft is down 21.84 per cent year-to-date, dragging 127 basis points off the S&P 500 on its own. Meta has shed 13.99 per cent. Software names have fared worse: Intuit is off 58 per cent, Salesforce down 39 per cent.

By contrast, the Russell 2000 has been broadly supported. Of 1,863 constituents measured, nearly 65 per cent have posted positive returns; more than a third have gained over 25 per cent. The year's standout names skew toward the infrastructure that larger tech companies need but no longer represent: Bloom Energy is up 211 per cent, Sterling Infrastructure 165 per cent, Credo Technology — a semiconductor connectivity firm — 60 per cent. The rotation, as one market note characterised it, runs "away from the megacap spenders punished for their capex and toward the smaller suppliers selling the picks and shovels."

The crowded exit

The institutional positioning data makes the supply-demand story explicit. According to Goldman Sachs Prime Services, hedge funds have reduced their Magnificent Seven exposure by seven percentage points since the start of 2026 — the steepest six-month reduction since the 2022 bear market. The group now accounts for just 14.5 per cent of total U.S. hedge fund exposure, near a three-year low. Goldman also recorded the largest weekly selloff of U.S. information technology stocks by hedge funds since 2016 earlier this month.

The structural problem is who is still holding. Active managers can reduce their positions; passive ETF investors largely cannot. Three funds carry the most concentrated exposure: the Invesco QQQ Trust, which tracks the Nasdaq-100; the Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK); and the Vanguard Information Technology ETF (VGT). The Roundhill Magnificent Seven ETF (MAGS), which equally weights all seven names, is most exposed of all. As hedge funds trim, their selling meets an ETF complex that has little discretion to follow — a structural mismatch between active de-crowding and passive lock-in.

One day's bounce does not settle the question

Monday's session offered a temporary reprieve. After Friday's AI-driven selloff sent the iShares Semiconductor ETF down 5.9 per cent in a single session, the Nasdaq 100 reclaimed 1.5 per cent, closing at 29,566. Semiconductors led: SOXX added 2.8 per cent, with Astera Labs up 14 per cent, Applied Materials 12 per cent, and KLA Corp 9 per cent. Tesla surged 5.8 per cent after Elon Musk announced a rollout of new Full Self Driving hardware.

The bounce confirmed that buyers remain willing to step in on weakness in large-cap tech. What it did not settle is whether last week's selloff marks a brief correction in a continuing bull run, or the opening phase of a more durable regime shift. The two readings are not mutually exclusive: small caps can continue to outperform even as the Nasdaq bounces. What matters is the differential — and that differential has been widening since January.

The immediate catalysts to watch are the Federal Reserve's next rate decision — small caps carry more floating-rate debt and are acutely sensitive to financing costs — and the forthcoming second-quarter earnings season, which will test whether the Magnificent Seven's revenue deceleration has stabilised. The first major results land in mid-July. If misses accumulate, the crowded-exit dynamic that has been quietly building for six months could turn disorderly.
