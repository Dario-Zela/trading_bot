HyProMag bets recycled scrap can crack the rare-earth magnet supply chain

A nickel-plated neodymium-iron-boron magnet block — the material at the center of HyProMag USA's recycling bet. — Saimon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
HyProMag USA, backed by CoTec Holdings, is racing to recycle old hard-drive magnets into new rare-earth magnets before China's export licensing squeezes U.S. supply further.

For a decade and a half, the rare-earth magnets that spin wind turbines and drive electric-vehicle motors have flowed through one country's refineries and factories almost regardless of where the raw material was dug up. China controls the overwhelming majority of rare-earth refining and finished-magnet manufacturing, and since April 2025 it has required an export license for any neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) magnet containing even trace amounts of dysprosium or terbium — a rule that was never part of the trade truces negotiated since and remains fully in force. Shipments to the United States have fallen every month since October 2025, and in June 2026 Beijing added magnet makers MP Materials and USA Rare Earth to its own export-control entity list.

HyProMag USA is betting the fix isn't a new mine — it's a scrapyard. The joint venture, majority-backed by CoTec Holdings Corp. (TSX-V: CTH, OTCQB: CTHCF), wants to pull NdFeB magnets back out of dead hard drives, motors and industrial scrap and turn them into new magnets domestically, at a cost that competes with Chinese imports rather than merely avoiding them.

Hydrogen, not smelters

The process is called Hydrogen Processing of Magnet Scrap, or HPMS — 15 years and more than $100 million of development originating at the University of Birmingham, now rated at technology readiness level 8, one step short of full commercial deployment. Hydrogen gas embrittles and separates magnet alloy from scrap without the smelting and chemical separation that conventional rare-earth refining requires, which HyProMag says cuts energy use by 88% and carbon emissions by 85% against the mined-and-refined baseline. In March, the venture commissioned its first U.S. pre-processing line, an Inserma unit installed at Intelligent Lifecycle Solutions' facility in Williston, South Carolina, built specifically to strip magnets out of end-of-life hard disk drives before they get shredded.

The Dallas bet

South Carolina and a planned Nevada site are feedstock outposts; the real facility is a proposed integrated recycling-and-magnet-making plant in Dallas-Fort Worth, where HyProMag USA is targeting 1,526 metric tons a year of finished NdFeB magnets and first revenue in the second half of 2027. The U.S. Export-Import Bank has issued a letter of interest for up to $92 million toward that plant under its Make More in America program, with repayment stretched over ten years — real money, but a letter of interest, not a signed loan. If a second and third plant follow by 2028-2029, HyProMag USA projects roughly 4,656 tons of annual capacity, which the company estimates could be worth more than $2 billion in after-tax net present value across the first three sites.

HyProMag USA is owned 50-50 by CoTec and HyProMag Limited, a UK entity controlled by AIM/TSX-V-listed Mkango Resources, with CoTec holding a further minority stake in that UK parent. CoTec itself is a roughly $120 million company on 117 million shares outstanding — small enough that the HyProMag bet is a meaningful swing for its stock, and thin enough that funding a multi-plant U.S. build-out will likely mean more capital raises or partners before Dallas breaks ground.

HyProMag USA is due to complete a Class 3 cost estimate — the engineering-and-costing benchmark investors use to judge whether a project's numbers hold up — in the third quarter of 2026. A U.S. listing for the venture isn't expected before late 2026 or 2027. Both will tell recycling advocates, and China, whether reshoring the magnet supply chain needs a permanent policy subsidy or can eventually pay for itself.
