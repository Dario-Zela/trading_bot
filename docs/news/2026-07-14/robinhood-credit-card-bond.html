Robinhood tests first bond sale backed by its credit-card bills

Robinhood co-founder and CEO Vlad Tenev, whose company is testing its first credit card-backed bond sale. — Pierce Larick / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Robinhood is testing investor demand for a $400–500M bond backed by its credit-card receivables — its first asset-backed securities deal ever.

Robinhood Markets Inc. spent its first decade persuading ordinary people to trade stocks and options from a phone. Now it wants those same customers carrying a Robinhood-branded credit card — and it wants Wall Street to help fund the balances they run up on it.

The company is gauging investor appetite for a bond backed by receivables from its consumer credit cards, according to a Bloomberg report — Robinhood's first foray into asset-backed securities. The structure is standard Wall Street plumbing: pool the monthly bills that cardholders owe, package the right to collect them into bonds, and sell those bonds to fixed-income investors. Banks, retailers and airlines have funded credit card programs this way for decades. Robinhood has not, until now.

The mechanics of the deal

Robinhood is seeking to sell at least $400 million of the securities, split into four tranches, with the total potentially reaching $500 million, Bloomberg reported. Initial price talk on the highest-rated slice sits at roughly 0.8 percentage point over the benchmark rate — a level that will tell Robinhood, and the market, how much confidence investors place in the credit quality of its cardholder base. Wells Fargo & Co. and Barclays Plc are running the sale.

That pricing matters more than it might appear. Asset-backed spreads are a direct read on how rating agencies and institutional buyers view the underlying borrowers. Robinhood's card programs skew toward its existing brokerage customers — a demographic the company has spent years courting with commission-free trading, crypto access and options tools — rather than the mass-market population usually securitized by the likes of Capital One or Synchrony. Whether that base pays reliably enough to justify tight spreads is precisely what this test is designed to discover.

From trading app to card issuer

The securitization attempt caps a rapid build-out of Robinhood's credit business. The no-fee Gold Card, tied to a Robinhood Gold subscription, went from a waitlist of roughly 100,000 cardholders in early 2025 to reported distribution of around 300,000 by last August. In March, Robinhood escalated further, unveiling a $695-a-year card plated in actual platinum — an invitation-only product aimed squarely at American Express's flagship franchise, undercutting Amex's $895 Platinum fee while promising more than $3,000 in annual credits and benefits.

That card business has now grown large enough, and seasoned enough, that Robinhood can point rating agencies and bond buyers to a real payment history rather than a projection. Securitization is typically the milestone that signals a lending book has matured past the point where a company can comfortably fund it off its own balance sheet — it is also, not incidentally, cheaper capital than equity or unsecured corporate debt, provided investors show up.

The timing lines up with a broader capital-markets push. Robinhood priced a separate $2 billion zero-coupon convertible bond in June, earmarking part of the proceeds for share buybacks and hedging instruments to limit dilution. Between the convertible raise and the prospective card-receivables deal, the company is tapping debt markets on two fronts within weeks of each other — a sign of a fintech treating Wall Street's funding toolkit as core infrastructure rather than a novelty.

What the market is really pricing

A card-backed bond is a referendum on Robinhood's credit underwriting, not its trading revenue. The company built its name on commission-free brokerage, a business that lives and dies with market volume and volatility. Credit card interest income does not; it depends on how disciplined Robinhood's own risk models are, and on how its customers — many of whom came to the platform for stock and crypto speculation, not banking — behave as revolving borrowers. Investors buying the top tranche of this deal are effectively betting that a company built for trading apps can underwrite consumer credit as competently as banks that have done it for a century.

If the deal prices well, it opens a repeatable, lower-cost funding channel for Robinhood's credit ambitions and validates the leap from brokerage app to diversified financial-services company. If demand is thin or pricing comes in wide, it will be read as skepticism about a fintech playing banker with receivables it has held for barely two years. Either way, the answer will come soon: asset-backed deals of this size typically price within days of the initial investor calls, giving the market — and Robinhood — a fast verdict on how far this pivot into traditional finance can go.
