Iran campaign grinds on: third night of strikes, blockade resumes, Trump talks tough

U.S. Navy ships transit the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint at the center of the renewed U.S.-Iran confrontation. — U.S. Navy photo by Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class Alex R. Forster, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
U.S. forces hit Iran for a third straight night as Washington reimposes a Hormuz blockade and floats a 20% transit toll, with Trump alternating threats and deal talk.

For the third night running, American warplanes and warships went back at Iran. U.S. Central Command said the operation began at 4:45 p.m. ET on July 14, striking coastal defence systems, missile and drone sites, and maritime capabilities across a run of Iranian targets — Bushehr, Chah Bahar, Jask, Konarak, Abu Musa and Bandar Abbas — over a five-hour mission. The stated aim, per CENTCOM's post on X, is to "impose a heavy cost on Iranian forces" and strip Tehran of the ability to threaten commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes.

The strikes did not arrive in isolation. They follow Iran's reported attack on two tankers in the strait a day earlier, and they land less than a month after Washington and Tehran signed a memorandum of understanding at Versailles meant to end the broader war. That truce is now effectively dead: the U.S. Navy has resumed its blockade posture in the strait as of July 14, and Trump has floated charging a 20 percent toll on cargo transiting Hormuz in exchange for American protection — a demand aimed at Gulf states including Saudi Arabia, Qatar, the UAE, Kuwait and Bahrain.

A blockade with a price tag, and Tehran's counter-offer

Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, answered in kind rather than with outrage. "POTUS is absolutely right," he wrote. "Whoever provides secure and safe passage of commercial vessels through the Strait of Hormuz should be compensated for this service. Iran has always been the GUARDIAN of the Strait and will remain so FOREVER. 20% is of course too much. We will be fair." It reads as sarcasm, but it also underlines how thin the underlying dispute now is — not whether ships should pay for passage, but who gets to collect.

Trump: deal-maker and bomber in the same breath

Trump has spent the past two days holding both postures at once. Speaking publicly on the strikes, he described the campaign as "knocking out Iran's offensive capability" while still insisting "a deal is possible." On the Hugh Hewitt Show, though, the tone hardened: "We're going to hit them very hard tonight and we're going to hit them hard tomorrow — and there's not a damn thing they can do about it." He dismissed the Versailles MOU as merely a "test" that Iran failed to honor, and brushed aside talk of daylight with Netanyahu — "sometimes I disagree with him, and I let it be known" — while affirming the relationship remains close.

The market has already been pricing this pattern of escalate-then-signal-openness: Brent crude jumped past $78 a barrel this week on the renewed strikes, having spiked and eased twice already since fighting resumed. Traders are not yet betting on a full Hormuz shutdown, but a third consecutive night of strikes, an active blockade, and a toll dispute with no settled number all point the same way — toward more volatility before any resolution. The next marker to watch is whether CENTCOM announces a fourth night, or whether Tehran and Washington quietly restart the talks the MOU was supposed to lock in.
