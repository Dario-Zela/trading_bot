Fed's Waller ranges across inflation targets, AI risk and the balance sheet

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher J. Waller, who used a July 13 speech to defend the Fed's inflation credibility and reject symbolic rate moves. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Waller says the Fed won't cut rates symbolically to please markets or the White House, and would treat another hot inflation print as a signal for a hike, not noise.

Christopher Waller does not want the Federal Reserve moving interest rates to make a point. Not for the White House, not for markets, not for anyone. That was the throughline of a lengthy address to the New York Association for Business Economics on Monday, in which the Fed governor ranged across nearly every live question in central banking — inflation measurement, the labor market, the balance sheet, even artificial intelligence — while returning again and again to a single idea: the Fed's credibility depends on being boring, predictable and unmoved by pressure.

"I think our credibility is intact — the market believes we will do our job" and bring inflation back to 2%, Waller told the room, in a speech pointedly titled "Monetary Policy at a Crossroads." The remarks land two weeks before the July 28-29 FOMC meeting and amid an unusually public campaign by President Trump for lower rates — pressure Waller, a leading contender for the Fed chair job before Kevin Warsh got it in May, has now twice this month declined to accommodate.

The credibility argument, and why he won't cut for show

Waller was explicit that he has no interest in symbolic rate moves — cuts to placate markets or a White House impatient for cheaper credit, hikes to prove toughness for its own sake. The data will decide. And lately the data have not been cooperative: core PCE inflation has climbed from 3.0% in December to 3.4% in May, and Waller flagged that roughly 70% of the categories the Fed tracks now show three- and twelve-month inflation running above 3%, a sign that price pressure has broadened well past the tariff and energy shocks that first drove it up.

"If I get another higher one, I'm going to treat that as signal, not noise," he said of the incoming CPI report — a not-so-subtle statement that a rate hike, not a cut, is the live risk if the numbers don't turn. He also used the moment to take a swipe at looser inflation-measurement philosophies gaining currency elsewhere in the debate. "Inflation becomes like pornography — I can't define it, I can't measure it, but I know it when I see it — and that's not how central bankers should think about inflation," he said, defending the Fed's reliance on a specific, published price index over vaguer, feel-it-when-you-see-it standards. It is a curious position for a man who, in the same speech, said he'd personally prefer swapping the Fed's 2% point target for a 1.5%-2.5% range — arguing a single number is "too restrictive" for judging success across different economic regimes. He was careful to frame that as a personal view, not a proposal with votes behind it; the 2% target has been Fed doctrine since 2012 and changing it requires the full committee, not one governor's preference.

A labor market that stopped being the problem

If inflation is where Waller is watchful, the labor market is where he sounds almost relieved. "Some of the job market uncertainty resolved itself this year — it's been a stunning turnaround," he said, pointing to a three-month average of roughly 111,000 jobs added — modest, but in his reading consistent with an economy at or near maximum sustainable employment given cooler labor supply growth. He waved off June's dip in labor-force participation as likely noise, backed by steady job-openings and separations data, and dismissed fears of another round of ugly payroll revisions: the Bureau of Labor Statistics, he said, is now capturing more survey responses later in its collection cycle, which should reduce the risk of the kind of large after-the-fact revisions that rattled markets in past years. His preferred gauge remains the unemployment rate — "traditionally been the best indicator of labor market health," he said, and one that has stayed stable.

That data confidence fed into a broader skepticism of the Fed's own forecasting apparatus. Waller said he has "been a skeptic of the dots for a long time" — the anonymous rate projections the Fed publishes quarterly — and would trim the projection horizon to 18 months and scrap the long-run estimates entirely, arguing they convey false precision about where rates settle years out. He'd also release the dots a day after the meeting that produces them, rather than bundling them into the same announcement. The theme connects to a sharper disagreement simmering under Warsh's young chairmanship: Waller argued flatly against withholding information from markets. "You want the markets to have as much information as possible. Surprising people is not a good idea," he said — a line read by several in the room as a direct contrast with Warsh's own preference for a more discretionary, less scripted style of communication.

AI's double edge, and a decade of MBS regret

Waller named tariffs, energy and AI-driven demand as the three forces pushing prices up, though he said the earlier worry that higher oil prices would spread broadly through the economy has "greatly diminished." AI is now doing more of the work: shortages of memory and storage chips tied to data-centre buildout are pushing up prices in retail categories that have historically pulled inflation down, he said. The same buildout cuts the other way as a financial-stability question — Waller warned that AI has been propping up broader financial conditions, and that a serious pullback in AI investment or valuations would trigger "pretty big changes" in those conditions. He was unusually candid about the limits of his own toolkit here: "not clear how easy monetary policy can deal with demand on AI," he said, an admission that interest rates are a blunt instrument against a demand shock concentrated in a handful of sectors.

On the balance sheet, Waller mixed contrition with patience. He said the Fed's mortgage-backed securities purchases — a crisis-era and pandemic-era tool now holding roughly $2.3 trillion on the balance sheet — were a mistake he wishes had never happened, and estimated it will take the Fed another decade to fully unwind the MBS book given how slowly the portfolio runs off. Even so, he sees no urgency: "I have a hard time seeing where the balance sheet at current size is causing problems," he said, adding he sees "no reason ever to be in a scarce reserves regime" and would be comfortable shrinking the balance sheet further if the Fed can find ways for banks to operate on fewer reserves. He acknowledged real disagreement inside the building over how much shrinkage the current reserve-demand framework can actually support, and said a new internal task force will take up the question along with several others — work he expects will have "valuable impact," though he stopped short of previewing conclusions.

What ties it together

Strip away the individual threads and the picture is of a Fed governor trying to hold a credible, rules-based institution together at a moment when the ground underneath it keeps shifting — a new chair with a more discretionary style, a president publicly agitating for cuts, and an AI boom the Fed's models were never built to price. Waller's answer to all of it is the same: fewer surprises, sharper indicators, no theatrics. The next test of that posture arrives fast. A CPI report lands before the July 28-29 FOMC meeting, and Waller has already said what he'll do with a hot number — treat it as signal. Markets pricing in patience from the Fed should watch that print as closely as he says he will.
