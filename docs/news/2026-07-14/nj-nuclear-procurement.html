New Jersey launches nuclear procurement with ratepayer safeguards

Hope Creek Nuclear Generating Station, part of the Salem–Hope Creek complex that supplies more than 40% of New Jersey's electricity — NRC / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
New Jersey signed a law to procure 1,100 MW of new nuclear power, with contractual safeguards meant to keep ratepayers from absorbing Vogtle-style cost overruns.

New Jersey has spent nearly half a century treating new nuclear reactors as something other states build. On Monday, Governor Mikie Sherrill signed a law that formally ends that posture — not by promising a plant, but by opening a structured process to buy one, wrapped in enough contractual guardrails that the state hopes to avoid the price blowouts that have made nuclear construction a byword for cost overruns elsewhere.

The Power NJ Act, passed unanimously by both chambers of the Legislature, directs the Board of Public Utilities and the state's Economic Development Authority to run a competitive procurement aimed at bringing at least 1,100 megawatts of new nuclear generation onto the grid — roughly the output of one large reactor. It follows an April law that quietly cleared the legal obstacle actually blocking new construction: an outdated provision in the Coastal Area Facility Review Act tied to radioactive waste disposal, which Sherrill's administration replaced with a permitting path tied to NRC-compliant storage standards.

Guardrails written against a known failure

The protections in the bill read like a direct rebuttal to Plant Vogtle, the Georgia project that finished seven years late and more than $18 billion over budget, with much of the overrun ultimately passed to ratepayers. New Jersey's version requires developers to line up federal financing before the state will lean on their project, shifting much of the risk off ratepayers from the outset. Construction costs cannot be charged to customers before a plant is operating. Any approved project must show a net benefit to ratepayers, and cost overruns remain the developer's responsibility rather than the state's. The process also builds in two public comment periods, a public hearing, and an independent review from the Division of Rate Counsel before the BPU issues a final order.

The timeline is specific enough to hold regulators to it: the BPU must open the request for proposals by January 9, 2027, developers get 60 days to respond, a 90-day provisional qualification period follows, then up to a year of negotiation over pricing and terms, with a final board order due by July 8, 2028 — a two-year runway before a project is even locked in, let alone built.

Not everyone is convinced the math works

The skepticism hasn't disappeared, it's just been redirected from safety to cost. Rate Counsel Brian Lipman's office has estimated the subsidy structure could add between $7.80 and $22.43 to the average monthly residential bill, with the higher end kicking in if wholesale energy prices fall — the opposite of what a fixed-price capacity contract is supposed to protect against. Lipman's broader concern is duration rather than dollars: the state, he has argued, is committing to roughly 40 years of payments largely justified by data-center demand growth that may not hold. "We're signing on for 40 years whether it's needed or not," he said during legislative hearings on the bill.

That tension — locking in long-dated capacity to hedge against a demand forecast nobody can be certain of — is the same one utilities and regulators across the country are wrestling with as AI-driven load growth collides with retiring fossil generation. New Jersey's answer is to try to have it both ways: pursue the dispatchable, carbon-free capacity nuclear offers, while pushing as much financial risk as possible onto developers and federal backers rather than the ratepayers who bore the cost at Vogtle.

What's already running, and what's next

Nuclear already supplies about 40% of New Jersey's electricity and 80% of its clean power, generated almost entirely by the Salem and Hope Creek stations on an artificial island in Salem County — the largest nuclear complex in the eastern United States, operated by PSEG Nuclear. No new reactor has been built in the state since Hope Creek came online in 1986; Oyster Creek, the state's oldest plant, shut down in 2018.

New Jersey is now the sixth state in a decade — and the second this year — to repeal a moratorium on new nuclear construction, part of a broader reversal among states that spent the post-Three Mile Island era writing reactors out of their energy plans. The next concrete marker is January 9, 2027, when the BPU is required to open the request for proposals. Whether the ratepayer protections actually hold when a developer is mid-construction and over budget won't be testable for years — but the contract language written now is what other states weighing the same bet will be reading first.
