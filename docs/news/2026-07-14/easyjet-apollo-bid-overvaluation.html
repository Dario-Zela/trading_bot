easyJet board backs £5.5bn Apollo bid as analysts flag 51% overvaluation

An easyJet Airbus A320, the workhorse of the fleet private-equity bidders are chasing. — Anna Zvereva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Apollo's £7.15-a-share bid tops Castlelake's, but a widely followed model puts easyJet's shares roughly 51% above fair value.

easyJet's board has spent the past week doing something boards rarely do twice in one sentence: accepting a takeover offer, then walking it back. On 6 July it agreed in principle to a £6.90-a-share cash bid from Castlelake, the Minneapolis-based alternative asset manager. Four days later it dropped that support after Apollo Global Management tabled £7.15 a share in cash — valuing the airline at roughly £5.5 billion on a headline basis, or about £5.7 billion once fully diluted shares are counted. easyJet said it was "no longer minded to recommend" the Castlelake deal. Shares jumped as much as 15% on the news, touching around £6.75 — their highest level since early 2022 — though still short of what Apollo is offering.

In one sentence: Apollo's £7.15-a-share cash bid has topped Castlelake's and won board backing, but a widely followed valuation model puts easyJet's shares roughly 51% above what the underlying business is actually worth.

A bidding war, sharpened by an unusual reversal

What makes this more than a routine private-equity approach is the sequencing. Castlelake's £6.90 offer was itself only agreed the previous Sunday — the board's about-face came within a week, a timeline that suggests either a very aggressive Apollo intervention or a board keeping its options open from the start. Apollo's price represents roughly a 22% premium to easyJet's closing price the Thursday before the news broke, and beats Castlelake's number by close to £1 billion in aggregate terms.

The offer isn't simply cash-and-done. Apollo has attached a "stub equity alternative": shareholders who want continued exposure can roll some or all of their stock into Apollo's acquisition vehicle rather than cashing out, reportedly retaining voting rights, though the mechanics of that structure are still being worked out. It's a feature increasingly common in take-privates where the acquirer wants existing holders — and their knowledge of the asset — to stay partially in the tent.

Neither side has finished playing its hand. Under UK Takeover Panel rules, Castlelake has until 3 August to either counter or formally walk away; Apollo faces its own deadline of 5pm on 7 August to convert this into a firm offer or drop out, a date that can be extended with the Panel's consent. That gap leaves roughly three weeks in which either the bidding climbs further or one side folds.

Why private equity wants a loss-making airline

The interest is not really about easyJet's income statement, which has been ugly lately. The airline reported first-half losses widening 27% year-on-year to £377 million, a hit driven largely by fuel costs spiking amid the Iran-related disruption to Middle East shipping and airspace. Chief executive Kenton Jarvis described the carrier as "well placed" to ride out the turbulence — language that reads more like reassurance to staff and lenders than a claim about near-term profitability.

What's drawing Apollo and Castlelake instead is the asset base underneath the P&L: a recognisable consumer brand, a young narrow-body fleet, a portfolio of scarce airport slots at capacity-constrained hubs, and a package-holiday arm that throws off steadier cash flow than scheduled flying alone. Those are the kinds of assets private-equity buyers like — durable, hard to replicate, and reasonably insulated from any single quarter's fuel bill. Analysts covering the deal also flag a complication that could cap what shareholders ultimately see: EU ownership rules restrict how much of a European carrier can sit in non-EU hands, which may constrain how the economics of any final structure get split between the buyer and existing investors.

The valuation gap the deal price obscures

Here is where the M&A story runs into a harder-nosed one. Simply Wall St's most-followed valuation narrative on easyJet puts fair value at around £4.48 a share — against a stock that, even before Apollo's intervention, was trading at £6.75. On that model, the shares sit roughly 51% above what the underlying business supports, a gap that widened, not narrowed, as the bidding war pushed the price toward Apollo's £7.15.

Zoom out and the pattern isn't new. easyJet has returned 49.8% to shareholders over the past three years, a genuinely strong run for a legacy short-haul carrier. But on conventional value screens the stock passes just two of six standard checks, and trades at 12.2 times earnings — cheaper than the sector average, yet not cheap enough to count as an obvious bargain once growth assumptions and margin risk are priced in. The polite way to put it: the three-year rally has already done a fair amount of the work that a takeover premium is supposed to do. The less polite way is that a chunk of today's price increasingly reflects deal speculation rather than what easyJet's flights, slots and holidays business will actually generate in cash.

What this means for anyone still holding the stock

The risk sits with whoever is left holding easyJet shares if the auction ends rather than escalates. If Castlelake walks on 3 August and Apollo doesn't raise its number by 7 August, the stock has further to fall than it has to rise — the £6.75 close already prices in a fair probability that £7.15 gets paid, or bettered. Conversely, anyone taking the stub equity option is choosing to keep exposure to a business that independent valuation work says is already generously priced, on the bet that Apollo's ownership and balance-sheet changes will unlock value conventional models don't capture.

The next hard date is 3 August, when Castlelake must show its hand; the harder one is 7 August, when Apollo's deadline forces a firm offer or a walk-away. Either outcome will tell shareholders more about whether this £5.7 billion number reflects easyJet's worth, or simply the price of winning an auction.
