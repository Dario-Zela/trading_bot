FuelCell leans on Siemens tie-up to chase AI data-centre demand

Data centers' surging power demand is what FuelCell Energy is betting its Siemens partnership will help it capture. — Fleshas / BalticServers.com, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
FuelCell Energy's Siemens tie-up lends technical credibility to its AI data-center push, but the deal is a non-binding MoU and past execution still raises doubts.

FuelCell Energy has spent years promising that its hydrogen and biogas-fed power plants would find a lucrative home somewhere beyond municipal sewage plants and university campuses. This week it got the kind of validation Wall Street actually listens to: an endorsement, of sorts, from Siemens.

On July 9 the Danbury, Connecticut-based company and the German industrial giant announced a memorandum of understanding to jointly develop fuel cell power systems for data centers and other energy-hungry customers. Under the deal, Siemens will design and supply the electrical balance-of-plant equipment — the switchgear, transformers and control systems that sit between a power source and the building it feeds — while FuelCell Energy supplies the fuel cells themselves. The target is commercial projects above 100 megawatts, well beyond anything FuelCell has deployed to date.

"By combining FuelCell Energy's fuel cell technology with Siemens' electrical infrastructure, service and integration expertise, we can deliver scalable, on-site power solutions for energy-intensive applications," said Kevin Brown, who heads sustainability solutions for Siemens Smart Infrastructure USA. FuelCell's chief product and technology officer, Shankar Achanta, framed it similarly: the tie-up, he said, brings "generation and electrical infrastructure together into a single, scalable solution" — the kind of packaged offering that large data-center developers have been asking for rather than stitching together themselves.

Investors liked the sound of it. FCEL shares jumped roughly 11% in the days after the announcement, clawing back losses from a rougher moment days earlier, when the company priced an upsized $225 million stock offering at $21 a share — a 19% discount that had stoked dilution worries.

What's actually been signed

Strip away the enthusiasm and the agreement is a memorandum of understanding — non-binding, with no financial terms, no named projects, and no delivery dates disclosed. Siemens is not committing capital or guaranteeing volume; it is agreeing to explore, alongside FuelCell, whether the two companies' technology fits together well enough to sell as a package. That is worth something: Siemens does not attach its name to distributed-power ventures casually, and the collaboration lands as FuelCell says its pipeline of prospective data-center projects has grown to roughly 4 gigawatts, up from about 1.5 gigawatts earlier this year. But a pipeline is not a backlog, and an MoU is not a purchase order.

The execution record still bites

FuelCell Energy's history gives reason for caution. In its fiscal second quarter, the company's net loss more than doubled to $77.6 million, driven largely by a $42.6 million impairment tied to upgrading its long-running Groton, Connecticut project — a reminder that legacy installations have a way of costing money years after they're built. Revenue actually fell 5% year over year to $35.6 million, and the order backlog, at $1.14 billion, was down nearly 10% from a year earlier even as the sales pipeline swelled. The company's cash position is healthier, at $440.9 million, but a good chunk of that came from selling more shares — the same dilution that shareholders are already twitchy about.

On the product side, there's more tangible progress: FuelCell launched a standardized 12.5-megawatt "Energy Block" in March, its first unit built explicitly for data-center-scale customers, and it is spending $200 million to $275 million over the next two years expanding manufacturing capacity at its Torrington plant to support up to 500 megawatts of annual output.

The Siemens name attached to a slide deck is not the same as steel in the ground. The next test is whether the MoU produces a named pilot project with a committed customer — and whether FuelCell's fiscal third-quarter results, due in September, show the backlog turning back upward rather than continuing to shrink beneath a growing pipeline.
