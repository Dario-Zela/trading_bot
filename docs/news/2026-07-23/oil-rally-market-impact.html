Oil rally splits equities: FTSE gains, Shell trimmed, chip stocks retreat

The former London Stock Exchange building on Old Broad Street, City of London. — Kaihsu Tai / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
Oil's surge past $88 is pushing the FTSE toward record highs, but defensive positioning, a chip-stock retreat and a trimmed Shell valuation suggest investors are hedging, not celebrating.

In one sentence: Oil's surge past $88 a barrel is lifting the FTSE 100 toward record territory even as chip stocks retreat and analysts quietly trim Shell's valuation — a split verdict on whether the rally is durable.

London's blue-chip index closed at 10,716.97 on Wednesday, up 1.24% on the day and within touching distance of its 52-week high of 10,934.94, having briefly traded as high as 10,763.44 — its best intraday level in weeks. On the surface, this looks like unambiguous good news: cheaper borrowing costs after UK inflation cooled to 2.6% in June, oil majors and gold miners doing the heavy lifting, and a market apparently unbothered by the geopolitical noise driving crude higher. Look closer, though, and the composition of the rally tells a more cautious story — one of investors piling into the safest corners of the index rather than betting broadly on growth.

Oil, gold and dividends do the lifting

The FTSE 100's gains have been concentrated, not broad-based. BP led the risers as Brent crude climbed toward $88 a barrel, up roughly 20% from its lows earlier this month, while precious-metals miners and the index's traditional dividend payers — utilities, tobacco, insurers — outperformed the wider market. That combination is a tell: gold and dividend stocks are classic defensive plays, the kind investors reach for when they want upside exposure without giving up downside protection. A market truly convinced the good times were back would be rewarding cyclicals and growth names, not commodity producers and payout machines.

The timing matters. Thursday brings 268 UK and global earnings reports, a concentration of corporate results dense enough to move the index either way. 3i Group, Anglo American, BT Group and Centrica are among the UK names reporting alongside a wave of global results, and the FTSE's push toward record levels on the eve of that many earnings prints looks less like conviction and more like positioning ahead of a catalyst nobody wants to be caught wrong-footed by.

A blockade, not just a price move

The oil rally itself has a sharper edge than a simple supply-demand story. On July 20, Yemen's Houthi movement announced a naval blockade barring Saudi-linked vessels from the Red Sea, and within a day three tankers carrying Saudi crude bound for China and India turned away from the Bab al-Mandab strait, rerouting toward the Suez Canal instead. Brent, which had been drifting near $88, pushed toward a six-week high above $94 as traders priced in the risk that a closed southern gateway would remove the last workaround to the Strait of Hormuz.

"The threats of a naval blockade on Saudi Arabia by the Houthis are significant because they raise the risk of disruption to another major oil exporter," said Tim Waterer of KCM Trade. Goldman Sachs has gone further, warning Brent could clear $120 by the fourth quarter if disruptions through both Hormuz and the Red Sea persist. Not every desk agrees the move has legs: IG's Tony Sycamore noted that overnight talk of de-escalation and US-Iran contacts is already capping the upside, even as President Trump has publicly downplayed the prospect of near-term talks with Tehran. The result is a market pricing genuine tail risk into crude without full conviction that it will materialise — which is precisely the kind of uncertainty that tends to show up as a rotation into gold and dividend stocks rather than a straightforward rally.

Chipmakers price a different risk entirely

While London's oil-and-gold trade has pushed the FTSE higher, the same week has hit growth stocks from the opposite direction. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF has fallen roughly 20% from its June 22 peak, with Arm Holdings down more than 5% in a single session after Taiwan Semiconductor's higher capital-spending forecast overshadowed an otherwise solid quarterly report. The selloff spilled into Asia, where SK Hynix — still volatile since its US listing — dropped more than 11% in Seoul.

The unease is less about any single company's numbers than about whether the broader AI infrastructure build-out still justifies current valuations. J.P. Morgan has flagged that many chip names trade on multiples that assume the data-centre investment cycle keeps accelerating; if it merely plateaus, those multiples look stretched. The next test arrives fast: Alphabet reports Thursday as the first of the mega-cap hyperscalers, with Tesla close behind, and both are expected to guide on AI capital spending plans that chip investors will parse line by line.

Shell's valuation moves against the tide

If the broad pattern this week is oil bulls versus chip bears, Shell sits awkwardly in between. Despite Brent's advance on Middle East supply fears, analysts tracked by Simply Wall St have trimmed Shell's fair value estimate from £37.91 to £37.05 — a modest cut, but a telling one, since oil majors are ordinarily the most direct beneficiaries of a geopolitical supply shock. The revision reflects a lowered revenue growth assumption, down to 3.82% from 4.42%, and a split narrative: bulls point to cash-flow strength and the recent ARC Resources deal, while bears cite reserve life, thinning refining and chemicals margins, and ongoing litigation risk.

That an oil major's valuation is being nudged down even as crude prices climb suggests analysts see the current spike as a geopolitical premium rather than a durable repricing of fundamentals — a premium that tends to evaporate as fast as it appears once tensions ease.

The read-through

Taken together, the week's signals point the same direction: relief, not conviction. The FTSE's push toward record levels is real, but it is being carried by gold, dividends and oil majors — the assets investors buy when they want to participate in a rally while keeping one hand on the exit. Chip investors are doing the opposite, pulling back from the most AI-exposed names ahead of guidance that could go either way. And even inside the energy trade, analysts are unwilling to extend Shell credit for a rally they suspect is geopolitical rather than structural.

Thursday's 268 earnings reports, led by Alphabet and Tesla, will be the first real test of which read is correct. A batch of reassuring guidance on AI spending and steady UK corporate results could turn this week's defensive positioning into genuine risk appetite. Disappointing numbers, paired with any escalation in the Red Sea, would do the opposite — and expose how much of this rally has been hedging dressed up as optimism.
