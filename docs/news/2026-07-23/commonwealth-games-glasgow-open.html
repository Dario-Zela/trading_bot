Glasgow 2026 Commonwealth Games open in scaled-back format

The Sir Chris Hoy Velodrome, built for Glasgow's 2014 Commonwealth Games and reused — without a single new permanent venue added — for 2026. — Geograph / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Glasgow opens a stripped-down Commonwealth Games — 10 sports, no new venues, £150m budget — after Australia's Victoria abandoned hosting over cost blowouts.

Glasgow lit the OVO Hydro tonight for a Commonwealth Games that, twelve years ago, would have looked almost unrecognisably small. Ten sports. Four venues, all of them already standing before a single spade went into new ground. Roughly 3,000 athletes from 74 nations and territories, versus the 5,000-plus who came through Birmingham four years ago. The 2014 Glasgow Games — the last time this city hosted — ran to 18 sports and a budget of £543 million. This one is being delivered for something closer to £150 million.

The shrinkage was not the plan. It was the fallback.

From Victoria to Plan B

In April 2022, the Australian state of Victoria signed on to host 2026 with a regional-Games concept budgeted around A$2.6 billion. By July 2023 that number had climbed toward A$6–7 billion, and the state government walked away, judging the Games no longer worth the money. Victoria paid roughly A$380 million in compensation for pulling out — a bill still far smaller than the one it would have faced by finishing the job.

That left the Commonwealth Games Federation searching for a host with about two years' notice, an impossible timeline for the kind of new-build extravaganza the Games had come to mean since the 2000s. Glasgow, sitting on venues built for 2014 — the Sir Chris Hoy Velodrome, Tollcross swimming pool, the Commonwealth Arena, Scotstoun Stadium — was one of the only cities that could plausibly say yes. It agreed to host on the condition that no new permanent venues would be built and that most of the roughly £150 million cost would come from the Commonwealth Games Federation itself, Victoria's compensation payment, and commercial revenue, rather than fresh public money.

The cost of the compression shows up in the programme. Badminton, hockey, shooting, wrestling and squash — all fixtures of past Games, and in several cases genuine medal draws for South Asian and African nations — are absent. What remains is athletics, swimming, artistic gymnastics, track cycling, netball, weightlifting, boxing, judo, bowls and 3x3 basketball, alongside para events in four of those disciplines.

A test case, not a template

Commonwealth Sport is framing the smaller footprint as a deliberate "Games Reset" rather than a one-off rescue, hoping a cheaper, lower-commitment model will draw hosts who would otherwise never bid. There is early evidence it might: Ahmedabad has already been confirmed for 2030, promising a fuller 15–17 sport programme built around India's push toward eventually hosting an Olympics. But Glasgow's improvisation was itself proof of how thin the queue of willing hosts has become — touted 2030 bids from Canada, South Africa and Australia never materialised, and Glasgow was, by most accounts, the last city standing when the Federation needed one.

The pattern is not confined to the Commonwealth Games. Cities and regional governments across the developed world have grown warier of underwriting multi-week sporting events against open-ended cost overruns, a caution that tracks tighter public finances and more risk-averse infrastructure spending generally. Whether Glasgow's low-cost model proves a durable template or merely a stopgap will become clearer over the next four years, as Ahmedabad decides how much of its own ambition it is willing to fund — and as the Commonwealth Games Federation looks for a host willing to say yes to 2034 with rather more than two years' warning.
