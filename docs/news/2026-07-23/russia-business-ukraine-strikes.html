Russian businesses strained as Ukraine steps up attacks on logistics and retail

A Wildberries storefront in Russia — the retailer's warehouses have become a repeated target of Ukrainian drone strikes. — Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Ukraine has hit Wildberries, Russia's largest online retailer, twice in two weeks, with losses estimated up to $3bn as strikes shift toward Russian commerce.

Ukraine has spent much of the summer widening the aperture of its long-range strike campaign — from oil refineries and military depots to something more mundane and, for ordinary Russians, more disruptive: the warehouses that keep online orders moving. The clearest sign came in the second half of July, when Ukrainian drones twice struck facilities belonging to Wildberries, Russia's dominant e-commerce platform and the closest thing the country has to an Amazon.

The first wave, overnight on 18 July, hit warehouses in Tambov and near Moscow. The BBC reported eight people killed and 62 injured, with seven deaths and 25 injuries at the Tambov site alone, roughly 475km south-east of the Russian capital. A second round of strikes on 22 July hit further Wildberries logistics hubs in the Krasnodar and Stavropol regions, prompting the company to evacuate the sites. Russia's state anti-drone defences appear to be struggling to keep pace: this was, by several accounts, the deadliest and costliest single strike on Russian commercial infrastructure since the war began.

Why an online retailer

Wildberries is not an obvious military target on its face — it is best known for clothing, electronics and household goods delivered through tens of thousands of pickup points nationwide. But Ukrainian officials argue that framing understates its role in sustaining the war effort. Serhii Kuzan, chairman of the Ukrainian Security and Cooperation Centre, told the BBC that Wildberries had become a "vital component" of Russian military logistics, its marketplace used by volunteers and units to buy walkie-talkies, body armour and drone components, many of them sanctioned imports routed through resellers.

President Volodymyr Zelensky framed the strikes in similar terms, saying they hit "major logistics facilities" used to "supply sanctioned components for drone production and navigation equipment," and cast the campaign as retaliation for Russian strikes on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure. Whatever the precise military share of what moved through those warehouses, the economic fallout has landed well beyond it.

The bill comes due

Estimates of the damage vary widely but agree on the order of magnitude. Russian analysts cited by the Moscow Times put total losses at up to 100 billion rubles (roughly $1.3 billion). Sergei Semko of the retail-data firm Data Insight went further, estimating that fires at the two hardest-hit facilities destroyed goods worth at least 150 billion rubles ($1.9 billion), with total losses — including business interruption — potentially reaching 170–230 billion rubles ($2–3 billion). Rebuilding the damaged warehouses alone could cost up to 35.8 billion rubles ($457 million), a bill insurers are unlikely to cover in full.

The pain falls disproportionately on the small merchants who make up the bulk of Wildberries' seller base, many of whom stored inventory at the destroyed sites with little insurance and no alternative distribution network to fall back on. Founder Tatyana Kim confirmed operations at the Krasnodar and Nevinnomyssk centres had been suspended.

A strategy shift, not a one-off

The Wildberries strikes sit alongside a broader pattern this year: Ukrainian drones have also pushed deep into Russia's refining sector, with strikes reportedly idling close to 40% of refinery capacity at various points. Analysts increasingly read this as a deliberate pivot. With the front line largely static, Kyiv appears to be betting that degrading the economic and logistical systems propping up the war — inflation, consumer supply, insurance costs, business confidence — does more strategic work than incremental territorial gains.

For Moscow, the exposure is awkward precisely because these are civilian-facing firms with civilian-facing constituencies: sellers, delivery workers, shoppers. Sustained disruption to retail and logistics compounds pressure already building from sanctions and a slowing economy, feeding into the kind of inflation and currency volatility that Russian officials have been trying to manage all year.

The next test is whether the campaign continues at this tempo or was a concentrated summer push. Wildberries' own repair and rerouting timeline, and whether further strikes hit other retail or logistics operators, will show whether this is now a standing feature of the war rather than an isolated escalation.
