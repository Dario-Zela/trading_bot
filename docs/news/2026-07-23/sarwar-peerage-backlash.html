Scottish Labour rivals brand Sarwar a 'chancer' over peerage and ministerial job

Anas Sarwar, who has resigned as Scottish Labour leader to take a peerage and a ministerial post in Andy Burnham's government. — Scottish Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Anas Sarwar quit as Scottish Labour leader for a peerage and a Burnham government job, drawing 'chancer' jibes just weeks after Holyrood's worst-ever result for the party.

Seventy-six days. That is how long it took Anas Sarwar to go from asking Scots to elect him First Minister to accepting a seat in the House of Lords he once vowed to abolish.

Sarwar resigned as Scottish Labour leader this week, confirming he will take a peerage and join Prime Minister Andy Burnham's government as a minister in the newly expanded Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade. Because MSPs are barred from holding a Holyrood seat alongside a Westminster or Lords post, he must also give up his Glasgow list seat — a resignation that hands his place to Monica Lennon, the left-wing rival he beat to the leadership back in 2021.

From First Minister hopeful to minister

The timing is what stings. In May, Scottish Labour recorded its worst-ever Holyrood result, finishing tied for second place with Reform UK on 17 seats apiece as the SNP won a fifth consecutive term with 58 seats and the Scottish Greens took a record 15. Sarwar had spent the campaign promising to become First Minister; asked directly in the run-up to polling day whether he would take a peerage, he said: "No, I'm focused on being First Minister of this great country." Days after the defeat he insisted he was "hungry for the fight ahead" and wanted to "play my full part."

That part, it turns out, was in London. Burnham's office is expected to confirm the ministerial appointment formally, with Sarwar taking a business brief in a department reshuffled since Labour's UK election win.

'Chancer'

The reaction from Holyrood's other parties has been blunt. Scottish Green co-leader Gillian Mackay led the charge, telling reporters Sarwar had "gone from promising to scrap the House of Lords to taking a seat in it," and asking: "If he cannot stand by his most basic political principles when power is on offer, why should anyone believe a word he says?" Reform UK Scotland's deputy leader, Thomas Kerr, pressed for clarity on when Burnham first raised the offer — and whether it was connected to Sarwar's earlier calls for Keir Starmer to quit as UK Labour leader. Scottish Conservative deputy leader Rachael Hamilton accused him of abandoning the voters he'd asked to back him for First Minister. Even inside his own party the mood was sour: one member of Scottish Labour's ruling executive committee told The National he had been "a failure as leader," while another called the Lords move "shameful." Only Scottish Liberal Democrat leader Alex Cole-Hamilton offered anything like sympathy, calling Sarwar a "friend" and praising his record on hospital safety.

What it signals

Strip away the personal barbs and the episode reads as a small but telling data point on how Burnham's coalition is managing its own failures. A leader who delivered the party's worst devolution-era result at Holyrood is not being sidelined — he is being promoted into government within weeks of the count. For a party still trying to explain a bruising defeat to its base, elevating the man responsible for it undercuts any claim that Holyrood setbacks carry consequences at Westminster. It also leaves Scottish Labour to sort out a leadership contest — Lennon, Michael Marra and Joe Fagan are among those being floated — while the row over Sarwar's own conduct is still running.

A new Scottish Labour leader is expected to be chosen in the coming weeks, and Burnham's government must still confirm Sarwar's ministerial title formally. Whether voters read this as shrewd talent management or reward for failure will likely shape how Scottish Labour's successor pitches the party's recovery.
