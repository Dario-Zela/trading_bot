Senate crypto market-structure bill stalls again on stablecoin-yield dispute

Senator Cynthia Lummis, the Wyoming Republican leading the CLARITY Act through the Senate. — U.S. Senate Photographic Studio / John Klemmer, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The CLARITY Act stalls again over stablecoin-yield rules, with no cloture vote set before August recess and passage odds sliding toward September at earliest.

The Senate's attempt to write the first comprehensive federal rulebook for crypto markets has stalled for a second time this year, and with the chamber breaking for its August recess on August 7, the bill's authors are running out of runway before the fight resumes in September at the earliest.

The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act — known to nearly everyone in Washington and on trading desks simply as the CLARITY Act — would hand the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and the Securities and Exchange Commission a clear division of labor over digital assets, something exchanges have wanted since the SEC spent the early 2020s regulating the industry through enforcement actions rather than rules. The bill cleared the Senate Banking Committee in May by a 15-9 vote. It has not had a floor vote since. Senate Majority Leader John Thune has not filed a cloture motion, the procedural step required to bring the bill up at all, and aides briefed on the timeline say a filing after July 25 would leave too little floor time to clear the two cloture votes the bill needs before recess.

The yield fight

The proximate obstacle is stablecoin yield — specifically, whether crypto exchanges can keep paying users a return on the dollars they hold in stablecoins without running afoul of last year's GENIUS Act, which bars stablecoin issuers from paying interest. Coinbase collects roughly $1.35 billion a year in what it calls USDC "rewards," paid not by the stablecoin issuer but by the exchange itself. The American Banking Association argues the CLARITY Act's current text opens a loophole letting platforms replicate bank-style interest payments while sidestepping bank-style capital and deposit-insurance requirements — deposit economics migrating out of the regulated banking system, in the trade group's telling. Crypto platforms counter that the rewards are a product feature, not interest, and that redefining them as such retroactively would gut a business line built in good faith under existing law. Neither side has moved, and the standoff has proven durable enough to survive several rounds of staff-level compromise language since the spring.

The ethics rider

Stablecoin yield is not the only thing holding up the bill, but it has outlasted a second dispute that looked, as recently as this week, like the harder of the two to solve. Senator Cynthia Lummis, the Wyoming Republican who has led the bill since introducing an earlier version in 2022, released revised text this week that would bar the president, vice president, members of Congress, federal judges and their spouses from issuing or sponsoring digital assets for pay while in office, with the restriction sunsetting on January 20, 2029. The provision is a direct response to the Trump family's memecoin and stablecoin ventures, and its inclusion drew Senator Kirsten Gillibrand, a New York Democrat whose vote is one of the seven to nine Democratic votes Republicans need to reach the 60-vote threshold, back into active negotiation with Lummis and Senator Bernie Moreno. Progressive groups have since accused Gillibrand of settling for too narrow a fix, arguing that a ban excluding adult children and other relatives leaves an obvious workaround. Prediction markets had odds of a pre-recess floor vote above 72% as of Tuesday, largely on news that the ethics language had firmed up — odds that assume the stablecoin-yield dispute gets resolved too, which as of Wednesday it had not.

What it means for markets

A second stall carries different weight than the first. Missing the July 4 target left room to argue the bill was merely delayed; missing August pushes any floor vote into a September session compressed by appropriations work and, not long after, the start of the 2026 midterm campaign season in earnest. Congressional aides and lobbyists tracking the bill increasingly frame September as the last realistic window before election-year politics make a bipartisan financial-services bill too risky for Democrats facing primaries. For exchanges and trading platforms, the practical effect of continued delay is neutral in the near term — the SEC and CFTC have begun filling some of the gap through interpretive guidance and memoranda of understanding rather than waiting on Congress — but it extends the period in which market-structure rules rest on regulatory discretion rather than statute, discretion that changes with each administration.

The next marker is Thune's decision on whether to file cloture before July 25; absent that filing, the CLARITY Act's fate for 2026 effectively rests on whatever the Senate can assemble in September.
