China's economy grows just 4.3% in Q2, weakest pace in three years

Shanghai's Pudong skyline — China's export and manufacturing engines are running hot even as domestic investment stalls. — David Zhang / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
China's growth slumped to 4.3% in Q2 as fixed-asset investment fell 5.7%, exposing an investment collapse that stimulus may struggle to fix even as consumers kept spending.

China's economy grew 4.3% in the second quarter from a year earlier, the government said this week — the weakest pace since the depths of the pandemic-era slowdown in late 2022, and well short of both the 4.5% economists had penciled in and Beijing's own 4.5-5% annual target. The headline number tells only part of the story. Beneath it sits a much sharper split: fixed-asset investment collapsed 5.7% in the first half of the year, while retail sales — the gauge of ordinary household spending — actually held its ground, edging up 1% in June after a rare dip in May.

That gap matters more than the growth miss itself. A slowdown driven by weak consumers is a demand problem, and demand problems respond, however imperfectly, to stimulus checks and rate cuts. A slowdown driven by an investment strike is something else — a signal that the people who allocate capital in China, from property developers to local government financing vehicles to private manufacturers, have collectively decided not to build.

Where the growth went missing

The investment numbers, released alongside the GDP print, show where the damage concentrated. Real estate investment plunged 18% in the first half, extending a slump that has run since 2021 and shows no sign of finding a floor — new commercial property sales were down 13.6%, and cement output fell 8%, a rough proxy for how little concrete is actually being poured. Infrastructure investment slid 2.4%. Manufacturing investment dropped 1.2%. Private-sector investment, arguably the most telling of the lot, fell 8.5% year-to-date, worsening from a 7.1% decline through May — evidence that the pullback is accelerating, not stabilizing.

Set against that, industrial output actually beat forecasts, rising 5.3% in June, and exports jumped roughly 17.6%, powered by AI hardware and electric vehicles finding buyers abroad. China's factories are working; its investors are not spending. That divergence — an export and manufacturing engine running hot while the domestic capital-formation side of the economy seizes up — is what economists mean when they describe the economy as increasingly unbalanced.

Part of the explanation is deliberate policy. Beijing has spent much of the past two years cracking down on the kind of debt-fueled, unproductive local-government infrastructure spending that inflated construction activity in the 2010s and left many provinces holding underused roads, bridges and industrial parks — projects authorities now blame for feeding industrial overcapacity and the deflationary price wars squeezing manufacturers' margins. Stricter scrutiny of local capital expenditure has throttled the investment tap on purpose. The property slump, by contrast, is not by design: developers that defaulted on debt in the 2021-23 crisis have never fully returned to building, and buyers remain wary of a market where completed-but-unsold inventory keeps growing.

A policy toolkit with the edges filed off

The soft data has intensified calls, from within China's own policy establishment, for a more forceful response. Premier Li Qiang has pressed for stronger counter-cyclical measures, and attention has swung to the Communist Party's Politburo meeting at the end of July, the venue where the leadership typically signals its economic priorities for the second half.

Most economists tracking the meeting expect rhetoric to shift, not the toolkit. Goldman Sachs economist Lisheng Wang said a broad, large-scale stimulus package looked unlikely even as the Politburo steps up its language on policy easing. Standard Chartered economists argued the meeting would prioritize accelerating the fiscal spending Beijing has already budgeted rather than announcing anything new, with monetary policy playing a supporting role at most. One government adviser told Reuters the most probable move is narrower still: allowing local governments to "moderately frontload" fourth-quarter debt quotas into the third quarter, so already-approved infrastructure projects simply get built sooner.

That restraint is not simply caution. China's policymakers are threading a needle: reviving investment without reviving the debt-driven overbuilding they spent two years trying to unwind, and without undercutting a separate campaign to fight deflation by curbing exactly the kind of excess industrial capacity that unrestrained investment tends to produce. A rate cut or a large bond-financed spending package would be the conventional response to a growth miss this size. Beijing's central bank has instead held its policy rate frozen for 14 months, the longest such stretch in the current cycle, wary of further pressuring the yuan or reinflating asset bubbles it has only recently begun to deflate.

What flows through to markets

The People's Bank of China nudged its yuan reference rate only marginally weaker after the data landed, a sign the currency response to the growth miss was contained rather than disorderly. Commodity markets showed a similar shrug on the day, with attention already diverted toward the oil-price effects of the widening Middle East conflict — crude's climb on Iran-related supply fears matters more for Chinese import costs and inflation than a quarter-point GDP miss does for global demand.

The more durable transmission channel runs through expectations rather than a single day's price action. China remains the marginal buyer for industrial metals, energy and a wide swath of emerging-market exports; a construction and property sector still shrinking by double digits keeps a lid on demand for steel, cement inputs and copper even as AI-linked manufacturing exports pick up some of the slack. Investors in copper, iron ore and industrial equities will be parsing every incremental China data point for confirmation that the investment slide is bottoming — or evidence it is not.

The next real test lands at the Politburo meeting in the closing days of July, when Beijing either signals it is prepared to accept a slower, more consumption-led growth path or moves — modestly, if the economists quoted above are right — to arrest the investment slide before it drags second-half growth further from target. August's investment and property-sales figures, the first full month after any Politburo guidance, will show whether frontloaded infrastructure spending is enough to offset a real-estate sector still contracting at double-digit rates.
