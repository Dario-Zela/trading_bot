Burnham rebukes ministers over prisoner-release 'joke'

Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street, days into his new coalition government. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham rebuked ministers Streeting and Mahmood over a hot-mic joke on prisoner releases, as PC Andrew Harper's widow called the policy "deplorable."

Six days into his premiership, Andy Burnham has had to publicly discipline two of his most senior ministers over a joke — and the row has collided with a grieving widow's plea that the government not play politics with her husband's killers.

The episode began in the garden of Downing Street, in the minutes after the first full meeting of Burnham's new cabinet. A hot microphone caught Defence Secretary Wes Streeting turning to Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood and gesturing towards Alex Norris, the newly appointed Justice Secretary. "You know he's the enemy now he's at justice, Shabana?" Streeting said. "He wants to let them all out." Mahmood, laughing, replied: "No he's not."

A joke with a history

The line was not spontaneous. Streeting later said it echoed a decades-old exchange between Conservative prime minister Theresa May and her chancellor-turned-justice-secretary Kenneth Clarke, who used to josh that she "locked 'em up" while he "let 'em out." The reference was pointed for a reason: Mahmood herself, as the previous justice secretary, oversaw the early-release scheme that let thousands of prisoners out after serving 40 percent of their sentences, a measure introduced to relieve overcrowded jails.

Both ministers apologised within hours. Streeting said there had been "no ill intent" and that he was "sorry for any offence inadvertently caused by this moment of levity." Mahmood offered a similar line, adding pointed criticism of the "vandalism inflicted on our justice system by the last Conservative government" — an attempt to redirect blame for the overcrowding crisis that made the release scheme necessary in the first place.

Burnham was not appeased. Asked directly whether the joke was acceptable, he said: "No, it's not, and certainly that's been acknowledged, and apologies have been made." For a prime minister barely a week into the job, publicly slapping down his defence and home secretaries is an unusual way to open a premiership — and a signal, deliberate or not, that discipline on sensitive justice policy will be enforced from the top.

A widow's rebuke

The timing made the joke considerably harder to laugh off. Lissie Harper, the widow of PC Andrew Harper — dragged to his death by a car while responding to a quad-bike theft near Reading in 2019 — has spent years fighting to keep her husband's killers behind bars. Two of the three men convicted over his death, Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers, were 17 at the time and received 13-year sentences for manslaughter; under the early-release rules, the Ministry of Justice has indicated they could be freed after serving half their terms. The driver, Henry Long, was sentenced to 16 years and remains ineligible for the scheme.

"It has taken me years to rebuild a life that was torn apart in a matter of moments," Harper said. "It is deplorable to me that we have reached a point where releasing prisoners early is even considered acceptable. They tell victims their suffering matters less, and offenders that the punishment handed down by our courts is no longer what they will actually serve." She added that politicians were "making decisions from behind a desk" with "real and lasting consequences" for families like hers.

Burnham has since said he will "review the whole of this policy" alongside Norris before any further releases proceed in September, while confirming Long will not benefit from early release regardless of the outcome. The Ministry of Justice, for its part, pointed to prison-building at what it called the fastest rate since the Victorian era as evidence the overcrowding pressure behind the scheme is being addressed.

What it exposes

Neither the joke nor the apologies changes the substance of the policy. But the sequence — a light-hearted jibe about a scheme that keeps deciding whether a police officer's killers walk free early, followed swiftly by that killer's widow saying she feels dismissed — has done real damage to a government trying to project competence in its first fortnight. It has also surfaced a genuine tension inside cabinet: Mahmood built the release scheme to manage a prisons crisis, and Norris now has to defend, review or unwind it under a spotlight neither he nor Burnham chose.

The review Burnham has promised is the next marker to watch, expected to conclude before the September release cohort is decided. Whether it produces exemptions for cases like Harper's, or simply reaffirms the existing thresholds, will say more about the durability of this apology than anything said in the Downing Street garden.
