Revolut secondary sale values fintech at $115bn, up 53% since November

Nik Storonsky, Revolut's founder and chief executive, whose stake is now worth more than $36bn on paper. — Harry Murphy / Web Summit via Sportsfile, Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Revolut's employee share sale prices the fintech at $115bn, up 53% since November, with Nvidia's venture arm among the buyers.

London banking-app maker Revolut has just made 10-year-old employees richer than most people get in a career, at least on paper. A fresh secondary share sale — the third in as many years — has priced the company at $115bn, up 53% from the $75bn it fetched in a similar sale in November 2025, and more than double the $45bn valuation it carried as recently as August 2024.

The transaction, confirmed by the company this week and first reported by Bloomberg and the Wall Street Journal, is a secondary sale rather than a capital raise: existing shareholders, chiefly staff who have accumulated equity, sell stock to a syndicate of institutional buyers at $2,017 a share. Revolut itself doesn't receive a cent of new money. What it gets instead is a price — one that now makes it, on paper, more valuable than Barclays' roughly $95bn market capitalisation and comfortably ahead of Stripe as the world's most valuable private fintech.

Who's buying, and why it matters that Nvidia is among them

The buyer list reads like a rerun of November's round, with one addition worth dwelling on. Coatue, Greenoaks, Dragoneer and Fidelity Management & Research returned as lead investors, joined by Andreessen Horowitz, Franklin Templeton and T. Rowe Price. Nvidia's corporate venture arm, NVentures, also took part — its second consecutive appearance in a Revolut round, after first buying in during November. Regulatory filings suggest NVentures holds roughly 141,000 Revolut shares, a stake worth in the region of $196m at the time it was built.

A chipmaker's venture desk backing a European digital bank is a small data point, but a telling one: it says the capital chasing fintech-as-platform bets isn't only generalist growth funds anymore, it's strategic money from adjacent industries with balance sheets swollen by the AI buildout. Founder and chief executive Nik Storonsky, who holds a stake historically put at just under a third of the company, would see that position valued at more than $36bn at the new price — before any of it is actually liquid.

The numbers behind the multiple, and the regulatory ledger

The valuation jump isn't happening in a vacuum. Revolut reported $6bn in revenue for 2025, up 46%, and $2.3bn in pre-tax profit, up 57% — figures that give the multiple something to stand on beyond investor enthusiasm. The company now serves more than 75 million customers across roughly 100 countries.

On regulation, the picture is more resolved than the triage suggested, but not finished. Revolut spent four years trying to convert a restricted UK banking licence into a full one, with the Prudential Regulation Authority and Financial Conduct Authority repeatedly flagging whether its risk controls could keep pace with expansion into dozens of countries at once. The PRA lifted those restrictions in March, making Revolut Bank UK a fully authorised lender in its home market for the first time. Days before this latest valuation, Australia's prudential regulator granted Revolut a full deposit-taking licence, the first time any global fintech has cleared that bar there. A US national bank charter application, filed with the OCC and FDIC in March, is still pending — Storonsky has floated a four-month target for approval, which would put a decision around now, though nothing has landed yet.

The contrast with Klarna is instructive. The Swedish buy-now-pay-later firm listed in New York last year and has since fallen more than 61% from its offer price, a reminder that public markets have been considerably less forgiving of fintech growth stories than the private buyers still bidding up Revolut's cap table. Storonsky has said an IPO is at least two years off, not before 2028, with internal chatter reportedly pointing to a target valuation near $200bn whenever it comes.

What the price doesn't tell you

Secondary sales are a useful signal, not a verdict. A relatively small slice of Revolut's cap table changed hands at $115bn — nowhere near the scrutiny a public offering would invite, and nowhere near enough volume to prove genuine market depth at that price. What it does confirm is that a specific pool of late-stage capital, now including strategic money from outside fintech entirely, still wants exposure to European neobank scale even as IPO markets stay thin and rivals like Klarna struggle post-listing. The next real test of that price is the US charter decision, and, further out, whatever valuation the market actually assigns once Revolut's shares trade freely rather than by invitation.
