UK inflation eases to 2.6% as BoE holds rate ahead of 30 July decision

The Bank of England's Threadneedle Street headquarters, which held Bank Rate at 3.75% ahead of its 30 July decision. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK CPI fell to 2.6% in June on cheaper fuel, but the BoE held rates 7-2 amid warnings that Ofgem's 13% energy bill rise will push inflation back toward 3% this year.

UK inflation cooled more than expected in June, with the Office for National Statistics reporting the Consumer Prices Index rose 2.6% in the year to the month, down from 2.8% in May and below the 2.7% economists had pencilled in. It is the lowest annual reading since March 2025, and on the surface it hands the Bank of England some breathing room a week before its next rate decision. Underneath, the picture is less settled: the Bank itself has already told markets to expect inflation to reaccelerate through the second half of the year, and this week it held Bank Rate at 3.75% for a second straight meeting, with two of nine committee members pushing to raise it rather than cut.

A cooler June, mostly on fuel

The deceleration was driven overwhelmingly by transport. The transport division's annual rate fell to 5.7% from 6.8%, with diesel prices dropping 10.7 pence a litre between May and June as petrol and diesel costs eased from their spring highs. Food and non-alcoholic drinks added to the relief: annual food inflation slowed to 1.7%, its weakest pace since August 2024, with monthly prices actually falling 0.2% as chocolate, margarine and beef all got cheaper. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, held flat at 2.6%, and services inflation — the measure the Bank watches most closely for signs of domestic price pressure — eased only marginally, to 3.6% from 3.7%.

That last figure is the tell. A one-tenth easing in services inflation is not the kind of move that settles an argument at the Bank, where policymakers have spent much of the year weighing sticky wage-driven services costs against a slowing economy. June's headline number flatters the trend more than the underlying data does.

Why the relief looks temporary

Economists were near-unanimous that June marks a low point rather than a turn. Yael Selfin, chief economist at KPMG, said the firm expects "June to mark the trough, with inflation set to edge up from July as higher household energy bills take effect coinciding with the rebound in fuel prices." The trigger is already locked in: Ofgem's energy price cap rose 13% from 1 July, lifting the typical annual dual-fuel bill from £1,641 to £1,862 — a roughly £18-a-month increase driven by higher wholesale gas prices tied to disruption in Middle East energy supplies. Gas bills alone are rising around 24%, against a smaller 5% increase for electricity.

The National Institute of Economic and Social Research expects inflation to resume climbing through the second half of the year for the same reason. And the Bank's own Monetary Policy Report is more explicit still, projecting CPI at "a little under 3%" in the third quarter and "a little over 3.25%" in the fourth — a full percentage point above target, reached not through a dramatic shock but through energy costs working their way back into the index just as June's fuel-driven dip fades out.

Holding pattern at Threadneedle Street

Against that backdrop, the Monetary Policy Committee's decision to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% at its June meeting reads less like caution and more like a genuine split. The vote was 7–2, with external member Megan Greene and chief economist Huw Pill both voting to raise the rate a quarter-point to 4%, arguing the disinflation narrative was too fragile to bank on. That is double the hawkish dissent recorded in April, and it puts the Bank in the unusual position of debating a hike at the same meeting where headline inflation was falling — a reminder that the MPC is reacting to where prices are going, not where they've been.

The Bank paired the rate hold with confirmation of its Q3 gilt sales schedule, running from 20 July to the week of 14 September: three auctions of short-maturity gilts (3-7 year residual maturity) worth £725 million each, and two medium-maturity auctions (7-20 years) at £600 million each, part of the ongoing unwind of the Asset Purchase Facility. It is routine housekeeping, but it signals a central bank managing its balance sheet on the assumption that policy stays roughly where it is for now, rather than preparing for an imminent pivot in either direction.

What to watch

The next test comes fast: the MPC's rate decision lands 30 July, accompanied by a full Monetary Policy Report and a press conference with Governor Andrew Bailey. Markets are not pricing a rate change, but the size and composition of the vote — whether Pill and Greene's hawkish camp gains or loses ground — will say more about the path ahead than the headline hold itself. Beyond that, the next CPI print, due in mid-August, will be the first to capture a full month of the higher Ofgem cap, and will show whether June's trough forecast holds up against the energy and food pressures already working their way through the pipeline. For sterling and gilt markets, the message from this data cycle is that the disinflation story is not being abandoned, but it is no longer being taken on trust.
