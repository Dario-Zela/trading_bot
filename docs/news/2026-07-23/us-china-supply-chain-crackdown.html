US tightens screws on Chinese-linked supply chains: FCC device ban, Senate targets Mercedes

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, who said the new rule would "fully close the component part loophole" for Chinese-made hardware. — Federal Communications Commission
In one sentence
The FCC banned devices with Chinese chip components and a Senate panel advanced a bill that could bar Mercedes-Benz from the US over Chinese ownership ties.

Two Washington moves this week, on different tracks but the same trajectory, tell device makers and automakers the same thing: the era of quietly routing around China restrictions is ending. On Wednesday the Federal Communications Commission voted to close what its chairman called the "component part loophole," barring the sale of any US device that contains logic-bearing hardware from blacklisted Chinese firms — even when the device itself is stamped with an American or European brand. Hours earlier, the Senate Commerce Committee voted unanimously to advance the Connected Vehicle Security Act, a bill that could bar Mercedes-Benz from the US market over a Chinese ownership stake most drivers have never heard of. Neither measure is final law. Both are aimed at the same target: supply chains that touch China, wherever they touch it.

Closing the loophole

The FCC has maintained a "Covered List" of Chinese firms — Huawei and ZTE foremost among them — since 2019, and since 2022 those companies have been barred from getting new equipment authorizations under their own names. But the rule had a gap large enough to drive a supply chain through: a router assembled in Vietnam, a security camera sold under a European label, a smart-home sensor built by a third-party contractor could still win FCC authorization and reach American retail shelves, provided the finished product carried someone else's brand — even if its core processor was HiSilicon silicon, Huawei's in-house chip design arm.

Chairman Brendan Carr said the new rule would "fully close the component part loophole," and the text does so bluntly: any device containing Huawei- or ZTE-made logic-bearing hardware — the chips that actually process data, not passive components like resistors — is now barred from sale in the US, regardless of whose name is on the box. It is the latest in a run of escalations from Carr's FCC, which last month banned imports of equipment from another cluster of Chinese manufacturers and this week separately proposed banning most military-grade drones. Each step moves the line further from "don't buy Huawei" toward "prove your entire bill of materials is clean."

That is the part with teeth for device makers. A finished-goods ban is something compliance teams can screen for at the SKU level. A component-level ban means auditing every sub-supplier's chip sourcing, and for consumer electronics — where component sourcing shifts between contract manufacturers season to season — that audit is neither quick nor free. The rule does not specify a phase-in period for products already in the sales channel, which leaves manufacturers guessing at how much existing inventory becomes unsellable overnight versus how much gets a wind-down window.

Mercedes in the crosshairs

The Senate's move reaches into a sector the China restrictions had mostly left alone: passenger cars. The Connected Vehicle Security Act, introduced by Sen. Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) and Sen. Elissa Slotkin (D-Michigan), would bar the import and sale of any connected vehicle — one equipped with wireless technology — built by an automaker more than 15% owned by Chinese, Russian, Iranian or North Korean interests, starting in January 2027.

That threshold was not written with Mercedes-Benz specifically in mind, but the company sits almost exactly on top of it. China's state-owned BAIC Motor holds a 9.98% stake in Mercedes — its single largest shareholder — while Geely founder Li Shufu controls another 9.69% through an investment vehicle. Combined, that is roughly 19.67% Chinese ownership, comfortably over the bill's line. The legislation gives Mercedes until 2030 and the option to apply for waivers, but the company is lobbying hard to have the threshold raised to 25%, which would exempt it outright. Autos Drive America, the lobbying group representing foreign automakers including Mercedes, has said it backs the "overall goal of this legislation" while warning against "unintended consequences that could create challenges for US manufacturing" — diplomatic language for asking Congress to redraw the line around its member.

The bill's supply-chain reach goes further than ownership tests: it also bans imports of battery systems from China's CATL that contain restricted electronics, pulling the battery giant that supplies much of the Western EV industry into the same net. The Commerce Committee's unanimous vote — a rarity for anything touching trade with China this Congress — and UAW president Shawn Fain's endorsement, calling it a matter of "common sense guardrails," suggest the bill has real momentum. Whether it survives contact with the full Senate floor, where automakers' lobbying and questions about retaliation against US carmakers in China are likely to surface, is a separate question.

The reshoring bill comes due

What connects a telecom rule about chip provenance and a Senate bill about foreign-automaker ownership is the enforcement logic both now share: it is no longer enough to avoid doing business with a company on a blacklist. Firms now have to prove the absence of a connection, several layers down their supply chain or capitalization table. That is a much harder — and more expensive — thing to demonstrate than simply not signing a contract with Huawei.

For device makers, the FCC rule means near-term costs before any benefit: re-qualifying alternative chip suppliers, re-testing products, and in some cases writing off inventory built on now-prohibited components — all before a single unit of "reshored" capacity comes online to replace it. Domestic and allied-nation chip suppliers stand to gain the business US and Vietnamese-assembled brands are being forced to redirect, but that shift takes quarters, not weeks. For Mercedes, the exposure is more binary: comply, restructure ownership, win a waiver, or lose access to its most profitable market, at a moment when its China sales are already sliding and it can least afford to lose the other side of the business too.

Neither the FCC nor the Senate has published the kind of granular timeline that would let a manufacturer model exact costs — the FCC order does not detail a grace period for existing inventory, and the Connected Vehicle Security Act still has to clear the full Senate and a companion House bill before Mercedes' 2030 deadline becomes binding. Both are worth tracking for the same reason: implementation details, not the headline vote, will determine whether this is a costly compliance exercise or an existential one.

The next marker is procedural but consequential — whether the Connected Vehicle Security Act gets floor time before the current Congress adjourns, and whether the FCC publishes implementation guidance narrowing (or widening) which component categories count as "logic-bearing hardware." Both determine how fast, and how expensively, the decoupling actually happens.
