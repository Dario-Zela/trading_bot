Labour's poll lead surges as Farage faces HMRC probe over £5m gift

Nigel Farage faces an HMRC referral over a £5m gift from crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne, as Reform UK's poll lead evaporates. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Tories have asked HMRC to tax Farage's £5m Harborne gift as disguised pay, as Labour's Burnham-led surge erases Reform's 18-month poll lead.

In one sentence: The Conservatives have asked HMRC to probe whether Nigel Farage's £5m gift should be taxed as pay, just as Labour's poll numbers surge past Reform UK for the first time in 18 months.

Nigel Farage has spent two decades cultivating an image as the insurgent nobody in Westminster can touch. This week, two separate institutions are testing that proposition at once — one with a polling sample, the other with a tax code.

On Tuesday, Conservative Party chairman Kevin Hollinrake wrote to HM Revenue and Customs asking it to examine whether a £5m payment Farage received from the Thailand-based crypto billionaire Christopher Harborne should be reclassified as taxable earnings rather than a personal gift. Two days later, Deltapoll put Labour level with Reform UK at 28% apiece — the first time in more than a year that Farage's party has not held a clear lead, and an eight-point jump for Labour in a single month. The two stories share a hinge: both trace back to the same £5m payment, and both raise the same question — whether the movement that has spent two years riding a wave of anti-establishment sentiment is now being undone by exactly the kind of scrutiny it long escaped.

A gift, or a wage in disguise?

The £5m at issue was one instalment of what Harborne has given Reform UK — he has donated more than £22m to the party since 2019, including a record £9m single gift last August, making him responsible for roughly two-thirds of everything Reform has raised. Most of that money has gone through the party's accounts as registered donations. This payment did not; it went to Farage personally, and was not declared as party income.

The Guardian reported on 15 July that three sources told its reporters Farage informed senior Reform figures, in March 2024, that he would need "a million a year" to cover lost earnings — chiefly his roughly £98,000-a-month GB News salary — if he stood for Parliament and resumed leadership of the party through the 2024 and prospective 2029 elections. On that arithmetic, five years of compensation comes to £5m. Weeks after those conversations, Harborne's gift arrived.

Dan Neidle, the tax lawyer who runs Tax Policy Associates and has a track record of flagging loan-charge and avoidance schemes to HMRC, says that timeline matters enormously. If the money was, in substance, a reward for taking up or continuing in the Reform leadership role, HMRC's "disguised remuneration" rules would likely apply — treating it as earnings rather than a gift regardless of how the paperwork describes it. Neidle's estimate is that this would leave Reform 2025 Ltd, the party's legal entity, liable for roughly £3m in PAYE income tax and National Insurance.

Hollinrake's letter asks HMRC to determine "whether the payment falls within the disguised remuneration regime or otherwise constitutes taxable earnings arising from an office, employment or political position."

Farage's camp rejects the framing outright. A Reform spokesperson called the Guardian's original report "fake and wholly incorrect," and lawyers for Harborne say he has previously stated the money was given with no expectation of anything in return and without any anticipation that Farage would return to the party leadership. Robert Jenrick has said Farage regards the whole affair as a "stitch-up," and Reform's Treasury spokesman has likened the HMRC referral to a "kangaroo court." None of that changes what HMRC will actually have to weigh: not what the money was called, but what it was for.

The regulator as political weapon

What makes the Hollinrake letter notable isn't the tax question itself — HMRC fields disguised-remuneration queries routinely — but who is asking, and when. A governing-adjacent party formally invoking the tax authority against its most dangerous electoral rival is a fairly direct use of state machinery as a campaign instrument, and it lands at the precise moment Reform's polling advantage has evaporated. Hollinrake would say the two are unrelated: the facts came from Guardian reporting, not Conservative Central Office digging. But the effect is the same either way — a party that spent a decade complaining about donor scrutiny of its own funding gets to watch its chief rival face exactly that scrutiny, amplified by an official government referral rather than left to journalists alone.

It also exposes a genuine regulatory gap. Harborne's wealth sits substantially in crypto — he holds a stake of roughly 12% in Tether, the dollar-pegged stablecoin whose reserves and banking relationships have drawn repeated scrutiny from US regulators and reporters, and he built his fortune partly through AML Global, an aviation-fuel and cross-border payments business that a 2023 Wall Street Journal investigation linked to Tether's efforts to secure US banking access. Political-donation rules in the UK were not built with crypto-derived, offshore-domiciled fortunes in mind; they assume donors are more or less legible entities giving money that is more or less traceable. A £5m payment routed to an individual rather than a party, from a donor whose core wealth sits in a stablecoin issuer that has itself faced years of opacity questions, is close to a stress test of how much daylight that framework actually requires.

The Burnham bounce

The polling collapse is the more immediately visible consequence, even if it is not solely a consequence of the tax story. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July, replacing Keir Starmer after the party's internal upheaval, and Deltapoll's fieldwork from 17–20 July — Burnham's first days in Downing Street — found Labour at 28%, up eight points on a month earlier, level with Reform's unchanged 28% and ending a run of Reform leads that stretched back roughly a year and a half. The Conservatives sit a distant third on 18%.

Separating the two drivers is not straightforward, and pollsters are candid about that. Ipsos data gathered in the weeks before Burnham's elevation already showed him rated the most likely of any senior politician — ahead of Farage and Kemi Badenoch — to do a good job as prime minister, suggesting a chunk of Labour's rally would have arrived regardless of what happened to Farage. But the timing of Reform's stall is harder to wave away: the party's support was essentially flat through a month in which its leader was fielding questions he plainly did not want to answer. Commentators at Deltapoll and elsewhere have taken to describing it as "Reform fatigue" compounding the Burnham bounce, rather than being displaced by it — a governing party's new leader landing well, while its chief challenger absorbs a slow-burn scandal about the reliability of its own account of its money.

What comes next

HMRC does not confirm or deny individual investigations, so the timeline from here runs on its own institutional clock rather than the news cycle's — an inquiry into disguised remuneration is not something the tax authority will resolve or comment on within weeks. What is faster-moving is the politics: Reform faces the Glasgow Commonwealth Games and the summer parliamentary recess with its poll lead gone and its leader's finances under formal government scrutiny, at exactly the point a new Labour prime minister is trying to convert a favourable first fortnight into something durable. The next Deltapoll and Ipsos releases, likely in the first half of August, will show whether the tie at 28% was the trough of the Burnham bounce or the new baseline — and whether Farage's polling problem outlasts a single bad news cycle about a £5m gift.
