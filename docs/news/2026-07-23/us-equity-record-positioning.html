Record bearish bets and record S&P concentration flash contradictory signals

The New York Stock Exchange's trading floor — where record bearish positioning and record index concentration are now colliding. — Eduard Hueber / Asymptote Architecture, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Short interest against US stocks hit a record $2.13tn even as just 24 companies came to hold half the S&P 500's value — tighter than the dot-com peak.

Two numbers, released within days of each other in July, describe the same market from opposite directions. The first: short interest against US equities has climbed to roughly $2.13 trillion, an all-time high, with S&P 500 short interest at 3.79% of free float — the richest since S3 Partners began tracking the data in 2010, and well above levels seen in the 2008 crisis or the 2020 pandemic crash. The second: just 24 companies now account for more than half the S&P 500's total market capitalization, a level of concentration that exceeds even the dot-com bubble's 2000 peak, when it took about 32 stocks to reach the same threshold.

Both numbers describe an index sitting near record highs. The S&P 500 is up roughly 9.3% year-to-date and 18% since late March. That the rally coincides with record hedging and record narrowness is the story — not a contradiction so much as two symptoms of the same underlying condition.

The bears are getting louder, not quieter

According to S3 Partners, short interest in Russell 3000 names has hit 6.3% of float, also a record, while NYSE-wide short interest reached 9% of shares outstanding in late June, according to Reynolds Strategy — more than 2011's financial-crisis-era stress. "Short selling has increased and the breadth of names shorted has increased," S3's Ihor Dusaniwsky said. Joseph Saluzzi of Themis Trading pointed to the source of the anxiety directly: "Concerns about AI spending and huge moves in the semiconductor sector have increased investor skepticism."

The positioning is not evenly spread. The Magnificent Seven and chipmakers such as Micron and Broadcom carry the largest dollar-value short positions, a bet that the AI capital-expenditure cycle — and the earnings assumptions built on top of it — is due for a reckoning. Even SpaceX, still private, has attracted $25 billion in short exposure equal to 29% of its float. When the market wobbled 1.6% one week in July on renewed China-competition worries, those shorts posted mark-to-market gains near 28%.

Fewer stocks are doing more work

The concentration data explains why the hedges cluster where they do. Visual Capitalist's analysis, built on Bloomberg figures, shows the number of stocks required to represent half the S&P 500's value falling to 24 — driven overwhelmingly by Nvidia, Apple, Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet. That is a tighter cluster than at the height of dot-com euphoria, when broadening leadership eventually pulled the ratio back out. It means the index's headline level increasingly tracks the fortunes of a handful of balance sheets tied to one theme: whether AI infrastructure spending translates into earnings, or merely into depreciation.

For passive funds, this mechanically deepens exposure to the same handful of names every time index inflows arrive, regardless of valuation. For active managers, it explains why hedging is concentrated in the exact stocks propping up the headline number — betting against the engine rather than the market as a whole.

The two data points point toward the same fault line: an index whose gains rest on ever-fewer companies, watched by an ever-larger pool of money betting those companies have run ahead of what their spending will earn back. The next earnings season from the Magnificent Seven, and any sign of AI capex slowing, will be the test of which side of that divergence is right.
