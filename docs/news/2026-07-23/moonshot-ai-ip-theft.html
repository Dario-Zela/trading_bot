White House accuses China's Moonshot AI of stealing Anthropic's models

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said sanctions and an Entity List designation are "on the table" if IP-theft allegations against Moonshot AI are confirmed. — U.S. Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A White House official accused China's Moonshot AI of covertly copying Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 and using banned Nvidia chips in Thailand; Treasury threatened sanctions.

Washington's latest broadside in the AI cold war landed on a Wednesday briefing call, not a courtroom filing. Michael Kratsios, the White House's science and technology adviser, told reporters the administration has "information indicating" that Moonshot AI, the Beijing-based startup behind the buzzy Kimi K3 model, ran a covert operation to copy the innards of Anthropic's flagship Claude Fable 5 system — and did it partly using Nvidia server hardware that isn't supposed to be anywhere near China.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent followed within hours with the part that actually has teeth: sanctions and an Entity List designation, the trade blacklist that can cut a company off from US suppliers overnight, are "on the table." His line for the cameras — "open source is not open season on American IP" — was clearly built to be quoted.

What Moonshot is accused of doing

The technique at issue is called distillation: training a cheaper model to imitate a more expensive one by feeding it the bigger model's outputs, essentially having a student copy a master's homework rather than working the problem from scratch. It's a legitimate and common practice — Anthropic and OpenAI both use it on their own models to build smaller, cheaper versions. Kratsios drew the line at scale and stealth, alleging Moonshot "developed a sophisticated internal platform to conduct large-scale distillation against U.S. models, allowing them to quickly switch between multiple methods of access to avoid detection."

The second, separate allegation is a hardware one: that Moonshot obtained servers fitted with Nvidia's GB300 chips — part of the Blackwell Ultra line the US bars from sale to Chinese firms — and additionally accessed GB300-equipped machines sitting in Thailand, likely to train its models. If true, that would point to Chinese firms routing around export controls through third countries, a workaround Washington has been trying to plug for years without much success.

Neither Moonshot nor Nvidia had responded publicly by Wednesday evening.

The timing problem

The accusation runs into an inconvenient calendar. Claude Fable 5 only became publicly available on 1 July. Moonshot shipped Kimi K3 as an open-weight release roughly two weeks later, on 16 July — a 2.8-trillion-parameter model the company calls the largest open-weight system built to date. AI researchers who've picked over the timeline argue that window is too short for the kind of large-scale distillation Kratsios described, and Dean Ball, now head of strategic futures at OpenAI, said days before the accusation that K3 "cannot be easily explained by distillation or other factors."

Kimi K3 has also been winning on its own merits, not just riding on borrowed work. It topped Arena.AI's Frontend Code Arena leaderboard, ahead of both Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol, and posted an Elo score on Artificial Analysis's composite ranking that was 732 points higher than its predecessor, Kimi K2.6 — a jump large enough that some in the field see genuine architectural progress rather than a copy job. Moonshot's own benchmarks still put K3 behind Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol overall, but ahead of Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 on coding and agentic tasks.

This isn't the first time this specific accusation has been aimed at Moonshot. Anthropic itself flagged the company in February, alongside DeepSeek and MiniMax, saying the three had used more than 24,000 fake accounts to run over 16 million exchanges with Claude models, harvesting outputs on agentic reasoning, coding and tool use. Moonshot alone accounted for 3.4 million of those exchanges, according to Anthropic's own accounting at the time. What's new this week isn't the underlying suspicion — it's a cabinet-level official putting a sanctions threat behind it.

Why the chips matter more than the code

The intellectual-property fight is likely to stay murky; distillation is hard to prove from the outside, and Kratsios offered no public evidence tying Fable 5's specific outputs to K3's training data. The chip allegation is the sharper of the two, because it's a binary compliance question rather than a judgment call about model behavior. Export controls on Nvidia's most advanced AI accelerators are meant to be enforceable at the point of sale; if Chinese firms are reaching Blackwell-class hardware via Thailand, that's a hole in the control regime itself, not a dispute about how a rival trained its neural network.

The episode also sits awkwardly next to the administration's own recent moves. Earlier this month, US officials pressured Anthropic to briefly suspend global access to Fable 5 and a companion model, Mythos 5, after a China-linked hacking incident, before lifting the restriction. And David Sacks, the White House's AI and crypto czar, has been publicly uneasy that a Chinese open-weight model reached the top of independent leaderboards while, in his words, "America is tying itself in knots" over data-centre permitting and chip policy. The distillation and export-control accusations give the administration a story about theft; the leaderboard results tell a story about a competitor that may simply be catching up on its own.

What happens next runs through Treasury. Bessent's sanctions threat is conditional — "when firms conduct covert, industrial-scale distillation attacks that cross the line into IP theft" — and no formal Entity List filing has been made yet. That decision, plus any export-control investigation into the Thailand server claim, is the next concrete marker to watch, alongside whether Moonshot or Nvidia break their silence.
