English midfielders headline a busy Premier League transfer window

Anthony Gordon playing for England at the 2026 World Cup, weeks before his £69.3m move from Newcastle to Barcelona — Bryan Berlin / WikiPortraits, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Reports of a 'record' window driven by English midfielders mix up wingers, foreign midfielders, and a spend total still well short of last year's £3.19bn.

Barcelona's unveiling of Anthony Gordon at the Camp Nou last week, in a deal reported at £69.3m, has been cast in parts of the British press as the marquee sale in "one of the most active windows in years" for English midfielders. Look closely at the paperwork, though, and the framing wobbles before the ink is dry.

Gordon is a forward. Everton developed him as a winger, Newcastle played him predominantly off the left, and Sky Sports' own report on the Barcelona deal describes him as an "England international winger." He is not a midfielder by any conventional reading of the word, and conflating the two categories is the kind of loose shorthand that turns a routine sale into a trend piece.

The deal that actually fits the headline

If there is a genuine English-midfielder story in this window, it belongs to Elliot Anderson, not Gordon. The Newcastle academy product moved from Nottingham Forest to Manchester City for £116m — briefly a British transfer record, until Chelsea paid Aston Villa £117m eleven days later for Morgan Rogers, another forward misfiled by some outlets as a midfielder. Anderson, a genuine box-to-box player who started at this summer's World Cup, is the closest thing to the story as advertised. Newcastle itself was also a seller of an actual midfielder at serious money: Sandro Tonali left St James' Park for Tottenham in a £92.5m-plus-£7.5m deal — good business, but Tonali is Italian, not English. Spurs' other marquee arrival, Mateus Fernandes from West Ham for £85m, is a midfielder too — and Portuguese.

Strip out the misclassified forwards and the non-English midfielders, and the actual cohort of "English midfielders sold for big fees this summer" numbers one: Anderson.

Record window, or the usual inflation?

The bigger unanswered question is whether 2026 is exceptional at all. Deloitte put last summer's Premier League outlay at a record £3.19bn, itself an increase on the previous high of £2.36bn two years earlier. This summer's business, still running behind a delayed calendar because of the World Cup, had topped £1bn by mid-July, with Tottenham the biggest spender at roughly £230m. That is a lot of money changing hands quickly, but it is also a fraction of last year's final total, reached before the World Cup pushed peak business later into August. Calling it a record now, with six weeks of trading still to go and no final Deloitte tally to compare it against, is simply premature.

What is true is that fees for young English players keep climbing — Anderson's rise from a £35m valuation at Forest to a £116m sale two years later is a sharper data point than any "midfielders" narrative. Whether that reflects a genuinely tighter market for homegrown talent, post-World Cup exposure, or simply continued inflation across a league whose broadcast revenues keep rising, is the question worth asking once the window closes on 1 September and Deloitte publishes its final count.
