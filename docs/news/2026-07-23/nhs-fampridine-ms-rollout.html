NHS England rolls out fampridine to help MS patients walk

An MRI scan showing lesions characteristic of multiple sclerosis, the disease fampridine's newly funded NHS rollout targets. — Favaretto et al., PLOS ONE (2015) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0
In one sentence
NHS England will fund fampridine, the first licensed walking-improvement drug for MS, for ~5,000 patients a year — five years after NICE rejected it on cost grounds.

For years, the calculus facing English patients with multiple sclerosis who wanted help with their walking was blunt: pay out of pocket, or go without. Aysen Slack, 65, from Eastbourne, chose the first option for a while — buying fampridine privately at somewhere between £200 and £600 a month — until the cost became unsustainable and she stopped, even though, by her own account, the drug "seemed to be working well."[^1] As of today, that trade-off disappears for an estimated 5,000 people a year. NHS England has begun routinely funding fampridine, the first medicine specifically licensed to improve walking ability in adults with MS.

It is a genuine clinical first for the NHS formulary, but not a global one. The drug has been available in the United States since 2010, under the brand name Ampyra, and has been prescribed in Wales since 2019, Scotland since 2020 and Northern Ireland since 2023.[^2] England was the last of the four UK nations to sign off — and the reason it took this long says as much about NHS drug-pricing mechanics as it does about the medicine itself.

What the drug does

Fampridine (marketed in the UK as Fampyra) is a reformulated version of 4-aminopyridine, a potassium-channel blocker. In a nerve fibre stripped of its myelin sheath by MS, the drug slows the leakage of potassium ions, which helps sustain the electrical signal travelling down the damaged axon to the muscle. NHS England's own framing is a "signal booster."[^3] It does not slow or reverse the underlying disease — it is a symptomatic treatment, taken as a twice-daily pill, aimed squarely at gait.

Under the newly published clinical commissioning policy, eligibility is defined by the Expanded Disability Status Scale, a standard MS severity measure: patients scoring between 4 (able to walk roughly 500 metres unaided) and 7 (unable to walk more than five metres, largely wheelchair-reliant) can be offered a trial.[^4] Because fampridine does not work for everyone, patients are given two to four weeks on the drug, with walking speed and distance measured before and after; only those who show a measurable improvement continue onto ongoing supply. In the trials that underpinned licensing, around 43% of patients responded with a meaningful gain in walking speed.[^5]

"Walking difficulties can have a huge impact on freedom and independence of people with MS," said Professor Frankie Swords, NHS England's national medical director, in the announcement. Professor James Palmer, NHS England's national medical director for specialised services, added that "thousands of patients will now be able to try fampridine on the NHS."[^3] Ceri Smith of the MS Society, which has campaigned on the issue for years, called fampridine "the only licensed treatment that helps improve walking ability for some people" with the condition, and said access would be "life-changing" for many, "allowing them to live more independently."[^3]

Why it took five years

The backstory explains the delay better than the science does. NICE, the body that assesses most new medicines for NHS use in England, twice declined to recommend fampridine — first in an initial ruling and then in a final decision in December 2021 — on cost-effectiveness grounds. NICE's own modelling put the price at just over £160,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained, several multiples above the roughly £20,000–£30,000 threshold NICE typically applies, and warned the figure would likely worsen once the cost of identifying which patients actually respond to the drug was factored in.[^6] At the time, Phillip Anderson of the MS Society called the rejection "deeply disappointing" and urged NICE, NHS England and the manufacturer, Biogen, to "find a solution."[^7]

That solution appears to have arrived via a different route than a NICE technology appraisal. Fampridine's rollout was instead approved through NHS England's Clinical Priorities Advisory Group (CPAG), the committee that assesses specialised treatments for clinical effectiveness and value for money outside the standard NICE pathway, with the finalised commissioning policy published on 16 July 2026.[^4] Fampridine's list price sits at roughly £4,700 per patient per year — well below the effective price implied by NICE's earlier £160,000-per-QALY estimate — and NHS England has negotiated a further confidential discount on top of that, the terms of which have not been disclosed.[^8]

Timing may not be a coincidence. Merz Pharma UK now holds the marketing authorisation for Fampyra in the UK, but the compound's patent protection lapses this month, opening the door to generic 4-aminopyridine competition.[^8] A branded product facing imminent generic erosion has every commercial incentive to strike a discounted volume deal with a single, enormous buyer like the NHS rather than watch the market disappear entirely. Whether that dynamic shaped the final price is not confirmed publicly, but the sequence — a decade of NICE rejection, then approval in the same month patent protection ends — is difficult to read as pure coincidence.

What it means going forward

For the roughly 150,000 people living with MS in the UK, today's announcement converts a treatment many already knew worked — because they had tried buying it themselves — into something the NHS will pay for and clinically monitor.[^9] For the health system, it is a small but instructive case study in how specialised commissioning can route around a NICE rejection when a manufacturer's pricing eventually moves, or when patent economics change the manufacturer's incentives. NHS trusts should now be setting up the assessment pathway locally; MS specialists expect the first prescriptions to begin within weeks. The next marker to watch is whether the entry of generic fampridine later this year pushes the negotiated NHS price down further — and whether that becomes a template other high-cost, previously NICE-rejected symptomatic therapies can follow.
