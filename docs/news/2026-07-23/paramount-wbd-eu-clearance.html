Paramount's $110bn Warner Bros Discovery takeover clears EU hurdle

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which granted conditional approval to the Paramount-WBD deal on 22 July. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
The EU approved Paramount's $110bn Warner Bros Discovery takeover if it exits a 45-year Universal distribution venture, but a US court block looms 3 August.

Brussels has cleared Paramount Skydance's $110.9 billion takeover of Warner Bros Discovery — with a catch. The European Commission's conditional approval, announced 22 July, requires Paramount to walk away from a distribution partnership with Universal Pictures that has run for 45 years, addressing regulators' worry that the combined company would otherwise control too much of the theatrical film pipeline outside North America.

It is real progress for a deal that has spent five months collecting stamps from antitrust regulators around the world. It is not, however, the hurdle that matters most. That one sits in a federal courtroom in California, where a coalition of state attorneys general has already won a temporary block on the transaction and will ask a judge in less than two weeks to make that block a lot more durable.

What Brussels actually asked for

The EU's objection was narrow and specific. Paramount and Universal have jointly owned United International Pictures, or UIP, since 1981 — a London-based venture that distributes both studios' films across most markets outside the US and Canada. Regulators worried that folding Warner Bros' catalogue into a company still tied to Universal through UIP would let the merged entity coordinate distribution terms with a rival studio, squeezing independent and rival distributors on release slots and terms.

Paramount's fix: sell its stake in UIP within 13 months of the deal closing, and commit to no new film-distribution tie-up with Universal anywhere in the European Economic Area for a decade. "These commitments fully address the competition concerns identified by the Commission by ensuring that the films of the merged entity will not be distributed jointly with those of Universal or Disney," the Commission said in its decision, adding that an independent trustee will monitor compliance. The remedy is confined to theatrical distribution — it does not touch Paramount+ or HBO Max streaming arrangements.

Makan Delrahim, Paramount's chief legal officer and a former US Justice Department antitrust chief, called the approval "another significant milestone in bringing Paramount and Warner Bros Discovery together," arguing the combination will let the merged group "truly challenge the tech platforms." With Brussels' sign-off, Paramount says it now has clearances or non-objections in 65 jurisdictions, including the US Justice Department, China's SAMR, and antitrust authorities in Australia, Canada and Brazil.

The deal Brussels is ruling on

The scale of what's being assembled is worth restating. Paramount Skydance agreed in February to buy all of Warner Bros Discovery for $31 a share in cash — $110.9 billion including roughly $33 billion of assumed debt — beating out Netflix, which had spent the prior autumn negotiating its own purchase of WBD's studio and streaming assets before walking away in February, calling Paramount's winning terms "no longer financially attractive."

The price tag folds together HBO Max, the Warner Bros and DC film studios, CNN, and cable networks including HGTV and Discovery's factual channels — a reunification that shelves WBD's own prior plan to split itself into a separate streaming/studio company and a cable-networks company. Financing runs to roughly $54 billion in debt from Bank of America, Citi and Apollo Global Management, backstopped by about $45.7 billion in equity from Oracle co-founder Larry Ellison, whose son David chairs and runs Paramount Skydance. WBD shareholders approved the merger on 23 April; a separate vote in April rejected an $886 million exit package for chief executive David Zaslav, who is nonetheless expected to walk away with roughly $500 million from the transaction.

Where the deal could still die

Brussels' approval barely moved the needle in New York because the real threat to the transaction sits in a US courtroom. On 13 July, a twelve-state coalition led by California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued to block the merger under the Clayton Act, arguing the combined company would hold outsized market share in wide-release theatrical distribution. Seven days later, US District Judge Araceli Martínez-Olguín granted a 14-day temporary restraining order, writing that "the combined firm resulting from the transaction will possess substantial market share in the wide-release theatrical distribution market" and that the court was "persuaded that it can presume the proposed merger is likely to violate antitrust laws." The Writers Guild of America has filed a parallel suit, calling the tie-up "presumptively illegal in the market for writing services for anticipated top grossing films" given a projected 35% combined share.

A hearing on a preliminary injunction — the ruling that would actually determine whether the deal can proceed on its current timeline — is set for 3 August. Unlike the EU's UIP divestiture, there is no obvious remedy on offer here; the state coalition is seeking to stop the deal outright, not renegotiate its terms. The US Justice Department itself has already cleared the transaction, leaving state attorneys general and private litigants as the remaining opposition.

Markets are pricing the split verdict. Paramount Skydance shares rose roughly 3% on the EU news, snapping a run of losses. Warner Bros Discovery's reaction was more muted — up about half a percent on the day — because the stock has been sliding since Monday's TRO, closing near $25.89 against Paramount's $31 offer. That gap, close to its widest since the deal was announced, is the market's own antitrust forecast: a bet that regulatory approval is no longer the binding constraint, and that a California courtroom might be.

Paramount has said it still intends to close the deal by the end of September. Whether that timeline survives now rests less on Brussels than on 3 August, when Judge Martínez-Olguín decides whether her temporary block becomes something closer to permanent.
