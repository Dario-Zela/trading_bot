30-year gilt breaks 6% for the first time since 1998 as borrowing overshoots ahead of Healey's Budget

In one sentence
The 30-year gilt crossed 6% on 1 October for the first time since 1998, and estimates put Healey's £23.6bn spring headroom at roughly £8.5bn–£14bn before the 28 October Budget.

The yield on the 30-year gilt rose above 6% on 1 October, the first time it had done so since 1998. The move came less than four weeks before John Healey's first Budget as chancellor, and it has cut into the room he has to work with.

City AM put the yield at 6.04% in early trading that Thursday. Other outlets reported intraday highs of 6.029% and 6.034%, and one reported 6.07% in the morning. The sources differ by a few hundredths of a point, but they agree that 6% was crossed. The yield on the 30-year gilt was around 5% in January, when the Office for Budget Responsibility last took a market snapshot.

A global sell-off, with a UK premium

The immediate trigger was a global bond sell-off in which UK debt was among the worst performers. City AM linked it to oil above $100 a barrel after reports that US–Iran talks had stalled, a price that raises the risk of energy-driven inflation. The FTSE 100 fell nearly 2% on the day.

The UK's weakness relative to other markets points to domestic causes as well. Pantheon Macroeconomics said the wide spread of gilt yields over peers was "fair given the UK's dire fiscal position". Analysts cite the following pressures:

  • Expectations of tighter Bank of England policy.
  • Heavy gilt supply.
  • Pressure to fund extra defence spending and cost-of-living support.
  • Uncertainty over how much more the government intends to borrow.

The sell-off built over weeks. One report cites a 5.89% peak in September, and the UK also sold 30-year debt at a record yield during the autumn.

The borrowing numbers beneath it

The fiscal data have not helped. The Office for National Statistics reported that public sector borrowing in July was £1.8bn, £700m more than a year earlier. Most economists had expected roughly zero, and the OBR had pencilled in a £500m surplus. Debt interest payments for the month were £7.7bn, up £700m.

The July miss needs context. Borrowing for the first four months of the financial year was £56.7bn, £6bn (9.6%) below the same period a year earlier. Figures for the first three months were revised down by £2.7bn. Income tax receipts were £17.1bn in July, up £1.7bn on the year, while social benefits spending rose £2bn. Total public sector debt stood at £2.985tn, or 94.1% of GDP.

One month's surprise does not make a trend, and the year-to-date total is lower than last year's. But a miss on a month the forecasters expected to be close to balanced reinforced the view that borrowing is hard to hold down. Thomas Pugh of RSM UK warned: "The risk is that more borrowing to spend fuels inflation and pushes up gilt yields further." Healey has said that "fiscal discipline is the bedrock of our UK economic stability and national security".

What it does to the headroom

Headroom is the margin by which the chancellor is forecast to meet her or his own fiscal rules. It stood at £23.6bn at the OBR's spring forecast in March. The triage brief put it at about £22.7bn, but no source found for this article supports that figure, so it is not used here. Every estimate found is lower than the March number.

The estimates depend on how much of the rise in yields is judged to be lasting:

  • Bloomberg Economics says higher borrowing costs and inflation pare about £12bn from the March figure.
  • RSM and other City firms estimate that the rise in yields alone could cut about £9bn.
  • Deutsche Bank's Sanjay Raja puts it at £13.8bn if current yields are used in the calculations. He starts from a spring figure of about £26bn on his own basis.
  • The most pessimistic estimate reported is as low as £8.5bn, roughly two-thirds below the spring level.
  • Most economists expect the figure at the Budget to land between £10bn and £15bn.

The arithmetic is simple enough. Each 0.25 percentage point rise in gilt yields adds roughly £2.5bn a year to debt-servicing costs. Economists estimate that each 1-point rise in rates adds about £15bn to costs by 2031. The OBR's spring forecast had debt interest at £134.7bn, and with yields higher since March that figure is likely to be revised up.

The OBR's final forecast will use market rates close to the Budget date, so the headroom number will move again before then.

The room is smaller than it was, and the government is also under pressure to spend. Deutsche's Raja suggests it would want to keep at least £10bn of headroom to reassure markets. Other analysts have urged that any extra debt issuance for investment next year stay below £10bn. The Treasury could accept a thinner buffer, but one analyst doubts bond markets would welcome that.

There is a political dimension as well. City AM linked part of the sell-off to Andy Burnham's plans to expand the state, including a free-at-the-point-of-use care service, and reported that he admitted a funding "shortfall". Healey has to set the Budget against competing spending demands and a bond market that has just shown how quickly it can react.

What to watch

Healey delivers his first Budget on 28 October. Before then, the next ONS public finances release will show whether the July overshoot was a one-off. Daily moves in the 30-year gilt will show whether 6% proves a ceiling or a floor.

The Budget will then show how the chancellor balances a thinner cushion against tax and spending choices. The same week's OBR forecast will show what debt interest costs it is willing to assume. Higher yields have already reduced the room the Treasury has, and the question for 28 October is whether the Budget leaves enough of it to reassure the market.
