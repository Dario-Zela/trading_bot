France drops its objection to UK inclusion in 'Made in Europe' rules as business urges Brexit repair

In one sentence
France has reportedly dropped its objection to UK firms counting as European suppliers under 'Made in Europe' rules, but no legal text has been seen and the scope is unclear.

Britain's exclusion from the European Union's planned 'Made in Europe' industrial rules has become one of the sharper post-Brexit trade irritants. Paris, which had been among the strongest advocates of tight origin rules, now appears to have shifted. The Guardian reports that France has dropped its objection to UK inclusion. The report would let British firms count as European suppliers under the scheme. This article could not independently confirm the report. No finalised legal text or vote has been seen, and the change is better read as a signal than as a settled outcome.

What the rules do

The rules sit in the Industrial Accelerator Act, which the European Commission put forward in March. Its aim is to steer public procurement and subsidies towards goods made inside the bloc. In the draft, vehicles bought for corporate fleets and small electric cars must be assembled in the EU to qualify for related support. UK-assembled vehicles fall outside that definition.

Carmakers have noticed. Nissan and BMW have warned of possible plant closures in Britain if the draft becomes law as written. The act still needs backing from member states and the European Parliament. One reported timetable has Parliament adopting it at the end of 2026.

France's shift

The move has been building. In late May, France's trade minister, Nicolas Forissier, told the Financial Times that Paris wanted Brussels to address Britain's exclusion. That was notable, because Paris has been the main champion of restrictive rules of origin in the Commission's draft.

The picture is not uniform. A trade report dated 28 September said France was ready to oppose any loosening of the act's core industrial requirements, and that European producers were pressing for stricter rules. Both can be true. Paris may accept a route for British inclusion while resisting dilution of the content thresholds that apply to everyone else. The scope of any UK carve-out, whether through a bespoke agreement, a trade-deal mechanism or a change to the definition of 'European', is the detail that decides who benefits. It has not been reported.

London's pitch

The British government has been lobbying for this. Euronews reported in September that Andy Burnham, the prime minister, planned to raise the scheme with Commission president Ursula von der Leyen under the message 'we are not your enemies', and that EU officials saw the issue as London's priority in the talks. The aim is to avoid what the government calls collateral damage to British industry. Burnham has also said Brexit has done more harm than good and that the UK needs to settle its long-term relationship with the EU. He is expected to set out options at a UK-EU summit, though the date has not been fixed.

Business groups are pressing the same case. The discovery note for this piece describes a CBI-style lobby urging the prime minister to make repairing Brexit's economic damage his priority. The body, its wording and the date could not be checked, so none is quoted here. The same note says Burnham has asked for remarks about Ann Widdecombe to be corrected. Searches found no account of that correction, and the detail is left out here. The Widdecombe row seems to be a distraction from the trade argument rather than part of it.

One carve-out at a time

The approach is incremental. Since the 2020 withdrawal, relations have been rebuilt through narrow, sector-by-sector arrangements rather than a single large deal. Each concession has to be negotiated separately, and each can be held up by a member state with a domestic industry to protect. France's earlier stance showed how that works. Its reported change shows the opposite case: one capital's calculation shifting.

For British manufacturers, especially in cars, the stakes are concrete. Qualifying as European suppliers would keep them in the market for public contracts and subsidised demand. Staying outside would put them at a disadvantage against competitors in France and Germany.

The next tests are the text and the timing. The first is whether member states' positions and the Parliament's amendments carry a UK provision into the act, and on what terms. The second is whether the UK-EU summit produces a commitment before the legislation is adopted at the end of the year. Until then, a French signal is not a legal change.
