Banks lobby as Healey weighs a higher profits surcharge or a time-limited levy

In one sentence
Banks say Chancellor Healey is weighing a higher profits surcharge or a time-limited levy, and are lobbying against both before his first Budget on 28 October.

The chancellor has asked the heads of Britain's largest banks to the Treasury, and the banks are using the occasion to argue against a bigger tax bill. Lenders say John Healey is weighing one of two options for his first Budget on 28 October: a higher surcharge on bank profits, or a levy that would expire after a set period. The Treasury has not confirmed either. It has said it does not comment on Budget speculation.

The story is therefore about lobbying and design, not a decision. Both options would raise money from banks. They would hit earnings, capital returns and share prices in different ways.

What is on the table

Banks already pay more than other companies. A surcharge of 3% sits on top of corporation tax, which is why the sector's headline rate is above the main rate. The TUC has called for the surcharge to rise to at least 8%. It estimates that would raise about £9bn over four years. Positive Money has also pushed for higher bank profit taxes to pay for cost-of-living support.

The alternative, a time-limited levy, would work like a windfall tax. It would fall on profits for a fixed period and then lapse.

Reports said invitations went to Lloyds, Barclays, HSBC, NatWest, Santander UK and Nationwide, for a meeting the Tuesday after the reports appeared. The Financial Times, citing sources, reported that Healey told the banks he faced a difficult situation but gave no details of any bank measures in the Budget. No account of a final decision has emerged.

The case from the banks

The industry's argument is about competitiveness and cumulative burden. JP Morgan's Jamie Dimon reportedly phoned Healey to warn against a windfall levy or a higher surcharge. He said higher taxes can push jobs elsewhere, and he cited a decline in finance roles in New York. UK Finance, the industry body, has pointed to an "increasingly cumulative tax burden" on the sector. The City of London Corporation's policy chairman, Chris Hayward, said his message to the chancellor was to "do no harm" and has urged no bank windfall tax.

The form of the tax matters to the banks as well as the size. A permanent surcharge lowers the return on equity in every future year. Analysts have to build it into long-run earnings forecasts, and it can lower the multiple investors will pay. A time-limited levy is a one-off hit to earnings and capital. Lenders could absorb it with less change to their payout plans, provided it really does end. Banks that have promised buybacks and dividends will want certainty on that point more than any single rate.

Who shapes the outcome

The Treasury is also weighing the political side. Reports say Healey wants to give households and businesses "breathing space" while meeting his fiscal rules. Sky News reported that the economic and geopolitical headwinds have prompted talk of a smaller fiscal buffer, which would reduce the need for tax rises. A smaller buffer lowers the pressure for a large bank tax. It does not remove it.

The backdrop is tight. The 30-year gilt yield has broken 6% for the first time since 1998, and borrowing is running above forecast. That raises the appeal of any measure that raises money from a sector with large profits. The lobbying campaign is public, and the meeting with bank chiefs shows the Treasury is taking soundings before it commits.

The evidence so far shows lobbying and sourcing from the banks' side, not a government position. The fight is also over the narrower question of permanent against temporary, and that is the question investors in UK lenders will be watching.

What to watch

The Budget on 28 October will settle the design. The details to read are the rate, any end date or sunset clause, and whether the levy is based on profits or on another measure. Bank share prices in the days before the statement will show how much of the risk the market has priced in.
