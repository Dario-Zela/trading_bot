STANDING WATCH · IRAN/HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-16 · 4 min read

Hormuz: record ship-attack reports, Washington wants a bigger IEA stock release

In one sentence
Reported attacks on Hormuz shipping have hit a record and the US is said to want 100m more barrels of emergency oil, as the Iran war's supply shock feeds into energy prices and yields.

The Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel through which a large share of the world's seaborne oil passes, is again the pressure point in the Iran war. Reports of attacks on shipping nearby have reached what the BBC describes as a record. Washington, meanwhile, is reported to be unhappy with the emergency oil release already agreed and to be pressing for at least 100 million barrels more. The two threads point the same way. The disruption has not been contained, and its effects are being felt in energy and bond markets as well as in the security picture.

Some of the specifics are not yet nailed down. The BBC's record claim comes without a published count or date range in the material available, and the Bloomberg report of US dissatisfaction has not been independently confirmed. Both are attributed accordingly below.

The shipping picture

The war began on 28 February, when US and Israeli strikes on Iran were followed by the effective closure of the strait. By 11 March, at least 14 vessels had reportedly been hit in the region, and traffic through the strait was described as nearly halted. Analysis citing CNBC has put observed crossings at a five-day average of about 10 a day, against roughly 130 a day before the war. That figure is secondary and undated, so it should be read as indicative.

What a record number of attack reports means for oil flow is the open question. The BBC's report ties the attacks to slower oil exports, but no volumes have been published. The International Energy Agency's executive director, Fatih Birol, has said Middle Eastern crude flows are recovering significantly. Refined products such as diesel and jet fuel remain "severely constrained", and the agency has cited a "real physical disruption in diesel supply". The pressure is therefore uneven: crude is returning faster than the fuels that economies actually burn.

Markets and the stock release

Brent traded at about $105 a barrel on 8 October, according to Euronews. It was near $92 in March, when the first release was announced. Reports of ship strikes have also coincided with moves in government bonds: one account has UK 10-year gilt yields rising from about 4.49% to 4.65% on a day when three cargo vessels were hit. That account is undated, so the link is suggestive rather than established. The mechanism is the familiar one. Dearer energy lifts inflation expectations, and higher expected inflation pushes up the yields that governments pay to borrow.

The emergency stocks are the main policy response. In March, IEA members agreed to make 400 million barrels available, the largest release in the agency's history. An internal document seen by Euronews says 326 million of those have been released. On 2 October the G7 announced a further 100 million barrels over four months, with a diesel component front-loaded into the first 20 days. On 7 October the IEA backed speeding up delivery, and it has said members still hold about 1.1 billion barrels of public emergency stocks, including more than 200 million barrels of diesel.

The new US lobbying for at least 100 million barrels beyond that is reported by Bloomberg, via Benzinga. It sits awkwardly with the existing arithmetic. Euronews's document suggests the G7's 100 million may simply complete the unfinished March action rather than add to it. If that reading is right, a request for 100 million more would be a genuine escalation. Washington had already pressed Europe, reportedly with the threat of a diesel export ban. Greece, Italy and Portugal have met their March pledges. France, Germany, the Netherlands and Spain are still being assessed, and Portugal says about 50 million barrels remain pending at EU level.

Escalation talk

CNN, relayed by Benzinga, reported separately that the US is weighing use of the GBU-43/B "Mother of All Bombs" against Iran. The sourcing is anonymous and there is no official confirmation. The weapon, about 21,600 lb, was last used in combat in Afghanistan in 2017. It is best read as a sign of how far options are being discussed, not as a decision.

What to watch

The IEA Governing Board met on 15 October to settle volumes, timing and product types. Its decision, and whether members accept Washington's larger ask, will show whether stocks are being treated as a bridge or as a longer-term substitute for Hormuz traffic. The next data points are the weekly shipping counts and diesel supply figures. If they stay weak, the pressure on yields will persist.
