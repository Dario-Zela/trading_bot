Trump calls off livestream of Fort Hood attacker's execution

In one sentence
Trump has scrapped Hegseth's plan to livestream Nidal Hasan's execution, but the Fort Hood attacker is still due to face a firing squad on 3 December.

President Donald Trump has called off a plan to livestream the execution of Nidal Hasan, the former Army psychiatrist who carried out the 2009 Fort Hood shooting. The execution itself has not been cancelled. Writing on Truth Social on Thursday, Trump said: "He will die, as scheduled, by firing squad, but this event will not be streamed."

The reversal came about a week after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth floated the broadcast. In an interview with the activist Jack Posobiec, Hegseth said: "We'll make sure that people are able to watch it, that it's public." Hegseth has said the execution is set for 1pm on 3 December at Fort Hood, Texas.

The reversal

Trump gave two reasons. He allowed that a broadcast might deter terrorism, but said "many very good and well meaning people would rather not watch him die, through streaming, or through any other visual device." Immediate families of the victims may attend in person if they choose.

The plan drew criticism across party lines. Some Republican senators said it would put the United States on a level with terrorist groups that film executions. House Speaker Mike Johnson warned that a recording could become "a recruiting tool for future terrorists." Vice President JD Vance said he would not watch. Australia's prime minister said the government was looking at ways to stop the stream being shown there.

Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell said Hegseth supported the decision and that the two were "completely aligned." Hegseth had discussed the cancellation with Trump before it was announced.

An execution with little precedent

Hasan was convicted by court-martial in August 2013 of killing 13 people and wounding more than 30 at Fort Hood on 5 November 2009. He was sentenced to death. No US servicemember has been put to death since 1961, when Army Private John Bennett was hanged. A firing squad execution would be the military's first since 1945.

Public execution has long gone out of use in the United States. The last one was in 1936, when an estimated 20,000 people watched the hanging of Rainey Bethea in Kentucky. Amnesty International counts Afghanistan and Iran as the only countries with known public executions last year.

This is the real weight of the episode. The date and the manner of this execution were announced by a defence secretary in a podcast-style interview. A president then decided, in a social media post, what the public would and would not see. The reporting I could find does not say whether Trump formally approved the execution date, and it says nothing about the status of Hasan's remaining appeals or who represents him. Those are the questions that decide whether 3 December holds.

What to watch

The decision on the broadcast has been settled for now. The legal process has not. Watch for any filing by Hasan's lawyers challenging the date or the method, for the Pentagon's rules on who may attend, and for whether the victims' families take up the offer to be present. The Fort Hood shooting is the Army's most lethal attack on a US base, and the case will be closely watched until the sentence is carried out or stayed.
