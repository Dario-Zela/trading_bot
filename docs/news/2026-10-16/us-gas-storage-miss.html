US gas storage builds 67bn cubic feet, below the 76bn estimate

In one sentence
US gas storage rose 67 bcf against a 76 bcf estimate, narrowing the surplus to the five-year average to 45 bcf, though one miss does not make a trend.

US utilities added 67 billion cubic feet (bcf) of gas to underground storage in the week ended 9 October. That was 9 bcf short of the 76 bcf analysts had expected, according to figures from the Energy Information Administration (EIA) first reported by Benzinga. A miss of that size is ordinary for a weekly series, and by itself it settles nothing. The number matters because of where it leaves the stockpile as the heating season approaches.

What the report showed

Working gas in the Lower 48 stood at 3,567 bcf on 9 October, up from 3,500 bcf a week earlier. That is 45 bcf, or 1.3%, above the five-year average of 3,522 bcf. It is 143 bcf, or 3.9%, below the level of a year ago (3,710 bcf).

The previous week's report, for the period ended 2 October, showed a larger injection of 85 bcf. Stocks were then 68 bcf above the five-year average and 130 bcf below last year. The surplus to the five-year average therefore shrank by 23 bcf in a week, and the deficit to last year widened by 13 bcf.

Noise or trend

The case for reading this as a tightening trend is thin. One week of a 9 bcf miss is well within the usual scatter between estimates and the reported figure. The prior week's injection of 85 bcf is a single data point in the other direction, and it does not on its own show a run of shortfalls. A trend would need at least a second consecutive injection below expectations, or a surplus to the five-year average that keeps eroding.

The cushion is still comfortable. Stocks sit above the five-year norm even after the narrowing, and the year-ago gap is unchanged in kind: inventories entered last winter higher than they are now.

October injections are also small and volatile by nature. Weather swings between heating and cooling demand, and the shoulder season has the least demand for either. A modest weekly miss can reflect early cold in some regions as easily as weak supply.

What it means for prices

The weekly storage balance feeds mainly into Henry Hub, the US benchmark, and into the economics of US liquefied natural gas exports. A smaller injection means less gas going into storage and slightly more tension in the domestic balance. The extra pull on LNG feedgas, if any, would come through those channels.

A link to European LNG pricing is possible but second-order. US cargoes are a significant source of European supply, yet no TTF or LNG market figure accompanies this report. A 9 bcf miss is too small to carry a European price claim without one.

What to watch

The next EIA storage report, due next Thursday for the week ending 16 October, is the test. Another injection below consensus, with the surplus to the five-year average falling below 45 bcf, would start to look like a pattern. A return to injections near or above expectations would confirm this week as noise.
