Reform's candidate vetting questioned after undercover filming of councillors

In one sentence
Farage says vetting cannot screen for future remarks, but ITV footage suggests Reform knew of Chris Parry's posts about David Lammy and kept him as a candidate.

Nigel Farage has defended Reform UK's candidate vetting after an ITV undercover investigation recorded party figures making racist remarks and discussing how the party handled a candidate who had targeted David Lammy. His central argument is that no vetting process can screen for what people say in future. The footage raises a narrower question: whether the party acted on what it already knew.

What the footage shows

ITV's Exposure team released extracts on 14 October, with the full documentary due on Sunday 18 October. Reporters filmed Reform councillors and candidates across England and Wales. According to ITV's reporting, the remarks included calling Muslims and Asian homeowners "parasites" and suggesting non-white people should be forcibly removed from Britain. A second strand came from an ITV reporter who took a job in Reform's Westminster press office and secretly recorded staff.

The Lammy remarks are not, on the reporting available, something a councillor said on camera. They are older social-media posts by Chris Parry, a former Royal Navy officer and, until recently, Reform's mayoral candidate for Hampshire. ITV reports that Parry posted that Lammy, who was born in Britain, should "go back to the Caribbean". It also says he questioned the loyalty of eight other Black and Asian politicians. Parry has said his posts concerned reparations and failures in government and policing, and that context should be considered.

Other cases in the footage touch on vetting directly:

  • Gavin Beales, a North Northamptonshire councillor, was registered on WhiteDate, a dating site for white people that promoted white supremacist and Nazi material. He was not suspended. Reform says that was because the registration was brief. Beales says it was inadvertent and that he abhors racism.
  • Alex Lomas, a Greater Manchester campaigner, said he failed Reform's vetting twice, first over offensive language and then over posts about Tommy Robinson. He says he was later told he had passed and stood in Oldham, finishing second by about 200 votes.
  • George Madgwick, Reform's Hampshire County Council leader, was found in the private Facebook group of the Portsmouth Patriots. He said he had been "really in with the Patriots" in the past.

These accounts come from ITV and from secondary coverage of the programme, and the full broadcast has not yet aired.

Farage's defence, and the protection claim

Farage's line, reported on 15 October, is that "you can't vet for future comments". Reform has added that not everyone recorded is a member, that investigations have begun where members behaved inappropriately, and that its vetting has improved. It has also conceded that some people who do not share its values may get through, "the same for all organisations". It maintains there is "no room for racism in Reform UK".

That defence works best for remarks made after selection. It fits less well with the Parry episode, because the posts predate his candidacy and were, in the programme's telling, known inside the party. In the recordings, communications director Ed Sumner calls Parry "clearly a racist" and expects him to be removed. Farage, ITV reports, decided an apology would be enough, saying Parry "must apologise to Lammy" if Lammy had taken offence. When Sumner suggested Farage was "protecting" Parry, Farage is said to have replied that it showed "a bit of sorrow". Sumner is later recorded saying he would have removed Parry months earlier.

The accusation of protection rests on that exchange and on Parry having stayed in post. Reform has not, in the coverage reviewed, disputed the recordings. Farage's "future comments" argument does not answer the question of what a leader does with a problem he has been told about.

In the same footage Sumner tells the reporter that press staff must "start defending the indefensible", and a colleague answers "many a time", naming Parry. That is the exchange most damaging to the claim of a disciplined operation. It suggests staff regarded the problem as recurring.

Why it matters beyond one party

Reform's pitch to voters has been that it is a professionalised alternative to the established parties, and vetting is the proof offered. The party was embarrassed in 2024 when a Channel 4 investigation of campaigners in Clacton-on-Sea led it to drop several candidates, so this is not the first test. The difference now is that the footage concerns serving councillors and the leadership's own handling of a known case, not only volunteers.

The case also tests how far a party can be held responsible for elected members' conduct. Councillors are answerable to their councils' standards processes as well as to their party, and a party can say a member has since left, or never joined. Reform's reply that not everyone recorded is a member relies on that distinction.

Lammy has called on Farage to "clean up" the party, and Reform dismissed his words as "crocodile tears". Calls for some of the councillors to resign have also been reported.

What to watch

The full documentary on 18 October will show whether the unaired material goes beyond the extracts. Reform's internal investigations will show whether the councillors are suspended, expelled or left in post. Parry's status and any written explanation of why he was retained will matter more to the protection allegation than Farage's general case about vetting.
