Russia says Ukraine hit an oil refinery after Zelensky said strikes would resume

In one sentence
Moscow says Ukraine hit an oil refinery after Zelensky said long-range strikes would resume; the plant, damage and any market effect remain unconfirmed.

Russia's defence authorities say Ukraine has struck an oil refinery on Russian territory. The claim comes shortly after President Volodymyr Zelensky said Kyiv's long-range strikes on Russian energy sites would resume. The Russian account is the only one available at the time of writing. The name of the plant, its location, the time of the attack and the extent of any damage have not been independently confirmed, and Kyiv has not, in the material available, claimed the specific strike.

What is known

Two things are on the record, both through reporting by the BBC. Moscow says a refinery was hit, and Zelensky has said that strikes would resume. The exact wording of his statement, and whether it describes a pause that has now ended or a continuation of a campaign that never stopped, is not established here. Nor is there any figure for lost refining capacity, any statement from the plant's operator, or any sign yet of a reaction in oil or fuel markets.

That gap matters. Russian officials routinely describe Ukrainian drone attacks in terms that minimise damage, while Ukrainian officials and open-source analysts often point to fires, halted units and repair timelines. Until satellite imagery, operator statements or regional authorities fill in the picture, the claim is best read as confirmation that an attack took place, not as a measure of its effect.

A campaign with a history

The strike fits a pattern now well established. Since early 2024, Ukraine has used long-range drones against Russian refineries, oil depots and export terminals, partly to cut the fuel supply that sustains Russia's military and partly to squeeze the oil revenue that funds the war. Through 2025 the attacks reached deep into Russia, and at points they forced temporary shutdowns of processing units and prompted fuel shortages and export restrictions in some regions.

Refineries are awkward targets to put out of action for long. Damage is often concentrated in a few units, such as the primary distillation columns, and repairs can take weeks rather than months, particularly when Russian operators can reroute crude to other plants. The cumulative effect depends on how often the same sites are hit and how quickly spare parts can be sourced under sanctions. A single strike, however dramatic the footage, rarely changes the national picture.

Why markets notice

Russia is one of the world's largest exporters of crude and refined products, so any sustained loss of refining capacity can shift flows of diesel in particular. The effect on prices is usually modest on its own. It becomes more noticeable when supply is already tight elsewhere, and this week's coverage of record ship-attack reports around the Strait of Hormuz and Washington's push for a larger release of emergency stocks suggests the market has little slack to spare.

The immediate questions are practical. Satellite imagery and local reporting over the coming days should show whether the plant is offline and for how long. Russian statements on fuel exports, and any Ukrainian confirmation of the target, will indicate whether this is an isolated hit or the first in a renewed sequence. Zelensky's own follow-up, including any detail on scope, will show how far the resumption goes.
