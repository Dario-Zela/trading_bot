MHRA seizes 12,000 fake retatrutide and tirzepatide pens in Birmingham

In one sentence
The MHRA seized over 12,000 counterfeit retatrutide and tirzepatide pens in Birmingham and found packaging for about 30,000 more; retatrutide has no UK licence.

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) has seized more than 12,000 counterfeit weight-loss pens from an industrial unit in Birmingham. The pens were labelled as retatrutide and tirzepatide. The raid, carried out with West Midlands Police on 14 October, also turned up packaging for about 30,000 more products. The haul is valued at around £2 million, according to the MHRA's release, which was published on 15 October.

The release distinguishes two things. The pens were seized. The packaging for roughly 30,000 further products was found at the same site. It is not described as a count of finished counterfeit pens. The release does not mention any arrests.

A drug with no genuine UK version

Tirzepatide is a licensed medicine in the UK, sold by Eli Lilly under brand names including Mounjaro. Retatrutide is a different case. The MHRA states that it is not authorised in the UK and that supplies sold outside legitimate clinical trials are illegal.

That matters for how the fraud works. A buyer who has seen a real Mounjaro pen has at least a rough idea of what genuine packaging looks like. For retatrutide, there is no licensed product, and so no authentic UK pen to compare against. Any box that looks professional can pass for the real thing. This is an inference from the regulatory position rather than something the MHRA has said in these terms. It fits the pattern the agency describes, though, in which sellers target demand for drugs that patients cannot legitimately obtain.

On the MHRA's account, the products have not been assessed for safety, quality or effectiveness. Tim Duffield, head of intelligence in the agency's Criminal Enforcement Unit, described the warehouse as "filthy". He said there was no guarantee of what the pens contain or whether they are contaminated. Injectables are especially sensitive to sterile manufacture, and a wrong dose is a separate hazard from contamination.

Not the first raid

The Birmingham seizure is the latest in a run of MHRA operations over about a year, as listed in the release:

  • October 2025: the first illicit weight-loss medicine production facility found in the UK was dismantled, with 5,000 pens and raw ingredients seized.
  • February 2026: about 2,000 doses awaiting dispatch were seized, along with manufacturing equipment and vehicles.
  • May 2026: two people were arrested after a raid on a country estate, where about 12,000 doses were recovered.

The pattern is of larger hauls and of production, not just resale. It also suggests a supply chain that is moving faster than enforcement. The MHRA says it is targeting organised criminal gangs. Even so, finding packaging for 30,000 more units indicates the intended scale of the operation was well above what was seized.

What the regulator advises

The MHRA tells the public to avoid weight-loss medicines from unregulated sources. It says people should speak to a healthcare professional before starting GLP-1 treatment and obtain medicines only from a registered pharmacy with a valid prescription. Suspected side effects can be reported through the Yellow Card scheme, and the agency's #FakeMeds website gives guidance on buying medicines online.

The open questions are practical. The MHRA has not said, in the material reviewed, what tests of the seized pens will show, or whether charges will follow. Those results, and any prosecutions that emerge from the investigation, are the next points to watch.
