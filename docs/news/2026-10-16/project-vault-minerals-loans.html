Project Vault to pick over a dozen US manufacturers for up to $1bn in critical-minerals loans

In one sentence
Project Vault, the US critical-minerals stockpile, is reportedly set to pick more than a dozen American manufacturers for up to $1bn in loans, though terms and names remain undisclosed.

The US government's critical-minerals stockpile is moving from design to allocation. Project Vault, the reserve announced in February, is set to choose more than a dozen American manufacturers to receive up to $1bn in loans tied to critical minerals, according to Bloomberg, as reported by Benzinga. The choice of recipients would be the first time the programme names specific beneficiaries of public credit.

The report's details have not been independently confirmed here. The recipients, the loan terms and the timetable for the selection were not available.

What Project Vault is

Project Vault was launched in early February as a public-private reserve of minerals that US industry depends on and that China dominates in supply or processing. Coverage at the time put the headline size at about $12bn. The largest part is a long-term direct loan of roughly $10bn from the Export-Import Bank to an alliance of manufacturers and private capital providers. The private portion was reported at about $1.67bn, though President Trump put it at $2bn. Sources differ on the exact split, and the bank's own release is the reference for official numbers.

The companies named in coverage include General Motors, Stellantis, Boeing, Corning, GE Vernova and Google, although some of that list rests on reporting rather than formal disclosure. Commodity traders Hartree Partners, Traxys North America and Mercuria are to handle purchasing for the stockpile. The scheme has also been reported to have secured its first $1bn of procurement commitments, with Glencore and Mercuria among the counterparties. That figure is a purchasing milestone. It is not new government spending, and it should not be confused with the loan pool in the latest report.

From stockpile to credit allocation

A stockpile is a passive instrument: the state buys material, stores it and releases it in a shortage. Loans to named manufacturers are different. They put public credit behind particular firms and, by extension, particular supply chains. The headline number is modest next to the $10bn facility. But "up to $1bn" spread across a dozen-plus borrowers implies an average below $100m each. That is a working-capital scale, not a plant-building scale, and the final spread will probably be uneven.

The open questions are the ones that decide whether this is a credit programme or a subsidy:

  • Which minerals and which borrowers qualify, and whether the list leans towards the firms already in the Vault alliance.
  • Whether the loans carry below-market pricing, and what collateral or offtake obligations attach.
  • How the loans relate to the stockpile itself, for example whether borrowers draw on Vault inventory or buy elsewhere.

None of these is answered by the headline.

What to watch

The immediate catalyst is the publication of the recipient list and loan terms, from either the programme's backers or the Bloomberg original. Beyond that, the useful test is delivery: the earlier reports did not say which minerals will be bought, in what volumes, from which countries, or when deliveries start. Until those details appear, Project Vault remains a financing structure with a large headline and few disclosed physical quantities.
