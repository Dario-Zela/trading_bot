Nandy 'minded to intervene' in Sky's £1.6bn bid for ITV's entertainment arm

In one sentence
Culture secretary Lisa Nandy says she is 'minded to intervene' in Sky's £1.6bn ITV deal, putting media plurality ahead of price as the gating question.

The UK culture secretary has told Sky she is "minded to intervene" in its £1.6bn purchase of ITV's entertainment business, according to BBC Business. The phrase is a procedural marker, not a verdict. It means Lisa Nandy is inclined to consider a public-interest intervention. It does not mean she has decided to block the deal or to clear it. The effect is to make media plurality, rather than price, the question that decides whether the deal goes ahead.

What 'minded to' means

Under the Enterprise Act 2002, the culture secretary can issue a public-interest intervention notice (PIIN) on grounds that include media plurality. Once one is issued, the Competition and Markets Authority reports on the competition effects while Ofcom assesses the plurality case. The secretary of state then decides whether to refer the deal for a fuller review, accept undertakings, or let it proceed. 'Minded to' is the stage at which the parties are told the notice is likely and are given a short window to respond before it is issued.

The wording is not new. Nandy used the same formula on 30 June about Paramount's bid for Warner Bros Discovery, and the companies were given until 6 July to respond. Trade reports in late July, citing a Global Competition Review briefing, said the government would issue a notice on the Sky–ITV deal "in due course". Today's statement is consistent with that. I could not confirm from available sources whether a formal notice has since been issued.

The deal

Sky, owned by Comcast, agreed terms to buy ITV's media and entertainment arm for £1.6bn. Reported terms are £1.2bn in cash at completion, plus up to £200m more if ITV's advertising revenue meets 2027 targets. ITV Studios, which makes Love Island and Coronation Street, would stay a separate listed company.

The plurality argument rests on scale. Analysts cited in coverage of the deal say the combined group would reach more than 20 million UK households and control more than 70% of the UK television advertising market. Channel 4 and Channel 5 are expected to object on competition grounds. Those figures are secondary-source estimates and have not been tested by the regulators.

Why it matters beyond Sky

A formal intervention would add a second review track on top of the CMA's competition work. That means more time, more disclosure, and the possibility of conditions on editorial or commercial arrangements. For ITV shareholders, the question is whether the £1.2bn upfront payment arrives on the expected timetable. For Sky, it is whether remedies would be needed. Rival consolidators will read the case as a guide to how this government treats broadcaster-to-broadcaster combinations: a deal that clears competition law is no longer assumed to clear the culture secretary.

Two cases in four months, Paramount–Warner and now Sky–ITV, suggest a pattern rather than an exception, though only the Sky case concerns a UK public-service broadcaster.

What to watch

The next marker is whether Nandy issues a formal PIIN once Sky and ITV have responded. After that come the timetable Ofcom and the CMA are given to report, and any remedies Sky offers. A fuller review would push completion well beyond the initial schedule.
