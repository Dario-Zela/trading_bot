Same-day test may detect hidden infections in critically ill children

In one sentence
A rapid sequencing-based test may identify infections in ventilated children within a day, but the same-day claim rests on a single-centre preprint awaiting peer review and replication.

A new rapid diagnostic that identifies infections in critically ill children within a day is being reported as a possible way to shorten the wait for answers in paediatric intensive care. The BBC reported the test on 16 October. The detail behind that report could not be independently checked, and the claim that results arrive the same day has not yet been shown to hold outside the original setting.

The problem: waiting for a culture

Children on ventilators in intensive care are at risk of lung infections that are hard to pin down. Standard practice is to send a respiratory sample to the laboratory and grow whatever is in it, which can take a day or more and can miss organisms that grow poorly or have already been hit by antibiotics. In the meantime, clinicians commonly start broad-spectrum antibiotics, accepting the cost of overtreatment to avoid undertreating a child who is deteriorating. A faster, wider test would let teams narrow or change treatment sooner.

What the evidence appears to show

The BBC article was not retrievable for this piece, and the triage notes carried no figures, named institutions or study details. The most closely matching published work is a preprint on medRxiv, "Implementing rapid pan-microbial metagenomics in paediatric intensive care". A preprint has not yet been peer reviewed, and it has not been confirmed as the study behind the BBC report.

The preprint describes a single-centre prospective study at a London hospital, running from November 2024 to February 2025. It used metagenomic sequencing — reading the genetic material in a sample to identify bacteria, fungi and viruses at once, rather than testing for one suspect at a time. Preliminary results were returned the same day, with final results the next day.

The reported numbers:

  • 177 respiratory samples from 122 patients were tested, and 174 (98%) passed quality control.
  • After 16 hours of sequencing, sensitivity was 89% for bacteria, 100% for fungi and 87% for viruses.
  • Specificity was at or above 99% across all three groups.

The authors also reviewed electronic health records to judge clinical impact and surveyed staff. Some web pages circulating this week claim the work came from Great Ormond Street Hospital, was published in The Lancet Microbe, and changed prescribing in about a quarter of cases. None of those claims could be traced to a reliable source. The Lancet Microbe paper that does exist concerns the adult version of this workflow, in an adult intensive care unit.

What would settle it

Three things matter before the same-day claim can be taken as established. The first is replication: one hospital's laboratory, staffing and sequencing schedule may not transfer elsewhere. The second is outcomes. Accuracy against culture is not the same as a child recovering faster or receiving fewer unnecessary antibiotics, and the clinical-impact data are what the preprint's reviewers will probe hardest. The third is practicality: sequencing equipment, specialist staff and cost determine whether most NHS paediatric units could run it.

A separate, peer-reviewed line of work, the RASCALS study, tested a 52-pathogen panel on samples from ventilated children. It reported 89.5% sensitivity and 97.9% specificity against culture, with a median time to result of about 26 hours. That is a different technology, but it shows the scale of the speed-up that targeted molecular tests already offer, and the bar that a same-day sequencing service must clear.

The next marker is peer-reviewed publication of the paediatric study, along with any multi-centre trial that tests the approach in other units.
