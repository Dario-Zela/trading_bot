TSMC posts record profit while the wider chip sector sells off

In one sentence
TSMC posted record quarterly profit of NT$763bn, yet chip stocks fell, and JPMorgan trimmed ASML's target to $2,350, leaving unresolved whether AI has broken the old chip cycle.

TSMC, the Taiwanese company that makes most of the world's most advanced chips, reported the biggest quarterly profit in its history on Thursday. Chip shares fell anyway. The sector's reaction to the results is a better guide to the market's mood than the results themselves.

The numbers

For the three months to 30 September, TSMC reported net income of NT$763.06bn, up 68.7% on a year earlier, according to its filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission. Revenue was NT$1,494.24bn (about US$46.7bn), up 50.9%. Diluted earnings were NT$29.43 a share. Gross margin was 66.4%, a figure that would be remarkable for a firm that spends heavily on factories.

The result also beat expectations. Analysts polled by LSEG had looked for net profit of around NT$740.8bn. The company guided to fourth-quarter revenue of US$52.3bn to US$54.3bn and a gross margin of 65% to 67%. Chief financial officer Wendell Huang said business was "supported by strong demand for our leading-edge process technologies", and pointed to "the steep ramp-up of our 2-nanometer technology" in the fourth quarter. Press coverage counts this as the sixth consecutive record quarter.

Nothing in that is a warning. Even so, TSMC's shares were indicated lower at the open, and AMD fell 5.7% as chip stocks slid on doubts about AI spending.

A sell-off without a culprit

The sell-off is not explained by TSMC's own accounts. Margins are above guidance, demand is described as strong and the next quarter is guided up. The same pattern showed at ASML, the Dutch maker of the lithography machines on which TSMC's output depends. Its third-quarter sales of €11.7bn rose 55% and its gross margin of 57.5% beat guidance, yet the shares fell. Investors judged that the good news was already in the price.

On 15 October JPMorgan trimmed its ASML price target to $2,350 from $2,400, according to MarketBeat, while keeping an overweight rating. The bank's worry is supply rather than demand: ASML's capacity constraints through 2027–28 may limit how quickly strong orders turn into revenue. The cut is small, about 2%, and other houses moved the other way. Barclays raised its target to €2,500 and Citigroup to €2,300, though Bank of America also lowered its target, to $2,858 from $2,915. The target moves are mixed, and the shares are falling regardless.

The wider tape was weak too. Elsewhere in today's edition, the Dow touched 52,000 while chips sold off on fears of a Federal Reserve rate rise. A sector can fall for macro reasons while its earnings keep climbing. A higher discount rate hurts long-duration growth stocks, and chips are now priced as exactly that.

The cycle argument

The sell-off matters because of what the industry's history says about peaks. Semiconductors have a long record of boom followed by glut: capacity is built when profits are high, arrives when demand has softened, and prices fall. Memory has been the classic case. Investors have long treated record earnings, heavy capital spending and rising orders as the signals that a peak is near.

Benzinga has quoted a chip-industry veteran who argues that the AI build-out has "broken the old demand cycle". The argument is not confined to one voice. An IDC analyst has said AI infrastructure spending has rewired demand, so memory pricing could stay elevated into 2027 and beyond. The Financial Times has described investors split over whether new capacity from SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron will bring a glut or whether AI has ended memory's boom-and-bust pattern. The relevant evidence is that the buyers are a small group of hyperscalers with very large budgets, and that advanced AI chips have lead times of six to twelve months, so orders are placed well before delivery.

There are reasons for caution. The claim that the cycle has ended has been made in earlier booms. A supply-constrained market looks like a permanent shortage right until capacity catches up. Bank of America's Vivek Arya has cited cloud and AI infrastructure spending nearing $1.5 trillion by 2027; that forecast is the thing the cycle argument rests on, and it is a forecast about customers' budgets, not TSMC's order book.

The sell-off complicates both readings. If the old cycle were intact, record profit would be the cue to sell, and the market is selling. If the cycle were broken, record profit would be a reason to hold. The market is selling at the very moment the evidence for the new regime looks strongest, which suggests it is pricing something other than near-term earnings: rates, the sustainability of customers' spending, or the sheer level of valuations.

What to watch

The next data points are concrete. TSMC's monthly revenue reports will show whether the fourth-quarter guide of $52–54bn is on track. Hyperscaler results over the coming weeks will show whether capital spending plans have changed, since those budgets are the demand in question. And the Fed's next decision will test whether this week's weakness was about rates, as the Dow-and-chips story suggests, or about something that earnings cannot fix.
