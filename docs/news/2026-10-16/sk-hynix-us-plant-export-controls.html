SK Hynix ties its new US factory to looser chip-equipment exports to China

In one sentence
Reuters reports SK Hynix is making a new US NAND factory conditional on Washington approving more US equipment for its Dalian plant in China; the company denies seeking expansion.

A South Korean memory-chip maker is reported to be bargaining with Washington. If it gets more US equipment into its Chinese factory, it will build a new plant in the US. That is the substance of a Reuters exclusive published on 14 October, which cites two people familiar with the matter. Commerce did not respond to Reuters' request for comment. The report has a single source, and it comes with little hard detail.

What was reported

According to Reuters, SK Hynix is seeking US approval to ship American chipmaking equipment to its site in Dalian, in north-east China. It has made that approval a condition of building a new factory in the United States.

The US project would belong to Solidigm, SK Hynix's American unit, which makes NAND flash chips, the type used in solid-state drives. Solidigm wants to install new equipment in unused space at Dalian to produce NAND there. One of the sources told Reuters that more output from China would help Solidigm fund a NAND facility in the US. Solidigm is also weighing a stock-market listing as early as next year.

SK Hynix disputes the framing. It says "no capacity expansion is being sought for the Dalian Mod B, which was completed in 2023", and that the work is being done in close consultation with, and under authorization from, the US government. The report does not say how much equipment is involved, what the US plant would cost, or where it would be built, and no officials are named.

Why the licence matters

The request runs against the direction of recent US policy. Last year the Commerce Department revoked authorizations that had exempted SK Hynix and Samsung from applying for licences to bring US equipment into their Chinese fabs. Commerce said it did not expect to approve licences for equipment that would expand capacity or upgrade technology there. Existing tools could keep running, but the plants were meant to stay as they were.

Dalian is already a partial exception. Reuters notes that a 2026 licence covers the site but limits the quantity of equipment. The new request would, in effect, ask for more room under that limit.

That is where the dispute lies. SK Hynix says it wants to fit out space that already exists, not add capacity. Washington's rule turns on whether a change expands capacity or upgrades technology. Filling an empty hall with new tools could be read either way. The report does not say how Commerce views it.

The bargain

The Trump administration has been pressing SK Hynix, Samsung and other chipmakers to build plants in the US, and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has said he is in talks with both Korean companies. Tying a US investment to export-licence relief is a trade the administration could accept or reject. Either way, it puts two policy goals in tension. One is to bring chip manufacturing onshore. The other is to hold back China's memory industry.

The cost side matters for the industry. Memory is a commodity business with thin margins in the downturns, and capacity decisions are made years ahead. If output in China helps pay for a plant in America, the firm is treating its Chinese fab as a source of funds for the US build. For a memory maker, that is a plain commercial argument, though it may not persuade officials who want the Chinese sites frozen.

Other coverage this week shows the chip sector is under strain. See TSMC's record profit alongside the wider chip selloff and the Dow's move to 52,000 as chips fell on Fed-hike fears.

What to watch

The first test is whether Commerce acts on the Dalian licence, and whether any approval is described as capacity-neutral. A second is whether SK Hynix or Solidigm announces a US site and a figure, which would show how far the talks have gone. A third is whether Samsung seeks similar terms for its own Chinese plants. Until one of those happens, the report describes a negotiating position, not a deal.
