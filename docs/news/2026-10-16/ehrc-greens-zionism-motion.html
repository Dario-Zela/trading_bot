Equality watchdog writes to Greens over 'Zionism is racism' motion

In one sentence
The EHRC has reportedly contacted the Green Party over its 'Zionism is racism' conference motion, testing where protected political speech ends and equality law begins.

The Equality and Human Rights Commission, Britain's equality regulator, has contacted the Green Party over a conference motion that equates Zionism with racism. The BBC reports that the commission may view the motion as discriminatory. The EHRC's reasoning and any response from the party were not available when this piece was written, and the contact itself rests on that single report.

What the Greens voted for

At the party's conference in Brighton, in early October, members backed the motion by 1,022 votes to 813. It says Zionism should be "treated as any other form of racism". It also calls for a single Palestinian state in place of Israel, "with equal rights for all". An amendment meant to reduce the risk of the motion being turned against Jewish members who are linked to Zionist institutions was voted down.

The vote did not carry the party's senior figures with it. Four of its five MPs publicly opposed the motion, as did the former leader Caroline Lucas. The Jewish Greens group said its members were too divided to take an official position. Zack Polanski, the leader, has said he will not accept exclusion within the party. Reports differ on the motion's standing. Some call it formal party policy. Others say it is a political policy motion with no disciplinary force over members.

Why the regulator is involved

The EHRC's approach did not come from nowhere. Labour and Liberal Democrat MPs have written to the commission asking it to consider whether the Greens broke equality law. The Labour MPs asked for a formal investigation under section 20 of the Equality Act 2006. The Liberal Democrats pointed to the Equality Act 2010. The Campaign Against Antisemitism has made its own referral, alleging discrimination against Jewish members and associates. The government's independent adviser on antisemitism has also called for a probe.

The Jewish Labour Movement had already pointed to the commission's earlier finding against Labour. In that case the EHRC found the party responsible for indirect harassment of Jewish members. The JLM warned Polanski that the Greens risked the same outcome.

The legal line

The question is where protected political speech ends and discrimination law begins. Parties are covered by the Equality Act. They cannot, for example, discriminate against or harass members because of a protected characteristic, which includes religion and race. But a resolution on a state's founding ideology is also political opinion, which carries strong protection under the European Convention on Human Rights, and Zionism is held in many different forms by Jews and non-Jews alike.

Critics argue that for many British Jews, Zionism is bound up with identity, so branding it racism exposes them to hostility within the party. Defenders of the motion argue that it targets a political project and says nothing about Jewish people. The EHRC's stated reasoning, when it appears, will show which of those readings it finds more persuasive. It will also matter whether the commission treats the motion as policy or as conduct towards members.

A letter is not an investigation. The commission can escalate through formal inquiries and unlawful-act notices, but the Labour case took years. The thing to watch is whether the EHRC publishes its correspondence, or whether the Greens answer publicly by amending, clarifying or defending the text.
