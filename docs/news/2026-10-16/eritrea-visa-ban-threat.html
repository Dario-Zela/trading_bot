UK threatens visa bans on Eritreans over returns; Asmara calls it a 'gimmick'

In one sentence
Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has threatened a full visa ban on Eritreans unless Asmara takes back migrants, and Eritrea's embassy has dismissed the threat as a 'political gimmick'.

The Home Secretary has told Eritrea to take back its nationals who have no right to be in Britain or face a complete ban on Eritreans entering the country. Asmara's London embassy has answered that the threat is "nothing more than a perfunctory political gimmick". So far the dispute amounts to a threat, a rebuff and some narrower penalties. No full visa ban is in force.

What has actually changed

Shabana Mahmood has said all visas for Eritreans could be blocked unless a returns deal is agreed. Some measures have already started. From Thursday, Eritrean VIPs and officials lost fast-track access to UK visas. Eritrean diplomats in London now face a restriction on travelling more than 60 kilometres beyond the capital. Both penalties fall on the state and its officials rather than on ordinary applicants.

The government says Eritrea "abruptly" withdrew cooperation on returns earlier this year. A limited arrangement had run from 2024 until March 2026. Eritreans have long been among the nationalities most often seen arriving in small boats, and many of them are granted asylum. That makes forced returns politically salient but practically hard. A country that refuses to accept its own citizens, or to issue them travel documents, can stall removals almost indefinitely.

Asmara's answer

The embassy said its government does not accept the forced return of its citizens and has told British officials so on several occasions. It said the UK had shown no interest in meaningful dialogue on "dignified" returns. Reporting found no further reply from the Eritrean government beyond that statement.

The rebuff matters because of the leverage involved. Coverage noted it was unclear how much the restrictions would bite, since relatively few Eritreans travel to the UK legally. A visa ban hurts most when a country's elites or its students want to come. Eritrea is a closed, tightly controlled state, and its government may count the cost of a ban as low.

The precedent

Ms Mahmood has used the tactic before. In November 2025 she gave Angola, Namibia and the Democratic Republic of Congo a month to cooperate on returns or face visa sanctions. Namibia and Angola agreed to cooperate in December, and the DRC later followed. London has also suspended study visas for nationals of Afghanistan, Cameroon, Myanmar and Sudan, citing asylum abuse.

Those cases were different from Eritrea. The countries that gave way have substantial links to Britain through trade, education and diplomacy, and had more to lose. Eritrea has fewer such ties, and its government says its objection is one of principle.

What to watch

The test is whether the targeted measures change Asmara's position, or whether London escalates to the full ban it has threatened. A blanket ban on a nationality that mostly arrives by irregular routes would do little to the people the policy is aimed at. It would also strengthen the argument, already made by Reform UK, that visa bans should be extended to more countries. Any new deadline, or a first removal flight under a revived arrangement, would show whether the threat has teeth.
