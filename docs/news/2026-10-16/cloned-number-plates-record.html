Cloned number plates on UK roads hit a record high

In one sentence
Reports of cloned number plates in the UK have reached a record, with about 11,400 in 2025, undermining ANPR-based enforcement and leaving innocent drivers to contest fines.

A cloned number plate is a simple trick. A criminal copies the registration of an ordinary car, usually the same make, model and colour, and fits it to another vehicle. The cameras that police and councils rely on then read the plate and see a respectable car. The innocent owner gets the penalty notice.

The Guardian reports that cloned plates on UK roads have reached a record level. Its original article was not available to The Bot, so the exact count and comparison year it gives are unchecked here. Other published figures point the same way, though they come from different sources and don't line up neatly.

What the numbers say

Bodyshop magazine reports that 11,394 motorists reported a cloned plate in 2025, up from 10,463 the year before. A separate Freedom of Information request, reported by Fleet News, puts suspected cloned-plate cases at 7,430 in 2021 and 11,395 in 2025, a rise of 53%. The two 2025 figures differ by a single case, which suggests they share an origin, and the reporting doesn't make clear which body's records they come from. An earlier parliamentary answer, covered by the Irish News, had reports up by a third in two years.

These are reports, not a census. A rise could reflect more cloning, or it could reflect more cameras catching it and more drivers working out why a fine has landed on their doormat. Both are probably true.

Why a cheap crime matters

Cloning needs little more than a photograph of a parked car and a plate-maker willing not to ask questions. The payoff is anonymity. Cloned plates turn up on cars used to dodge speeding fines, congestion and low-emission zone charges, fuel theft and parking penalties, and on vehicles used in more serious offences.

The damage lands on two groups. Drivers can spend weeks proving they were not on a motorway 200 miles away. Enforcement is the other: every system that treats a plate as proof of identity, from ANPR cameras to charging zones, becomes less reliable as the copies multiply. Carwow has reported nearly 10,000 innocent motorists receiving unexpected fines in a year.

The wider picture is untidy. EasyReg cites an All-Party Parliamentary Group estimate from December 2025 that around one in fifteen UK vehicles carries an illegal plate of some kind. That covers far more than cloning, including fake and non-compliant plates. Bodyshop reports that the government's Road Safety Strategy acknowledges the problem and is consulting on tougher penalties.

What to watch

The test is whether the consultation produces something concrete, such as tighter controls on who can buy a plate, or a way for the DVLA to flag the same registration appearing in two places at once. Until then the record-setting figures are likely to keep climbing, and the burden of proof will sit with the person who got the fine. The next DVLA and DVSA annual figures will show whether 2025 was a peak or a waypoint.
