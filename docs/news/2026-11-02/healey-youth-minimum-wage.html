Healey risks breaking minimum wage promise for younger workers

In one sentence
Reports say Healey may fall short of Labour's pledge on pay for younger workers, a cost question for retail and hospitality that also tests the government's credibility.

A Labour pledge to end lower minimum pay for younger workers looks increasingly hard to keep, and Healey is the minister reported to be at risk of missing it. BBC Politics, with Laura Kuenssberg reporting, is the named source. If the promise slips, the cost question lands on retail and hospitality, the sectors that employ the most under-21s.

The Bell Tribune has not been able to read the original BBC report. The detail below comes from secondary coverage of the minimum wage decision and should be read with that caveat.

The pledge

Britain has a tiered minimum wage. Workers aged 21 and over receive the National Living Wage, and 18-to-20-year-olds receive a lower rate. Labour went into government promising to give all adults the same pay protection, which means closing that gap.

The party has never attached a date to it. Reports say ministers have declined to set a timeline for ending lower rates for 18-to-20-year-olds. The gap has also been the subject of a long-running argument about whether sharp rises make employers less willing to hire the young.

What the reports say

Several outlets, citing the Times, reported that the main rate would rise by 3.9%. That would take the adult rate to £13.21 an hour. The 18-to-20 rate would rise more slowly, to £11.27, which would leave a gap of nearly £2 an hour. These figures come from secondary reporting around the Budget and are not confirmed against official announcements.

If they hold, the younger rate would be rising more slowly than the adult one. That is the opposite of what narrowing the gap requires. A slower rise on its own does not break the pledge, because the promise has no date. A wider gap at the end of the next uprating round would make the promise harder to defend.

The TUC is reported to have argued that holding back youth pay would be a mistake. Business groups have argued that fast rises discourage hiring of younger workers. Those are the two positions the government is caught between.

Why retail and hospitality

Shops, cafés, pubs and restaurants run on thin margins and rely heavily on younger staff. Raising the 18-to-20 rate towards the adult rate would raise their wage bills directly. It would also push up the pay of slightly older staff, because employers tend to preserve differentials. These employers have already absorbed several years of rises in the adult rate and in employer National Insurance costs.

The pledge matters for the government's credibility as well. A promise on pay for the young, made on the grounds of fairness, is hard to drop quietly when youth unemployment is a live political worry.

What to watch

The next test is whether the government publishes a timetable for equalising the rates, or a formal response to the Low Pay Commission's recommendations on the younger bands. The Budget statement and any accompanying remit for the Commission would show whether the pledge is being kept, deferred or reworded. The original BBC report should be checked for Healey's exact words before any of the figures above are relied on.
