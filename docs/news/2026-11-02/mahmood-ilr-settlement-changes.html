Mahmood to make migrants wait 10 years or more for settlement

In one sentence
Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is due to confirm that many migrants will wait 10 years or more to settle, though reported concessions may spare care workers already in the UK.

Shabana Mahmood is due to set out in Parliament today how long migrants must wait before they can settle permanently in Britain. For many, the answer will be 10 years or more. That would double the five years that has long been the standard route to indefinite leave to remain (ILR), the status that ends most immigration restrictions and is the usual step before citizenship. The Home Secretary has framed the change as turning settlement from a default entitlement into something that has to be earned. The exact terms had not been published when this article was written, and they may differ from the plan that has been in circulation for a year.

From five years to ten

The idea is not new. Mahmood first set it out at the Labour conference on 29 September 2025, and the government published detailed proposals on 20 November 2025 under the label of "earned settlement". The consultation ran until 12 February. The core rule was a 10-year baseline, with shorter routes for people judged to contribute more. Reports of the proposals mention a wait as short as three years for high earners.

The plan also set conditions and penalties. Applicants would need a clean criminal record, a high standard of English, no debts and a record of paying national insurance. Reports describe waits of up to 20 years for those who had claimed benefits for more than a year, and for overstayers and people who entered illegally. The government estimated that about 1.6 million people could have become eligible to settle between 2026 and 2030, and the new rules would push many of them back.

Mahmood presented this as a question of fairness and control. Her argument, in the words reported at the time, was that settlement is "not a right but a privilege". The Hong Kong British National (Overseas) route was left at five years.

The care and NHS problem

The sharpest dispute was over lower-paid workers. Under the 2025 proposals, people in jobs below graduate level, RQF level 6, faced a 15-year wait. That included social care workers on health and care visas, who were recruited in large numbers from 2022 to fill vacancies the sector could not. The rules were to apply to people who had arrived since 2021 and were already part-way along the five-year route. That retrospective element caused the most anger.

Reports say the government later exempted NHS nurses, doctors and teachers. Private-sector nurses, healthcare assistants and residential care staff were not. NHS Employers, in its response to the consultation, called a 15-year route for below-RQF6 roles unfair and harmful to retention. Care workers protested outside the Labour conference in Liverpool, and their main demand was that people already in the UK keep their five-year track.

The pressure has had an effect. Since September, several outlets, including HuffPost UK and the Guardian-reported briefings that prompted this piece, have said Mahmood would soften the plan. They say she is expected to exempt care workers already living in the UK from the longer wait, and that she has been meeting union leaders to outline concessions. Those accounts rest on briefings, not a published document. Whether the exemption extends to new arrivals, and whether the 10-year baseline survives intact, will only be clear once the statement is made and the rules are laid out.

Labour supply and the politics

The economic argument is simple. Settlement matters to employers because it gives a worker the security to stay, buy a home and bring a family. If the wait lengthens, some staff may leave for countries with quicker routes. Care providers, NHS trusts and universities have said this could worsen shortages and raise recruitment costs. The sectors affected include hospitality, which also relies heavily on migrant staff.

The politics are as awkward. A government that has promised to bring down net migration has to be seen to tighten the rules, and a significant part of the Labour party has objected to doing so retrospectively. The backlash has reportedly been loud enough to shift the Home Secretary's position. Any softening will be read by critics as a retreat, and any hard line as a breach of faith with people who came on the terms the government itself offered.

What to watch

The first test is the text of today's announcement: the headline waiting period, the treatment of people already in the UK, and any carve-outs for health and care roles. After that comes the legislative route. Changes to the immigration rules are laid before Parliament and can take effect without a full bill, so the date they come into force, and whether the government gives transition arrangements, will determine how many people are caught. Employers and unions will be looking for those details before they say how far the concessions go.
