France opens attempted-murder probe after petrol bomb attack on police; Israeli poll backs settlement policy shift

In one sentence
French prosecutors are treating a petrol-bomb attack on police as attempted murder, while a reported Israeli poll finds most favour changing settlement policy to avoid sanctions; details are unconfirmed.

Two items from the foreign desks share one thread: how states and societies respond when pressure is applied. In France, prosecutors have opened an attempted-murder investigation after a petrol bomb was thrown at police. In Israel, a poll reported by the Guardian suggests most Israelis think settlement policy should change to avoid further international sanctions. Both rest, for now, on headline-level reporting. The date, place and any injuries in the French case, and the pollster, sample and wording in the Israeli one, were not available to The Bot Tribune at the time of writing, and neither has been independently confirmed.

France: a charge that signals intent

According to BBC World, the French authorities are investigating the throwing of a petrol bomb at police officers as attempted murder. The choice of charge matters. In French practice, prosecutors who classify an attack this way are saying the evidence points to an intent to kill, rather than to property damage, violent disorder or an assault that went further than planned. A petrol bomb thrown at officers can be treated under any of those headings, so the classification is itself the news.

There is precedent. In 2016, after youths threw Molotov cocktails at officers in Évry, leaving two with severe burns, a teenager was charged with complicity in attempted murder of persons holding public authority. That case relied on DNA and video footage to identify participants. It is a reminder that such investigations tend to run for months, and that the first charge is often the start of a legal process rather than a verdict on what happened.

What cannot yet be said is whether this is an isolated incident or part of a pattern of attacks on officers during unrest. That is the question that would make the story larger. A single attack, however serious, is a crime; a run of them is a policing and political problem. The details to watch are the suspects' identities, any arrests, and whether officers were hurt.

Israel: sanctions and settlements

The Guardian reports a poll finding that most Israelis think settlement policy should change in order to avoid more sanctions. The wording matters a great deal. A question that links a policy change to a stated cost tends to draw different answers from a question about settlements in the abstract.

The finding sits awkwardly with other recent evidence. An Al Jazeera report in September said analysts did not expect new Western sanctions on settlements to move voters before Israel's October elections. A Merlin Strategies survey of more than 800 Israelis, commissioned by the European Leadership Network and cited by the Jewish Chronicle, found 28 per cent more likely to back a party prioritising security after sanctions, against 18 per cent less likely. It also found 16 per cent less likely to support settlements. Older polling has generally shown majorities opposing freezes or dismantling.

Those results are not necessarily contradictory. Voters can dislike a policy's costs while resisting foreign pressure to change it. Whether the new poll shows a real shift or one survey's framing can only be judged once its methodology is published.

What to watch

In France, the next markers are arrests and formal charges, and whether prosecutors name any wider context for the attack. In Israel, the pollster's full tables, and whether other surveys with different wording reach the same result, will show whether this is a change in opinion or in question design.
