Binance users withdraw $3.6bn after record hack as Tether and Circle freeze linked stablecoins

In one sentence
Binance says a hack cost it about $6.3bn and it will cover all losses, while roughly $3.5bn left the exchange and Tether and Circle froze about $2.4bn of linked stablecoins.

Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, says a hack on 29 October cost it about $6.3bn and that it will cover every dollar of user losses. The promise is being tested in public. Reported outflows of roughly $3.5bn to $3.6bn have been tied to the incident, withdrawals have been paused, and the two largest stablecoin issuers, Tether and Circle, have frozen tokens linked to the stolen funds. If the pledge holds, it sets a template for how an exchange backstops a breach. The freezes show that the stablecoin plumbing under crypto trading answers to a handful of issuers.

One caution on the numbers. Reports differ on what the $3.6bn is. The headline figure comes from the triage of BBC, Techmeme and investingLive coverage, which describes it as users pulling money out. Other reports describe about $3bn or more draining from Binance's Ethereum hot wallets, with an on-chain outflow tally near $3.5bn. Chainalysis estimated about $5.9bn had left the exchange, against Binance's own $6.3bn. Those are separate quantities, and public reporting does not cleanly split the attacker's take from customer withdrawals made around it. Everything below should be read with that uncertainty.

What Binance says happened

Binance detected the incident late on Thursday 29 October and suspended deposits and withdrawals on several networks while it investigated. Its founder, Changpeng Zhao, said it looked like a hack. The exchange's preliminary explanation, as relayed in press reports, is that a compromised engineer's laptop allowed unauthorised withdrawals. That is an early account and not a finished forensic report.

Binance has said user funds are safe and that it will make customers whole, drawing on its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU) and company money. It has given mixed signals on timing. One report says withdrawals could resume within 48 hours, while another notes no target date has been set.

The sums are large against that backstop. SAFU has historically been sized in the low billions of dollars, and a $6.3bn loss would exceed it. The pledge therefore depends on Binance's wider balance sheet and on how much is recovered.

The freezes

The recovery effort leans heavily on stablecoin issuers. Tether chief executive Paolo Ardoino said the company had frozen more than $1.57bn of USDT. Circle chief executive Jeremy Allaire said Circle had frozen more than $800m of USDC. Together that is about $2.4bn, the figure investingLive used in its report. Binance's own on-chain tally of frozen assets is higher, at about $3bn so far.

A freeze restricts tokens. It does not return them to Binance, and any restitution would have to follow a separate process. Still, the mechanism worked as designed: tokens issued by a private company can be made unspendable by that company, within days, on request or on its own judgment.

For Binance the effect is helpful, because it shrinks the amount the attackers can cash out. For the wider market it is a reminder that a dollar-pegged token carries issuer risk. The same control that locks a thief out can lock out an innocent holder whose tokens are swept into a freeze. The hack's target was one exchange, but the controls that responded sit with two issuers.

The suspects and the market

Early attribution points to North Korea. TRM Labs, relayed by Techmeme, and BBC Business describe Lazarus-linked actors as prime suspects, and ZachXBT and Blockaid have made similar calls. This is a suspicion based on on-chain behaviour and past patterns, not a confirmed finding or an official government attribution. North Korean groups have been blamed for several earlier large thefts, which is why the label arrives quickly. It is not proof.

Bitcoin was reported sliding towards $77,000 around the time of the hack. The reports give no size or timing for the move, and nothing establishes that the hack caused it. A move of that kind could reflect broader risk sentiment, which was also weak in Asia that week as equities sold off (see the AI capex selloff piece). The more reliable market signal will be exchange outflows and stablecoin balances once withdrawals reopen.

What the run tests

The pause itself bears on the user-run question. With withdrawals suspended, the pressure is on what happens at reopening. A run is a confidence problem, not an arithmetic one. Binance can say customers are covered, but customers will test that claim by asking for their money back the moment they can.

Three things will settle it:

  • Reopening. Whether Binance resumes withdrawals at the pace it has hinted, and whether net outflows after reopening are modest or heavy.
  • The accounting. Whether Binance publishes a full reconciliation of the loss, the frozen amount and the reserves behind its guarantee, rather than the preliminary figures now circulating.
  • Recovery. How much of the roughly $2.4bn to $3bn frozen can be returned, and how quickly.

For anyone running automated strategies that rely on Binance liquidity or on USDT and USDC settlement, the lesson is concrete. Exchange solvency and issuer freeze policy are operational risks, and both can change within hours.
