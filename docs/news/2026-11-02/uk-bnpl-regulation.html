New UK buy now pay later rules bring Klarna and Clearpay under regulation

In one sentence
The FCA has regulated buy now pay later since 15 July 2026, forcing Klarna, Clearpay and rivals to meet affordability, disclosure and Ombudsman standards, with full-authorisation applications due by about January 2027.

Britain's buy now pay later market has been inside the Financial Conduct Authority's perimeter since 15 July 2026. The rules are in force, not pending. The change covers the interest-free, pay-in-three or pay-in-30-days products offered by Klarna, Clearpay and others, which for years sat in a gap in consumer-credit law. For the firms it is a compliance project. For shoppers it has so far mostly meant different paperwork at checkout.

What changed

The government closed an exemption that let short-term, interest-free instalment credit sit outside the Consumer Credit Act regime. The new category is "deferred payment credit". Lenders offering it must be authorised by the FCA, or be in the regulator's temporary permissions regime, which let firms keep trading while their applications are prepared. According to the FCA's published timetable, firms could register for that regime between 15 May and 1 July 2026. They then have six months from commencement to apply for full authorisation, which puts the deadline around mid-January 2027. One law-firm summary gives an earlier registration cut-off, so the exact dates are best checked against the FCA's own notice.

The scope is narrower than the headlines suggest. Merchants that offer instalments under their own agreements are outside it, and agreements signed before 15 July stay unregulated. The regime follows the lender, not the shop.

What the lenders must now do

Three obligations carry most of the cost:

  • Affordability and creditworthiness. Providers must assess whether a borrower can repay, to FCA standards. Many BNPL products have been approved with light-touch checks and small limits.
  • Disclosure and conduct. The rules bring set requirements on what customers are told and how arrears and difficulty are handled, in line with those for cards and loans.
  • Redress. Customers can complain to the Financial Ombudsman Service about agreements made after 15 July. Reports on the rules also say purchases between £100 and £30,000 gain Section 75-style protection.

None of this is exotic. Card issuers and personal-loan lenders have worked under comparable rules for years. For a BNPL provider, though, the checkout flow is the product. Anything that adds friction, such as a fuller credit search or a longer disclosure, works against the pitch of approval in seconds.

Who pays

The economics are the open question. BNPL providers earn mainly from merchant fees rather than interest, so the extra cost of checks, complaints handling and authorisation falls on their operating margin. It does not appear in a rate they can raise. Heavier checks also cut both ways: they should screen out customers who are over-extended, but they may decline some thin-file borrowers, such as younger people and those with little credit history. These are the users for whom a small, interest-free instalment plan was the main draw.

Neither Klarna nor Clearpay is a stranger to regulators, and both are widely reported to have prepared for the regime. This reporting found no firm-specific authorisation status for either; the FCA's Firm Checker is the place to confirm it. The sector-wide effect is more likely to be a gradual rise in compliance cost than a one-off market event.

What to watch

The date that matters now is the end of the temporary permissions window in mid-January 2027, by which time firms must have applied for full authorisation. Which providers apply, and which exit the UK market or narrow their products instead, will show how much the new costs are biting. The first Ombudsman complaint data will show whether the new protections are being used.
