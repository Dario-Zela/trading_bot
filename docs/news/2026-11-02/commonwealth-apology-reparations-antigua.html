UK open to a slavery apology, rules out reparations, as the King visits Antigua

In one sentence
UK ministers are reportedly open to apologising for slavery but rule out reparations, and the Guardian says London pushed to keep the issue off the Commonwealth agenda as the King visits Antigua.

The British government is prepared to say sorry for slavery. It is not prepared to pay for it. That is the position that, according to reports, ministers are carrying into the Caribbean this week as the King visits Antigua, where he is expected to address the legacy of the slave trade directly.

The distinction matters because it is the live fault line between London and a number of Caribbean governments. An apology costs little in budgetary terms and can be delivered in a sentence. Reparations, meaning compensation, debt relief or development funding framed as redress, carry legal, fiscal and precedent risks that successive UK governments have refused to take on.

An apology, but not a cheque

The reporting rests on a thin base. Burnham is described as "open" to an apology for slavery while ruling out reparations. The Guardian reports that the UK was "vehement" that reparations stay off the Commonwealth agenda. That is the newspaper's characterisation of the British stance, not a quoted document, and the details of any proposed apology, including its form, wording and timing, have not been published.

The formula is not new. David Cameron, speaking in Jamaica in 2015, urged Caribbean leaders to "move on from the painful legacy of slavery" and offered investment in a prison in place of compensation. Tony Blair expressed "deep sorrow" in 2006, stopping short of a formal apology. What would be different now is a government moving from regret to apology while holding the line on money. Ministers appear to be betting that the first can be given without conceding the second.

The royal dimension

The King's tour makes the timing awkward. A separate strand of reporting, from the Press Gazette diary and BBC UK, describes a King and Queen's Caribbean tour that includes Antigua, with Commonwealth talks on slavery and reparations scheduled to coincide. Neither the dates nor the exact link between the tour and the summit agenda has been confirmed in the material available.

The monarch's position is constitutionally delicate. The King acts on the advice of ministers, so any remarks on slavery will be read as reflecting government policy as much as personal feeling. In 2022, Prince William described the slave trade as "abhorrent" and said it "should never have happened" during a tour that drew protests in Jamaica and elsewhere. Several Caribbean states have since weighed removing the monarch as head of state, which raises the stakes of any royal gesture.

What the royals will say in Antigua, or whether they will say anything beyond acknowledgement, is not yet known.

Why the agenda fight matters

Caribbean governments, working through the regional body Caricom, have for years pressed a ten-point reparations plan covering formal apologies, debt cancellation, development funding and educational programmes. The Commonwealth, with its consensus-based communiqués, is where such demands can acquire collective weight. Keeping reparations off the agenda is therefore a procedural choice with substantive effect: if an item is not formally tabled, there is no communiqué language to negotiate and no collective position for London to resist.

That is the reading the Guardian's "vehement" suggests. It is an inference from one report, and other outlets will need to confirm how hard the UK pushed and how other member states responded.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the line holds. The first is the wording of any royal remarks in Antigua, and whether they go beyond earlier expressions of regret. The second is whether ministers set out a formal apology and in what forum. The third is the Commonwealth meeting's final communiqué: if reparations appear in it, even in softened language, the UK will not have kept the issue off the table. If they do not, Caribbean leaders will have to decide whether to push the matter outside the Commonwealth framework.
