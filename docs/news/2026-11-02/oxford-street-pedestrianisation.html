Oxford Street pedestrianisation moves forward

In one sentence
A plan to pedestrianise Oxford Street is listed in this week's news diary, but its status and detail are unverified, so it is a watch item rather than a confirmed decision.

A plan to pedestrianise Oxford Street, London's best-known shopping street, is listed in this week's Press Gazette news diary. That listing is the only thing behind the headline. No plan document, decision, consultation paper or date has been checked, so it is not clear whether the scheme is a proposal, a consultation or an approved project. This is a watch item, not a confirmed development.

What is known

Oxford Street runs through Westminster in central London and is home to flagship stores. Selfridges and Next are among the retailers most often associated with it. Both points rest on general knowledge, not a current source.

The diary entry says the topic is on the news calendar. It does not say what is being decided, by whom, or whether any step this week is binding. The headline's "moves forward" is therefore a description of the listing, not of a ruling. Attempts to take traffic off the street have a history of stalling. A scheme floated in 2017 was abandoned after local opposition, according to widely reported accounts. This time may differ, but nothing retrieved so far shows it.

Why retailers would care

If a scheme is confirmed, the commercial questions are fairly narrow, and none can be answered yet.

  • Footfall. Removing buses, taxis and general traffic could change how many people linger and where they walk. Whether that lifts sales depends on the design and on how shoppers arrive. No figures for pedestrian counts or likely effects have been seen.
  • Delivery access. Stores that rely on frontage or rear-service deliveries would need a servicing plan. Time-restricted loading is a common compromise elsewhere. Any rules here are unknown.
  • Rents. Landlords may price in any expected footfall gain. Tenants may argue that disruption and access limits offset it. Neither argument can be tested without a scheme to price.
  • Precedent. A confirmed scheme on the country's most prominent retail street would give other high streets a template, and its results would be closely read.

What to watch

The useful evidence is documentary. A published decision or consultation paper from the relevant authorities would settle the scheme's status. It would also show the boundaries of any car-free zone, the timetable, and the treatment of buses and deliveries. Retailer statements, including any from Selfridges and Next, would show how the trade reads the plan. Until one of these appears, the diary listing tells readers that something is expected, not what it is.
