Seoul warns of 'further action' unless Ukraine apologises over POW row

In one sentence
President Lee Jae Myung says Seoul will take unspecified 'further measures' unless Ukraine publicly apologises for disclosing, then denying a confidential transfer of two North Korean POWs.

South Korea and Ukraine, two governments that have spent years on the same side of the argument over Russia's war, are now in an open quarrel over two prisoners. President Lee Jae Myung said on 2 October that Seoul will take "further measures" unless Kyiv publicly apologises for revealing, and then disputing, a confidential arrangement over captured North Korean soldiers.

What happened

The dispute concerns two North Korean soldiers captured fighting for Russia. According to the Korea Herald, they arrived in South Korea in mid-September in no serious medical difficulty. Seoul says Ukraine had asked that the transfer be kept quiet.

On 23 September, Volodymyr Zelensky disclosed the transfer in his address to the UN General Assembly. Seoul says it did not consent. On 25 September, Kyiv denied that any confidentiality agreement had existed. South Korea lodged a formal protest on 27 September and summoned Ukraine's acting ambassador. Senior officials demanded an explanation and an apology.

Mr Lee escalated on Friday. "This is not true," he said of Kyiv's denial, adding that the matter concerned "the dignity of the Republic of Korea and its people." His conditional threat was blunt: "If Ukraine continues to refuse to acknowledge the facts and publicly apologize, we will take further measures."

He did not say what those measures would be, and no timeline has been given.

Kyiv's response

Ukraine's foreign minister, Andrii Sybiha, has called it a "diplomatic misunderstanding" and said he hopes for a swift resolution. He has not offered the public apology Seoul wants. That leaves the two sides disagreeing on a narrow factual point: whether Seoul was promised discretion, and who said what.

Why a small row carries weight

The prisoners are few, but the context is sensitive. South Korea's National Intelligence Service has assessed that Mr Zelensky's disclosure appeared intended to build public support in South Korea for supplying arms to Ukraine, according to the Korea Herald. If that reading is right, Kyiv's UN remarks were aimed at a domestic audience in Seoul, and they have instead hardened its government.

Seoul has also long avoided sending lethal weapons directly to Ukraine, a policy rooted in its wish to manage relations with Moscow and its concern about the Korean peninsula. The South Korean arms industry is a major exporter, and Ukraine has repeatedly sought its munitions and systems. A public dispute makes any shift in that policy harder to argue for at home. The reverse also holds: a quiet settlement would remove an obstacle.

The prisoners are themselves a political matter. Seoul has said it is willing to accept North Korean captives, who under South Korean law can claim citizenship. The handling of two men is therefore tied to how the country treats the North Korean state and its soldiers. It is also bound up with Pyongyang's role in Russia's war. The disclosure came days after North Korean mines exploded in the demilitarised zone on 21 September, injuring three South Korean soldiers, which has left public attention on the North high.

There is no evidence yet of any change in arms flows or sanctions, and the threat is unspecified. Markets are unlikely to price this directly. Any read-across to defence-sector sentiment would depend on Seoul actually curtailing cooperation, which it has not announced.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether Kyiv offers some form of acknowledgement, or whether Seoul names its measures. The signals to follow are the status of Ukraine's acting ambassador in Seoul, any further diplomatic downgrades, and whether Mr Sybiha's "misunderstanding" framing is followed by a direct contact with Mr Lee's government. A face-saving formula that stops short of a full apology is the likeliest middle path, but Mr Lee's wording leaves little room for it.
