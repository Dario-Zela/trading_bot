Fed officials keep more hikes open as construction spending beats forecasts

In one sentence
Kashkari, Jefferson and Schmid signalled caution and optionality after September's hike, while a 0.9% jump in August construction spending kept the economy-is-resilient case alive.

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September, and the officials now explaining that decision are not speaking with one voice. Three of them, Neel Kashkari, Philip Jefferson and Schmid, have each said something in recent days about what comes next. The common thread is caution. None of them has announced a plan for further increases, and none has ruled one out.

The remarks arrived alongside a US construction spending report for August that was stronger than expected. Taken together, they show a central bank that wants room to move in either direction.

Three officials, three emphases

Kashkari, who runs the Minneapolis Fed, said he has no strong view on whether the Fed should raise rates again in October. That is a modest statement, but it carries information. An official committed to a tightening path would say so. Saying there is no strong view treats the October meeting as open, with the decision resting on data that has not yet been published.

Jefferson, the Fed's vice chair, went in the same direction. He indicated that the central bank may take more time before making its next move. The phrase implies a slower pace, not a halt. Taking more time is a different thing from stopping, and it commits the Fed to nothing beyond waiting to see what the economy does.

Schmid, who leads the Kansas City Fed, pointed somewhere else. He named energy prices as the main policy challenge. That widens the frame beyond the core inflation gauges that usually dominate Fed commentary. Energy is volatile and driven largely by supply, which rate policy influences only weakly. A Fed that is worried about energy has a harder problem, because tightening does little to add barrels. The same pressure shows up in fuel markets, where diesel supplies are tightening in Europe and China.

The three comments do not amount to a position. Read together, they say the Fed has not chosen a destination and is leaving more than one option available.

A construction number that complicates the picture

The August data points the other way. US construction spending rose 0.9% in the month, against economist forecasts of no change at all. A miss of nearly a full percentage point on a series that usually moves in small steps is large.

Construction matters to policymakers for two reasons. It is closely tied to business investment and hiring. It is also among the sectors most sensitive to borrowing costs, so strength there after a year of higher rates suggests the economy is absorbing tighter policy better than forecasters assumed.

That helps the argument Kashkari and others have made for keeping rates elevated. A resilient economy gives the Fed less reason to ease, and it makes a pause easier to defend, because policy is not obviously choking activity. It does not, by itself, make the case for another increase. One monthly reading, particularly one that beat a flat forecast, is a data point, not a trend, and construction figures are routinely revised.

What the divergence means for markets

The headline reading, that more hikes remain possible, is accurate but incomplete. The officials have kept that option open, and the construction data gives them a reason to. What they have not done is guide markets toward it. The emphasis in all three sets of remarks is on timing, patience and conditions.

That matters for pricing. Traders had built in additional increases, and the language from the Fed's side suggests the path is less predetermined than that implies. If the committee treats each meeting as a fresh decision, near-term hike probabilities should narrow. Hedges bought against aggressive tightening, such as options on rate volatility, would then look more expensive than the risk they cover.

The backdrop is not calm. Long-term borrowing costs have been climbing, with the 10-year Treasury yield reaching 5.25%, a 24-year high, which does some of the Fed's tightening on its own. Officials have also begun flagging AI as a market and inflation risk, a sign that the list of things they are watching keeps growing.

What to watch

The next test is the data that arrives before the October meeting: employment and inflation readings, and the energy prices Schmid singled out. A soft labour report or easing fuel costs would let Kashkari's lack of a strong view settle into a pause. Another upside surprise on the scale of the construction number would make Jefferson's more time harder to sustain. Until then, the Fed is signalling that it will decide in October, on the numbers available then.
