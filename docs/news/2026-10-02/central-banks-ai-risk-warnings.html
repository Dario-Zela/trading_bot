Bank of England and Fed officials flag AI as a market and inflation risk

In one sentence
The Bank of England warns AI-linked valuations risk a sudden correction, while the Fed's Lisa Cook calls AI the top 2027 risk and says it is already creating pockets of inflation.

Two of the world's most watched central banks have now named artificial intelligence as a threat to stability. They disagree about the mechanism. The Bank of England's Governor, Andrew Bailey, has cautioned that the valuations and investment flows tied to AI leave markets exposed to a sudden correction. Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook has gone further on the price side, naming AI as the top macroeconomic risk for 2027 and saying it is already producing "pockets of inflation". One warning concerns asset prices and the other concerns consumer prices. Both come from officials who are paid to be dull about such things.

The Bank of England's concern: concentration

The Governor's argument rests on concentration. A large share of recent equity gains and corporate capital spending has gathered in a narrow set of AI-exposed companies and the suppliers around them. When that many investors hold the same bet, the market has few natural sellers if sentiment turns. A reversal then tends to arrive quickly rather than gradually.

The point is about structure, not a prediction of timing. Central banks do not usually say a market is overvalued. They say it is vulnerable to a repricing, and that such a repricing could spread through leveraged positions and credit lines. The BBC's reporting frames the Governor's remarks in those terms, as a financial-stability consideration rather than a view on any single stock.

The distinction matters because it moves the topic from analyst commentary into the vocabulary of regulation. Official warnings of this kind often precede closer scrutiny of leverage, margining and the behaviour of systematic funds. The triage material does not show any rule change proposed. Whether the Bank's Financial Policy Committee follows its Governor's language with specific requirements is the open question. Algorithmic traders that run momentum or concentrated sector exposure would feel the effect of any such rules first.

The Fed's concern: bottlenecks, not froth

Cook's warning, reported by Benzinga, approaches the subject from the other side. The common story about AI and inflation is demand-driven: spending on data centres and chips overheats the economy. Cook instead emphasised supply. Investment on this scale strains the supply chains for components, power and construction capacity, and shortages in those areas push up prices locally. Her phrase for the result was "pockets of inflation".

She also warned about inflation expectations becoming "unanchored". That is the more serious part of the message. A few pockets of price pressure can be tolerated. Expectations that drift away from the Fed's target cannot, because they make inflation harder to bring back down. By pairing the two points, Cook is signalling that the Fed sees an AI-driven supply shock as a possible threat to price stability, not just a sector story.

This fits with other recent Fed commentary. Other officials have kept further rate rises open after stronger-than-expected construction spending, and a bond sell-off has pushed the US 10-year yield to 5.25%. Supply-side pressure from AI-related building is one plausible contributor to that backdrop, though the sources do not establish a causal link.

Two warnings, one exposure

The two warnings are connected. If AI investment drives bottlenecks and inflation, rates stay higher for longer. Higher rates reduce the present value of the distant earnings that justify AI valuations. That is the route by which a Fed inflation concern could become a BoE valuation concern. The chain is plausible rather than proven, and neither official has described it explicitly in the material available.

For investors the practical result is a change in who is expected to be the buyer of last resort. Markets have long assumed that central banks would ease if asset prices fell sharply. If the same investment boom is also feeding inflation, that assumption weakens, and the Fed has less room to cushion a correction without risking its price target.

What to watch

Three things will show whether the warnings harden into policy. The first is the Bank of England's next Financial Stability Report and Financial Policy Committee record, which will show whether concentration risk is described as a priority and whether leverage in non-bank funds is mentioned. The second is the next US inflation release, where components tied to electricity, construction materials and electronics would be the first evidence for Cook's bottleneck argument. The third is the next Fed communication on inflation expectations, since the word "unanchored" will be tested against survey and market measures. If those readings stay calm, the warnings will remain cautionary. If they move, the AI trade will be priced as a macro risk, not just a growth story.
