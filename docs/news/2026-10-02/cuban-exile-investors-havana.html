The wealthy Cuban Americans waiting for Havana to fall

In one sentence
Wealthy Miami exiles are drafting plans, from a stock exchange to power barges, for a post-communist Cuba, but the money is pledged, not deployed, and no regime change is in sight.

Somewhere in Miami, an 82-year-old tobacco entrepreneur is designing a stock exchange for a country whose government has not fallen. Juan Omar Sixto chairs the newly created Cuban-American National Chamber of Commerce. He says he and his colleagues are ready to "go and do business in Cuba right away", once change comes. The condition matters more than the confidence. This is a bet on an event, not a position in a market.

A government-in-waiting, with a balance sheet

The group is a loose network of wealthy and politically connected Cuban Americans. According to reporting by the BBC and the Miami Herald, they are sketching a post-communist Cuba in unusual detail. The ideas include a stock exchange, emergency floating power stations to patch a failing grid, airport renovations and billions of dollars of private investment.

The scale is stated as an ambition, not a fact. Sixto told the Herald that if the group reaches its target of 100 wealthy Cuban-American families, their combined net worth would be around $200 billion. That is a recruiting goal, not a fund. The group's first gathering took place in Miami in March. Sixto also says an investment summit is being organised for Havana.

What is, and isn't, known

The reporting does not name funds, bond purchases, property deals or committed sums. There is no trading volume to point to and no price in any listed asset that can be tied to this activity. Cuba has no investable market in the usual sense, and the island's defaulted sovereign debt is the obvious instrument for anyone who wanted exposure. None of the accounts reviewed says anyone is buying it.

The "US pressure" behind the timing is also loosely sourced. Some coverage links it to Washington's wider posture after Venezuela, and notes that the war with Iran is seen as limiting how much attention the White House can give Havana. No specific policy action or deadline has been cited. Havana, for its part, told exiles in March that the "doors are open" to invest, so the government is courting some of the same people who are planning for its end.

Why it matters beyond Miami

For traders, the interest is in the structure of the bet. Capital here is pledged, not deployed. It is conditional on a regime change that no one can date, and it is paid for mostly in planning and political access. That is closer to an option than a position. It tells investors where wealthy people think a tail risk sits. It does not show money moving, and it gives no reason to expect a repricing of anything before an actual political break.

The record of exile optimism is long. "Next year in Havana" has been a toast in Miami for more than six decades. A stock exchange blueprint is a better prepared version of the same hope.

The things to watch are concrete. One is whether the Havana investment summit is held and who attends. Another is any move by Washington on sanctions or the embargo that would make the conditional capital less conditional. The third is whether anyone puts a name and a dollar figure on a first deal.
