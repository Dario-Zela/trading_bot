PM warned Rosebank oil field could breach sanctions

In one sentence
Campaigners and the Greens have warned that approving Rosebank could collide with UK plans to sanction settlement-linked firms, because 20% owner Ithaca is controlled by Delek Group.

The government has been warned that approving the Rosebank oil field off Shetland could cut across its own plans to sanction firms that profit from illegal Israeli settlements. The reason is one of the field's owners. Ithaca Energy, which holds 20% of Rosebank, is controlled by Israel's Delek Group, a company the UN lists as supporting "the maintenance and existence" of settlements in occupied Palestinian territories.

The warning follows letters from Green Party leader Zack Polanski, and from campaign groups including Stop Rosebank and Greenpeace, according to BBC reporting. Polanski wrote to Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband. Miliband announced last month that the UK would draw up sanctions on companies trading with illegal settlements in the West Bank.

A test of the sanctions regime

Polanski framed Rosebank as a test case.

"Rosebank is a test of whether Labour's proposed sanctions regime is fit for purpose,"

He asked how the government would square approving the field with its stated intent to sanction those who finance or facilitate illegal settlements. He also said the government "seems to be getting ready to greenlight Rosebank."

The money involved is not trivial. Revenues to Delek from Rosebank alone are expected to reach about £235 million, according to the Green Party's own figures. Ithaca is the second-largest operator in the North Sea, so the question reaches beyond one field.

The company disputes the premise. A spokesman told the Daily Telegraph that it no longer has any involvement in Israeli settlements and was wrongly included in the UN database. Delek has said it intends to challenge the listing formally. The Foreign Office declined to comment.

What the warning does and does not establish

The distinction matters for anyone judging the project's prospects. The UN database is a listing, not a sanctions designation under UK law. The UK's own regime has been announced but, on the reporting so far, not yet written into rules. No UK authority has said Rosebank would breach anything. The warning is that approval could become awkward, or legally contestable, once the regime exists and if Delek is caught by it.

That is compliance friction rather than a verdict. Even so, Rosebank is a project where friction counts.

The existing legal cloud

The field is Britain's largest untapped oil resource, with an estimated 240 million barrels of recoverable oil and gas, and roughly 500 million barrels of oil equivalent projected over its lifetime. Equinor operates it with a 40% stake. Suncor holds another 40%, and Ithaca, via its 2022 purchase of Siccar Point, holds the remaining 20%.

The original 2023 approval was struck down by a Scottish court in January 2025. The court found that the environmental assessment had to consider downstream emissions from burning the extracted fuel. No oil can be produced until a fresh consent is granted, so the decision now pending carries two burdens: the climate-emissions test and now a sanctions question.

What to watch

Two things will show whether this is a footnote or a real obstacle. The first is the decision on Rosebank's fresh consent, which the government is reported to be preparing. The second is the drafting of the settlement sanctions regime. If its scope covers companies on the UN list, or those that finance them, Ithaca's stake becomes a live legal question. If it is narrower, Delek's challenge to its UN listing may matter more. Either way, the government will have to say publicly how the two policies fit together.
