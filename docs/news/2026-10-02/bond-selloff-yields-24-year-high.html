Bond sell-off pushes US 10-year yield to 5.25%, a 24-year high, as equities fall and Micron disappoints

In one sentence
A global bond sell-off pushed the US 10-year yield to about 5.25%, a 24-year high, dragging gilts up and the S&P 500 down as Micron disappointed.

The price of borrowing in the United States has reached a level last seen 24 years ago. The yield on the 10-year Treasury note rose to about 5.25% on Friday as a bond sell-off that began abroad spread through developed markets. Equities, which had been priced for lower rates, weakened. The S&P 500 fell, helped down by a disappointing report from Micron Technology.

The sequence matters. This was not an equity wobble that dragged bonds along with it. Government debt moved first, and shares followed.

A 24-year high

The 10-year yield is the benchmark against which mortgages, corporate loans and the present value of almost every other asset are set. At roughly 5.25% it is at its highest since 2002. A yield is the inverse of a price, so the move reflects investors demanding more compensation to hold US government debt, whether for inflation, for supply or for both.

The move was not confined to Treasuries. UK gilt yields rose in step, according to the Guardian's market coverage, which points to a common driver rather than a local one. When two of the world's largest sovereign markets reprice together, the usual suspects are shifting expectations for policy rates and a higher premium for holding long-dated paper. Both are consistent with recent central bank commentary, including Fed officials keeping further rate rises open (see related coverage in this edition).

The arithmetic of equities explains why the bond market sets the tone. A share is a claim on future earnings, discounted at a rate anchored to the risk-free yield. When that yield rises, the same earnings stream is worth less today. Long-duration assets, meaning those whose profits sit far in the future, suffer most. Higher yields also give investors a competing option: a government bond paying more than 5% asks for no earnings forecast at all.

Equities take the strain

The S&P 500 declined as capital moved toward fixed income. The triage material for this story refers to eight of the 11 S&P sectors falling, though sector-by-sector magnitudes were not available at the time of writing and are not given here.

The trade-off is the heart of the matter. Equity risk premiums, the extra return stocks must offer over bonds, narrow as yields climb. Either share prices fall to restore the premium, or investors accept less compensation for risk. Friday's session suggested the former, at least for now.

That said, a single session does not establish a regime. Rotations of this kind persist only while yields stay elevated and attractive relative to earnings yields. A retreat in Treasury yields would remove the pressure quickly; a push higher would intensify it.

Micron adds a company-specific problem

Micron's quarterly report gave equity investors an additional reason to sell. The memory-chip maker disappointed, and the shares were among the casualties. Specific figures for revenue, earnings per share and forward guidance were not confirmed in the material available for this article, so the size of the miss is not quantified here.

The reaction is nonetheless informative. Memory is the most commodity-like part of the AI hardware chain, with prices that swing sharply and capital spending that must be justified by demand years out. When financing costs rise, such businesses are among the first to be marked down. Coverage of the session also pointed to a Broadcom deal as a sign of investors favouring other corners of the AI trade, though the terms and customers involved were not established in the source material and should be treated cautiously.

A bullish dissent

Not everyone read the tape as a warning. Tom Lee of Fundstrat told investors to "buckle up" for a strong fourth quarter, citing historical seasonal strength in the S&P 500, according to Benzinga. The call is a statement about calendar patterns rather than about rates, and it came without, in the reporting available, a stated price target or return range.

The conflict is real. Seasonality is a statistical tendency; a 24-year high in the 10-year yield is a present fact about the price of money. If the two collide, the more plausible winner is the one that changes the discount rate. Lee's argument works best if yields stabilise, since a calm bond market would let the usual year-end pattern assert itself.

What to watch

The next test is whether the 10-year can hold at or above 5.25% or whether buyers step in at that level. Gilts offer a parallel read: continued moves in the same direction would confirm a global repricing rather than a US-specific one. Upcoming remarks from Federal Reserve and Bank of England officials, and the next US inflation and labour-market releases, are the likeliest catalysts. Until yields stop rising, equity valuations will keep being measured against a bond market that is paying more than it has in a generation.
