BEYOND THE TAPE · CRYPTO CRIME · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-10-02 · 3 min read

Crypto thieves attack man in his home in 'horrific' robbery

In one sentence
A Solihull businessman was beaten and his pregnant wife threatened in a crypto-motivated home invasion that the BBC describes as part of a growing pattern of UK 'wrench attacks'.

A businessman was beaten with hammers in his own home in Solihull, in the West Midlands, while masked intruders threatened to kill his pregnant wife's unborn child unless he handed over cryptocurrency. The BBC, which spoke to the man — identified only as James — reports that the attack was an example of a growing crime that investigators call a "wrench attack".

The name comes from a well-known cartoon. It makes the point that the most sophisticated encryption can be beaten by hitting someone with a wrench until they hand over the keys.

What happened

According to the BBC's account, three intruders forced their way into the couple's home. The assault lasted about 45 minutes. His wife, then around seven months pregnant with their third child, was threatened with a knife. In the account, she was held down on a sofa with a pillow over her face while she screamed that she could not breathe.

The attackers were not working alone. They took instructions from someone connected by live video call, and the couple were coerced into moving hundreds of thousands of pounds of cryptocurrency to a wallet the gang controlled. The baby survived and was born at full term. Crimestoppers is offering a £10,000 reward for information.

A different kind of risk

The detail that matters is the video link. It suggests a division of labour: someone off-site directs the transfer while others apply the force. That looks less like opportunistic burglary and more like organised crime that has found a way to use violence to bypass digital security.

Crypto has an awkward property here. A stolen bank transfer can often be frozen or reversed. A coin sent to a wallet the thieves control is hard to recall, and it moves quickly. That makes a frightened person with a phone a more attractive target than a locked vault.

The usual advice for crypto holders concerns passwords, hardware wallets and phishing. None of it helps when the attacker is standing in the living room. Wealth-protection planning for conventional assets assumes that a thief cannot simply order the owner to hand the assets over. The BBC's reporting treats this case as part of a pattern, with experts describing wrench attacks as a growing category in the UK. The triage material for this piece did not include a national count, and none is cited here.

The categorisation gap

The case also raises a question about how such crimes are classified. Recorded as digital theft, an attack like this sits among online fraud. Recorded as what it is — an armed, planned home robbery with threats to kill — it belongs with a very different set of statistics, and a different response from police, insurers and exchanges. Which of the two applies shapes what gets counted and what protection is offered.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the Solihull investigation, backed by the Crimestoppers reward, identifies the gang and whoever was directing it over video. Beyond that, the thing to watch is whether police forces and insurers begin to record these cases as violent robbery rather than cybercrime.
