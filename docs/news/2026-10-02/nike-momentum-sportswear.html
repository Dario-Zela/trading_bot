Nike's lost momentum weighs on the sportswear sector

In one sentence
Nike's weak quarter and high-single-digit full-year revenue forecast put pressure on retailers like JD Sports and raise the question of whether sportswear demand is softening more widely.

Nike has told investors that its sales will keep shrinking. That matters well beyond Beaverton, Oregon, because the company supplies a large share of what the rest of the sportswear trade puts on its shelves.

The figures for the quarter to the end of August, reported on 30 September, were mixed. Revenue was $11.21bn, short of the $11.35bn analysts had pencilled in, and down 4% on a reported basis (5% at constant currency). Earnings per share of $0.48 beat the $0.44 forecast. Gross margin rose to 42.8%, helped by supply-chain changes and lower overheads. Those two lines are the cost-side good news. Sales are the problem. Shares fell sharply in after-hours trading.

Where the weakness sits

The split by region is uneven. North America grew 2%, so the home market is not collapsing. Greater China fell 26%, and that decline is the main reason the group total is negative. Nike also reported lower sales in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, according to MarketScreener's account of the results.

The forecast did more damage than the quarter. Nike now expects full-year revenue to fall by a high-single-digit percentage, which would make it the company's weakest year since the one ending in May 2020. It also announced job cuts as part of a restructuring. In July, Nike had guided to a "low- to mid-single-digit" decline, so the outlook has worsened in three months.

A cut in costs can lift margins for a while. It cannot produce demand, and demand is the open question.

The read-across

Sportswear retailers are the most exposed. JD Sports, the British trainer retailer, relies on Nike for roughly 45% of its sales by one broker estimate. When Nike warned in July, JD shares fell 2.3% to 82.78p. Peel Hunt called the read-across "clearly not positive". It also said the risk was skewed slightly to the downside "absent a dramatic improvement in consumer confidence".

Adidas is a less clean comparison. The German group has taken market share from Nike in recent years, so a Nike stumble can help it at the margin. But its own shares fell 8% in March after profit guidance disappointed, which shows the sector's problems are not confined to one company. In July, Nike itself pointed to tariff uncertainty, Middle East disruption and weak consumer sentiment tied to high oil prices.

This piece does not have verified closing prices for Adidas, Puma or JD Sports for 1 October. Whether the three move together will show up in those prices and in the next trading updates.

That is the point of the read-across. If only Nike were struggling, the cause would be company-specific: a thin product pipeline, the loss of wholesale accounts, a slow reorganisation. If its rivals and its retail customers are also struggling, the cause is more likely to be consumers spending less on discretionary items such as trainers. Nike's own wording points to the second explanation as well as the first. The evidence so far is mixed. A 2% rise in North America and a 26% fall in China argue against a single global cause. The macro warnings in July and Adidas's March guidance point towards a wider one.

What to watch

The next checks are the retailers' trading updates and Adidas's third-quarter results, due later this month. JD Sports's interim results come after that. Together they should show whether the slowdown is Nike's alone or the sector's. A repeat of Nike's China decline at Adidas would point to the sector. Steady Western sales at both would point to Nike.
