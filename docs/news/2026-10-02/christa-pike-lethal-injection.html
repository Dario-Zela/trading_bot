BEYOND THE TAPE · EXECUTION · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-10-02 · 3 min read

Christa Pike in critical condition after surviving two lethal injections, lawyer says

In one sentence
Tennessee's attempt to execute Christa Pike with two doses of pentobarbital failed, leaving her critically ill and handing challengers to lethal injection their strongest case yet.

Tennessee tried to execute Christa Pike on Wednesday evening, and she did not die. She is now in a hospital in critical condition, her lawyers said on Thursday. They added that they have not been able to see or speak with her since she was taken there, and that they do not know her prognosis.

Pike, 50, was to have been the first woman executed in Tennessee in 200 years. She was sentenced to death for the 1995 killing of Colleen Slemmer, 19, a fellow student at a Knoxville job programme for troubled teenagers. Pike was 18 at the time.

What happened in the chamber

The state uses a single drug, pentobarbital. According to accounts of the evening, the curtains to the execution chamber opened at 7:27 p.m. A second dose was administered at 8:26 p.m. At 8:53 p.m. the microphone was cut off and media and witnesses were escorted out.

Before that, witnesses reported that Pike stayed conscious, raised her head and asked officials whether her arm was supposed to feel the way it did. Labored breathing and snoring were audible behind the curtains. Reports describe her as alive and snoring after both doses.

Her lawyer, Randy Spivey, was blunt. "Last night was not just inefficient, it was cruel and it was torturous," he said. He also said Pike "has endured punishment greater than any other person in the history of the American death penalty."

One account notes that seven other prisoners have survived execution complications, but that none had previously survived after receiving the lethal drugs.

A second failure in five months

This was not Tennessee's first difficulty this year. In May, officials abandoned the execution of Tony Carruthers after more than an hour of failed attempts to place an IV line. Medical professionals later noted that the physician responsible had not placed an IV in a patient for more than a decade.

The Department of Correction says it "followed every step" of its protocol. The protocol, however, reportedly has no procedure for a prisoner who survives the second dose.

Governor Bill Lee has halted the remaining executions scheduled for 2026 and ordered "a comprehensive, third-party review to determine exactly what occurred." He also said that "carrying out a lawfully imposed sentence is among the state's most important responsibilities." The state does not appear to have retreated from capital punishment itself.

The legal question

The case lands on an argument that was already in court. Several Tennessee death row inmates have sued, saying the pentobarbital protocol carries "a high risk of a tortuous death." The federal government stopped using pentobarbital for executions in January 2025 after concluding it may cause unnecessary suffering.

The Eighth Amendment bars cruel and unusual punishment, and courts have so far set a high bar for challengers. They generally have to show a method carries a substantial risk of severe pain and propose a workable alternative. Two failed attempts on one prisoner, with a state protocol that has no plan for survival, give her lawyers and those suing a concrete record. They no longer have to rely on a predicted risk.

Other states that use lethal injection will be watching. A ruling against Tennessee's protocol would not bind them directly, but it could push them to rewrite their own procedures or pause executions.

What to watch

The first marker is the findings of the governor's third-party review, and whether they are made public. The second is Pike's condition, since it will shape any legal action her lawyers bring. The third is the pending lawsuits over the pentobarbital protocol, which now have a far stronger factual basis than they did a week ago.
