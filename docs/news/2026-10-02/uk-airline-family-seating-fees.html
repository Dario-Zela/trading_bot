UK to ban airlines charging parents to sit with children

In one sentence
The UK will bar airlines from charging parents to sit with children under 14, a consumer win whose revenue impact on carriers looks modest but signals tighter scrutiny of ancillary fees.

The UK government said on 1 October that it will ban airlines from charging parents to sit next to their children. Under the planned rule, children under 14 must be seated near an accompanying adult at no extra cost. The measure is expected to be written into the Civil Aviation Bill, which is moving through Parliament. Ministers aim to have it in force before the summer holidays of 2027.

For listed carriers the arithmetic is unremarkable. The policy matters more as a marker of regulatory direction than as a hit to earnings.

What changes

The Department for Transport says sitting together can currently cost a parent and child up to £104 on a return journey. The same package will require airlines to let passengers correct simple name errors on a booking, such as a misspelling, without paying a fee.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham called the charges "a rip-off, plain and simple", and said the changes target "small irritations that catch people out, waste their time and cost them money." Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander said the reforms would make flying "safer for staff and passengers and protect people from unnecessary charges." The wider package also tightens measures against disruptive passengers; airlines logged more than 1,000 serious incidents in 2025.

Why the revenue effect looks small

The rule follows an investigation this year by the Competition and Markets Authority into Ryanair. The CMA found that the airline charged about £8 per seat each way for family reservations covering children aged 2 to 11.

Ryanair has already adjusted its approach. Parents can pay for seat selection while the child sits free nearby. Chief executive Michael O'Leary has said the arrangement has trade-offs: "families may have to wait until after check-in to find out seat allocation and are more likely to be seated at the rear."

Two points limit the financial significance. First, the fee applies only to the subset of passengers travelling with young children, who are a minority of any flight's load. Second, at least one major carrier has already moved away from the most direct form of the charge. Neither the government nor the airlines have published an estimate of lost revenue, so the "modest" assessment rests on this structure rather than on disclosed figures.

The larger unknown is behavioural. Seat-selection fees are a lever for ancillary income across the whole cabin. If families are guaranteed adjacent seats, some adults may stop paying for preferred seating they would otherwise have bought to avoid being split up. That effect is plausible but unquantified.

The broader direction

The more relevant signal for investors is the sequence: a competition-regulator probe, followed by a ministerial announcement and legislation. A similar proposal for a family-seating fee ban was put forward by the US Department of Transportation in 2024, so the UK is not alone in looking at the practice. Ancillary charges for seats, bags and booking changes are a core part of low-cost carriers' economics, and each rule that narrows one of them invites scrutiny of the next.

A single measure on child seating does not change that picture. A pattern of them would.

What to watch

The next markers are the Civil Aviation Bill's passage through Parliament and any secondary rules setting enforcement and penalties. Airline commentary on ancillary revenue at the next round of results would show whether carriers regard the change as noise or as the start of a trend.
