Kellogg's removes rice grains from Rice Krispies, citing changing weather

In one sentence
A claim that Kellogg's has removed rice grains from Rice Krispies because of weather is unverified: no figures, dates or company statement were available, and the BBC original is unchecked.

A headline circulating under the BBC's name says Kellogg's has taken rice grains out of Rice Krispies because of changing weather. As submitted, it comes with no figures, no dates and no company statement. The claim could not be checked against the BBC original or against Kellogg's, and it should be read as unconfirmed until both are in hand.

The gap matters because the idea is plausible on its face while the wording is odd. Rice Krispies are, by name and by design, a puffed-rice cereal. A recipe that removes the rice would be a different product altogether. The more ordinary reading is that something narrower has changed: the origin of the rice, the grain variety, a blend of sources, or an ingredient alongside it. The headline does not say which, and without the underlying report there is no way to tell whether it overstates a smaller change.

What is known

The corporate backdrop is settled. Rice Krispies is a Kellogg's brand. The cereal business now sits within WK Kellogg Co, which Ferrero acquired in 2025. Any statement on ingredients or sourcing would therefore come from a company in the middle of a change of ownership, and it would be worth asking whether any reformulation predates or follows that deal.

Beyond that, the submission offers nothing that can be tested: no named supplier, no growing region, no harvest figure, no spokesperson. The only cited source is BBC UK, and the original report was not available to check.

What would make it a story

Rice is among the crops most exposed to weather. It needs reliable water, and both drought and flooding can cut yields. Heat during flowering can also reduce grain quality. Manufacturers do periodically adjust sourcing and recipes when supply tightens. If Kellogg's has done so here, and has said weather was the reason, it would be a concrete case of climate pressure reaching a mainstream supermarket product, which is the kind of evidence that abstract warnings about food security rarely supply.

That case would need specifics to stand up:

  • which ingredient changed, and by how much;
  • where the affected rice was grown, and what happened to the harvest there;
  • whether Kellogg's itself attributes the change to weather, or whether the link is the reporter's inference;
  • whether the change applies in the UK only or across markets.

Until those are answered, the weather attribution is a claim about motive, and motives for recipe changes are often mixed. Cost, supplier contracts and ownership changes can all play a part alongside crop conditions.

What to watch

The immediate test is simple. The BBC original should say whether Kellogg's confirmed the change and in what words. A statement from the company, or updated packaging and ingredient lists, would settle the factual question of what is in the box. Only after that is it worth asking whether the weather explanation holds, which would need supply-side evidence from the growing regions concerned. If the BBC report cites Kellogg's directly, this brief should be replaced with a reported piece. If it does not, the headline should be treated as an unverified summary rather than a finding.
