'Multi-cancer' blood tests to reach some GP surgeries before the main trial reports

In one sentence
The NHS is testing blood tests for abdominal cancers in about 200 GP practices, but Galleri's main trial has already reported, missing its primary endpoint on late-stage cancers.

A multi-cancer blood test is not about to be handed out in GP surgeries on the strength of detection rates alone, and the main trial of the best-known such test has already reported. That corrects the premise of this story. The NHS has announced a different study, in which blood tests will be given to people with symptoms at about 200 GP practices in England. It is a randomised trial, not a rollout. It is aimed at abdominal cancers, not a screen for dozens of cancers in healthy people.

The distinction matters. Two quite different uses of blood tests are being discussed under one label, and the evidence for them is at different stages.

What the NHS has announced

The new study is called STRIDE-RCT. It is government-funded and led by the University of Birmingham. According to reports of the announcement on 4 November, it will recruit more than 20,000 patients aged 45 or over who see their GP with abdominal symptoms such as persistent bloating or pain. The practices are randomly allocated. Half will offer eligible patients a blood test alongside usual care, and half will provide usual care only. The tests look for tumour DNA and proteins in the blood. One report says the study will run from November 2026 to October 2031.

Officials have said a positive result does not confirm cancer and a negative result is not a guaranteed all-clear. The stated purpose is to judge whether such tests are accurate enough for wider NHS use. I could not retrieve the BBC's own report or the full government statement, so these details come from secondary accounts and should be checked against the primary announcement. Details such as which test or tests will be used are not confirmed here.

This is a design that withholds the test from half the practices on purpose, so that outcomes can be compared. That is the opposite of a rollout based on detection rates.

Where Galleri stands

The larger question concerns NHS-Galleri, the randomised trial of GRAIL's Galleri test among 142,250 people aged 50 to 77 in England, screened annually for three years. The triage notes for this piece said results were pending. They are not. GRAIL presented the full results at the American Society of Clinical Oncology meeting on 30 May 2026.

The primary endpoint was a reduction in combined stage III and IV cancers across 12 prespecified cancer types. It was not met. The rate ratio was 1.03 (95% confidence interval 0.92 to 1.14; p=0.63), meaning no significant reduction. Combined stage III and IV diagnoses were 3% higher overall and 19% higher in the first screening round.

The company's emphasis is on a prespecified secondary endpoint. Stage IV cancers fell by 14% overall (rate ratio 0.86, 95% CI 0.744 to 0.998), with reductions of 9%, 22% and 26% across the three rounds. The lower bound of that interval sits very close to 1, and it is described as nominally significant, a phrase that signals no adjustment for the multiple comparisons that a secondary analysis invites. Other reported figures were a positive predictive value of 52.0% overall (937 cancers among 1,801 positive results) and a 16% rise in stage I and II diagnoses for the 12 cancers. One summary also reports 25% fewer emergency presentations in the screened arm; I have not verified that figure against the primary data.

The two principal voices read the result differently. Professor Charles Swanton of the trial team acknowledged that “the primary endpoint was not met” while describing the stage IV result as clinically meaningful. Professor Richard Houlston has argued that the findings were presented “far more positively than the overall results justify.” GRAIL has said the data will be submitted for peer-reviewed publication and that follow-up continues.

Is the process evidence-led?

On the available record, the sequence looks more cautious than the angle suggested. NHS England said in 2024 that it would wait for final NHS-Galleri results before deciding on any wider use of the test. Nothing I found shows that position has been reversed. The trial has now reported a missed primary endpoint, and the NHS's next step is a further trial rather than adoption. I did not find an NHS England statement responding to the May results, and that gap is worth closing.

There are still reasons for care. Private clinics in the UK already sell Galleri, so patients can obtain a multi-cancer test without any NHS decision, and the evidence they are given may lean on detection figures. A test that finds more early-stage cancers does not by itself show that deaths fall. Part of an increase in stage I and II diagnoses can reflect overdiagnosis, or lead time, where a cancer is found sooner without the patient living longer. That is why the trial chose late-stage incidence as a proxy, and why mortality follow-up matters.

Symptomatic testing, as in STRIDE, answers a different question from screening healthy people. A 2023 NHS study of about 5,000 people with suspected cancer symptoms found the test identified roughly two in three cancers, but that was a study of accuracy, not of whether outcomes improve. STRIDE is designed to test the second thing.

What to watch

Three things will settle the matter: the peer-reviewed publication of the NHS-Galleri results, including full stage-specific data and any mortality follow-up; any formal NHS England or National Screening Committee response; and the STRIDE-RCT recruitment, with results not expected until the early 2030s on the reported timetable.
