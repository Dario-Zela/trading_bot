Carney says Canada-US trade talks are intensifying, with some red lines removed

In one sentence
Carney says Canada-US trade talks have progressed, with some red lines gone and the US asking for less, but no deal is done and the US disputes parts of his account.

Canada's prime minister says trade talks with Washington are moving, that some red lines have gone, and that the US is asking for less than before. Mark Carney has not said a deal is done. The account is his own, and nothing independent confirms it.

The headline claim rests on a single Benzinga item, with no dates, figures or direct quotes. Wider coverage from recent weeks fills in some context but not the specifics.

What Carney has said

In late October, Carney said talks with the Trump administration had picked up slightly. He credited any progress to Washington moving closer to Canada's demands, rather than to Ottawa giving ground. Reporting at the time quoted him on the August offer: "We had very clear bottom lines, red lines, which meant that we were not willing to sign what was offered in August." That was a day after Trump said the US was "getting close to a deal" with Canada.

In September, Carney described a harder US stance, a "our way or the highway, take-it-or-leave-it attitude", and said those red lines no longer seemed to be there. The latest remarks extend that line: fewer immovable conditions on the US side, and a narrower set of asks.

The specifics are still missing. Which concessions have been dropped, which sectors are affected and what the US is now asking for have not been stated in anything reviewed here.

The other side of the table

Washington has disputed parts of Carney's telling of how the talks failed. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has denied Carney's claim that Canadian French-language requirements were a sticking point. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has said Canada added "crazy ideas" in the final hours of negotiation. Talks broke down in August after the two sides had appeared close. Reports differ on the tariff consequences, and the figures should be checked against official US sources before being relied on.

Two parties each saying the other is moving toward them is common late in a negotiation. It is also what a party says when it wants to shape the narrative. Trump's "getting close" comment is some corroboration of direction, but it is not a statement of terms.

Why markets care, and the clock

The practical stake is tariff exposure for Canadian and US exporters and the cross-border supply chains that trading desks price. A narrowing of US demands, if real, would reduce the tail risk of a prolonged standoff. Without terms, though, there is nothing to price beyond the direction of travel.

The timing question is political. Carney has framed the US shift as tied to the midterm elections, with results arriving as this edition goes to print. A deal that is close before the vote may be harder or easier to close after it, depending on the result and on how the administration reads it. Neither is knowable yet.

What to watch: any published text or joint statement naming the dropped conditions, a US official confirming the narrower asks, and whether talks resume formally once the midterm count is settled. Until one of those appears, the story is a claim by one side that the other has moved.
