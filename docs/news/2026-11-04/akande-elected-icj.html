British lawyer Dapo Akande elected to the International Court of Justice

In one sentence
Oxford's Dapo Akande has reportedly been elected to the ICJ, returning a British judge to the UN's top court for the first time since 2017.

Britain is back on the bench of the International Court of Justice. Dapo Akande, a professor of public international law at Oxford, has been elected to the court in The Hague, according to the Guardian. The election was not independently verified for this article. It ends a gap of nearly nine years in which no British judge sat on the UN's principal judicial body.

How the seats are won

The ICJ has 15 judges, each serving a nine-year term. Five seats come up every three years, so the court renews itself in stages rather than all at once. Candidates are nominated by national groups and then must win twice: separately in the UN General Assembly and in the 15-member Security Council. In each chamber they need an absolute majority, which means 97 of the Assembly's 193 members and eight of the Council's 15.

The dual process favours candidates who can build support across regional blocs. The Security Council's permanent members hold no veto here, and the General Assembly can outvote them. That is how Britain came to lose its seat in the first place.

A seat that was lost

In 2017 the UK's sitting judge, Christopher Greenwood, was left competing with India's Dalveer Bhandari for the final seat. Bhandari led in the Assembly while Greenwood held the Council vote. After repeated inconclusive rounds, London withdrew its candidate rather than force a confrontation. It was widely read as a sign that the Assembly's majority no longer deferred to the permanent five, and the court has had no British judge since.

Mr Akande's election is therefore a recovery, though a modest one. He is an academic rather than a career diplomat, known for work on the law governing the use of force and on the immunity of state officials. He is also a co-founder of the widely read EJIL:Talk! blog. Such a profile tends to travel well among the other states that vote. It also fits the court's convention that judges are chosen as jurists and sit in a personal capacity, not as delegates of their governments.

What one seat changes

The court's make-up matters because its docket runs through the hardest questions in the field: territorial and maritime sovereignty, the lawful use of force, and the responsibility of states for wrongdoing. It has also been drawn into politically charged cases in recent years. A British judge does not carry the UK's position into the room. Judges are bound to independence, and the court has ruled against the governments that nominated them.

What a national judge does supply is familiarity with the common-law tradition and a voice in the deliberations, where a single colleague can shape drafting and reasoning. One vote among 15 is an incremental change. For Britain it is mostly a measure of whether its standing among the UN's 193 members still carries enough weight to win contested elections, at a time when its wider international footprint is narrowing. Aid has been cut and diplomatic bandwidth is stretched, as shown by the dispute over aid to Commonwealth states at this week's Chogm summit.

What to watch

Mr Akande's term begins when the current mandates of the outgoing judges expire, in February 2027, according to the court's usual cycle. The more telling test comes later. It will show whether the UK can keep a seat at the next election in 2035 and whether it can influence nominations in the cycles between. Until then, the result shows that London can still win a contested vote when it fields a candidate with a respected academic record.
