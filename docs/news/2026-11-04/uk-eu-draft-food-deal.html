UK and EU agree draft food deal to cut Brexit paperwork, but industry concerns and political opposition remain

In one sentence
A draft UK-EU food-safety deal could cut Brexit paperwork from mid-2027, but industry concerns are unresolved and the 20 November summit has already slipped once.

The United Kingdom and the European Union have a draft food-safety agreement that, if it survives, would remove most of the routine certificates and border checks that have followed British food exports since Brexit. It is a draft, not a treaty. The people who would have to live with it, from fresh-produce importers to food manufacturers, say important questions are still open. The decision point is a UK-EU summit in Brussels on 20 November.

The gap between the political announcement and what a business can put on a lorry is the real subject. Prime Minister Andy Burnham is preparing to present the food deal as part of a wider package that also covers energy and emissions trading. The details of that package, including the 'billions of pounds' figure attached to it in early briefings, have not been set out in any document available to this writer.

What a sanitary and phytosanitary deal does

Since 2021 most animal and plant products crossing the Channel have needed export health certificates, inspections at border control posts and, for some goods, separate paperwork on each consignment. A sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement would replace much of that with a shared rulebook. Britain would align with EU food-safety, animal-health and plant-health law, and in return the routine checks would largely disappear.

The government intends the arrangement to take effect in mid-2027, with 'summer 2027' the date used in trade reporting. That timetable matters because businesses need time to change systems, packaging and supply contracts. Any slippage in the summit shortens the window to prepare.

The pressure from exporters is real. The Food and Drink Federation's latest trade snapshot shows UK food and drink export volumes fell 11.7% in the first half of 2026. A senior figure at Marks & Spencer has said the 20 November summit is the moment 'finally to get the deal done'.

The costs the headline does not show

The main trade-off is the one at the heart of all single-market-style deals. Lower friction at the border is paid for with alignment. The UK would follow EU food rules, including future changes, without a vote on how they are written. Supporters point to an estimate that the wider arrangement could add about £9bn a year to the economy by 2040. The origin and method of that figure have not been independently confirmed, and it should be read as a projection, not a forecast of what shoppers will see.

The benefits and costs also fall unevenly. The Fresh Produce Consortium, which represents importers, has said its position and the government's are 'totally opposed'. Its argument is that alignment would add checks to produce from outside the EU. It says such imports make up about 54% of seasonal fruit and vegetable imports, roughly four million tonnes. The consortium puts the extra cost to the food sector at up to £300m a year and calls the Defra response to its concerns 'ambiguous and unsatisfactory'. Those are the lobby's own estimates, and the government has not endorsed them.

Manufacturers have raised different issues. Labelling is one: the 'Not for EU' marks that were required on some products sold in Northern Ireland would have to be unwound, a job one respondent to an industry poll said government tends to underestimate. Horticulture groups worry that fewer border inspections could weaken plant biosecurity. The Chartered Institute of Export & International Trade has asked for a clear roadmap from government and more dialogue with businesses.

A summit that has already slipped once

The 20 November date is not the first. A summit planned for July was postponed after Keir Starmer's resignation. When Burnham met Commission President Ursula von der Leyen at the United Nations in September, he called the talks 'constructive', but the Grocer reported that other files were holding things up, notably a youth-mobility arrangement and the EU's 'Made in Europe' industrial policy. A senior food-sector source told the trade magazine the summit could be put off until early next year. The date has since been confirmed, but the underlying linkages have not been publicly resolved.

That matters because the food deal is not being negotiated in isolation. Brussels has treated the SPS agreement as one part of a broader reset, and sticking points elsewhere can hold up a deal that is technically close.

Will it outlast the next election?

The politics may prove harder than the paperwork. The Conservatives and Reform UK have both said they would cancel any such deal, according to briefings on the package. Neither position is quoted or dated in the material seen here. The Conservatives' shadow environment secretary, Victoria Atkins, has criticised the SPS talks as conducted 'in secret, without a clear mandate or firm red lines', saying businesses were barely consulted.

For exporters that creates an awkward calculation. Investing in new systems for a deal that might be reversed after the next general election is a risk, and the possibility of a reversal makes some companies slower to commit. Supporters will reply that a deal in force and visibly cutting costs is harder to unpick than one that exists only on paper.

What to watch

The first test is whether the summit takes place on 20 November and produces a text rather than a statement of intent. If it does, three things will show how workable it is: whether the government publishes the full list of regulations covered, which was last updated in June; whether it sets out a transition period and a roadmap, as industry has requested; and how it answers the consortium's complaint about non-EU imports. A signed agreement would still need legislation and then the mid-2027 start date to hold. Until then, exporters face the same certificates on each shipment.
