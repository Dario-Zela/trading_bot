Parliament warned of 'Notre Dame-style fire' risk as restoration costs climb

In one sentence
A Commons committee warns Parliament risks a Notre Dame-style fire while the postponed restoration decision adds an estimated £420m a year, though the report does not say safety work itself has stalled.

The Commons Public Accounts Committee has warned that the Palace of Westminster faces "real and rising risks", among them a fire on the scale of the one that gutted Notre Dame, because the long-running programme to restore it remains undecided and its price keeps climbing. The report was published on 4 November.

The committee, chaired by Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, says it first flagged these dangers three years ago. Its warning covers a "catastrophic event" that could injure people on the estate or disrupt the working of Parliament, and it includes the possibility of the Commons chamber going dark during the State Opening.

What the numbers say

The figures describe a bill that grows while the decision waits. Maintenance on the parliamentary estate is expected to rise from £1.5m to £2m a week. The committee estimates that each year of delay adds £420m to the cost of delivering the programme. Clifton-Brown put it bluntly: "each kick of the can down the road costs hundreds of millions in public money."

The options are far apart. A "full decant", with both Houses moving out, could cost up to £15.6bn and take up to 24 years. A partial decant, with MPs and peers working around the builders, could run for more than six decades and cost close to £40bn. The first phase, which covers temporary homes for both Houses and their offices, is capped at £3bn.

Is the cost delaying the safety work?

The Notre Dame comparison invites a particular question: is the rising bill holding up the fire-safety work itself? The report coverage available to this newspaper does not say so. It does not describe fire-safety work as stalled, and it gives no new technical findings on the building's wiring or fire protection.

What has slipped is the decision. Votes on the programme's future have been postponed, and the committee fears that temporary accommodation for MPs and peers may not be ready when the move out is needed. Current timescales put that in 2032. The delay is in the full restoration, not in day-to-day fire precautions, and the report's argument is that the two are linked: the longer the old building stays in service, the longer its ageing systems and combustible materials stay in place.

This fits earlier alarms. Lord Hain told peers that fires had broken out 45 times in the Palace over a decade. The restoration programme itself has warned of "vast quantities of combustible materials" and ventilation shafts that let fire spread, and of asbestos on a scale its delivery chief executive called "enormous". The Notre Dame comparison is a fair one in kind, since both are historic buildings with large concealed voids. The difference is that Westminster is still occupied daily, which raises the stakes.

A Parliament spokesperson said the report "provides welcome scrutiny" and that work continues to keep people on the estate safe. No government response was reported.

What to watch

The next test is the postponed vote on the programme's future. Until the Commons and Lords choose between a full and a partial decant, the £420m-a-year meter keeps running and the 2032 move-out date comes closer. The committee's case rests on a simple proposition: that a decision made late costs more than one made now, in both money and risk.
