Mahmood won't cost the care worker climbdown as Labour MPs hold back on immigration changes

In one sentence
The Home Secretary would not say what the government's retreat on care worker settlement rules will cost, leaving MPs and the sector unable to judge a policy first sold on a £10bn figure.

The Home Secretary has been asked what it will cost to back away from one of the government's most contested immigration changes. She has not said. Shabana Mahmood declined to put a figure on the government's retreat over settlement rules for care workers, according to BBC Politics, the only source behind the original report. The refusal sits awkwardly beside the way the policy was first sold, which rested on a very large number.

The detail of the retreat is thinner than the politics around it. Public reporting available at the time of writing confirms a partial softening, not a full abandonment. No published Home Office statement or costing for the revised rules has been found. Where this article describes the final shape of the climbdown, it relies on the BBC's summary and on secondary reporting that is not always consistent on dates.

The rule that started it

The proposals were unveiled in November 2025 and put to consultation, which closed on 12 February. Ministers wanted to extend the wait for indefinite leave to remain, the UK's form of permanent settlement, from five years to ten for most migrant workers. Care workers on the post-Brexit health and social care visa would face the longest route of all, 15 years, and the changes would apply to people already in the country who had not yet settled.

Some groups were spared. Higher earners above £50,270, public-sector health and teaching staff, family-visa holders and Hong Kong BNO visa holders were to keep a five-year route. Mahmood called settlement a "privilege not a right". High earners and higher-rate taxpayers could cut the wait to as little as three years, while people claiming benefits could lengthen it.

The justification was fiscal. The Home Office cited a £10bn lifetime welfare cost for the cohort affected. The department has since said that figure was an illustration of lifetime cost, not a forecast of savings. The IPPR thinktank argued it could be reached only if most care workers left the UK, and that such departures would carry significant costs of their own to the Treasury. One critique put the possible added cost at £11bn to £22bn, though that comes from a less established source and should be treated with caution.

Backbench pressure, then quiet

The policy drew resistance inside Labour from the start. Around 40 Labour MPs raised concerns early in 2026, describing the retrospective element as "un-British" and as "moving the goalposts". Tony Vaughan said retrospectivity "undermines our sense of fair play" and should be abandoned. Rachael Maskell warned of a worsened skills shortage. More than 100 Labour MPs later wrote urging that the changes not apply to people already here, and a symbolic Commons vote was threatened. UNISON and some councils also opposed the plans.

By late September, Mahmood told the Labour conference in Liverpool that she could water the plans down, and Downing Street sources floated "transitional arrangements" that could shorten the wait for some existing migrants. That is the background to the BBC's observation that Labour backbenchers have now largely held back criticism of the new measures. The BBC brief gives no named MPs, votes or quotes explaining the shift, so the reason is an inference: the central grievance, retrospectivity, appears to have been at least partly met. It is not established that every MP's concern has been answered.

The missing number

A government that justified a policy with a £10bn figure is now declining to say what reversing part of it costs. There are two ways to read that. One is that the costing is not finished: the revised rules may still be under negotiation, and a number published too early would be out of date within days. Mahmood is reported to have said she would set out the full cost later. The other is that any honest estimate would expose how little the original saving was worth. Analysis cited in coverage suggested a direct saving of roughly £2,000 per care worker, a long way from £10bn.

The distinction matters beyond Westminster. Social care is a sector heavily reliant on overseas recruits, and employers have said the longer wait would push workers elsewhere. Without a costing, neither MPs nor providers can judge whether the retreat is a modest adjustment or a large reversal of the original fiscal case.

What to watch

The test is the detail of the transitional arrangements: who is covered, whether the 15-year route for new care workers survives, and whether a Home Office impact assessment accompanies them. The Office for Budget Responsibility's treatment of settlement-related welfare costs in its forecasts would give the first independent figure. Until one of those lands, the climbdown has a headline but no price.
