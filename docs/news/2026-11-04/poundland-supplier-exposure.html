Poundland supplier warns it would lose millions if the retailer collapses

In one sentence
Highcrest, a £50m-turnover confectionery supplier, says a Poundland collapse would cost it a few million pounds, while smaller suppliers fear for their survival.

A Bradford confectionery supplier has put a number on what a Poundland failure would cost it: a few million pounds. Highcrest Retail Brands, which has turnover of about £50m, told the BBC that the loss would leave a hole in its balance sheet next year. The firm is not Poundland's largest vendor. Its figure is a rough guide to how far a collapse of the discount chain would spread through suppliers' accounts.

The exposure

Adam Turner, Highcrest's sales director, said the company would "lose a few million" if Poundland became insolvent. Poundland is one of Highcrest's biggest customers, and the two firms have traded for decades. Highcrest sells mainly confectionery. Turner described it as a "longstanding business that can stomach this".

The BBC report gives no precise figure, so scale has to be inferred. "A few million" against £50m of annual sales means a loss of several percent of one year's revenue. That is a serious hit but survivable, which matches Turner's own account. The pain is likelier to land on suppliers with less to fall back on. Smaller firms, he said, had told him that "if Poundland goes under, so do they".

Highcrest's own numbers are the only supplier-level figures available. Poundland's total trade creditors have not been published in the sources reviewed, so nobody can yet say how Highcrest's loss compares with the full amount owed to vendors.

A wider circle

Poundland's founder, Steve Smith, warned on Monday that between two and three thousand suppliers that work only for Poundland would be directly affected by a collapse. He also pointed to 12,000 Poundland staff and potentially tens of thousands more jobs in the wider supply chain. Buyers are reportedly interested in only about half of the roughly 600 stores, which would leave about 6,000 employees without a new employer.

Timing makes the exposure worse. Suppliers are owed most in the Halloween-to-Christmas period, when stock is delivered ahead of sales. Unsecured trade creditors in an administration typically recover only a small fraction of what they are owed.

Credit insurance was already thinning. Retail Gazette reported in September that insurers had stopped writing new cover for some Poundland suppliers amid uncertainty over ownership, and that large brands had tightened credit lines. Poundland denied that stock was affected. Where cover has been withdrawn, a supplier shipping on credit is carrying the risk itself.

Where Poundland stands

Poundland is not in administration and continues to trade. Gordon Brothers bought it from Pepco Group for £1 just over a year ago. In October the union Usdaw warned that administration could come within days. Reports then described a management-led rescue, with a possible bid from Martin Property Group, running alongside interest from other parties. Gordon Brothers was reportedly seeking repayment of a £30m shareholder loan. Poundland said it remained "open to any proposal that delivers the best outcome for the business, employees and suppliers". MPs have this week pressed representatives of Gordon Brothers for clarity on its plans.

The account is single-sourced, resting on the BBC's interview with one supplier and Smith's comments. Poundland's reply to this particular warning is not recorded in the material reviewed.

What to watch

The next marker is Gordon Brothers' decision between a rescue deal and administration. An administrator's first report would show how much is owed to trade creditors. That figure is what would test whether Highcrest's loss is typical or an outlier.
