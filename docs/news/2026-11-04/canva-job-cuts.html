Canva announces major job cuts after 'our hardest year yet'

In one sentence
Canva is cutting or reshaping about 22% of its workforce, 1,271 roles, after CEO Melanie Perkins said slowing growth made 2026 'our hardest year yet'.

Canva, the Sydney-based design platform used by millions of people to make presentations, social posts and posters, is cutting or reshaping about 22% of its workforce. Founder and chief executive Melanie Perkins told staff that 2026 has been "our hardest year yet".

The company says 1,271 roles are affected. That figure covers people leaving, people whose roles change, and people being relocated to central hubs, so it is not all redundancies. The 22% implies a workforce of roughly 5,800. It is the largest restructuring in Canva's 13-year history, and the company had rarely cut jobs before.

What the memo says

Perkins's memo, reported on 3–4 November, ties the cuts to a business that has stopped growing at its old pace. "Our growth has slowed for the first time in a decade," she wrote, adding that "small changes won't be enough." She described the aim as to "refound Canva" around smaller, more empowered teams, and called it "without doubt the hardest moment in our thirteen-year history", according to Forbes Australia.

The numbers behind that sit outside the memo. In August Canva cut its 2026 revenue-growth forecast from 30% to about 20%. Investors have marked down their holdings: T. Rowe Price cut its Canva valuation by 20%, to an implied US$30.4bn, from the US$40bn at which the company entered the year. Blackbird, its largest shareholder, trimmed its own valuation by 17%.

What changes inside

The memo cites organisational complexity. Product, Growth and Platform teams, with their handovers between them, are being merged into a single product organisation with fewer layers and broader roles. Sydney becomes the main product centre. From January, most hybrid staff are expected in the office two to three days a week. Perkins said remote-first working had made it harder to get the right people together.

Costs are part of the story too. Forbes Australia reports that Canva delayed some product rollouts because serving AI features to its large base of non-paying users was expensive. Canva says the cost of each AI task has fallen by nearly 90% since April, while the number of designs generated has roughly tripled. It is also promoting its own Craft foundation model, which it says will reduce reliance on OpenAI, Anthropic and Google.

The memo does not say AI is replacing the roles being cut. That is worth stating plainly, because the company's recent history invites the assumption. In April 2025 it cut 10 of its 12 technical writers while encouraging staff to adopt generative AI. This round is framed as a response to slowing growth and organisational bloat.

The terms

Departing employees get at least six months' salary, including statutory entitlements, wherever they are based. They also get 12 months of healthcare where it is currently offered, 12 months of employee-assistance support, interview coaching and a year of LinkedIn Premium. The one-year vesting cliff is removed for people who joined in the past year, and leavers keep their company MacBook. A fund offers up to US$10,000 to staff who start a venture of their own.

A wider pattern

Canva joins a crowded list. Forbes Australia notes that Atlassian cut 1,600 jobs in February and Block 4,000 in April this year, and that Microsoft offered voluntary redundancy to a very large number of staff. Canva is a different kind of company, though: a consumer-facing product that grew fast on a free tier and is now finding that free users running AI features cost real money.

The next test is whether the leaner structure restores growth. The first public evidence will be any update to the 20% revenue-growth forecast, and the next round of investor valuation marks.
