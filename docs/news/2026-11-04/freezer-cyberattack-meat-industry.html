Cyber-attack on European supermarket freezers hits UK meat industry

In one sentence
Hackers who overheated freezers in hundreds of French supermarkets are reported to have hit the UK meat industry too; UK impact details remain unconfirmed.

Someone reached into the control systems that keep supermarket freezers cold, and turned the heat up. The attack on French grocers in October is now reported to have spilled into the UK meat industry. It is a reminder that the thermostat on a freezer is a networked computer, and that many of these computers sit on the open internet.

The reach into the UK is the least documented part of the story. A BBC Technology report says the fallout touched British meat producers. At the time of writing, the details had not been independently confirmed: how many firms, which sites, and what stock was lost. The French end is much better documented.

What happened in France

According to reports from 12 and 13 October, attackers gained internet access to refrigeration controllers and remotely pushed temperatures in freezers and fridges as high as 50C. They did it overnight, when shops were empty and nobody would notice. Reports put the number of affected stores between roughly 400 and more than 500.

Intermarché was reported to have lost at least 300 stores, including its Netto brand. Coopérative U had more than 100 supermarkets hit and Carrefour about 80. Monoprix, E.Leclerc and others were also named. Retailers said millions of euros of food was spoiled. In some cases the heat caused fires or melted equipment.

France's national cybersecurity agency said the affected systems were made by the Danish firm Danfoss, and it named two models, the AK-SM800 and AK-SC355. Danfoss told AFP that most affected units were "legacy" products running outdated firmware, and that it had previously urged customers to update. French reporting said the attackers appeared to trigger a forced defrost, a normal function of these units. One account said the defrost interval was stretched so that alarms stayed quiet.

Nobody has been identified as responsible. French authorities have opened an investigation and are talking to other European countries that may also have been hit. How the attackers got in has not been publicly established.

Why a freezer is not an office laptop

A refrigeration controller is operational technology: a device that runs a physical process. Ordinary IT security is about protecting data. Here the damage is physical and fast. Meat held above safe temperatures for a few hours cannot be put back on sale, and it may have to be destroyed in bulk.

These controllers are often installed once and left for a decade or more. They are connected so that engineers can monitor stores remotely, which is convenient and also the weakness. Reuters reportedly found at least 4,500 Danfoss interfaces reachable from the open internet, though it could not say how many ran vulnerable firmware.

The UK has its own exposure. Analysis by UK Cyber Defence, shared with The Grocer, found the control interfaces of more than 800 UK retailer fridges reachable by anyone who knew where to look. That is a finding about exposure, not a report of attack. It does show the same conditions exist on this side of the Channel.

Meat is a plausible place for damage to concentrate. Processors and cold stores hold large volumes of high-value product with narrow temperature tolerances, and they supply the supermarkets that were in the line of fire. The industry has been here before in other forms: JBS, the world's largest meat processor, paid about £7.8m in ransom after a 2021 attack, and a Somerset chilled-logistics firm supplying Tesco, Sainsbury's and Aldi was hit by ransomware in a separate incident. Those were conventional IT attacks. The French case is different because the attackers acted on the cooling equipment itself.

What to watch

The immediate questions are factual. British regulators and industry bodies have yet to set out publicly which UK businesses were affected and whether any stock was lost. The French investigation may establish the entry route, which would show whether this was a known firmware flaw or something new. Attribution will matter too: President Macron ordered protection of critical infrastructure from Russian "hybrid attacks" last month, but no link between that and the freezer attacks has been shown.

For operators, France's agency has given plain advice. Disconnect affected controllers from the internet or restrict access, and install updates. Retailers, processors and cold-store operators running connected refrigeration will need to find out which of their units are exposed. For equipment that cannot be patched, the choice is replacement or isolation.
