Shell finds light oil off Namibia and exits Raizen as CEO flags years of demand support

The West Bollsta semi-submersible rig, used by Shell in the Orange Basin off Namibia, 2019. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's Merlin-1X well delivers Namibia's most promising Orange Basin results yet, even as the company pumps €577m into a distressed Brazilian biofuels JV and its CEO forecasts a decade of rising oil prices.

Shell reported on Tuesday that its Merlin-1X exploration well in the Orange Basin off the coast of Namibia had encountered light oil in the Coniacian play, describing the results as the most promising subsurface findings yet in the company's decade-long effort to unlock the block. The well, the tenth drilled in Petroleum Exploration License 0039 since Shell spudded the campaign, penetrated a formation that previous wells in the same licence had not reached in commercially useful form.

Shell operates PEL 0039 alongside QatarEnergy, which holds a 45% participating interest, and Namibia's state-owned NAMCOR with 10%. The Merlin-1X result, which showed good reservoir quality with limited associated gas, represents a marked improvement on earlier wells in the 12,000 square-kilometre deepwater block sitting more than 250 kilometres offshore. Further drilling is under consideration later in 2026 as the partnership builds its case for commercialisation.

Eugene Okpere, Shell's executive vice-president for exploration, strategy and portfolio, called the results "encouraging" and said they "add to our understanding of the Orange Basin potential." Saad Sherida Al-Kaabi, Qatar's energy minister and QatarEnergy's president and chief executive, went further, describing Merlin-1X as "a significant step that further strengthens confidence in the Orange Basin as an emerging world-class hydrocarbon province."

Brazil bailout, not exit

Separately, and in a less straightforward fashion, Shell is also anchoring one of Brazil's largest-ever corporate debt restructurings — a R$65 billion ($12.6 billion) recapitalisation of Raízen, the ethanol and downstream fuels joint venture it co-owns with Cosan. Shell holds roughly 44% of Raízen, and is injecting R$3.5 billion (approximately €577 million) in fresh equity to backstop the plan, which secured backing from more than 75% of creditors last week and is now heading to a São Paulo court for formal approval.

About 45% of Raízen's restructured debt converts to equity, altering the company's ownership structure while Shell says it intends to maintain governance influence. The JV ran into trouble through a combination of high capital requirements, adverse harvests, and rising financing costs. Shell said discussions about Raízen's future structure remain ongoing and did not rule out an eventual split of assets — leaving the door open for a more significant strategic move once financial stability is restored.

The juxtaposition is pointed. Shell is committing exploration capital to a frontier deepwater basin in sub-Saharan Africa at the same time as propping up a downstream biofuels venture in Latin America that warned as recently as February about significant uncertainty over its operational continuity. One is a bet on future supply, the other a defensive hold on a transition-era asset.

Chief executive Wael Sawan, speaking at The Wall Street Journal's Leadership Institute CEO Summit on Wednesday, provided the strategic frame. Sawan said he expects oil prices, currently trading in a $60–70 per barrel range, to rise over the next five to ten years. "All the easy oil and gas has been found," he said, adding that demand continues to grow even as the most accessible reserves are depleted. He had made a starker version of the same argument in May, telling Shell's first-quarter earnings call that the market faces "close to a billion barrels" of crude shortage as a result of Iranian supply disruptions, with balances set to remain "tight for the coming months, if not the next year-plus."

For traders, the signal is clear enough: Shell's management is not acting as though the energy transition compresses crude demand over the near horizon. What remains unresolved is whether Raízen, which sits at the intersection of biofuels and fuel distribution, represents Shell's hedge against that thesis — or simply a legacy position that proved expensive to defend. The São Paulo court process, and any subsequent announcement on Raízen's ownership structure, will clarify which.
