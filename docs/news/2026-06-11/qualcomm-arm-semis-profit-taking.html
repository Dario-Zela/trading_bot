Qualcomm and Arm slip as profit-taking sweeps semiconductor momentum names

Qualcomm's La Jolla campus in San Diego, headquarters of the company's push beyond smartphone chips into automotive, IoT, and data centres. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Qualcomm drops 6% and Arm falls 4.5% in a semis-wide selloff, with JPMorgan's $69B non-handset thesis now awaiting its first test at Qualcomm's June 24 Investor Day.

Profit-taking swept through the semiconductor sector on Wednesday, knocking Qualcomm (QCOM) down 6.07% to $192.95 and dragging Arm Holdings (ARM) 4.46% lower to $310.79 — a pair of declines that illustrate the fragility of AI-fuelled rallies once momentum traders begin to exit.

Neither move was driven by company-specific bad news. The VanEck Fabless Semiconductor ETF (SMHX) fell 2.98% and the iShares PHLX SOX index (SOXX) shed 2.65%, confirming the rotation was sectoral rather than selective. The macro backdrop was unhelping: US May CPI printed at a three-year high — adding to rate-path uncertainty — while renewed Iran risk premiums kept a lid on appetite for high-multiple growth names broadly.

The $69 billion question

The sharper pain in Qualcomm reflects a valuation that had run ahead of near-term fundamentals. The stock is now trading 10.8% below its 20-day simple moving average of $222.82, having spent much of the spring above it on AI enthusiasm. Year-to-date it is still up 17%, outpacing both the S&P 500 (+8%) and Nasdaq 100 (+16%) — which means there was ample runway for profit-taking.

The structural bull case, laid out in detail by JPMorgan's Samik Chatterjee ahead of the stock's run, rests on a wholesale pivot away from handsets. Chatterjee kept a Neutral rating but raised his price target sharply — from $160 to $265 — projecting Qualcomm's non-handset QCT revenue growing at a 40%-plus compound annual rate from roughly $13 billion in fiscal 2026 to $69 billion by fiscal 2031. By that date, handsets would account for barely a third of revenue. Data centres alone would represent $35 billion, with automotive and IoT contributing approximately $17 billion each.

That is a significant reshaping of a company still widely read as a smartphone chipmaker. Chatterjee lifted his fiscal 2027 earnings-per-share estimate to $11.50 (from $10.45), driven largely by a new $3 billion-plus data centre revenue line for that year, and derived his $265 target by applying a 23-times multiple to that figure.

The thesis gets its first public test on June 24, when Qualcomm holds its Investor Day. Chatterjee expects management to lay out a three-pillar data centre strategy built around custom silicon, merchant CPUs, and AI accelerators, alongside edge AI work that includes a recently announced partnership with oilfield services firm SLB. If the targets management presents fall short of what sell-side models have pre-loaded, the day could sharpen the near-term pain rather than relieve it.

Arm in the slipstream

Arm Holdings has less to prove structurally — its AGI CPU is gaining design wins at Oracle and ByteDance, and the stock remains up more than 190% year-to-date despite two consecutive days of selling (it fell 5.88% on June 9 before Wednesday's 4.46% decline). But it is exposed to the same momentum-unwind dynamic that punishes high-multiple names once sentiment shifts.

CEO Rene Haas drew attention this week for his remark that US export restrictions on AI CPUs targeting China would be "difficult to enforce because CPUs are widely used across many applications and cannot easily be tied to AI workloads" — a comment that cuts both ways on demand visibility. Supply chain constraints around the AGI CPU were also flagged internally, complicating the near-term revenue picture even as long-cycle design wins accumulate.

For both names, the week's selling does not invalidate the underlying theses — it tests whether those theses can hold investor conviction when the macro mood turns. Qualcomm's June 24 Investor Day is the clearest near-term checkpoint: management will either confirm Chatterjee's $69 billion roadmap or expose the gap between analyst projections and what the company is actually prepared to commit to.
