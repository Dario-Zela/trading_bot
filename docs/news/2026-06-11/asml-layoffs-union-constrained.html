ASML to cut fewer jobs than planned after union deal constrains restructuring

ASML's headquarters in Veldhoven, the Netherlands, where more than 1,000 employees staged a walkout in March against the 1,700-job restructuring plan. — A ansems / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
ASML's plan to cut 1,700 jobs — its first restructuring in 16 years — has been curtailed by Dutch unions, barring forced layoffs until May 2027 and likely preserving hundreds of roles.

ASML's bid to slim its management layers — its first large-scale restructuring in sixteen years — has run into Dutch organised labour, and the result is a narrower, slower set of cuts than the chip-equipment maker originally sought.

The Veldhoven-based company announced in January that it planned to eliminate roughly 1,700 positions, around 4% of its global workforce, targeting management, IT, and engineering roles. The internal case was straightforward: an 18-month review found engineers spending up to 22% of their time on peripheral tasks, and concluded that more than 3,000 of ASML's 4,500 technology-management positions were redundant. The structure had grown with the boom; the question was how fast it could be unwound.

Workers did not accept the terms quietly. On 24 March, more than 1,000 employees staged a lunchtime walkout at the Veldhoven headquarters — an unusual show of collective action at a company whose labour relations had, until this year, been largely uneventful. The four main Dutch unions — FNV, CNV, De Unie, and VHP2 — along with Belgian union ACV, entered formal negotiations and made clear that an unmediated redundancy programme was not acceptable.

The terms

The social plan finalised on 10 June moves the restructuring from a rapid headcount reduction to a managed, multi-year redeployment exercise. Forced redundancies are barred until 1 May 2027. In the intervening period, ASML must first attempt to place employees whose roles are designated at risk into internal vacancies before any compulsory departures can proceed. The company has acknowledged this redeployment process could preserve "hundreds" of the originally targeted positions — leaving the final reduction well below 1,700.

Severance terms for those who do leave equal one gross monthly salary per year of service; workers over 50 receive additional compensation, with total settlements capped at €400,000. An unusual provision also allows employees whose own positions are not at risk to volunteer for departure at 80% of the standard package, freeing a slot for a colleague who would otherwise be made redundant. The plan runs for two years, retroactive from 1 June. CNV members voted roughly 70-30 in favour.

The financial stakes are not abstract. ASML posted Q1 2026 gross margins of 53% on net sales of €8.8 billion, sitting within its full-year guidance band of 51–53%. The company is simultaneously running a €12 billion share buyback programme — a signal of confidence in the cash flow, though one that makes the union's observation about management pay and profit-sharing politically pointed. The restructuring was designed to reduce organisational drag and defend the margin line; the union deal has pushed most of that benefit into 2027 at the earliest, and reduced the eventual scope.

Employees will be notified this month of whether their roles are affected. Those designated at risk then enter a redeployment window lasting until May 2027, after which compulsory exits can begin. ASML has not issued a revised headcount-reduction target; the final number of actual departures will not be clear until the redeployment process runs its course.
