Lloyds draws attention at £57bn market cap after recent share price moves

Lloyds Banking Group's City of London offices at 25 Gresham Street — Carcharoth / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Lloyds sits ~15% off its January high at ~97p as strong earnings and a £1.75bn buyback compete with unresolved motor finance liability risk.

Lloyds Banking Group's share price has traded between 73p and 114p over the past year, a 57% range that reflects genuine uncertainty rather than noise. It closed Tuesday at around 97p — roughly 15% off the January peak — giving the UK's largest retail bank a market capitalisation of approximately £57bn.

The stock has two stories running in parallel, and investors are still deciding which one wins.

The case for the upside

On the operational side, Lloyds has had a decent 2026. First-quarter pre-tax profit came in at £2.025bn, up 33% year on year and ahead of analyst estimates. That performance backed a £1.75bn share buyback announced in January, which is visibly running: in the first nine days of June alone, the bank cancelled more than 30 million shares through Goldman Sachs International in weekly tranches.

On Monday, the bank added a strategic move to the ledger. Lloyds announced that it is rolling out Stripe's payments infrastructure to its more than one million business customers under a new suite called Lloyds Accept — offering terminal devices, tap-to-pay, and payment links that can be activated within minutes. It is the first time Stripe has partnered directly with a traditional UK lender. "World-class financial tools shouldn't be gated by size," said Stripe's chief revenue officer Eileen O'Mara. Amanda Murphy, CEO of Lloyds Business and Commercial Banking, framed it as essential infrastructure: "Simple, flexible payment solutions are essential for growth."

The deal is not a revenue line in itself — Lloyds is white-labelling Stripe's infrastructure — but it signals intent to compete with challenger banks on the SME front, where the bank has historically been vulnerable.

The motor finance drag

Against that, Lloyds carries a liability that has no fixed price tag. The motor finance mis-selling saga, which reached the Supreme Court last August, remains unresolved in terms of quantum. The FCA is consulting on a redress scheme; industry-wide costs have been estimated anywhere from £9bn to £18bn. Lloyds has set aside £1.95bn in provisions, but some analysts have pencilled in a Lloyds-specific exposure closer to £3.9bn depending on how the FCA's scheme is structured.

That gap — between what is booked and what may ultimately be owed — explains much of the discount to the 52-week high. The analyst consensus sits around 115–120p with a "buy" rating, implying the market is already over-pricing the liability risk. But consensus has been at those levels for months, and the stock has not recovered the ground it lost.

The FCA is expected to publish its final redress framework later this year. Until then, the buyback provides mechanical support below 100p, and the Stripe partnership offers a reminder that the underlying business is still moving. The catalyst that resolves the discount, however, remains the regulator's pen.
