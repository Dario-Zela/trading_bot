Saylor sells Bitcoin into a declining market, testing institutional conviction

Michael Saylor, Executive Chairman of Strategy Inc., has been the most prominent corporate voice for Bitcoin as a treasury asset. — ReasonTV / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Strategy Inc. sold 32 Bitcoin — its first in four years — to cover preferred dividends, then bought 1,550 coins the following week, leaving markets to argue over what the signal means.

Strategy Inc. — the company built around a single thesis that corporate balance sheets should hold Bitcoin — sold 32 of its coins between May 26 and May 31, marking the firm's first disposal of the asset since 2022. At an average of $77,135 a coin, the transaction raised roughly $2.5 million, proceeds directed at dividends owed on STRC preferred shares. That number, against a position of roughly 845,000 Bitcoin, is a rounding error. The fact that it happened at all is not.

The sale was disclosed in an 8-K filing submitted June 1, after market close. Bitcoin had already broken below $73,000 on May 28 and was heading toward $62,000 by the time analysts had finished parsing the filing. Strategy's average acquisition cost sits at approximately $75,681 per coin — meaning the company's $63.9 billion Bitcoin position now carries an unrealised loss approaching $12 billion. The 32 coins were sold, notably, slightly above that average. What followed was a market that read the signal rather than the spread.

Wintermute, the algorithmic trading firm, put it directly: "32 BTC is immaterial. Saylor selling for the first time in four years, into a market already bleeding flows, is not." Spot Bitcoin ETFs had logged eleven consecutive sessions of net outflows totalling more than $3.4 billion before the disclosure landed; May's net outflows of $2.43 billion represented the worst month of 2026. The Wintermute line landed because it named what the data already implied — the last reason for bulls to dismiss deteriorating flow data had just traded away.

The company's answer

CEO Phong Le, who has led Strategy's day-to-day operations since Michael Saylor stepped back to the Executive Chairman role, was deliberate in framing the sale as procedural rather than philosophical. "We wanted to inoculate the market and we wanted to test our processes," Le said. "We learned that everything works." He added that dividend obligations could have been met without touching Bitcoin — that the company holds other capital-raising levers — and characterised the move as a one-time stress test of internal systems.

The follow-through supported that reading, in part. Between June 1 and June 7, Strategy acquired 1,550 Bitcoin at an average of $65,332 per coin — well below both its long-run average and the price at which it had sold the previous week. That makes the firm a net buyer across the two-week window, its cost basis diluted slightly lower into the downturn. Saylor has maintained his public position that Bitcoin is a hedge against inflation and large government.

What the episode has exposed, however, is a structural overhang. Leveraged products tracking MSTR — including MSTU, which offers two-times daily exposure — have amplified losses on the underlying; MSTU is down roughly 91% over the past year against MSTR's own 67% decline. Apollo Crypto has described this as a feedback loop in which MSTR weakness pressures these instruments, investor redemptions add to MSTR selling pressure, and so on.

The next filing to watch is Strategy's weekly 8-K, which the company now discloses on a rolling basis. If Bitcoin tests and fails to hold $60,000 — a level analysts are watching closely given the combination of ETF outflows, macro headwinds from elevated Treasury yields, and geopolitical uncertainty from the US-Iran conflict — the question of whether dividend obligations force further sales will move from theoretical to urgent.
