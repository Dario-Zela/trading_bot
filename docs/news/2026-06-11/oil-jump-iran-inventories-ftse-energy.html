Oil jumps on Iran risk and falling US inventories as energy lifts London equities

The Strait of Hormuz, seen from 35,000 feet — the waterway has been closed to commercial shipping since March 2026 following US and Israeli strikes on Iran — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
WTI crude rose 2.1% to $91 and Brent to $93 as Trump threatened fresh Iran strikes and US inventories posted a 7.2m-barrel drop to four-month lows, lifting London's energy stocks.

Oil reversed an early decline to settle sharply higher on Wednesday, as President Trump issued some of his most pointed threats yet against Iran and weekly US inventory data showed crude stocks falling to four-month lows — a combination that put fresh upward pressure on a market that had been drifting since April's peak.

West Texas Intermediate rose 2.1 per cent to close above $91 a barrel. Brent crude tracked close behind, gaining roughly 2 per cent to settle near $93. Both benchmarks remain well below the highs recorded after the initial outbreak of US-Iran hostilities in late March — WTI touched the mid-$120s in April — but the direction on Wednesday was unambiguous.

The threat and the context

Trump said the United States would "hit Iran hard again today" and declined to specify targets. The remarks broke from what had become a pattern of qualified warnings and represented the latest iteration of a maximum-pressure policy toward a country he has accused of using drawn-out ceasefire negotiations to buy time.

The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed to commercial shipping since March, following US and Israeli strikes on Iran. Middle East oil producers have cut output by more than 11 million barrels per day relative to pre-conflict levels, making the waterway's status the single largest variable in the global supply equation. Trump's comments diminish near-term prospects for a negotiated reopening — and, by extension, for any easing of the supply shock that has reshaped energy markets since late winter.

"Oil prices are rising once more as Trump warns Iran has to pay the price for taking too long to negotiate."
— Fawad Razaqzada, StoneX

Razaqzada assessed that upside risks to crude price forecasts remain elevated. Shell chief executive Wael Sawan, speaking at a conference earlier in the week, described the market as "trying to find some equilibrium" and "more driven by short-term headlines," while acknowledging that inventories are being drawn down rapidly. The tension he identified goes to the core of current pricing: fundamentals are tightening, but sentiment remains hostage to each development in US-Iran diplomacy.

The inventory signal

The secondary driver on Wednesday was the weekly US petroleum status report, which showed commercial crude oil stocks declining by 7.2 million barrels. National inventories are now at their lowest since early February, sitting approximately 3 per cent below the five-year seasonal average. Stockpiles at Cushing, Oklahoma — the WTI delivery hub — also fell, though they remain above minimum operational levels.

A drawdown of this scale would, in a normal supply environment, represent a meaningful tightening. Against the backdrop of a Hormuz closure removing roughly a fifth of seaborne crude trade, it functions as confirmation that demand is absorbing supply faster than the market had recently priced. Combined commercial crude and Strategic Petroleum Reserve inventories have fallen some 90 million barrels from their recent peak — a cumulative draw reflecting both consumption and the structural limits of reserve releases as a substitute for actual production.

Crude is still down more than 25 per cent from its April high, a gap that reflects the dampening effects of weaker Chinese demand and coordinated SPR releases by consuming nations. But each week of deep inventory draws narrows the floor under prices, regardless of what is said at the podium.

London: energy lifts, but the tone is cautious

The FTSE 100 closed 0.3 per cent higher at 10,254.8, though the modest gain did little to shift the index from its position near three-week lows. The session's character was defensive rather than broadly constructive: the energy sector rose 1.9 per cent as oil majors tracked the crude move, while consumer staples — Tesco and Unilever each adding more than 2 per cent — provided the other main source of support.

Banks retreated, with HSBC and Standard Chartered each falling more than 1 per cent, partly on concerns about Beijing's capital controls and their effect on Hong Kong-related earnings. WH Smith plunged 16.2 per cent after cutting profit forecasts and announcing an equity raise. Energy and staples leading while financials pull back is the textbook defensive rotation — investors shielding themselves against a scenario they are not yet prepared to call resolved.

That the FTSE 100 closed green owes much to its structural composition. As a commodity and energy-heavy benchmark, it tends to absorb oil price rises in a way that more technology-oriented European indices do not. London's relative resilience on days of elevated geopolitical risk has been a recurring pattern since the conflict began — an artefact of index construction more than a reading of broader economic confidence.

What to watch

The next scheduled EIA petroleum status report arrives in a week. If inventories continue to decline at the current pace — or accelerate — the case for WTI sustaining a move above $95 strengthens regardless of diplomatic signals. The more immediate variable is Trump himself: markets have learned over the past three months that his rhetoric and follow-through are not perfectly correlated, but they are also aware that some threats have been acted upon.

The specific question for oil traders in coming sessions is whether negotiations toward a partial Hormuz reopening — reported last week as still technically ongoing — survive another escalation in public posture. A credible signal of progress would likely cap crude near current levels; a fresh breakdown could push WTI back toward the $100 mark it last traded in April.
