Gilead's HIV franchise holds firm despite approaching patent cliffs

Gilead Sciences headquarters in Foster City, California, where the company's HIV franchise generates more than $20 billion in annual revenue. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Gilead raises HIV growth guidance to 8% on Yeztugo's $1B launch and Biktarvy's 2036 patent cover, as BIC/LEN awaits an August FDA decision.

Gilead Sciences has spent much of the past decade building an HIV business that the rest of pharma has regarded with something approaching envy. Biktarvy, the company's dominant treatment pill, now holds more than 52 per cent of the HIV treatment market and helped drive record franchise revenues of $20.8 billion in 2025 — a figure that would be remarkable for any single therapeutic area. Descovy, its daily oral prevention option, simultaneously reached record U.S. market share in the PrEP segment.

That momentum has carried into 2026. Gilead raised its full-year HIV sales growth guidance to approximately 8 per cent year-over-year — up from the 6 per cent projected in February — driven by better-than-expected first-quarter results and the early trajectory of Yeztugo, the company's twice-yearly injectable lenacapavir. In Q1 alone, Yeztugo posted $166 million in net sales; Gilead subsequently lifted its full-year forecast for the drug from $800 million to $1 billion, calling it an "unprecedented launch trajectory." Yeztugo — approved by the FDA as a first-in-class capsid inhibitor for HIV prevention — requires only two injections a year rather than a daily pill, an adherence profile that public health advocates have long argued matters considerably in PrEP uptake. Whether that advantage translates to broad commercial dominance over Descovy, rather than supplementing it, remains to be established in practice.

The unspoken caveat in that picture is that investor attention is turning to what lies beyond the current blockbusters. Biktarvy's U.S. patent protection now extends to April 2036 following settled litigation with generic manufacturers — a longer runway than many had anticipated, and one that forestalls the sharpest cliff edge. But a runway ending in 2036 is still a runway ending, and the question being pressed is whether next-generation products can sustain the revenue line in the years that follow. Gilead has not quantified the exposure.

Succession on the pipeline

The case Gilead is making runs through lenacapavir in several forms. The FDA has granted priority review of a new drug application for a once-daily single-tablet combining bictegravir and lenacapavir (BIC/LEN), with a Prescription Drug User Fee Act decision date of August 27, 2026. The filing draws on ARTISTRY-1 and ARTISTRY-2 trial data presented at CROI 2026, which showed maintained viral suppression and improvements in fasting lipid profiles among patients switching from complex regimens — clinically modest but meaningful for an aging HIV patient population with comorbidities.

Separately, Gilead and Merck announced on June 8 that the Phase 3 ISLEND-1 and ISLEND-2 studies of an oral once-weekly islatravir/lenacapavir tablet met their primary efficacy endpoints at Week 48, demonstrating non-inferiority to standard daily regimens in virologically suppressed adults. A once-weekly oral therapy would represent a genuine dosing advance; however, regulatory submissions have not been filed and an approval timeline remains speculative. Further out, GS-3242 — a long-acting integrase inhibitor currently in Phase I — could eventually combine with lenacapavir for a twice-yearly injectable treatment option, though data at this stage are too early to weight commercially. The company has pointed to as many as seven HIV launches by 2033.

Whether that pipeline is sufficient to offset Biktarvy's eventual erosion is the quantitative question the available disclosures do not answer. The August BIC/LEN decision is the nearest concrete catalyst.
