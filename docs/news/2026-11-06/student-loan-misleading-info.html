'Misleading' student loan information omitted higher-salary repayment examples

In one sentence
School student-loan talks from about a decade ago reportedly omitted higher-salary repayment examples; critics call them misleading, though one expert says the information was accurate.

A set of student-loan presentations given to English school pupils about a decade ago left out examples of what higher earners would repay, and used an analogy that critics now call misleading. The story is a BBC report from March 2026, and the claims in it are contested. It does not show that a regulator failed, and it gives no measure of how many borrowers were affected.

What was said

According to the BBC's reporting, as syndicated online, the talks were delivered to schoolchildren by graduates hired for the purpose. They were reportedly told to avoid words such as "debt". The presentations compared Plan 2 student loans to a £30-a-month phone contract. Plan 2 is the repayment plan for those who started English undergraduate courses between 2012 and 2023.

The National Union of Students' president, Amira Campbell, called the phone-contract comparison "deeply misleading". She said the script had "aged so poorly".

Funmi Olufunwa, a financial educator, focused on what was missing. She said that leaving interest-rate details off the slides was "strange", because that is "one of the most fundamental pieces of information". She was also reported as saying that repayment examples at higher salaries, such as £50,000 or £60,000, should have been shown. Under an income-contingent loan, the repayment is a share of earnings above a threshold. Examples that stop at modest salaries therefore show only part of what a borrower might pay.

The counter-argument

Nick Hillman of the Higher Education Policy Institute took a different view. He said the tours showed that the government did explain to young people how the system worked, and that the information presented was accurate. That is a narrower claim than the critics make. A presentation can be accurate on each point and still mislead through what it leaves out or how it frames the loan.

The Student Loans Company said the tours were "commissioned, contracted and led" by the government of the day. That places responsibility with ministers of that period rather than with the lender. It does not say what any regulator did or should have done.

What is not established

The report does not settle several points that would matter to any question of oversight. No named regulator appears in the accounts available. The slides themselves were not available to this writer, and the BBC article could not be read in full. There is no estimate of how many pupils saw the talks. There is also no evidence that anyone took out a loan, or chose a course, because of them. The talks took place about ten years ago, and the people who attended are now adults who may already be repaying.

The broader record is more substantial. MPs on the Treasury Committee have previously said that the government's promotion of student loans in England and Wales amounted to mis-selling. They also said the Student Loans Company did not make clear that terms could be changed after a loan was taken out. Those findings concern a related but separate matter, and their exact wording should be checked against the committee's report.

What to watch

The test is whether the Department for Education, which now holds responsibility for the scheme, publishes the original presentation materials and says how the content was approved. The Treasury Committee could also return to the subject. Until then, the case rests on contested descriptions of decade-old slides.
