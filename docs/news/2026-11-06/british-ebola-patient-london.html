British Ebola patient evacuated from DR Congo arrives at London hospital

In one sentence
A British medic infected with Ebola in DR Congo has been flown to London's Royal Free isolation unit; UKHSA says the risk to the wider public remains low.

A British health worker who caught Ebola while working on the outbreak in the Democratic Republic of Congo has been flown to London and admitted to the Royal Free Hospital. Reports on 5 November said the patient is being treated in the hospital's high-level isolation unit. The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) says the risk to the wider public remains low.

The case is a test of Britain's specialist capacity and of its willingness to bring infected nationals home. It is not, on present evidence, a sign that the outbreak is spreading in Britain.

What is known

The details come from a small number of reports, and the main account is the Guardian's. This article could not read the Channels Television wire copy, and it has not seen a full UKHSA or NHS statement. The following should therefore be read with that caveat.

  • The patient. Reports describe a medic who contracted the virus while working on the response in DR Congo. One outlet calls the person a doctor and another a healthcare worker. The patient has not been named, and no update on their condition has been found since the transfer.
  • The hospital. The patient is at the Royal Free in Hampstead, north London. It houses the country's principal high-level isolation unit.
  • The public-health position. Renu Bindra, deputy director of the all-hazards public health response team at UKHSA, said "the risk to the wider public remains low".

This is not the first such case this year. In July, a UK-resident humanitarian worker was brought to London after possible exposure to Ebola in DR Congo. UKHSA said that person had been treating patients, and reports said they had no symptoms and that the move was precautionary. The new case is different: the patient is reported to be infected.

Why the unit matters

The Royal Free's unit has treated British Ebola patients before. William Pooley, the first Briton to contract the virus, was flown there from Sierra Leone in August 2014. Pauline Cafferkey was treated there the following year. These units are built to contain pathogens that spread through contact with bodily fluids. Patients are nursed in negative-pressure rooms by staff in full protective equipment, and the case for treating them in the UK rests on that kind of care.

Ebola spreads through direct contact with the blood or other fluids of someone who is symptomatic, or with contaminated materials. Transmission by people who are not yet unwell is not considered a significant route. That is the basis for the low-risk assessment, though it holds only if the patient was isolated early and moved under strict controls. Those details have not been published in the reports seen.

Reports put the death toll in the wider outbreak above 5,300, the vast majority in DR Congo, citing the continent's top health agency. That figure comes from a single summary and has not been checked against the agency's own data. If it is correct, the outbreak is large and prolonged, and the exposure of foreign responders is a continuing risk rather than a one-off.

What to watch

Beyond this one patient, little changes unless the case reveals something further. Three points will show whether it does:

  1. The patient's condition. UKHSA or the hospital trust may issue an update. Survival after treatment in a high-level unit has historically been far better than in field settings.
  2. Contact tracing. An account of who the patient was exposed to, in DR Congo and on the evacuation flight, would show whether any imported-infection risk exists.
  3. Responder safeguards. Aid organisations will ask how the infection occurred, and whether protective equipment or procedures failed. That may shape how readily agencies deploy staff.

Until UKHSA publishes a fuller account, the facts above remain provisional.
