Bank of England raises Bank Rate to 4%, its first hike in over three years

In one sentence
The Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75% to 4% by 8–1, its first rise since 2023, but only Bailey's 'another increase is likely' guidance hints at more, and Taylor says this is not a hiking cycle.

The Bank of England raised Bank Rate by a quarter of a percentage point to 4% on Thursday. It is the first increase since August 2023, and it ends the run of cuts that took the rate from its 5.25% peak down to 3.75%. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 8–1. Its governor says another rise is likely unless the conflict in the Middle East eases. A member of the committee who voted for the rise, though, says it is not the start of a hiking cycle.

The decision

Eight members backed a 25-basis-point move: Andrew Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Megan Greene, Clare Lombardelli, Huw Pill, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor. Catherine Mann dissented in the hawkish direction, preferring 50 basis points and a rate of 4.25%. According to CNBC's account, she described her 25-basis-point votes as a "down payment" against second-round inflation effects and said a half-point move would have been "good policy".

The vote was well flagged. CNBC reported that markets had priced a hike at better than 99% by the morning of the announcement. A Reuters poll cited by Sovereign Magazine found 54 of 63 economists expecting a rise. A month earlier, roughly nine in ten had expected the Bank to hold. The change in the economists' view tracks the change in the Bank's inflation forecast, which is the real news in the minutes.

Why now: energy, not wages

CPI inflation was 3.5% in September, and the Bank's summary attributes about one percentage point of that to direct energy effects, with motor fuel the main driver. The committee's central projection has inflation at around 4½% in the first quarter of 2027 and about 5% in the second, before it falls back as energy effects fade and policy stays restrictive.

The forecast rests on a sharp rise in commodity prices. In the 15 working days to 27 October, Brent crude averaged $108 a barrel and UK front-month gas 216 pence a therm. Those figures are 38% and 81% higher than at the July forecast round. The Bank also notes that energy supply buffers were run down during 2026. New Budget measures are expected to take about a quarter of a point off CPI by mid-2027, which softens the peak but does not remove it. Growth, meanwhile, has been stronger than the Bank expected, though the committee judges it likely to "fall back somewhat" in coming quarters.

The policy case is therefore one of insurance. The committee judged that a price shock this large, arriving on top of years of above-target inflation, risked feeding into pay and price-setting if it was left alone. Mann's dissent, with her own projection of 4.9% by mid-2027, shows how close she thinks that risk sits to the central case.

How firm is the 'reversal'?

The tidy reading is that the easing cycle is over and a tightening bias has replaced it. The Bank's own language supports that only in part. The summary says policy is "not on a pre-set path" and that the committee will "adjust Bank Rate as necessary" to return inflation to 2% in the medium term. Taylor went further, saying "this is not the start of a hiking cycle" and calling the move a risk-management "one-for-now" step.

Bailey was more guarded about what comes next. According to Sovereign Magazine, he said "another increase is likely" unless the Middle East conflict is resolved. CNBC's account has him seeing a case for one more rise and then a more conditional approach, with risks to energy prices and inflation skewed to the upside absent progress towards a settlement. Taken together, the governor's remarks and Taylor's point to one more move at most, with energy prices deciding whether it happens. They do not describe a campaign of steady tightening.

Market pricing sits in that gap. CNBC reported traders putting about a one-in-four chance on a further rise at the 17 December meeting. Deutsche Bank, cited in a market summary, expects the committee to reach 4.25% in either December or February. A rate cut, which was the working assumption for much of the past two years, is now off the table in the near term.

Gilts, sterling and households

The immediate market reaction was small, which is what a fully priced decision looks like. The 10-year gilt yield was about 5.15%, up 2 basis points, and the 2-year yield rose 4 basis points to about 4.78%. Yields had been climbing before the announcement. Sterling, which CNBC says reached a post-Brexit high against the dollar on Wednesday, was flat against both the dollar and the euro on Thursday afternoon. A currency that had already rallied on expectations of a hike had little reason to rally further once it came.

The larger effects will take longer to show. The 2-year yield is the usual anchor for fixed-rate mortgage pricing, and at nearly 4.8% it already sits above the new Bank Rate, so lenders had been repricing before the decision. The people who feel the quarter-point rise directly are those on tracker and standard variable rate mortgages, who see their payments rise within weeks. Those coming off fixed deals will refinance into a market that is now pricing a higher path than it was a year ago. The Bank's summary does not quantify the mortgage effect, and the sources reviewed here do not either. For the public finances, a 10-year gilt yield above 5% means higher debt-servicing costs, arriving a week after the Chancellor's Budget.

The move also has consequences for rate-sensitive companies, a theme explored in National Grid's half-year results, where the call turned on investment and allowed returns after the rate rise.

What to watch

Three dates and one variable will settle whether this was a one-off or a first step. October CPI, due before the next meeting, will show whether energy costs are still lifting headline inflation towards the Bank's 5% peak. The committee's next decision, with minutes, is on 17 December, and the vote split will show whether Mann's call for a larger move gains support. The governor's own condition is the most direct signal of all: a settlement in the Middle East that brings oil and gas prices down from the levels in the Bank's forecast would remove the case for a second rise. Without one, the case Bailey described becomes harder to argue against.
