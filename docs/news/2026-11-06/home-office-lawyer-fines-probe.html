Home Office investigates after BBC finds lawyer offering to make fines disappear

In one sentence
The Home Office is investigating after BBC recordings allegedly showed a Bradford solicitor offering to deal with a £45,000 illegal-working fine for a 10% up-front fee, which he denies.

A BBC undercover investigation has alleged that an immigration solicitor was willing to help an employer escape a five-figure penalty for hiring illegal workers, for a percentage fee paid up front. The Home Office has since opened an investigation. The allegation matters beyond one firm because it suggests that civil penalties, which are meant to be settled through a formal process, may be treated by some intermediaries as something that can be negotiated away for money.

What the BBC filmed

According to the Law Gazette's account of the BBC report, a reporter posed as the owner of a mini-mart facing a £45,000 fine for employing illegal workers. The reporter secretly recorded a conversation with Mohammed Alias Yousaf, a solicitor admitted in 2005, at Liberty Solicitors in Bradford.

Yousaf appears on the recordings to say his services would cost 10% of the fine, payable in advance. On a £45,000 penalty that would come to £4,500. He also apparently suggested the liability could be shifted onto a person who was no longer in the country. The Gazette does not give the full wording, and the BBC's original footage should be read against it.

Yousaf denies ever advising anyone to give false information. He says he has acted lawfully at all times. Nothing has been proved, and these are allegations drawn from a journalist's recordings.

A wider network?

Secondary summaries of the BBC's coverage describe a related strand. They say a fixer was filmed offering to wipe out fines of up to £60,000 for firms employing illegal workers, and that a paralegal at the firm was suspended or dismissed afterwards. Those summaries disagree on his employment status. Both men named in them reject the claims. This reporting has not been checked against the BBC's own pages, so the scale of any wider operation remains unconfirmed.

Who is looking into it

The Home Office has said it is investigating. Reports say it is working with the National Crime Agency and police forces, including West Yorkshire Police, though the Law Gazette article does not mention the Home Office response. The Solicitors Regulation Authority, which regulates solicitors in England and Wales, has said it will examine the BBC report. It is understood to have looked at Yousaf's conduct before and will now review the new material. Its spokesperson said it would consider the information "to see if there are any issues arising which we need to look into further".

The two inquiries look at different questions. The Home Office is concerned with whether penalties were evaded or manipulated. The regulator is concerned with whether a solicitor breached professional standards. Either could lead to further action, but neither has reached findings.

What it means

Civil penalties for employing illegal workers are one of the main tools the government uses against unauthorised working. Their effect depends on the belief that they are collected. A fee-for-fixing market, if it exists at any scale, would weaken that, and would also leave employers and workers exposed to advisers who may not be acting lawfully.

The next markers are the outcomes of the SRA's review of the BBC material and the Home Office's findings, including whether any further firms or individuals are named.
