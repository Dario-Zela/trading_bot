French blood storage disrupted by freezer hacks as culprit theories swirl

In one sentence
A cyberattack that overheated freezers at hundreds of French supermarkets is confirmed, but no source reviewed links it to blood storage, and nobody has been named as the culprit.

A cyberattack on refrigeration systems in France has been confirmed, but the victims were supermarkets. Reporting that freezers holding blood were hit could not be verified. Searches of English-language coverage turned up no account of blood-storage equipment being disrupted. Nothing was found from French authorities or the manufacturer either. The one source behind that framing, a BBC Technology item, could not be located. Until it is, the blood-storage claim should be treated as unconfirmed, and possibly a mislabelling of the supermarket incident.

What is established

On the night of Sunday 11 October and into Monday 12 October, fridges and freezers at hundreds of French supermarkets were remotely driven to high temperatures. Some units reached about 50°C (122°F), according to Al Jazeera's account. Stores had to throw away fresh and frozen stock, and several reported fires.

The retailers named include:

  • At least 300 Intermarché and Netto stores.
  • More than 100 Coopérative U outlets.
  • About 80 Carrefour supermarkets.
  • Roughly 5% of Monoprix's 490 or so stores.
  • "Several" E.Leclerc stores.

Intermarché's chief executive, Thierry Cotillard, put losses at "tens of millions of euros nationwide in a single day." Spain, Portugal, Poland, Germany and Belgium were also reportedly affected, according to an analyst at the security firm Sekoia.

The weakness

The Paris prosecutor's office called it a "large-scale" attack on connected refrigeration. A source close to Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said the national cybersecurity agency, ANSSI, had traced it to a vulnerability in software from the Danish firm Danfoss. These are the controllers that run a store's cooling.

Danfoss says the affected units were running outdated firmware. It says almost all were legacy products that no longer receive security updates. Researchers at Claroty had published flaws in some Danfoss controllers on 9 August. Investigators have not said publicly whether those specific flaws were used. One report said some units were still on default passwords.

Who did it

No attacker has been named, and no group has claimed responsibility. Commentators have offered competing readings. One holds that well-known, unpatched flaws mean this was an open door that needed little sophistication. Another asks why a state actor would disrupt only a subset of fridges. Neither theory is backed by evidence so far.

The medical question

The link to health is a warning, not an event. One analyst, Steven Lachance, said future targets could include energy infrastructure or hospitals. That is a forecast. Hospital blood banks and pharmacy cold stores use similar kinds of connected monitoring and control equipment, so the concern is plausible. But no source reviewed reports damage to blood or other medical stock. Whether this is one breach or a wider weakness in cold-chain systems is also open. The supermarket evidence points to a wide, known flaw in one vendor's ageing hardware. It does not yet show a problem across medical systems.

What to watch

Two things would settle this. The first is the ANSSI and prosecutors' findings on how the controllers were reached and by whom. The second is any statement from French health authorities or the national blood service, the Établissement français du sang. If either confirms that blood storage was affected, this story changes. Until then, the verified record is a supermarket attack.
