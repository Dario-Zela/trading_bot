Macau jails pro-democracy campaigner in its first national security case

In one sentence
Macau has jailed former lawmaker Au Kam San for 12.5 years in its first national security case, showing the 2009 law now reaches political campaigning.

A Macau court has jailed Au Kam San, a veteran pro-democracy campaigner and former lawmaker, for 12 and a half years in the city's first national security prosecution. The sentence, handed down by the Court of First Instance, shows that the security law Macau enacted in 2009 is now being used against a political figure, not only against the street-protest cases that made Hong Kong's experience so well known.

The case

Au, 69, sat in the legislature for nearly two decades and was among the most recognisable voices of Macau's small democratic camp. He was arrested in July 2025. His detention was the first publicly known prosecution under the city's national security framework.

Reports say he was convicted of subversion of state power, breach of secrecy, and establishing contact with organisations or individuals outside Macau to commit acts endangering national security. Subversion alone carries up to 25 years in prison under the law, and a conviction on several counts can lead to a longer total. The 12.5-year term is therefore well short of the maximum.

The trial was held behind closed doors over three days in September. The court said the judge chose a closed hearing so that national security interests would not be undermined. A reform approved in March 2026 gave Macau's courts explicit power to decide whether such trials are held in public. The verdict was not delivered until weeks after the hearings ended.

Why the 'first' matters

Macau's law dates from 2009, when the city passed legislation under Article 23 of its Basic Law, the mini-constitution it has operated under since the 1999 handover from Portugal. For sixteen years it went unused in any publicly known prosecution. Beijing's critics in Hong Kong took the opposite path: a 2020 law, imposed from above, was followed by mass prosecutions, including the case of 45 activists who received four to ten years.

Macau has been far quieter. Its democratic camp is small, and its politics are dominated by establishment and business-aligned figures. Au's case suggests that the law's reach into political campaigning does not depend on large protests. The charge of contact with outside groups is notably broad. It gives prosecutors a route to treat ordinary links with overseas organisations as a security matter, and the evidence in a closed trial is not open to public scrutiny.

That interpretation rests on the structure of the charges and the closed hearing, not on any published reasoning from the court. Without the judgment's full text, outsiders cannot say what conduct was held to cross the line.

Reaction

Au's wife, Virginia Cheang Mio San, has called the proceedings a political trial and "baseless persecution" in an open letter. Human rights groups have criticised the case as a crackdown on peaceful dissent and urged his unconditional release. The court has said it approved prison visits for his family. Macau's leader has publicly praised the city's justice system in connection with the case.

What to watch

Two questions follow. The first is whether Au appeals, and whether any appeal hearing is open to the public. The second is whether prosecutors bring further cases against other activists or former politicians, which would show whether this was a one-off or the start of a pattern. For investors and gaming operators, whose business depends on the city's legal predictability, the practical test is whether the law is applied narrowly to dissidents or widens to cover wider commercial and civic life. One conviction does not answer that.

The 'first' claim and sentence length come from wire and news reports found in this research. The full court judgment was not available.
