Minister tells energy suppliers to do more on unpaid household bills this winter

In one sentence
Ministers are pressing energy suppliers to do more on unpaid household bills this winter, but without published figures it is unclear whether this is a policy shift or a warning.

Ministers have told energy suppliers to do more about unpaid household bills as the cold weather starts. The detail is thin. The Guardian's politics coverage, the only named source, carries the headline, but the minister's name, the exact wording and any figures were not available when this piece was written. That leaves open whether this is a policy shift or a warning for the winter.

What is known

The direction is clear. Government wants suppliers to take more responsibility for customers who are falling behind. Suppliers already have obligations here. They are required to work with customers to agree affordable payment plans, and Ofgem's rules set out how arrears are handled.

The scale of the problem is large, though the figures below were not checked against official data. A secondary report puts household energy arrears at a record of about £6bn at the end of June 2026. The same report says Cornwall Insight expects bills to reach a three-year high this winter. Neither claim has been confirmed against Ofgem or government figures.

A precedent, with caveats

The closest precedent is August 2024, when the then energy minister, Miatta Fahnbulleh, called suppliers to a meeting. Energy UK put sector debt at about £3.2bn at that point. Afterwards the government said suppliers would spend a combined £500m helping struggling customers, through loans and reduced standing charges.

That episode shows how these exchanges usually work. Ministers set expectations in public, and suppliers then announce voluntary support, often framed as spending they were already planning. Nothing in the available material suggests this round is different, and no supplier response has been published.

Who pays

The commercial question is where the cost lands. Debt support has three possible funders: suppliers' margins, other customers through bills, or the Exchequer. Ministers pressing suppliers to take on more suggests the first, at least in rhetoric.

Centrica, owner of British Gas, is the largest listed UK supplier and so the most visible exposure. Others are also affected, including unlisted suppliers with thinner balance sheets. No Centrica figures or statements on arrears were available. Any effect on earnings therefore depends on the size of the commitments and on how much falls under the regulated price cap. The cap's allowance for bad debt is meant to cover part of this cost.

What to watch

Three things would settle the question. First, whether the government publishes a figure or a scheme, rather than a general call. Second, whether Energy UK or individual suppliers respond with a spending pledge, as in 2024. Third, whether Ofgem changes its debt rules or the cap's debt allowance. Centrica's next trading update would show whether arrears provisions have moved.
