America's rare-earth deadline is under 60 days away, with a new Congress about to convene

In one sentence
A US ban on Chinese-origin rare earths and magnets in defence supply starts 1 January 2027, two days before a new Congress sits, with domestic capacity behind schedule.

The United States will stop letting defence contractors buy certain Chinese-origin materials on 1 January 2027, 56 days from today. Two days later, on 3 January, a new Congress is sworn in. The industry meant to replace those imports is behind schedule, and any relief, whether a waiver, an extension or new money, is likely to run through a legislature that has just changed hands.

The deadline

Under federal procurement rules, defence and other manufacturers are barred from 1 January 2027 from buying rare earths, magnets, tungsten, molybdenum and tantalum originating in China, Russia, Iran or North Korea. The rule is aimed at the most concentrated link in the chain. Rare earths must be processed before they can be made into magnets, and that processing is dominated by China.

A Reuters report in July, syndicated by BNN Bloomberg, said the administration may have to allow some Chinese material because US industry is struggling to meet the date. A separate report on permitting delays points to slow approvals as a drag on new domestic capacity.

The other clock is Beijing's. China's suspension of its broader October 2025 rare-earth export controls is reported to lapse on 10 November, with a separate pause on gallium, germanium and antimony curbs ending on 27 November, according to GoldInvest. That source is a trade-press outlet and the dates should be treated as reported, not confirmed. If accurate, they fall inside the same window, so supply could tighten just as the US ban approaches.

A Congress with little to show

The legislative record is thin. An August analysis from The Breakthrough Institute found that, despite dozens of critical-minerals bills, Congress had enacted just one. It listed items that would fall to the lame-duck session or the next Congress, among them the deadline for the USGS Earth MRI mapping programme and the Export-Import Bank's charter, which expires at the end of 2026.

That matters because the Export-Import Bank is one of the tools the government can use to finance overseas mineral projects. If the charter lapses or is patched at the last minute, the financing picture for allied supply deals gets harder to read.

What changes in January

The midterms handed Democrats control of Congress, according to this edition's lead coverage. Critical minerals have drawn support from both parties, so a clean partisan split is not the expected outcome. What changes is who sets the agenda and how much oversight the administration's deal-making attracts. Any waiver for Chinese-origin material would be hard to defend on either side, since the ban was written to cut dependence.

The cost is already visible downstream. Aerospace and defence buyers depend on specialist alloys and magnets, a squeeze covered in the materials race in aerospace.

The source reporting does not name a single deal at the centre of the deadline, and the link between the procurement date and a specific congressional fight is an inference from the calendar, not a reported plan. The near-term markers are clear. China's reported 10 November expiry comes first, then the lame-duck session's handling of the Export-Import Bank charter before year-end, then 1 January. Whether the Pentagon and the Commerce Department issue waivers before the new Congress arrives will show how seriously the date is being treated.
