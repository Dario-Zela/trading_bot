National Grid half-year results: what the call said about investment and returns after the rate rise

In one sentence
National Grid's half-year print matters for how the Bank Rate rise affects its debt costs and regulated returns, but no verified figures or call evidence were available.

National Grid, the FTSE 100 networks group, reports half-year results this week, and the question hanging over the print is a simple one. Does the Bank of England's rise in Bank Rate to 4% change what the company can earn on the grid it is building, or what it pays to fund it? The material available for this piece cannot answer that. The seed source is a Motley Fool preview. It cites no results release and no call transcript, and no 2026 figures could be verified. So this article sets out the framework and does not report numbers it cannot support.

What the period covers

National Grid's financial year ends on 31 March. The half-year results therefore cover April to September, the first six months of the fiscal year, and they are the company's main mid-year check on its capital programme. The figures that usually matter are capital investment to date, the regulated asset base (RAB) on which allowed returns are earned, underlying earnings per share, and net debt.

Why a rate rise matters to a regulated utility

The link between interest rates and a utility like National Grid runs through two channels, and they do not move together.

The first is funding. National Grid carries a large debt load to pay for network investment, and higher rates raise the cost of new borrowing and of refinancing maturing bonds. Some of the cost is passed through, because regulators set allowances for debt that track market costs. The pass-through is partial and lagged, and the regulator's methodology decides how much of a rate move reaches customers and how much stays with shareholders.

The second is valuation. Regulated utilities are often treated as bond proxies, so share prices tend to fall when gilt yields rise. A rate rise can therefore hurt the shares even if the operating results are unchanged.

Regulated returns are set in multi-year price controls, so a single rate move does not reset them. That is why the commentary on the call matters more than the headline profit figure. Management's language on allowed returns, on how much of the new debt cost is recoverable, and on whether the investment plan is still fully financeable shows whether the rate environment is changing the economics.

What to look for in the call

The useful evidence would be specific:

  • Investment pace. Whether capital spending is on track against the full-year plan, and whether any projects are being re-phased because of financing costs or supply-chain constraints.
  • Debt costs. The average cost of debt against the allowance in the regulatory settlement, and the refinancing needed over the next two years.
  • Funding mix. Whether management signals any reliance on new equity, asset sales or hybrid capital to fund the programme.
  • Regulatory read-across. Any comment on how Ofgem's current framework treats rate moves between reviews, and whether the company sees the returns as adequate.

Without those answers, the print is a routine half-year update from a large regulated utility. A rate rise is a reason to look closely at it, but the rise alone does not make the results a story.

What comes next

The immediate test is the share price reaction and any change in analysts' estimates once the call transcript is public. The longer-run test is the next gilt-yield move. Further Bank Rate changes would feed into debt-cost allowances and, in time, into the debate over whether regulated returns need revisiting. A confirmed set of the company's own figures should replace the framework above once the primary release is checked.
