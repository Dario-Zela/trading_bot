California governor reportedly commutes sentences of all 548 death row inmates

In one sentence
Gavin Newsom has announced the commutation of all 548 California death sentences to life without parole, though the state Supreme Court must still approve roughly two-thirds of them.

California's governor has moved to commute the death sentences of every person on the state's death row. Gavin Newsom announced on 5 November that all 548 condemned prisoners, 529 men and 19 women according to his office, would have their sentences converted to life without the possibility of parole. The move would empty the largest death row in the United States. It is not yet fully in force, because the state Supreme Court has to sign off on a large share of the cases.

The story first surfaced as a single-source report. It has since been confirmed by the governor's own account and by several outlets, among them CNN, NPR, the Washington Post, CalMatters and the Death Penalty Information Center (DPIC). Early reports were loose on one point: whether the sentences were already commuted or were being commuted. The answer is a bit of each.

What Newsom did, and what remains to be done

The instrument is executive clemency. California law lets a governor commute a sentence on his own authority. For anyone with more than one felony conviction, though, the governor needs the state Supreme Court's approval. That condition applies to more than half of the condemned. DPIC puts the figure at about two-thirds, and NBC's reporting gives 382 cases. The grants take effect at once for the remaining third or so. For the rest they are final only once the justices have ruled.

The commutations also do not trigger automatic resentencing. Those affected move from a death sentence to a life term without parole, and with that change they lose the right to the appointed appeal lawyers that capital cases carry.

Newsom framed the decision in moral and practical terms. "By every measure, California's system for administering the death penalty is a failure," he said, adding: "I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California's values."

The practical case rests on a long record. California has carried out 13 executions since reinstating capital punishment in 1978, the last in January 2006. It has spent an estimated $6 billion on the machinery of capital punishment. A May 2026 report by the Habeas Corpus Resource Center found that about 70 per cent of death row prisoners were still waiting for a lawyer to be appointed for their appeals. Newsom imposed a moratorium on executions in March 2019 and had the state begin dismantling the row in 2022. This act finishes what those steps began.

A record, set against a thin history

The scale is without precedent. DPIC calls it the largest mass commutation in US history. By its count it is the eighth time in the modern era that a governor has cleared an entire state's death row, and it more than doubles the capital clemencies granted over the past half-century. CalMatters, working with the Marshall Project, reports that only about 440 death-row prisoners have received commutations nationwide since 1972.

The nearest comparison is Illinois in 2003, when Governor George Ryan commuted 167 sentences on his way out of office. The timing here is similar: Newsom's term ends in January. DPIC estimates that roughly 1,388 people will remain under sentence of death across the country, the lowest figure since 1984. California's share of the national total falls by nearly a third.

Robin Maher, DPIC's executive director, called it a "decisive moment in the history of the death penalty in the United States."

Reaction, and the limits of the act

Reaction has divided in ways that do not follow simple lines. Fifty relatives of murder victims had urged universal clemency in a letter days before the announcement. Jess Nichol, whose sister Polly Klaas was murdered in 1993, backed the move, saying her recovery had "absolutely nothing" to do with whether the killer was executed. Her father figure in the case, Marc Klaas, took the opposite view. "Baby killers, cop killers, spree killers and serial killers have all found an ally in Gavin Newsom," he said. Prosecutors and police groups have also criticised the decision.

The commutations do not abolish the death penalty. California's statute was set by ballot measure, so only voters can repeal it, and they declined to do so in 2012 and 2016. Prosecutors can keep seeking death sentences in new cases, and counties such as Riverside and San Bernardino are likely to go on doing so. A future governor could in principle try to resume executions, though with the row emptied there would be no one to execute without fresh sentences.

What to watch

The first test is the state Supreme Court. The justices must rule on the multiple-conviction cases, and the pace and the grounds of their decisions will decide how soon the commutations are complete. The second is political: Newsom leaves office in January, and the question is whether his successor keeps the moratorium in place. The third is nationwide. Governors such as Ohio's Mike DeWine and Kansas's Laura Kelly face calls to follow California's example, while the Republican governors of Alabama and Oklahoma have recently commuted individual sentences for accomplices who did not carry out the killing. Whether California proves to be an outlier or a model will become clearer in how those governors respond.
