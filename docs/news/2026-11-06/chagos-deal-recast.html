Burnham wants to 'recast' the Chagos deal after US resistance

In one sentence
Andy Burnham says Britain will rework the Chagos treaty after Donald Trump called it 'terrible', leaving the Diego Garcia lease and the handover to Mauritius unresolved.

The Chagos Islands treaty, signed in May 2025 to hand sovereignty of the archipelago to Mauritius, is now being reopened. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said Britain will work with both Mauritius and the United States to find a way forward after Washington refused to back the deal as drafted. The ratification legislation has not completed its passage through Parliament, and the treaty is stalled. It is not formally dead.

The account below rests on reporting from late September. It found no public text of a new agreement, and no confirmation of what 'recasting' would involve in practice.

How the deal got here

The dispute goes back to 1965-66. The UK separated the Chagos Islands from Mauritius before the latter's independence. Diego Garcia, the largest island, later became a joint UK-US military base. In 2019 the International Court of Justice issued an advisory opinion that Britain's continued administration of the islands was unlawful and should end as rapidly as possible.

The May 2025 treaty was London's answer. Sovereignty would pass to Mauritius, and the UK would lease Diego Garcia back for 99 years. The lease carries annual payments reported at a minimum of £120 million. The arrangement was meant to settle the legal question without disturbing the base, which supports US and UK operations across the Indian Ocean.

Washington's objection

The difficulty is that the base is a shared one. Reporting by Maritime Executive notes that changing the 1965 UK-US arrangements under which the base operates requires US assent, so any settlement has to be acceptable to Washington as well as Port Louis.

At his first meeting with Burnham, at the UN General Assembly in New York in September, Donald Trump called the deal 'terrible'. Burnham described it as something he had inherited. He said Britain would 'work hard' to find a resolution and stressed that protecting the base matters to both countries. A government minister then confirmed the deal would be reworked. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting said Trump's remarks gave 'absolute clarity' that his position on the deal as currently drafted was not changing. Streeting also said Britain would not, 'at this stage', make the annual payments to Mauritius.

The government's line has shifted. In July, commentary on Burnham's early statements suggested he was signalling no change, and he told Mauritius's prime minister he remained committed to implementing the treaty. A Foreign Office minister has since declined to call the treaty dead and said the government would keep working with the US and Mauritius.

The Conservatives want it scrapped. Shadow defence secretary James Cartlidge said the deal should be 'binned entirely'.

What recasting could mean

The options are narrow, and each has a cost.

  • Amend the lease terms. This could mean changes to the length, to control over the surrounding waters, or to the payments. Mauritius would have to agree, having signed a deal it regards as settled.
  • Shelve the treaty. This leaves the legal position the ICJ called unlawful in place. It also leaves the UK exposed to the accusation that it ignored an international court. Reports differ on how far the deal is paused or dead, and that ambiguity may be deliberate.
  • Carry on regardless. This would put the UK against its closest security partner over the base the treaty is meant to protect.

There is a fiscal angle too. Withholding payments sits uneasily with a treaty the UK has signed, and money is one of the few levers Mauritius holds.

What to watch

The first marker is whether ministers bring forward a revised text, or say publicly which terms they want changed. The second is Mauritius's response, since it has said the deal secures the base. The third is how the stalled legislation is handled in Parliament. Until one of these moves, the base is as secure as before and the sovereignty question remains open.
