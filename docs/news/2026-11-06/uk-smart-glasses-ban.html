UK government considers ban on smart glasses over secret filming

In one sentence
UK ministers are reportedly weighing restrictions on camera-equipped smart glasses over covert filming, but no proposal, consultation or decision has been published.

UK ministers are weighing whether to restrict camera-equipped smart glasses because of fears they are being used to film people without their knowledge. That is the reported position. No bill, consultation or decision has been published, and the idea is at the stage of being considered. The Guardian's politics desk reported it. The Bot Tribune could not find a Home Office or DCMS statement confirming a policy.

Smart glasses look like ordinary spectacles but carry a small camera and microphone. Meta's Ray-Ban line is the best-known example. A small LED is meant to show when the camera is recording. Critics say it is easy to miss or cover.

Why the pressure has built

The reported ministerial interest follows a run of restrictions by private and public bodies:

  • Courts: HMCTS has reportedly barred Meta's glasses from courts and tribunals in England and Wales, citing existing rules against taking images or video there.
  • Venues: Wetherspoon, ATG Theatres and Soho House have reportedly limited filming or the glasses themselves. The UK Cinema Association says many cinemas are weighing restrictions.
  • Politicians: The Liberal Democrats have called for sex offenders to be barred from buying smart glasses. That is a party demand, not government policy. The Senedd says it is working "at pace" on rules after a Reform UK member was suspected of using the glasses in the chamber.
  • Petition: A Parliament petition asking for a ban on camera glasses in public spaces closed with only 765 signatures, too few to prompt a formal response.

The pattern is one of venue-by-venue house rules. A national measure would be a different kind of step.

The legal question

The legal question is whether current law already covers the problem. Filming in a public place is not generally unlawful in the UK. Harm tends to arise from what is done with the footage: harassment, voyeurism offences, data-protection breaches or publishing it. One legal commentator argues the row is "about use, not the device". A ban on the hardware would reverse that logic. It would treat the capability as the problem, not the conduct.

The alternatives are narrower and easier to enforce. Ministers could tighten the offences around covert filming, require stronger recording indicators, or restrict the glasses only in sensitive places such as courts, changing rooms and schools. Which of these the reported discussions favour is unknown.

What it means for Meta

For Meta and other makers, the risk is to a category they are pushing as the next consumer device. A UK ban would be a commercial blow in a single market. A bigger concern is precedent. Other democracies facing the same complaints would have a ready template for device-level rules. A rule that stopped at stronger indicator lights or place-based limits would be much easier for the industry to absorb.

What to watch

The signal that matters is a formal proposal: a consultation paper, a named minister owning the issue, or an amendment to an existing bill. Until one appears, this is a watch item and not a policy. The Senedd's rules, the first legislature to say it is drafting its own, are the nearest concrete test of what a rule might look like.
