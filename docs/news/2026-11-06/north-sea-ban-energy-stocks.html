North Sea licensing ban hits energy shares, with service firms likely more exposed than producers

In one sentence
A North Sea licensing freeze should hit contractors that depend on new drilling harder than producers with approved fields, but Thursday's share moves are not yet confirmed in detail.

The question raised by this week's sell-off in energy shares is who bears the cost when a government stops issuing new North Sea licences. The obvious answer is the oil companies. The better-supported answer is that the cost falls mostly on the firms that drill, build and maintain for them. That answer rests on how the two kinds of business earn money. It has not yet been checked against a full set of share-price moves for this week.

That gap is the first thing to state plainly. Oil and gas shares fell on Thursday 5 November, according to a single wire report. No index level, percentage move, named company or stated cause has been confirmed. Searches for coverage of that day found nothing more specific. This piece therefore does not claim that service stocks fell further than producers on Thursday. It sets out why they might, and what the record from earlier episodes shows.

What the policy does, and what it leaves alone

The Labour government, in office since July 2024, pledged to end new oil and gas exploration licences in the North Sea. In practice the policy has been less absolute than the manifesto wording. Later coverage describes the government as more flexible on licensing, with development near existing fields still possible. Tiebacks, which connect small new discoveries to infrastructure already in place, have been the main carve-out. The current policy text and any 2026 changes should be confirmed before relying on this summary.

The distinction matters because a licensing freeze acts on the future and not on the present. A producer with approved fields keeps producing from them. Harbour Energy, Serica and EnQuest earn their revenue from barrels already in the ground, sold at prevailing oil and gas prices. A freeze limits their ability to replace those barrels in the long run. It does not switch off this year's cash flow.

The tax regime sits alongside the licensing policy and was, in earlier episodes, the larger driver of share prices. The Energy Profits Levy was set at 38% from 2025, for a headline rate of 78% on North Sea profits. Whether it still applies at that rate in November 2026 should be checked. Pending consent decisions on Rosebank and Jackdaw are the most concrete test of how the policy works, and their status also needs confirming.

Why service firms look more exposed

A drilling or engineering contractor has a different earnings profile. Its order book is a queue of future work: rigs contracted, platforms to be fabricated, wells to be plugged. New licences feed that queue several years ahead. When they stop, the queue shortens, and the contractor cannot fall back on a producing asset.

There is a counterweight. Decommissioning, maintenance and work on existing fields continue regardless of licensing, and some contractors are moving into offshore wind and carbon storage. The exposure is therefore uneven across the sector. Firms weighted to exploration drilling are likely to be hit harder than those weighted to maintenance or decommissioning. This is a hypothesis about earnings, and share prices would have to confirm it.

The evidence from earlier episodes is mixed. When Labour set out its plans before the 2024 election, Reuters reported that Harbour Energy fell 3.8% by 1330 GMT, while Serica Energy and EnQuest each dropped around 8%. Those were producers, and the weakest were the smaller, more UK-concentrated ones. Later reports said the market reaction to the clarified licensing stance was modest, with Harbour, EnQuest and Serica down between 1% and 3%. Taken together, these suggest the market reacts more to tax than to licensing, and that smaller, UK-only producers move most. None of those episodes tested service firms directly. A direct comparison on 5 November would need the day's moves for named producers and contractors, which are not yet available.

Jobs, and the political argument

The human side of the exposure is in the north-east of Scotland. Industry bodies have long estimated that the North Sea oil and gas sector supports around 100,000 jobs across the UK, many of them in and around Aberdeen. Much of that employment sits in the supply chain rather than with the producers.

John Swinney, who became Scotland's First Minister in May 2024, has made the jobs-and-energy-security case against Westminster's restrictions. The row between the Scottish and UK governments is long-running. It turns on who pays for the transition away from North Sea production: workers and contractors in Scotland, or the Treasury through support for new industries.

What to watch

Three things would settle the question. The first is the closing prices for 5 November, which would show whether contractors fell by more than producers. The second is the Rosebank and Jackdaw consent decisions, which would show how strictly the policy is applied to projects already in the system. The third is the next trading updates from the listed service firms, whose order-book figures will show whether work is being deferred. If contractors' backlogs shrink while producers' output holds up, the case that the supply chain bears the cost will be made by the companies' own numbers.
