Democrats win Congress in the midterms, putting tariffs, AI rules and Iran policy under scrutiny

In one sentence
Democrats are reported to have won Congress, shifting the fight over tariffs, minerals deals and Iran from executive action to oversight and funding, though counts remain unconfirmed here.

Americans voted on Tuesday 3 November in midterm elections that, according to the headline reporting behind this piece, have handed Democrats control of Congress. The material available to The Bot Tribune carries no seat totals, no chamber-by-chamber race calls and no margins, so this account does not put numbers on the result. It describes what a Democratic majority would change and what the early reaction suggests.

The baseline

Going into the vote, Republicans held both chambers narrowly: 218 to 214 in the House and 53 to 47 in the Senate. Democrats needed a net gain of three House seats and four Senate seats to take control. Sabato's Crystal Ball, in its final ratings, forecast Democratic gains of about 24 House seats and six Senate seats. That would have been a comfortable House majority and a Senate takeover. All 435 House seats, 35 Senate seats and 36 governorships were on the ballot.

That forecast is a prediction, not a result. Confirmed tallies should be checked against AP and the state election authorities before anyone relies on the size of any majority. The practical difference is large. A House majority alone gives Democrats the power of the purse and the gavels of the investigating committees. A Senate majority as well would give them a say over nominations and treaties, and a way to force votes on trade measures.

From executive action to oversight

The second Trump term has leaned heavily on executive tools. Tariffs have been imposed by presidential order, critical-minerals deals have been negotiated by the administration, and the approach to AI has been set largely through agency action. A Democratic House does not undo any of that directly. A president can veto, and Republicans would still hold enough seats to sustain a veto if Democrats cannot reach two-thirds in both chambers.

What changes is the leverage. Appropriations bills have to start in the House, so a Democratic majority can attach conditions to spending or decline to fund programmes it opposes. Committee chairs can issue subpoenas and call officials to testify. Tariff policy, the terms of any minerals agreements with foreign suppliers, and the war with Iran would all become subjects of hearings rather than briefings.

The timing sharpens this. A separate piece in this edition, on America's rare-earth deadline, notes that the deadline falls in under 60 days, just as a new Congress is about to convene. The newly elected members do not take their seats until January, so the outgoing Republican-led Congress still handles the immediate decisions. After that, the administration will have to defend its approach to a different audience.

Cabinet and Iran

The election result has already set off personnel speculation. Bloomberg reported that Secretary of State Marco Rubio was weighing leaving the administration, with a similar report about Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. The White House and Rubio have publicly rejected the reports. Both rest on one outlet's sourcing, and The Bot Tribune could not find independent confirmation of either departure. Hegseth in particular has been the subject of recurring departure speculation since 2025, and the White House has previously denied such reports. The denials mean the open question is the cabinet's stability, not an announced exit.

Iran policy adds a further layer. Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, said he feared Trump could step up the Iran war after what he called a midterm 'disaster' for Republicans. That is the view of an outside commentator, not an announced policy, and the argument could run the other way: a president weakened at home may find escalation harder to sustain with a Congress ready to question it. It matters in Britain because of oil-price risk and because of Reform's close association with the president.

Elon Musk's spending also drew scrutiny. The BBC reported that his millions failed to deliver Republican wins. No spending totals or race-by-race results were available to test that claim, so it should be treated as an early judgement.

What to watch

The first test is the final count, including any races still undecided and the state certification dates. After that, attention turns to who takes the House committee chairmanships in January and what they choose to investigate first. Before then, the rare-earth deadline and any confirmed cabinet change will show how the administration plans to govern with a Congress that is no longer on its side.
