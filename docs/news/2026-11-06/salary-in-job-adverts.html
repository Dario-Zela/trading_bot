Firms must publish salaries in job adverts ahead of new pay-transparency law

In one sentence
The UK has only proposed requiring pay in job adverts; the consultation closed 27 October, with no bill, threshold, penalty or cost estimate yet published.

The headline overstates where the policy stands. UK employers are not yet legally required to put salaries in job adverts. The government has proposed it, and the consultation closed on 27 October 2026. No bill has been confirmed, so there is no effective date and no penalty regime. What exists is a direction of travel, and a good deal of detail still to be written.

What is actually proposed

The consultation opened on 15 July 2026 as part of a wider reform of equal pay law. It would create a statutory duty to include pay information in adverts. Where a role is not advertised, employers would give candidates the information in writing before interview. It would cover England, Wales and Scotland.

Most of the substance is left open. The government asks whether employers should publish a range, a single salary or a benchmark rate. It also asks how wide a permitted range can be, and whether the duty extends beyond base salary to items such as collective bargaining provisions, annual adjustments and other financial benefits. According to the law firm Lewis Silkin's summary, no headcount threshold or sector exemption is specified, and the summary does not discuss penalties. Much of the detail is expected in later regulations, and the government has signalled a long implementation process.

The stated aims are to reduce pay discrimination, close information gaps for candidates and filter out mismatched pay expectations before interview. The government also says it wants to keep the burden on employers low and does not intend to add new reporting requirements.

The compliance question

The cost case rests on how many employers would have to change behaviour. Estimates of current practice vary with the survey. One figure cited in the Lewis Silkin summary puts the share already publishing pay information in adverts at about 21.6%. CIPD research found that 41% of employers always share salary ranges in external adverts. Either way, a majority of employers do not do so consistently.

No official cost estimate has been published that this desk could find. For large employers with HR systems and formal pay bands, the direct cost is probably small: it is mostly a matter of editing templates. For smaller firms, the harder cost is internal. A published range invites comparison with existing staff, and a firm without defined bands has to create them first. That is a plausible mechanism, not a measured effect.

Does pay pressure follow?

The bargaining-power argument is that candidates who can see the range will anchor on its upper end, and that current staff will notice when new hires are advertised above their own pay. The consultation offers no evidence on wage effects, and none has been established for the UK. Any wage pressure is therefore a hypothesis, not a finding.

The external pull is clearer. The EU's Pay Transparency Directive required member states to transpose it by 7 June 2026, with employers obliged to disclose pay or a pay range before interview. UK firms that recruit in the EU will meet the rule regardless of what Westminster decides, which weakens the case that the UK is acting in isolation.

What to watch

The next data point is the government's response to the consultation, which has not yet appeared. Three details will decide the economic weight: whether a size threshold is set, how wide a range may be, and what enforcement looks like. A narrow range and strict enforcement would raise the cost and the wage-compression effect. A wide range and light enforcement would change little. Until the response is published, the rule is a proposal and not yet a cost.
