BEYOND THE TAPE · AEROSPACE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-11-06 · 3 min read

Engine parts that 'fight the laws of the universe': the materials race in aerospace

In one sentence
Engine makers are pushing materials harder, and the scarce, slow-to-certify supply of superalloys may now matter as much as design, though hard shortage figures remain unconfirmed.

A jet engine is, at heart, a argument with metal. Air is squeezed, fuel is burned, and the resulting gas is aimed at turbine blades and discs that must keep spinning at temperatures hot enough to melt many of the alloys they are made from. The industry's usual answer is a family of nickel-based superalloys, cooling channels, and coatings. The question now being asked in aerospace boardrooms is whether the supply of those materials, rather than the cleverness of the design, is what sets the pace.

The thesis is not new, but it has gained urgency as engine makers such as Rolls-Royce and Curtiss-Wright push components into hotter, more stressed regimes. A caveat first: the specific parts, alloys and tonnages behind that pressure were not independently confirmed in the reporting behind this piece, and no figures are offered here for them. What can be pinned down is the shape of the supply chain.

A narrow pipe

Take Rolls-Royce. Its long-term relationship with Allegheny Technologies (ATI) is a useful window. In 2009 the two signed a ten-year sourcing agreement for nickel-based superalloy products of "disc quality" for commercial engines, with potential revenue put at between $750 million and $1 billion. The deal has since been extended through 2029, covering rotating-disc nickel alloys for the Trent engine family, next-generation engines and spares for those in service.

That is revealing in two ways. First, the contracts run for decades because qualifying a material is slow: a new alloy, or a new melt source for an old one, must be certified before it can sit inside a rotating part where failure is not an option. Second, the number of mills able to produce disc-grade metal at the required purity is small. An engine maker cannot simply shop around when demand jumps.

Where the margin goes

For investors the point is mechanical. A manufacturer that cannot secure enough qualified material caps its own output, however full its order book. Delayed engines mean delayed deliveries, and the cost of expediting scarce metal lands on margins. Suppliers with certified capacity, by contrast, hold something close to pricing power, which is why long-dated agreements of the ATI kind are watched as closely as the engine programmes they serve.

Curtiss-Wright sits at a different point in the chain, supplying engineered components and systems to aerospace, defence and other demanding markets. Its exposure to the same constraint is plausible rather than documented here, and the case for a larger story rests on evidence the available sources did not supply: named shortfalls, delivery slippage, or disclosed cost increases.

What to watch

The next test arrives with company reporting. Look for any mention of material availability in Rolls-Royce's updates on engine deliveries and in the order commentary from specialty-metal suppliers, and for whether contract extensions like ATI's to 2029 become more common. Until a named shortage or a quantified delay appears, the materials bottleneck remains a credible hypothesis about where the industry is heading, not yet a measured fact.
