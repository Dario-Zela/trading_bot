Met chief says policing is 'heading for failure' and urges merging England and Wales's 43 forces

In one sentence
Met commissioner Sir Mark Rowley will say policing is 'heading for failure' and propose replacing 43 forces with eight to 10 regional ones, but police commissioners dispute the merger case.

The most senior police officer in England and Wales has put a long-running argument about the structure of policing on the table. Sir Mark Rowley, the Metropolitan Police commissioner, is due to say in a speech on Friday that policing is "heading for failure" unless the country's 43 territorial forces are consolidated. Accounts of the speech, which were not checked against a full transcript, say he will propose eight to 10 regional forces. They would be supported by hundreds of local policing commands and a new National Police Service.

The case for change

Rowley's argument is that the system was built for another era. The 43-force model dates from the 1960s, and he contends it cannot cope with criminals who operate across borders and online. Reports of the speech cite two measures of decline. Positive outcomes for reported crimes are now at less than half the level of 12 years ago, and public confidence has fallen by 9%. Those figures come from secondary summaries of a BBC London report and should be treated with caution until the speech text is available.

He has been heading this way for some time. In July 2025 he called for replacing the 43 forces with 12 to 15 "mega forces", describing it as the first serious reform of the policing model in over 60 years. The new proposal goes further, with fewer and larger regional forces. It also separates the most specialised work into a national service, an idea that police leaders have been more willing to endorse than outright mergers.

A reform already in motion

The speech lands in a debate the government has opened itself. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood launched a consultation on the structure of policing in January. The Home Office has said the current model has not changed since the 1960s and is no longer fit for purpose. Rowley's intervention therefore adds weight to a process that is already under way. It does not start a new one.

The Met is also not a neutral party. It is already by far the largest force in the country. Some rural police and crime commissioners have called the plans "political manoeuvring" by larger forces to "load the dice in their favour". That charge is hard to separate from the underlying merits, because any merger plan will leave some forces larger and others absorbed.

Where the resistance lies

The sharpest division is between modernisation and merger. The Association of Police and Crime Commissioners has said the case for modernisation and a National Police Service is clear. It has said the case for cutting 43 forces to nine regional "mega-forces" is not. Opponents of consolidation have a recurring argument: that bigger forces become distant from the places they serve. When Rowley first floated mega forces, Staffordshire's police and crime commissioner, Ben Adams, warned of "the end of Staffordshire Police". He said a force approaching the Met in scale would be inefficient and unresponsive. Critics also point to Police Scotland, which was created by merging eight forces in 2013, as a cautionary example.

The stakes are practical as well as constitutional. Mergers would change who sets budgets, who answers to local electors and who runs policing in each area. They would also reopen a question that previous governments dropped. A push to merge forces in 2006 was abandoned after resistance from police authorities and local politicians.

What to watch

The next marker is the government's response to its consultation, for which this reporting found no confirmed date. The test will be whether ministers back the national service and regional structure, or settle for the narrower modernisation the police and crime commissioners say they can support. Until then, the commissioner's warning keeps the choice in front of Parliament and the public.
