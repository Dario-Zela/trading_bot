UEFA votes to boycott FIFA competitions over Infantino's $20bn World Cup stake sale

FIFA president Gianni Infantino, whose plan to sell a stake in the World Cup's commercial rights has triggered a boycott vote by UEFA. — ©UNIS Vienna / Nikoleta Haffar, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
UEFA's 55 members voted to boycott FIFA competitions, and Concacaf rejected it too, over Infantino's plan to sell a $20bn stake in a new FIFA commercial arm to Kushner-linked investors.

European football has never been shy about disliking Gianni Infantino. This week it decided to stop competing for him.

At an emergency meeting on Thursday, all 55 of UEFA's member associations voted to boycott FIFA competitions — including the World Cup — if the FIFA president pushes ahead with a plan to sell a 20% stake in a new commercial subsidiary to private investors. Concacaf's 41 members, meeting separately, unanimously rejected the same proposal, though the North and Central American body stopped short of UEFA's boycott language, framing its objection instead around "the lack of due process" and an "artificially short deadline." Between them, the two confederations account for 96 of FIFA's 211 voting members — ten short of a majority, but enough, if the numbers hold, to make the 2026 World Cup's two biggest footballing regions absent from it.

The pitch and the backlash

The vehicle at the centre of the row is FIFA Forward Enterprise, a proposed entity that would house FIFA's commercial and event operations at a valuation of roughly $20 billion. FIFA wants to sell a 20% stake to outside investors, raising up to $4.2 billion, with JPMorgan running the fundraising. The lead investor is expected to be Thrive, the venture firm founded by Joshua Kushner, brother of Jared Kushner. To sweeten the deal, Infantino has offered each of FIFA's 211 member associations an immediate $20 million payment if they sign on by September 19.

UEFA was unmoved. "The World Cup cannot be treated as an investment product," the confederation said. "No part of it should ever be surrendered to private investors. The World Cup is not for sale." It called the manner in which the plan surfaced — with no consultation of member federations — "irresponsible and indefensible." Former FIFA president Sepp Blatter, rarely one to pass up a chance to needle his successor, added that "football belongs to the people." In London, UK culture minister Lisa Nandy echoed the sentiment: "Football belongs to the fans, not billionaire investors."

FIFA's response, issued the same night, was to hold the line rather than blink: the governing body said it "acknowledges and respects feedback and concern aired in public" while blaming "erroneous" media reporting for the scale of the backlash. Infantino has insisted the sale is a proposal, "not an obligation," for member associations.

Leverage, misjudged

What makes this more than a governance squabble is the arithmetic. FIFA's commercial model depends on the World Cup being played by the game's strongest confederations; strip out UEFA and Concacaf and the tournament's broadcast and sponsorship value collapses along with it. That is precisely the leverage private equity is meant to prize — predictable, monetisable cash flows — and it is precisely what a boycott threatens to destroy. Infantino, who faces re-election in March 2027, appears to have underestimated how threatening a $20 billion outside stake would look to confederations that have spent decades treating FIFA's commercial rights as their own leverage, not someone else's asset to sell.

The first test comes September 5, when FIFA's Under-20 Women's World Cup kicks off in Poland — the earliest event UEFA's boycott could touch. The bigger one lands September 19, Infantino's deadline for member associations to accept the $20 million offer. Whether enough of the other 115 federations take the money, or wait to see if Europe and North America really sit out a World Cup, will decide whether FIFA Forward Enterprise survives as a going concern.
