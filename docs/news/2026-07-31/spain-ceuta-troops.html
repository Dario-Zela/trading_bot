Spain deploys troops to Ceuta after thousands cross border from Morocco

The double border fence separating the Spanish enclave of Ceuta from Morocco. — Youtryandyoutry / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Spain sent troops to Ceuta after 2,000–3,000 migrants breached fences and swam from Morocco, killing at least nine — the worst surge since 2021.

The border crossing at Tarajal, the strip of beach and breakwater separating Morocco from the Spanish city of Ceuta, gave way on Thursday. State broadcaster TVE put the number who got through at 2,000 to 3,000 — some scaling the six-metre double fence that has ringed the enclave for decades, many more wading and swimming around its seaward end on inflatable inner tubes and improvised flotation devices. At least nine people died in the water. Ceuta's Civil Guard, a few hundred officers on a good day, could not hold the line.

"The situation is absolute chaos — there are thousands of migrants crossing," said Rachid Sbihi, who heads a Civil Guard officers' association in the enclave. On the Moroccan side, Achraf Maimouni of the Moroccan Association for Human Rights said the Tarajal crossing had been "opened under unusual circumstances this morning," a description Rabat did not contest and did not explain.

A decade-old fence, breached again

Ceuta and its sister enclave Melilla are the European Union's only land borders with Africa, an accident of sixteenth-century Spanish conquest that leaves roughly 85,000 residents living behind razor wire eight kilometres from Algeciras and 12,000 kilometres of Moroccan coastline on the other side. Pressure on that border builds and releases in bursts, and this was the sharpest since May 2021, when more than 8,000 people crossed in 48 hours after Rabat was widely understood to have loosened its own patrols in anger at Madrid's decision to let a Polisario Front leader receive hospital treatment on Spanish soil.

No comparable trigger has been offered this time. Ceuta's government said 1,500 to 2,000 people had already entered over the preceding ten days before Thursday's surge tipped the count into the thousands. Juan Jesús Vivas, the enclave's regional president, declared an "absolute humanitarian and social emergency" and pressed Madrid to go further still — a formal national emergency declaration alongside the troop deployment.

Troops in, ministers inbound

Spain's government answered Thursday by ordering the armed forces garrisoned in Ceuta to reinforce the Civil Guard, a step short of full militarisation of the border but a visible escalation. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez and Interior Minister Fernando Grande-Marlaska were due in the city on Friday. Morocco's Interior Ministry, for its part, said it was "closely cooperating" with Spain and that its own police were intercepting "numerous people" attempting the crossing — a claim Madrid has not disputed but that sits awkwardly against footage of an apparently unguarded Tarajal gate.

The two governments have separately agreed to work toward returning everyone who entered Thursday, though a recent Spanish Supreme Court ruling complicates summary removals by requiring due process for migrants who arrive by sea — a legal wrinkle unlikely to have caused the surge but one that will shape how quickly any of Thursday's arrivals actually leave.

What happens next depends less on fences than on diplomacy neither side is yet discussing in public. The 2021 crisis took months of quiet negotiation to defuse; this one has, so far, no acknowledged cause and no announced resolution — only ministers flying in and a death toll still being counted.
