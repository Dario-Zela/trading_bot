BOE RATES · By M. Halloway · 2026-07-31 · 5 min read

Bank of England holds rate at 3.75% as three MPC members push for hike

The Bank of England's headquarters on Threadneedle Street, London. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
The BoE held rates at 3.75% in a 6-3 vote, but three MPC members — Greene, Mann and Pill — dissented for an immediate hike over energy-driven inflation risk.

The Bank of England left its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 3.75% on Thursday, extending a hold that has now run for five consecutive decisions this year. On the surface, it is the least eventful outcome the Monetary Policy Committee could have delivered. Underneath, the vote was anything but settled: three of the nine rate-setters wanted to raise borrowing costs immediately, and the minority pushing for a hike has grown at every meeting since April.

The committee split 6-3, with Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill voting for a 25 basis-point increase to 4%. All three cited the same worry — that energy prices, aggravated by the wider Middle East conflict, are about to do to UK inflation what they did in 2022, and that the Bank should not wait to find out how far it spreads.

A dissenting bloc that keeps growing

The arithmetic tells its own story. In April, Pill was the lone dissenter arguing for higher rates. In June, Greene joined him, making it 7-2. On Thursday, Mann crossed over too, making it 6-3 — a swing of three votes toward tightening in three meetings, even as the Bank's official policy stance has not moved an inch.

Pill, the Bank's chief economist, has been the most vocal of the three. Speaking on a BBC Walescast podcast earlier this month, he said flatly that rates would need to rise, and has now voted that way twice running. He was careful, in remarks to the Press Association after the decision, to frame his position as a matter of analysis rather than provocation: "I would emphasise that I'm not dissenting because I want to get my name in the papers, or I want to be a troublemaker on the committee." He added that over his time on the MPC he has "probably, if anything, erred on the side of supporting the institutional view," which makes two consecutive dissents notable in itself. "It's not an easy choice for me to dissent at a personal level," he said.

Greene, reappointed by Chancellor Rachel Reeves this year to a second three-year term running to 2029, has argued her case in risk-management terms: with CPI inflation having run above the Bank's 2% target for more than five years, she says the burden of proof should sit with holding rates, not raising them. Mann's move was the most consequential, since she had held the line in June, telling colleagues she was watching business pricing behaviour and 2027 wage settlements as the trigger for reconsidering her vote. That trigger appears to have arrived sooner than she expected.

Energy prices and an unfinished war

The backdrop to all three dissents is the same: a jump in the perceived risk to energy prices since the collapse of the US-Iran memorandum of understanding and the widening of the conflict in the Middle East. UK headline CPI inflation has actually fallen to 2.6% since the Committee's last meeting, and the Bank's minutes describe "tentative evidence that inherited inflation persistence may be weaker than had been presumed" — in plain terms, the underlying disinflation trend that was in place before the conflict has not been derailed.

But the Bank's central forecast still has inflation climbing to around 3.2% by the fourth quarter of this year, driven overwhelmingly by energy. Higher wholesale gas and oil costs feed through directly to household bills and indirectly as firms pass costs down supply chains. The minutes flag the risk as skewed to the upside because of "the possibility of repeated resumptions of conflict," combined with European gas storage running below normal seasonal levels and a fall in global refining output — a narrower, more fragile supply picture than markets had priced going into the summer.

What kept the majority on hold, according to the minutes, is that financial conditions have already tightened significantly since the conflict began — gilt yields and market rate expectations have done some of the work a rate hike would otherwise do. The view among the six is that this tightening, plus the current level of Bank Rate, is "providing sufficient insurance against the upside risks to inflation stemming from fluctuations in energy prices," buying time to see whether the shock proves temporary.

Bailey plays down the hawks

Governor Andrew Bailey used his press conference to lean hard against the reading that a hike is imminent. "Do not leave this room thinking that the BoE is edging towards a hike," he told reporters, adding that the Committee was "not talking about an insurance hike." He also said there was no evidence yet of the kind of second-round effects — inflation expectations feeding into wage and price-setting — that would justify pre-emptive tightening, while cautioning that "disinflation is proceeding but at a slow pace."

Markets took the cue. Two-year gilt yields fell 11 basis points to 4.34%, their steepest drop since mid-June, as traders trimmed bets on a September hike. Sterling slipped modestly against the dollar. The market reaction suggests investors read the 6-3 split as noise around a genuinely settled policy stance, rather than a leading indicator — for now, Bailey's press conference did more to move prices than the dissenters' votes did.

What the split actually signals

The substance of Thursday's decision is a hold. The subtext is that the Committee's comfort with that hold is thinning. A dissenting bloc that has widened from one to two to three members in successive meetings is not, on its own, proof that a hike is coming in September. But it does raise the bar for what would need to go right — a swift de-escalation in the Middle East, energy prices retreating, and no sign of pass-through into wages — for the doves to keep control of the vote.

The next test comes at the 17 September meeting, when the Committee will have two more months of energy-price and wage data, and a clearer read on whether the geopolitical shock is fading or compounding. If Mann, Greene and Pill are joined by a fourth vote before then, Bailey's insistence that the Bank is not edging toward a hike will get harder to sustain.
