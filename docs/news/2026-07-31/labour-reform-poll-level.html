Labour draws level with Reform UK in polling for first time in over a year

Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street with his wife Marie-France van Heel, 20 July 2026. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Labour matched Reform UK at 24% each in Survation polling for the first time in 16 months as Burnham replaced Starmer — but his fiscal plans remain undefined.

For the first time in more than a year, Britain's two leading parties are level in the polls — and the timing says as much as the numbers do.

Survation's Westminster voting-intention survey, fielded 10–14 July among 2,057 adults, put Labour and Reform UK at 24% each. It was Labour's largest single jump in the series since the 2024 general election — up five points in under a month — while Reform slid three points to its lowest share since the aftermath of last year's local elections. Reform had led every Survation poll for the previous sixteen months.

The fieldwork closed six days before Keir Starmer formally left Downing Street. Survation's own write-up was blunt about why: "The public appears ready to move on from Keir Starmer." He departed with a net favourability of −33 — 22% liked him, 55% did not.

A Manchester mayor's fast return

Starmer announced on 22 June that he would resign as Labour leader and prime minister, after local election losses in May left him without the confidence of his MPs. Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester mayor with no seat in the Commons, got one fast: a sitting Labour MP resigned the Makerfield constituency so Burnham could win it in a by-election on 18 June, days before Starmer's announcement became public. Burnham was confirmed Labour leader on 17 July with the backing of 379 of 401 Labour MPs, and became prime minister three days later — Britain's seventh in a decade.

The bounce has kept building since. A second Survation poll, taken after Burnham's first week in office, put Labour on 26% against Reform's 24% — the party's first lead in any Survation poll since 3 May 2025, according to chief executive Damian Lyons Lowe. More In Common found an even wider gap, 28% to 24%; YouGov had the parties tied at 22%.

What the polls don't price in

The rally has outrun the policy detail behind it. Burnham has said he will keep the previous government's fiscal rules but use whatever "flexibility" they allow, floated raising the income-tax-free threshold, and named tackling social care and housebuilding as priorities — while installing John Healey as chancellor over the more fiscally orthodox Shabana Mahmood many had expected. Bond markets reacted before voters did: gilt yields jumped within hours of Burnham's fiscal-flexibility remarks, with the 10-year yield rising 8 basis points to 5.04% — the highest in the G7 — and the 30-year adding 9 basis points to 5.75%. "This headline about him being willing to use any flexibility within the fiscal rules" was enough to move the market, Mizuho's Evelyne Gomez-Liechti said at the time.

That gap — a governing party gaining ground on personality and relief at Starmer's exit, without a costed fiscal platform to match — is the real story for anyone pricing UK risk. Gilt yields have stayed elevated since Burnham took office, and the next test is less a poll than an event: Healey's first budget statement, which will show whether "flexibility within the rules" means a specific, quantifiable loosening or just rhetoric that markets have already discounted.
