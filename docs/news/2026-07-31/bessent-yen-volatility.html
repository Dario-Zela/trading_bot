Bessent flags yen volatility amid suspected Japanese intervention

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose Fox Business remarks on yen volatility drew notice from currency desks. — US Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bessent called excessive yen volatility 'unhealthy' and said the yen is 'very undervalued,' as USD/JPY plunged 480 pips on suspected Japanese intervention.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Thursday that excessive volatility in the yen "isn't healthy," a comment that landed just as traders were already convinced Japan had quietly stepped into the currency market to defend its currency.

In a Fox Business interview, Bessent went further than the usual boilerplate that Treasury secretaries reach for when a G7 ally's currency is under strain. He said the yen looked "very undervalued" and had "substantially overshot its equilibrium price," adding that he was untroubled by the prospect of the yen strengthening against the dollar. He also said Japan may have intervened to support the currency — an unusually direct acknowledgment from Washington of another government's FX operations, and one that reads, to currency desks, as tacit endorsement rather than complaint.

A dollar drop with fingerprints on it

The backdrop is a currency pair that has been thrown around hard this week. USD/JPY plunged roughly 480 pips on Wednesday, breaking below the psychologically loaded 160 level and trading as low as the 158 handle during the New York session — the dollar's sharpest single-day fall against a basket of currencies since 2022. The move came a day after the Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 3.50%–3.75% with three officials dissenting in favor of a 25-basis-point hike, and after US second-quarter GDP came in at 1.5% annualised growth, short of the 2.1% forecast. Traders read the combination — a Fed under internal pressure and a weaker-than-expected US economy — as the moment Japan's Ministry of Finance chose to strike, catching short-yen positioning off guard.

No Japanese official has confirmed the operation outright. Atsushi Mimura, the vice finance minister for international affairs who oversees currency policy, acknowledged "voices of concern" about the weak yen without confirming specifics — the standard hedge Tokyo uses when it wants markets to assume intervention without owning the number. The Bank of Japan's policy meeting concluded the same day; the bank held rates steady, as expected, though board members Naoki Tamura and Hajime Takata were watched for dissents favoring a hike, and investors parsed Governor Kazuo Ueda's language for any signal on timing.

Why Treasury is talking at all

Washington rarely narrates another government's FX management this openly. The convention, especially among G7 partners, is silence or the flattest possible language about "market-determined" exchange rates. Bessent's remarks break that pattern in a way that matters for two reasons. First, they suggest the US has no objection to Tokyo defending the yen with actual intervention, rather than jawboning alone — support that Japan's Ministry of Finance has needed, having already deployed a record ¥11.73 trillion (roughly $73 billion) in yen-buying operations back in April and May, only to watch the currency slide past 160 again by June. Second, the comments arrive with the yen carry trade — where investors borrow cheap yen to fund positions in higher-yielding dollar assets — still a live source of risk. A sharp, intervention-driven yen rally is exactly the kind of move that forces rapid unwinds and spills into equity and bond volatility beyond Japan.

The next data points to watch are Ueda's post-meeting remarks for any shift in the BOJ's hiking timeline, and whether Tokyo's Ministry of Finance confirms Wednesday's operation in its monthly intervention data, due in late August. Until then, desks are pricing in the risk that Washington's rhetorical cover gives Japan room to intervene again.
