Burnham floats 'NHS principle' collective-contribution tax to fund social care overhaul

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government is weighing a new collective-contribution levy to fund social care reform. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham floats an 'NHS principle' levy to fund social care and fast-tracks the Casey review, while gilt yields already reflect the fiscal uncertainty.

Eleven days into the job, Britain's new prime minister has picked his first serious fiscal fight — and it is not with the opposition. It is with the actuarial reality of an ageing population.

Andy Burnham, acclaimed Labour leader on 17 July after Sir Keir Starmer's resignation and sworn in as prime minister three days later, used a visit to a care home this week to say that "difficult decisions" lie ahead on funding adult social care, and that a new form of collective contribution — modelled loosely on "the NHS principle" — is on the table. He stopped short of naming a figure. He did not stop short of naming the problem.

"I will stick to the manifesto. That is important. There are commitments there on tax. We will be honest with people about those, we will put them before the country at the right time."

What 'the NHS principle' actually means

Burnham has been circling this idea for close to a decade, from his time as Greater Manchester mayor, when he floated a care insurance model in which — in his words — "if everybody contributed a little, it could mean everybody could protect the majority of what they'd worked for." The pitch now is the same instinct applied at national scale: care funded through pooled, universal contribution rather than individuals selling homes to pay bills, echoing the principle that founded the NHS in 1948 — free at the point of use, paid for collectively.

Officials have reportedly modelled several mechanisms rather than settling on one. They include a 1.8 percent levy on earnings above £6,240 for workers over 34, a wealth-linked contribution from better-off pensioners, and — as an alternative rather than an addition — a roughly 10 percent charge on estates at death, the so-called "death tax" option Burnham appears to be steering away from. Separately, reporting around Burnham's early fiscal positioning has pointed to a wider package worth close to £24 billion, including around £18 billion to move social care toward NHS-style free provision, £4 billion to raise the income tax-free allowance, and £2 billion tied to keeping Thames Water running under administration. None of this is confirmed policy. All of it is under active discussion inside government, which is itself the story: three weeks ago, none of it was.

Casey's review, fast-tracked

The institutional vehicle for all this is the Independent Commission on Adult Social Care, chaired by Baroness Louise Casey since April 2025. Its full findings were originally due in 2028. Burnham has ordered the timeline pulled forward to 2027, alongside the launch of a nine-month public consultation exercise — the "Big Conversation on Care" — an online platform meant to gather views before any legislation is drafted.

Casey has been blunt about what the commission keeps running into: an eligibility system that is inconsistent to the point of arbitrary, where some people qualify for meaningful state support while others with "very significant disabilities" get comparatively little. That is the unglamorous heart of the social care problem — not simply that funding is too low, but that entitlement itself is drawn in the wrong place. A tax mechanism only solves half of that.

A consensus Burnham needs, and may not get

Burnham has invited Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch and Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey into the process, pointedly excluding Reform UK's Nigel Farage. Davey joined by video call from France; Badenoch skipped the meeting in person and sent shadow health secretary Stuart Andrew instead, after writing to challenge Burnham to rule out tax rises or extra borrowing outright. Andrew's line afterward captured the gap precisely: his party welcomed cross-party coordination in principle but wanted the funding detail first, adding that Burnham "needs to produce the detail, and pledge not to raise taxes or increase borrowing to pay for change."

That is the structural tension in play. Burnham cannot fund a national social care settlement without either taxing more, borrowing more, or both — and the two other parties he needs at the table have each pre-committed to opposing whichever one he picks. Cross-party talks can build legitimacy for a policy; they cannot manufacture agreement on arithmetic that has no third option.

Markets are already pricing the risk

Gilts have not waited for the White Paper. The 10-year gilt yield closed up eight basis points at 5.049 percent this week, and the 30-year yield pushed to around 5.75 percent, its highest in two months, as Burnham signalled he would use "any flexibility" available within the government's fiscal rules. Sterling slipped back below $1.35 against the dollar over the same period. None of this reflects the social care levy specifically — no levy has been announced — but it reflects how bond investors are treating a new prime minister who talks about spending flexibility before he talks about spending discipline. A chancellor change, from Rachel Reeves to John Healey, has compounded the uncertainty rather than settled it.

What comes next

Burnham has signalled that any formal funding decision on social care is likely to wait for the next Budget, giving the Casey commission room to report — now expected in 2027 rather than 2028 — before legislation is drafted. Between now and then, three things are worth watching: whether the Big Conversation on Care produces anything more than sentiment; whether Badenoch's conditional cooperation survives contact with an actual tax proposal; and whether gilt markets keep charging a premium for a prime minister who has been explicit about wanting fiscal flexibility but has not yet been explicit about what he will do with it.
