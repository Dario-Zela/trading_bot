Tim Cook says Apple's Mac sales 'particularly strong' in Greater China

Apple CEO Tim Cook, who flagged Mac's record Greater China quarter on his final earnings call before handing the role to John Ternus. — Wikimedia Commons / Templar52 (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Tim Cook called Mac sales 'particularly strong' in Greater China, but the region's overall revenue missed forecasts — a narrow win, not a turnaround.

Tim Cook did not have many superlatives left to hand out on his final earnings call as Apple's chief executive. Nearly every headline number — iPhone, Mac, Services, the top line itself — had already been described as a record by the time he got to the one that mattered most for investors watching Beijing: Greater China.

"We had an all-time revenue record" for Mac in the region during the June quarter, Cook told analysts on Apple's fiscal third-quarter call. It was, by his account, the best quarter for the Mac in the company's history in the market — a standout inside a broader Greater China performance that landed short of what Wall Street had modeled.

In one sentence: Tim Cook called Mac sales 'particularly strong' in Greater China last quarter, but the region's overall revenue missed forecasts — a reminder that one hot product line isn't the same as a China turnaround.

A bright line inside a soft region

Greater China revenue came in at $18.82 billion for the quarter, up 22.4% from a year earlier but below the roughly $19.6 billion analysts had penciled in. That gap — a beat on growth, a miss on magnitude — is the tension running through Apple's China story right now. iPhone also notched a June-quarter record in the region, and Cook cited Worldpanel survey data showing the iPhone was the top-selling model in urban China during the quarter. But the region-wide number still fell short, and Apple's shares dropped roughly 4% in after-hours trading following the report, with services and China cited by traders as the two soft spots weighing against an otherwise clean beat.

That pattern — a specific product category outperforming while the regional total disappoints — is what makes "particularly strong" a more precise description than "recovering." Apple's Mac line grew 29% globally in the quarter, powered by the entry-level MacBook Neo and a higher-priced MacBook Pro refresh, numbers Cook himself called "extraordinary" on the call. In China specifically, Counterpoint Research data from earlier this year had already shown the MacBook Air as the country's top-selling laptop model and the Mac mini leading desktop sales, suggesting the strength predates this quarter rather than arriving as a sudden pivot.

Why the distinction matters

Investors have spent the past two years parsing every Apple comment on China for a signal of stabilization, given the market's size — Apple's largest revenue base outside the US — and its role as the company's principal manufacturing base. A Mac-specific record is a genuinely good data point. It is not, on its own, evidence that Apple has resolved the broader competitive and macro pressures that have weighed on iPhone share and services monetization in the country against domestic rivals like Huawei and Xiaomi.

The rest of the quarter's China-adjacent numbers underline the point. Services revenue, which leans heavily on App Store activity across all regions including China, grew 12.1% but came in near $500 million below expectations. iPad sales fell 6% year-over-year, a decline Apple attributed to a tough comparison against a low-cost iPad launch a year earlier rather than to China specifically — but it adds to a picture of unevenness across the product lineup that a single Mac record doesn't erase.

What to watch next

Cook's comments came on his last call before handing the CEO role to hardware chief John Ternus on September 1, meaning the next earnings report — Apple's fiscal fourth quarter, expected in late October — will be delivered by new leadership and will show whether the Mac's China strength was a quarter-specific pop tied to product launches or the start of a steadier trend. Analysts will also be watching whether memory-chip cost pressures, which Cook flagged as a margin risk heading into the holiday quarter, complicate the pricing strategy that has underpinned Mac's recent growth.
