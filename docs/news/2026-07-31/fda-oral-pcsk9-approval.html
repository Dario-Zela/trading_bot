FDA approves first oral PCSK9 inhibitor for high cholesterol

In one sentence
FDA approved Merck's Lipfendra, the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor, but outcomes data are pending and investors remain fixed on GLP-1 drugs.

The Food and Drug Administration on July 16th approved Lipfendra, a once-daily pill from Merck that lowers LDL cholesterol by blocking a protein called PCSK9 — the first time that mechanism has come in tablet form rather than a needle. For a drug class that has existed since 2015, this is the moment its central complaint gets addressed: nobody likes injections, and a meaningful share of patients who are prescribed injectable PCSK9 inhibitors stop taking them.

The case for Lipfendra rests on numbers that hold up. Across two Phase 3 trials — CORALreef Lipids and CORALreef HeFH, enrolling 3,207 adults with hypercholesterolemia, including those with heterozygous familial hypercholesterolemia, on top of maximally tolerated statins — the drug cut LDL cholesterol by 56 to 59 percent relative to placebo at 24 weeks, essentially matching the injectables it's meant to unseat. Side effects were mostly mild: diarrhea in 7 percent of patients versus 2 percent on placebo, dizziness in 9 percent versus 4 percent, in the HeFH trial. Merck has set the price at $315 a month, undercutting the $500-to-$600 monthly list price of Amgen's Repatha and Sanofi/Regeneron's Praluent, which between them still account for roughly 70 percent of PCSK9 sales worldwide.

A caveat cardiologists keep repeating

The approval is for LDL-lowering, not for a proven reduction in heart attacks or strokes — that evidence is still being gathered in CORALreef Outcomes, a trial enrolling more than 14,500 participants whose results are not yet in. "This adds another tool to the LDL-lowering arsenal but does not replace existing options," Dr. Jeffrey Berger of NYU Langone said of the approval, a formulation echoed by other cardiologists who note that LDL reduction is a surrogate marker, not the outcome patients actually care about. Writing in STAT, cardiologist Vishal Khetpal was blunter, questioning whether enlicitide — Lipfendra's generic name — is the practice-changing drug its launch materials suggest, given how few patients who need more aggressive LDL control are even on maximal statin therapy in the first place.

A crowded pipeline, a distracted market

Merck also isn't alone chasing this niche: AstraZeneca has its own oral PCSK9 candidate, AZD0780, still in development, leaving Merck with a first-mover window rather than a monopoly. Wall Street's response was positive but restrained — Merck shares rose about 3 percent on approval day, and Guggenheim reiterated a Buy rating with analysts penciling in peak annual sales above $5 billion. That is a solid outcome for a single drug, but it barely registers against the GLP-1 category, where Eli Lilly alone now carries a market capitalization north of $1 trillion and where obesity drugmakers are racing to add their own cardiovascular claims. PCSK9 inhibition remains a second-line therapy in a global market worth roughly $1.4 billion a year; the investment story of 2026 has largely moved on to a different injection altogether.

The next checkpoint is data, not sales figures: results from CORALreef Outcomes will determine whether Lipfendra earns a cardiovascular-risk-reduction label to match its injectable rivals, or remains a convenience upgrade rather than a clinical one.
