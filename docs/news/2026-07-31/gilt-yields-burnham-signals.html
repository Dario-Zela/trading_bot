Gilt yields hold near two-month highs as markets digest Burnham's fiscal signals

Andy Burnham, whose first-day remarks on fiscal flexibility triggered a gilt selloff. — Official portrait of Andy Burnham MP, Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
30-year gilt yields near a two-month high of 5.75% as markets read PM Burnham's fiscal 'flexibility' as spending risk, not reassurance.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for barely a fortnight, and the bond market has already delivered its verdict on his opening remarks: not convinced.

30-year gilt yields sit at 5.72–5.75%, close to their highest level since late May, after Burnham signalled on his first full day in office that he intended to seek "flexibility" within the UK's fiscal rules. The 10-year yield, at 5.04%, is the highest among G7 economies and has now spent longer above 5% than at any point since July 2008. Sterling slipped as well, down around 0.2% against the dollar — a modest move, but one traders read as part of a broader "sell Britain" reflex.

What makes the reaction notable is that Burnham didn't actually announce new borrowing or a rule change. Asked about the public finances on entering Downing Street, he told reporters: "I've already said we'll stick to the fiscal rules, and by that I mean the existing fiscal rules, and use obviously any flexibility within them." That is, on its face, a statement of continuity. Markets treated it as the opposite.

Rhetoric versus receipts

"The market has shown some emerging signs of nervousness about the mood music four hours into the new regime," said Neil Wilson of Saxo Markets, pointing to what he called "big spending vibes" radiating from the new administration's leftward tilt. That is the crux of the gap between the political signal and the market price: Burnham's language was calibrated to sound cautious, but investors heard a chancellor being handed room to spend more, not less. Rachel Reeves's exit and the arrival of John Healey at the Treasury only reinforced the sense that the fiscal anchor had shifted, even before any budget detail existed to price.

Gilts are also having to price a second, unrelated shock: oil hovering near $100 a barrel, which complicates the inflation outlook the Bank of England has to weigh alongside any fiscal loosening. Traders are effectively running two models at once — one for spending risk, one for imported inflation — and both currently point the same way, toward higher long-end yields.

The IMF's line, echoed by several desks this week, is that policy credibility comes from predictability, not reassurance. Burnham's team has offered plenty of the latter and none of the former: no revised deficit target, no spending review timeline, no detail on how "flexibility" would be defined or bounded. Until one of those things exists, the 30-year yield looks less like a verdict on Burnham himself than a placeholder for the uncertainty he has yet to resolve.

The next test is less a date than a document — whichever fiscal statement or spending review first attaches numbers to the flexibility Burnham has promised. Until then, expect yields to drift on headlines rather than data, with the BoE's own rate path, still contested internally after this week's split vote, adding a second variable to the same trade.
