Burnham unveils plan to free mayors from Treasury 'death grip' with new tax powers

Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street after becoming prime minister, 20 July 2026. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham pledged mayors a share of income tax and business rates to break Treasury control, but the money mostly replaces existing grants and detail waits for the autumn Budget.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for eleven days. On Thursday he used them to make his first big domestic promise: regional mayors in England will get to keep a share of the income tax raised in their own patch, ending what one of his fellow mayors called the "death grip of the Treasury" over local finance.

From 2028, mayoral authorities will retain a portion of income tax generated in their region. Business rates devolution comes sooner — by April 2027 — building on a pilot already running in Greater Manchester and the West Midlands, the only two combined authorities currently allowed to keep all locally generated business rate income. In Greater Manchester that amounted to £100m in 2024-25, of which £25m was retained by the mayoral authority itself. Downing Street has floated illustrative figures for the wider scheme: London could receive roughly £2.3bn a year, Hull and East Yorkshire around £135m, based on a hypothetical 2.5% share of the basic rate.

"Under our plans, more of the taxes raised in a community will stay in that community," Burnham said, framing it as a direct extension of the "Manchesterism" agenda he built as Greater Manchester's mayor before Keir Starmer's resignation swept him into No 10 on 20 July. "I know what it's like to be ignored by politicians in Westminster. I'm not going to make that same mistake now I'm PM."

Oliver Coppard, the Labour mayor of South Yorkshire, welcomed the shift: "It's really important because it gets us out of the death grip of the Treasury and gives us that long-term certainty around income." Tees Valley's Conservative mayor, Ben Houchen, floated using his authority's share to fund direct tax rebates for residents — though officials note mayors currently lack the individual taxpayer data to make that technically straightforward.

Replacement, not addition

The caveat sits at the centre of the policy. The retained tax revenue will replace existing Treasury grants rather than sit on top of them — meaning the immediate fiscal effect for most mayors is a change in the source of funding, not its scale. Full detail on the split between mayors and Whitehall, and on new borrowing powers mayors are being promised for infrastructure spending, won't arrive until Chancellor John Healey's autumn Budget.

That gap matters. The Treasury has resisted devolving revenue-raising power for a decade, wary in part of a repeat of the council borrowing failures that left several local authorities nursing bad bets on commercial property. Analysts at the Institute for Fiscal Studies and the House of Commons Library have both noted that genuine fiscal devolution — letting mayors vary tax rates, not just retain a fixed share of receipts already levied at a national rate — is a bigger, more contentious step than anything confirmed so far. England's income tax devolution, unlike Scotland's or Wales's, still stops short of giving mayors any lever over the rate itself.

There is also a geography problem: roughly a quarter of England's population lives outside a mayoral combined authority — including Dorset, Somerset, Oxfordshire, Gloucestershire and Wiltshire — raising the prospect of a two-tier settlement between devolved city-regions and everywhere else.

Gilt markets have already registered the announcement as a signal of Burnham's fiscal instincts, with yields holding near two-month highs as investors weigh what his premiership means for borrowing more broadly. The real test of this policy lands in the autumn Budget, when Healey has to put numbers — and a revenue split — against Burnham's rhetoric.
