Samsung and SK Hynix shares plunge in historic chip-stock rout as Kospi sinks 10.8%

Samsung Electronics' headquarters complex in Seocho, Seoul, before the sharpest single-day drop in the company's shares since the 2008 financial crisis. — Oskar Alexanderson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Samsung and SK Hynix posted their worst one-day falls in decades after a $487bn Shanghai listing for China's CXMT convinced investors Chinese memory chips are now a real competitive threat.

South Korea's stock market absorbed its fourth-largest single-day loss in history on Tuesday, and the reason fits in a sentence: investors decided, more or less all at once, that China can now make memory chips well enough to matter.

Samsung Electronics fell 13.4%, its steepest drop since the depths of the 2008 financial crisis. SK Hynix, the world's dominant supplier of the high-bandwidth memory that feeds Nvidia's AI processors, dropped 14.7%. The Kospi, South Korea's benchmark index, sank 10.84% — a 732-point fall that took it to 6,023.66 and triggered a marketwide circuit breaker, the eighth of 2026. Tokyo's Nikkei 225 fell 4% in sympathy, dragged down by Kioxia, Japan's own memory maker, which lost 18.3%. Nearly 96% of the 917 stocks traded on the Kospi finished the day lower.

In one sentence: Samsung and SK Hynix suffered their worst one-day share falls in decades as a $487bn Shanghai listing convinced investors that Chinese memory chips are no longer a future threat but a present one.

A listing that changed the arithmetic

The proximate cause traces to Shanghai's STAR Market the previous Monday, where ChangXin Memory Technologies — known as CXMT — made its trading debut. Shares priced at 8.66 yuan closed their first session at 49 yuan, a gain of roughly 466%, valuing the Hefei-based chipmaker at about 3.3 trillion yuan, or $487bn. That is a sum in the same league as Samsung Electronics itself, assigned to a company that did not exist as a serious DRAM producer five years ago.

The number mattered less than the trajectory behind it. CXMT's share of the global DRAM market has climbed from roughly 3% to 8% in the space of a year, according to industry trackers, making it the fourth-largest producer behind Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron. Its advantage is not sophistication — it remains absent from the high-margin, AI-linked HBM segment where SK Hynix and Samsung concentrate their newest capacity — but price. CXMT sells conventional DDR5 memory 15–20% cheaper than its Korean rivals, and market chatter has it in talks with Apple to supply RAM for devices sold in China. For a market that had priced Samsung and SK Hynix as AI-infrastructure plays with limited competitive threat, an $8.6bn Shanghai fundraising round reframed the picture: China's chipmakers are no longer years behind on the commodity end of the business, only on the frontier end.

The earnings that lit the fuse

CXMT's listing alone might have unsettled investors without triggering a rout. What turned unease into panic was SK Hynix's own earnings, released the same week. The company reported a record quarterly operating profit of roughly 60.5 trillion won ($42bn) — and missed analyst consensus, which had been set closer to 64 trillion won, on the back of slower-than-expected shipments of its next-generation HBM4 memory. A company posting its best quarter ever and still falling short of expectations told the market something uncomfortable: even record AI-driven demand may not be enough to justify the valuations built on it, especially with a cheaper competitor now scaling in the background.

Foreign investors sold roughly 5 trillion won ($3.4bn) of Korean equities on the day, while retail buyers stepped in for about 4 trillion won — a familiar pattern of institutional flight met by local dip-buying, with margin debt still near June's record of 29.8 trillion won. The selling was not confined to memory names. Samsung SDI fell 11.4%, LG Innotek 16.3%, and SoftBank Group — whose valuation has become a proxy for AI-infrastructure sentiment through its stakes in Arm and various chip ventures — dropped 4.4% on the day and nearly 7% the following session. Nvidia's reported willingness to extend up to $250bn in financing guarantees to support OpenAI's compute buildout added to a separate worry running alongside the China story: that AI chip demand is increasingly underwritten by supplier financing arrangements rather than organic end demand.

Reading the panic

Analysts were divided on whether the move was a repricing or an overreaction. Kim Seok-hwan of Mirae Asset said investors were trading on "prospective capacity rather than present earnings" — betting on where CXMT could be in three years, not where it is now. Cameron Systermans of Mercer pushed back on the scale of the fear, noting CXMT "is still several years behind their Korean competitors in the area of high-bandwidth memory," the segment that actually commands AI-era pricing power. Jing Jie Yu of Morningstar called Tuesday's trading "largely a knee-jerk reaction and overdone," while Matt Simpson of StoneX offered a blunter diagnosis: the market had entered "the despair part of the selloff."

Both readings can be true. CXMT poses little near-term threat to the HBM chips Nvidia depends on — that business requires manufacturing precision China's export-controlled fabs have not demonstrated. But the commodity DRAM market, worth tens of billions of dollars annually and long treated as a stable, Korea-dominated duopoly-plus-Micron, is a different story. If a Chinese producer can undercut Samsung and SK Hynix by a fifth on standard memory while ramping capacity with state-linked capital, the profit base that has funded those companies' AI-chip research for a decade looks less secure than it did a month ago. The Kospi's concentration — Samsung and SK Hynix together account for more than half its market weight — meant that a story about DRAM pricing power became, within a single session, a story about the entire Korean stock market.

What happens next depends on numbers that do not yet exist. CXMT has not disclosed performance specifications or a commercial timeline for the immersion-DUV technology that unsettled investors, and its prospectus notably contained no HBM production plans. Samsung reports its own quarterly results in the coming weeks, and traders will be watching less for the headline profit figure than for management's language on DRAM pricing and Chinese competitive pressure — the same axis that moved 1.5% of South Korea's entire economic output in a single afternoon.
