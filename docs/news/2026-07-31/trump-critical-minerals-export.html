Trump delegates authority to restrict exports of recoverable critical minerals

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who now holds delegated authority to restrict exports of recoverable critical minerals and materials. — Daniel Torok / U.S. Department of Commerce, public domain via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump signed a Defense Production Act determination letting Commerce restrict exports of recyclable critical minerals like battery black mass and rare-earth scrap.

President Trump has signed a presidential determination under the Defense Production Act delegating authority to the Commerce Department to restrict exports of recoverable critical minerals and materials — the scrap, batteries and spent magnets that already sit inside American recycling streams, rather than the ore still in the ground.

The determination, signed July 30 and detailed in a White House fact sheet, hands Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick the power to block foreign buyers from acquiring what the administration calls "recoverable CMMs": black mass — the shredded remains of lithium-ion batteries — along with end-of-life rare-earth magnets, tungsten-bearing scrap and other manufacturing waste. The stated finding is blunt: these materials are "critical industrial resources necessary to promoting the national defense," and the United States is exporting too much of them before anyone here can reclaim the metals.

Why the DPA, and why now

The Defense Production Act dates to the Korean War and is more commonly associated with compelling domestic output — as it was during the pandemic for ventilators and vaccine components — than with restricting what leaves the country. Using it here signals that the administration is treating mineral scrap as a strategic asset rather than a commodity to be sold to the highest bidder abroad. The U.S. sends out roughly 33,000 metric tons of e-waste a month, much of it laced with lithium and rare earths that could otherwise feed domestic recyclers. A Government Accountability Office review has flagged 60 minerals as vulnerable on national-security grounds, and the administration puts the country's China-sourced share of rare-earth imports at around 70%, with at least 15 minerals almost entirely import-dependent.

The move follows a run of related steps this year: permitting streamlined for domestic mineral production in March, a $2bn private-capital, $10bn Export-Import Bank stockpile dubbed Project Vault in February, and a 15% federal stake taken in MP Materials, the operator of the only active U.S. rare-earth mine. Beijing has already retaliated in kind, adding MP Materials and USA Rare Earth to its own export-control list — restricting the two firms' access to Chinese-origin dual-use goods just as Washington leans on them to anchor a domestic mine-to-magnet supply chain.

What traders should watch

For now this is a delegation of authority, not a published restriction list — Commerce has yet to say which materials or countries are affected first, or on what timeline. That leaves the market to price two possibilities: a genuine tightening of scrap and battery-material exports that squeezes buyers overseas and repricing domestic recyclers upward, or a negotiating lever aimed at trade talks that never gets fully exercised. Either way, it lands in the same week South Korean chipmakers took their worst-ever single-day hit, underscoring how supply-chain nationalism on inputs — minerals, chips, or both — is becoming a standing feature of markets pricing rather than a one-off headline. The next marker is Commerce's implementing rule, which will show whether this is posture or policy.
