Trump's Board of Peace says Hamas has agreed to 'complete disarmament' in Gaza

In one sentence
Trump says Hamas agreed to disarm over seven to eight months, but neither Hamas nor Israel has confirmed the terms, and their public positions still conflict.

Donald Trump said on Thursday that mediators have secured Hamas's agreement to disarm completely in Gaza, calling it a "monumental step" toward ending the war. The claim came from the US president himself, via his Board of Peace, the body he set up in January to oversee the territory's postwar transition — not from Hamas, and not from Israel, both of which have stayed conspicuously quiet on the specifics.

That gap matters. Trump has announced Gaza breakthroughs before. The October 2025 Sharm el-Sheikh declaration was billed at the time as the war's end; more than 1,200 people have been killed in Israeli strikes on Gaza since, according to figures cited by CNN. Markets that priced in de-escalation after that signing spent the following months adjusting back. The question this week is whether Thursday's announcement is a genuine sequel or a rerun.

The roadmap, on paper

The framework, negotiated with Egypt, Qatar and Turkey — with the UAE assisting — sets a 14-day deadline for mediators to draft a detailed disarmament roadmap, extendable only with sign-off from an international verification body and all parties. Officials briefed on the plan told the Jerusalem Post they expect full implementation to run 200 to 300 days, roughly seven to eight months, echoing the phased approach used in October's ceasefire.

Mechanically, it starts small: police-grade weapons transfer first, then heavier armaments decommissioned in stages. Crucially, the weapons will not go to Israel or a foreign power but to a Palestinian "National Committee,” stored under a Palestinian administration's authority, with oversight from an international verification committee. Once that process advances, an International Stabilization Force — potentially drawing personnel from Turkey and Indonesia — is meant to work alongside a newly formed Palestinian police force as Israeli troops withdraw in parallel. Trump framed the endpoint bluntly: "Gaza will finally be in the hands of a new Palestinian government that serves its PEOPLE."

Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's Bulgarian envoy, put the harder truth alongside the announcement: "Implementation and verification have to be real. Withdrawal must move in lockstep with decommissioning." That sequencing — each side's next step contingent on the other's last one — is precisely where the October ceasefire came under strain.

Two sides, two different deals

Hamas has not issued a formal statement accepting the terms; Axios reported US officials characterizing the group as having signed on, but the group's own public position is narrower. Ghazi Hamad, a member of Hamas's negotiating delegation, called the talks "difficult and hard" and said negotiators "tried to reach the best possible formula" — then attached a condition that undercuts the finality of Trump's language: Hamas "would not implement any part of the agreement if Israeli forces failed to fulfil their obligations," with disarmament tied to a full Israeli withdrawal and progress on reconstruction. Palestinian Islamic Jihad, a smaller but armed faction operating alongside Hamas, has separately voiced reservations about the wording.

Israel's position is the mirror image. Israeli media reported the government is insisting on complete Hamas disarmament — including removal of all weapons from Gaza and full demilitarisation of the strip — as a precondition for withdrawal, not a parallel or reciprocal step. White House officials, relayed through US press, described Israeli leaders as "skeptical that Hamas will disarm, but not much they can do except see it happen." Israel has issued no formal government statement endorsing Thursday's announcement. In effect, Trump has announced a deal whose two named parties have each described a different deal.

Why the market read should stay cautious

The substantive case for treating this as more than rhetoric is real: a 14-day drafting deadline is a concrete, checkable date, not an open-ended pledge, and the mediating bloc — Egypt, Qatar and Turkey, all with direct leverage over Hamas — is the same coalition that delivered the October ceasefire, however imperfectly it has held. A credible disarmament timetable, if it survives contact with implementation, would matter for the same reasons the region's risk premium has swung on Gaza news before: oil benchmarks and defence-sector positioning both carry a discount for sustained Middle East de-escalation, and shipping through the wider Red Sea corridor remains sensitive to the war's trajectory.

But the hedges are doing a lot of work in Thursday's language. "Trump says" is not "Hamas confirms" or "Israel accepts." Both principals have public, incompatible red lines on sequencing, and the precedent of the last ten months is that announced frameworks have repeatedly outrun what happens on the ground. The 14-day roadmap deadline — falling in mid-August — is the first hard checkpoint: if it slips, or if the document that emerges doesn't reconcile Hamas's reciprocity demand with Israel's precondition, Thursday's announcement will read less like a breakthrough and more like the opening bid in a negotiation still very much underway.
