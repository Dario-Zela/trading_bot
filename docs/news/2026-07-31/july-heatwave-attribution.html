World Weather Attribution: July heatwave 'virtually impossible' without climate change

In one sentence
World Weather Attribution says July's US heat and humidity, like Europe's June heatwave, would have been virtually impossible without human-caused warming.

Scientists at World Weather Attribution have concluded that the heat and humidity that smothered the eastern United States over the July 4th weekend would have been "virtually impossible" in a world without human-caused warming — the latest in a string of rapid-response studies this year confirming that record heat is no longer just correlated with climate change, but directly attributable to it.

The analysis, published under the title Fossil Fuels Are Heating America's 250th Birthday, examined the heat dome that pushed heat-index values to 105–115°F (41–46°C) across more than 20 states from late June into early July. More than 140 million people were placed under heat alerts, and county-level tallies compiled since then put the death toll at 70 or more, though researchers caution that America's patchwork of heat-death reporting likely understates the true number.

WWA's team modelled Wet Bulb Globe Temperature — a measure combining heat, humidity and radiant load that better captures physiological danger than air temperature alone — against a reconstructed pre-industrial climate. In a world 1.4°C cooler, the WBGT levels recorded over the holiday weekend would not have occurred at all. "Humidity was a key factor driving this event, both in terms of impacts and event rarity," the study notes, pointing out that WBGT has risen faster than temperature alone as the atmosphere holds more moisture.

A pattern, not an outlier

The July finding lands three weeks after WWA reached a similar verdict on Europe's June heatwave, which pushed France, Spain and Portugal past 40°C and contributed to an estimated 2,700 excess deaths, according to Carbon Brief's analysis of French public health data. That event, the group calculated, would have been "virtually impossible" as recently as 1976 — a comparable heatwave half a century ago would have peaked roughly 3.5°C cooler. "Climate change is running rampant," said Friederike Otto, the Imperial College London climatologist who co-founded WWA, adding that scientists are "beginning to sound like a broken record" issuing near-identical warnings year after year.

The two studies sit alongside a separate, slower-moving signal: global sea-surface temperatures. The Copernicus Climate Change Service recorded a daily global reading of 20.86°C on 21 June, edging past the previous record set in 2023 and 2024, with the Copernicus Marine Service logging 21.0°C the same day. C3S director Carlo Buontempo said the readings "could indicate the beginning of a new phase, leading, once more, to uncharted territory," with a developing El Niño likely to push ocean temperatures higher still through the second half of the year.

Why the speed matters

WWA's rapid-attribution model — publishing findings within days rather than the months or years earlier attribution science required — is designed explicitly to close the gap between an extreme event and the policy conversation about it. Each study that finds an event was "virtually impossible" without warming removes a line of argument for delay: it is no longer a probabilistic hedge but a documented counterfactual. That shift matters most where liability, insurance pricing and regulatory triggers depend on causal language rather than correlation, and it arrives as insurers and central banks are already recalibrating climate-risk models against a summer that has produced record ocean heat, a virtually-impossible European heatwave and now a virtually-impossible American one within the space of five weeks.

The next data point is likely to come from the Atlantic hurricane season, where record-warm sea-surface temperatures are the standard fuel source for rapid intensification — the metric WWA and similar groups are expected to scrutinise as soon as the first major storm forms.
