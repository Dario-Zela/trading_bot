Shell profit doubles and BP cuts 700 jobs as FTSE 100 hits record on energy earnings

A North Sea oil platform — the kind of asset at the centre of both BP's cost-cutting and Shell's trading windfall. — Gary Bembridge / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
BP is cutting 700 jobs and warning of oil oversupply the same week Shell's profit doubled on Middle East disruption and the FTSE 100 hit a record.

The FTSE 100 touched an intraday record of 10,978.87 on Thursday, its second fresh high in two days, and the two companies doing the heaviest lifting were also delivering the clearest signal that the oil industry's next few years look nothing like its last few. Shell reported its best quarterly profit since 2022. BP, on the same morning, told roughly 700 staff their jobs were going.

That pairing is the story. One producer is banking a windfall from a war. The other is preparing, in public, for the windfall to end.

Shell's best quarter in four years

Shell's adjusted earnings came to $9.84bn for the second quarter, more than double the $4.26bn booked a year earlier and well ahead of the $8.92bn analysts had pencilled in. Income attributable to shareholders was $10.8bn, cash flow from operations topped $21bn, and the company launched a fresh $3bn share buyback — on top of $1.2bn carried over from the previous programme — alongside a dividend of $0.3906 a share.

The proximate cause was not strategy but geography. About 20% of Shell's oil and gas output, roughly 550,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, sits in the Middle East, and the region has spent the quarter in various states of disruption tied to the Israel-US-Iran conflict and its spillover into the Strait of Hormuz. Refinery utilisation hit 102% in the April-to-June period, up from 99% in the first quarter, as fewer maintenance outages coincided with a trading desk built precisely for this kind of volatility. Chief executive Wael Sawan put it plainly: "Shell's operational performance enabled very strong results during another quarter of severe disruption in global energy markets." In a separate interview he was more direct still — "volatility is the new normal," he said, and Shell has built itself to be "the name to go after" when markets swing.

The caveat sits in Qatar. Shell's Pearl GTL plant took damage in a March 18 strike on Ras Laffan Industrial City, and the company now says the affected second production train needs roughly a year of repairs, stretching into the first quarter of 2027 — a reminder that this quarter's tailwind was a byproduct of instability Shell does not control and cannot bank on repeating. Trading gains and refinery uptime are not structural cost advantages; they are the kind of quarter that looks brilliant until the disruption that caused it resolves.

BP prepares for the opposite

BP's announcement, delivered the same day, reads like the mirror image. The company is cutting about 700 roles — 8% of its 8,500 non-frontline positions — under new chief executive Meg O'Neill, who took over in April and became, notably, the first woman to lead a Big Oil supermajor. A BP spokesperson framed it as tidying up: "We are building a simpler, stronger, more valuable BP. As part of this process, we are proposing changes that would result in a reduction in roles." The cuts follow O'Neill's decision to fold BP's structure from three divisions into two — upstream and downstream — a reorganisation that took effect at the start of this month.

What makes the timing notable is the warning attached to it: BP is telling the market it expects an oversupply of oil and gas, with downward pressure on prices, even as Brent crude has been trading in the high $70s to low $80s a barrel on Iran-driven spikes. That is not the language of a company riding a temporary price surge — it is the language of a company positioning its cost base for the years after the surge fades, when new non-OPEC supply and a slower demand recovery reassert themselves. BP is choosing to cut now, while margins are still generous enough to absorb restructuring costs, rather than wait for a downturn to force the issue.

A record built on a narrow base

That divergence — one producer monetising disruption, the other bracing for its absence — is also what carried the FTSE 100 to its record. Thursday's intraday high followed Wednesday's own record close, with energy names doing much of the work alongside upgrades to Rolls-Royce and BAE Systems, and against a Bank of England decision to hold rates at 3.75% despite three MPC members pushing for a hike. Shell and BP both rose on the day, roughly 1.3% and 1.5% respectively, as crude firmed.

The index's dependence on a handful of large, energy-and-mining-heavy constituents is well known, and it cuts both ways here: the same Middle East volatility inflating Shell's trading book and crude prices is exactly what BP is warning will not last. A record index level built substantially on a war premium is a different thing from one built on broad-based earnings growth, and the distinction matters for how durable the current high actually is.

The next test comes with Shell's third-quarter numbers, due in late October, by which point the Pearl GTL repair timeline and any easing in the Strait of Hormuz standoff should start showing up in the trading and refining lines that drove this quarter. BP's cuts, meanwhile, are a bet on where oil and gas prices sit once the current disruption clears — a bet the market will start pricing in well before the numbers confirm it either way.
