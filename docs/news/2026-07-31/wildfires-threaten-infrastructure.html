Wildfires threaten French defence plants and force evacuations near UK's Sizewell B

The Sizewell nuclear complex on the Suffolk coast, where a wildfire this week burned to within roughly three miles of Sizewell B. — UK Environment Agency / Wikimedia Commons (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Wildfires halted Rafale production near Bordeaux and burned to within three miles of Sizewell B, testing France and the UK's critical infrastructure resilience.

The wildfire that broke out near the village of Saumos, in France's Gironde department, on July 22 has since become the country's largest in decades — a blaze so intense it generated a pyrocumulonimbus cloud, a fire-fed thunderstorm never before recorded on French soil, whose lightning went on to seed fresh fires beyond the original perimeter. By July 28 the fire had burned more than 42,000 hectares, an area roughly four times the size of Paris, destroyed some 240 homes and forced the evacuation of about 220,000 people. Three thousand firefighters and 1,500 soldiers have been fighting it since, and as of this week the interior ministry describes it as "stabilised" but not contained.

In one sentence: Record wildfires near Bordeaux have halted Rafale fighter production and evacuated missile-propellant sites, while a separate blaze in Suffolk burned to within three miles of Sizewell B before winds turned it away.

What makes this fire different from France's other blazes this summer is what sits in its path. Bordeaux's western suburbs host one of the densest concentrations of French defence and space-industrial capacity anywhere in the country — and this week, for the first time, the fire came close enough to force an actual halt in production, not just a precautionary evacuation.

Flames within nine kilometres of the deterrent

ArianeGroup, which builds the propulsion stages for Europe's Ariane rockets and the M51 submarine-launched ballistic missile that underpins French nuclear deterrence, evacuated three sites west of Bordeaux: Candale, where M51 propulsion units are integrated, roughly 12 kilometres from the active fire line; Saint-Médard-en-Jalles, where the missile is fuelled, 13 kilometres away; and Le Haillan, 16 kilometres out. The company said only that "all necessary measures have been taken to secure them," declining to detail what those measures were. Roxel, the MBDA-linked propellant maker that shares the Saint-Médard site, was evacuated alongside it.

The clearest evidence that this is more than a proximity scare came from Dassault Aviation. The company relocated sensitive tooling from its Martignas wing-assembly plant and Cestas logistics hub — both within 9 kilometres of the fire at points this week — to its Mérignac final-assembly site and the adjacent Air Base 106. Mérignac itself, where Dassault assembles both the Rafale fighter and Falcon business jets, was "at a standstill" on July 27, according to trade outlet AeroTime, with staff told to stay home and only equipment-transfer and site-security teams on-site. That halt lands awkwardly for Dassault, which had been pushing toward 28 Rafale deliveries this year; because Mérignac is the final link in the assembly chain, stopping it stops deliveries, not just output further up the line. Neither Dassault nor France's armament directorate has said how long the closure will last. Thales's nearby Mérignac facility, which supplies Rafale avionics, has also been shut since Monday, and the CEA's Cesta site — home to the Laser Mégajoule facility used in nuclear-weapons research — closed as the fire came within 7 kilometres. Airbus Atlantic evacuated its Salaunes plant, which makes fuselage sections for the A220 and A350, at the authorities' request.

Sizewell's near miss

Two days later and 1,100 kilometres north, a different fire produced a similar test. A blaze on Dunwich Heath in Suffolk, first reported at 5:30pm on Wednesday, grew roughly tenfold overnight to around 150 hectares — about 210 football pitches — and prompted Suffolk Fire and Rescue Service to declare a major incident. Evacuation orders went out that evening: about 50 residents were moved to a rest centre at Westleton village hall, and a nearby holiday park of some 200 caravans was cleared entirely. By Thursday more than 100 firefighters, 18 engines and a helicopter were on the line, with officials warning the fire could burn for another 48 hours.

The fire burned to within roughly three miles of Sizewell B, Britain's only pressurised-water reactor, and the adjacent Sizewell C construction site. EDF, which operates the plant, said the blaze was "not affecting operations" and that the station continued normal monitoring. Suffolk's chief fire officer, Jon Lacey, said the fire was not believed to threaten the nuclear facilities, and the fire service cited both wind direction — pushing flames and smoke toward the sea rather than inland — and the remains of the River Minsmere, which separates Dunwich Heath from the Sizewell complex, as reasons the site was never in direct danger. Contingency plans were activated regardless, in case the wind turned. The dryness behind it is stark: Suffolk had received just 2% of its average July rainfall, with temperatures above 30°C the day the fire broke out. July 2026 is already the busiest month for wildfires in England and Wales on record, with 393 incidents recorded nationally.

A shared vulnerability, not a coincidence

Separated by a few days and the English Channel, these two fires are not the same story told twice — they are the same underlying pressure applied to two different kinds of critical infrastructure. Neither France's nor Britain's defence and energy planners built their industrial geography around wildfire risk; Bordeaux's aerospace corridor and Suffolk's nuclear coast were sited for reasons of history, logistics and land value; the climate risk arrived afterward, and is now arriving more often. What distinguishes this week from a routine bad-fire-season story is that one of the two sites, Mérignac, actually stopped producing — a concrete, measurable disruption to a NATO member's fighter-jet output — while the other, Sizewell B, held throughout, insulated by wind and a river rather than by design.

That gap between what happened and what nearly happened is the detail worth tracking. If Gironde's fire reignites toward Candale or Saint-Médard, France's missile-propellant supply chain, not just Rafale deliveries, would face a test that hasn't yet occurred this summer. If a shift in Suffolk's coastal winds is followed by another dry, hot spell, EDF's contingency protocols — activated but so far untested — would face their own. Both outcomes hinge on weather patterns that are becoming less exceptional each year: watch the containment percentage out of the Gironde prefecture and whether Dassault sets a date to restart Mérignac, and watch the wind forecast for the Suffolk coast over the fire's next 48 hours.
