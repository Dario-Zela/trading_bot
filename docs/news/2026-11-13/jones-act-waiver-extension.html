White House expected to extend Jones Act waiver for oil and commodity shipping

In one sentence
A single Benzinga report says the White House will extend a Jones Act waiver letting foreign-flagged ships carry oil and commodities between US ports; terms and confirmation are missing.

The White House is expected to extend a waiver of the Jones Act, according to a report from Benzinga. If it does, foreign-flagged ships would be able to carry oil and other commodities between US ports, a trade normally reserved for American vessels. The extension is so far only expected. It has not been announced, and the report is the only source the desk has found.

What the waiver does

The Jones Act, Section 27 of the Merchant Marine Act of 1920, requires cargo moved between two US ports to travel on ships that are American-built, American-owned, American-flagged and crewed mainly by US citizens. The rule covers coastwise trade: a tanker carrying crude or refined fuel from a Gulf Coast terminal to the Northeast, for instance, or a bulk carrier moving commodities between domestic ports.

A waiver suspends that requirement for a defined period or class of cargo. Federal law allows such waivers on national-defence grounds, and they have historically been granted in response to supply disruptions. Under the arrangement described in the Benzinga item, foreign-flagged vessels could move oil and commodity cargo between US ports in the usual sense of the word, at whatever rates the wider charter market offers rather than those of the more limited domestic fleet.

What is not known

The gaps are large. The reported extension has not been checked against an order text or an official statement. The desk could not establish:

  • how long the extension would run;
  • which agency would issue it;
  • when it would take effect;
  • what rationale the administration has given, or whether it covers all commodities or only some.

The word "extend" implies an existing waiver, but its terms were not available for this report. Without them, it is not possible to say how much of a change an extension represents against the status quo. If the existing waiver already lets foreign vessels carry this cargo, continuing it alters little. A new or broadened one would matter more.

Why energy logistics watch it

Domestic shipping capacity is the issue. The US-flag fleet is small and expensive to operate, and the rule limits how much tonnage can serve any given domestic route. Opening those routes to foreign-flagged ships would widen the pool of vessels available, which in principle could ease freight rates and give regional fuel suppliers more options when pipelines or rail are constrained. Whether it would do so depends on the waiver's scope and duration. Charterers are unlikely to reposition foreign tonnage for a short or uncertain window.

The usual counter-argument comes from US shipowners, mariner unions and shipbuilders, who say the rule sustains domestic shipbuilding, crews and defence-relevant sealift capacity, and who have opposed waivers on those grounds. That opposition is part of the background to any announcement, though the Benzinga item does not report any reaction.

What to watch

The first marker is an official document: a notice or order from the issuing agency stating the term, covered cargo and start date. Confirmation from a second outlet or a named official would also move this from expectation to fact. After that, the readable signals are domestic tanker and bulk charter rates and any announced foreign-flag fixtures between US ports. Until then, this is a watch item resting on a single report.
