Milburn review proposes fewer benefits for some under-25s and a local fund in place of PIP for some disabled young people

In one sentence
The Milburn review reportedly proposes cutting some under-25s' benefits and swapping PIP for a local fund for some disabled young people, though details and status are unconfirmed.

A government-commissioned review of young people who are out of work has proposed reducing benefits for some under-25s. For some young disabled people it would replace Personal Independence Payment (PIP) with a local fund they could apply to. That would move part of their support from a national entitlement, paid to anyone who meets set criteria, to a discretionary pot that varies by area. The details matter a great deal here, and several are not yet confirmed.

What is reported, and what is not

The proposals come from the review led by Alan Milburn, the former Labour health secretary, into young people not in education, employment or training, known as NEETs. According to BBC and Guardian reporting, the review proposes cuts to benefits for some under-25s. It also proposes that some young disabled people lose PIP and instead apply to a local fund.

This article could not find the published text behind those headlines. The eligibility rules, the sums involved, the size of any local fund and the timetable are therefore not stated here. Nor is it established whether the measures are formal recommendations to ministers or settled government policy. Reviews of this kind advise; the government decides what to adopt. Ministers' response is the point at which any of this becomes policy.

The case the review has made

The earlier work gives the logic. The interim report found that in 2024-25 the Department for Work and Pensions spent roughly £25 on benefits for young people for every £1 it spent on helping them into work. Nearly a million people aged 16 to 24, about one in eight, are classed as NEET. The review has warned that the share could rise to one in six without reform. It has put the cost at around £125bn a year in lost output and tax revenue, plus extra benefit and health spending.

It has also stressed how sticky claims are. One summary cites findings that seven in ten young people on a health or disability benefit are still claiming a decade later. Only one in four PIP recipients are in work by the age of 24. A review built on those numbers is likely to argue that the current system parks young people rather than moving them on. Replacing a fixed payment with locally run, work-linked support fits that argument.

The interim report took a softer line on at least one point. It did not recommend removing the Universal Credit health element from disabled young people under 22. The reported final proposals appear to go further, though that comparison cannot be checked without the full text.

The objections

Disability charities have argued that cutting benefits is not the answer to rising youth worklessness. A Commons debate on the interim report on 3 November heard the same warning about the health element: that cuts would fall on people who cannot work.

The local fund raises its own questions. PIP is a national entitlement that goes to people who meet the criteria, wherever they live. A fund administered locally gives councils or other bodies the discretion to decide who gets help and how much. Support could then differ from one area to the next. Whether the fund would be ring-fenced, how applicants would appeal a refusal, and whether it would be funded at PIP's cost are all questions the proposals must answer.

The political test

The proposals also land on a sore spot for Labour. The government has already retreated on welfare once after backbench resistance, and cuts aimed at young people, especially disabled ones, could reopen that split. Andy Burnham's role in managing it is unverified here and needs checking before it is relied on.

What to watch is the government's formal response and any legislation. Ministers will have to say which proposals they accept, how much each would cost or save, and whether PIP changes would apply to existing claimants or only new ones. Until then, the headline numbers are the review's argument rather than a change in anyone's payments.
