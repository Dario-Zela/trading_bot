Sadiq Khan and Labour figures urge PM not to water down electric vehicle targets

In one sentence
Sadiq Khan and other Labour figures are urging the PM not to soften the electric vehicle mandate as the government is reported to weigh a cut to the 2030 target.

Pressure is building inside the Labour Party over the future of the electric vehicle mandate. Sadiq Khan, the mayor of London, has joined other Labour figures in urging the prime minister not to water down the rules, according to the Guardian's politics coverage of 12 November. The government is reported to be weighing a cut to the 2030 electric car target. That has turned a technical dispute over carmaker compliance into a test of how firmly ministers will defend their climate commitments.

What the mandate requires

The zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate obliges manufacturers selling cars in Britain to make a rising share of their sales fully electric. Under the current schedule the share reaches 80 per cent of new car sales by 2030. Firms that miss the target face fines, though the rules have already been loosened once, with added flexibilities for carmakers.

Reporting since June has said ministers were considering a further dilution. Some of those reports put the 2030 figure as low as 50 per cent. Those accounts rest on briefings, not a published government decision, and the exact figure under discussion has varied between outlets.

The argument inside Labour

The dispute does not split neatly along left and right. Several outlets report that a group of Labour MPs has warned against weakening the targets before talks on the decision. Others, including backbenchers from industrial constituencies, argue that a lower target would be more realistic for carmakers and for households that cannot yet afford an electric car. PoliticsHome has reported MPs raising concerns that the transition could leave poorer areas behind.

Reports of a cabinet-level disagreement describe business and transport ministers favouring a bigger cut than the energy department, which is said to want a figure closer to 80 per cent. The specific percentages attributed to individual ministers come from secondary reports and could not be confirmed against a primary source here.

Khan's intervention fits his record. He has repeatedly declined to retreat on London's air-quality and green policies, and he recently urged Andy Burnham not to back away from net-zero goals after a summer of heatwaves and wildfires. I could not retrieve the full text of his latest remarks, so his exact wording is not quoted here.

Industry on both sides

Carmakers are often cast as the lobby for a weaker mandate. The picture is more mixed. Electric vehicle industry bodies have written to the transport secretary urging the government to hold the current targets. They warn that a retreat would undermine billions of pounds of investment already committed to charging, battery and vehicle production. Manufacturers that have built plans around the 2030 schedule would, in effect, be penalised relative to rivals that have lagged.

The counter-argument is about demand. If buyers are not purchasing electric cars at the pace the mandate assumes, firms must either discount heavily, cutting margins, or pay fines. Ministers worried about jobs in the car sector have weight on that side of the scale.

What to watch

The next marker is the government's decision itself, reported to be close. The headline figure for 2030 will show which side prevailed. A cut to around 60 per cent would be read as a compromise, while anything near 50 per cent would signal that industrial anxiety has beaten the climate wing of the party. Either way, the fight shows how much of Labour's climate policy now has to be defended in public, not simply announced.
