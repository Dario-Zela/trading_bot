'Housefishing': when does AI use in property listings become misleading?

In one sentence
AI-edited property photos are spreading faster than the rules on labelling them, leaving portals' own policies, and general consumer law, as the main check on 'housefishing'.

Estate agents have long had tricks for making a home look better online: a wide-angle lens, a tidy-up before the photographer arrives, a well-timed bright day. AI has made the tricks cheaper and more powerful. A listing photo can now have furniture added, walls repainted, a grey sky swapped for dusk, or a patchy lawn turned green, all without anyone lifting a paintbrush. The practice has picked up a name, "housefishing", a play on catfishing. The question the BBC has put to the property industry is where legitimate presentation ends and misrepresentation begins.

What the BBC found

The BBC's reporting, as summarised by secondary outlets (the original could not be fetched, so details below come from those write-ups), centres on property hunters in Sussex. They said AI-generated furnishings in online listings were misleading and wasted their time. Rightmove, the UK's biggest property portal, allows AI imagery only when it is clearly labelled. The BBC reportedly found many listings where it was not. Some carried watermarks such as "Virtually staged" or "Artificially enhanced"; many carried no disclosure at all.

Estate agents defending virtual staging point mainly to cost. Digitally furnishing an empty room is far cheaper than hiring furniture. Rightmove says agents are responsible for what they upload, and that it can require changes to a listing or remove it.

How common is it, and does it work?

Hard numbers are scarce, and the best available come from an industry-commissioned survey, so they warrant caution. House Buyer Bureau commissioned a poll of 906 UK people who had bought a home in the previous year, published in August 2026. One in five said they had viewed a property whose photos had been digitally enhanced. Among those buyers:

  • 53% said the images persuaded them to book a viewing they might not otherwise have made.
  • 54% ruled the property out as soon as they saw it in person.
  • Only 34% went on to make an offer, and 38% lowered their maximum price because the presentation felt misleading.
  • 82% did not complain. Of the rest, 15% went to the agent and 2% to the seller.

The pattern suggests that enhancement does draw people in, but it costs agents goodwill once reality arrives. It also costs buyers time and travel. For renters, who often have to view quickly and pay holding fees, the cost may be higher still, though no figures for that were confirmed.

The regulatory gap

No single rule is aimed squarely at AI-edited listing photos. The nearest UK tool is consumer law. According to coverage of National Trading Standards' position, misleading listings can fall under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which prohibits misleading actions and omissions. That covers a photo that gives a false impression of a property, whether or not AI made it. What it does not do is say what counts as acceptable editing, or require a label.

In the EU, the approach is more explicit. Article 50 of the AI Act requires AI-generated or modified content to be labelled, and Euronews has asked whether that could curb housefishing. Whether the labelling duty applies to an agent's routine photo edit, and who would enforce it, is a live question. Nothing confirmed here shows enforcement action against a UK or EU listing.

In the UK, then, the main control is the portals' own rules. Rightmove's label requirement is only as strong as its checking, and the BBC's finding of unlabelled listings suggests checking is patchy.

What to watch

The practical test is whether trading standards officers or portals treat an undisclosed AI-furnished room as a misleading action, and say so publicly. Clearer guidance on what may be edited (dust and lighting versus furniture and room size) would settle most of the argument. Until then, disclosure rests on the portals' labelling rules and on buyers who, in the survey at least, mostly do not complain.
