Rayner approves Piddington military-base asylum centre despite local opposition

In one sentence
The government is pressing on with an asylum site for about 1,250 men at a former military base near Piddington, Oxfordshire, over objections from residents and Cherwell council.

The Home Office's plan to house asylum seekers on a former Ministry of Defence site near Bicester has moved from a national argument about accommodation to a local one about planning. The approval, announced under Angela Rayner's name, goes ahead over the objections of residents of Piddington, the Oxfordshire village nearest the site, and of the district council.

The site

The proposal is for a temporary asylum housing centre on part of the Bicester Garrison site, which Cherwell District Council was told about by the Home Office in September. It is one of three new sites the department has identified to house about 3,700 people, an average of roughly 1,200 each. At Bicester the plan is for 1,256 men, in a village-scale landscape: the base sits around 0.8 kilometres from Piddington.

The logic is the one ministers have used since the government began moving away from hotels. Jonathan Reynolds has said a former military site is "a far more suitable location" than a hotel in a town or city centre. Hotels have been the most visible and politically costly part of the asylum system, and a fenced, previously developed site away from a high street is cheaper to manage and easier to defend in argument. The Home Office minister Anna Turley, responding to local concerns, said: "We understand people's concerns and I will look into the issues raised by the good people of Piddington."

The local objection

Opposition was organised early. Residents told reporters they were distressed by the plan, and in September villagers held a referendum on it. The vote had no legal force, but it gave the objection a number and a date. Cherwell council, for its part, discussed and approved a consultation response setting out why it objects to the proposal.

Those objections have now been overridden. That is the point of the planning route as the government has used it: a site in public ownership, for temporary use, can be pushed through without the usual leverage that a district council holds over a private developer. The sources available do not set out the precise consent mechanism or any conditions attached to it, and those details will matter.

What the argument is now about

The objections that carry legal weight are narrower than the ones voiced at village meetings. Capacity of local GP surgeries and schools, road access, policing, and the safety and welfare of the residents themselves are the kinds of issue that can be raised in a planning process and, if ignored, tested in court. A single-sex site of more than 1,200 people next to a village is also a different proposition from a hotel with a few dozen rooms, and ministers will be asked how it will be run and how long "temporary" lasts.

There is a precedent in the debate. Similar plans at sites such as Linton-on-Ouse in North Yorkshire drew local opposition and legal challenge, with the matter becoming a test of how far the Home Office can proceed against a council's wishes.

The next markers are practical. Cherwell council will decide whether to seek a legal review of the decision, the Home Office will need to publish timings for the first arrivals, and residents will look for the commitments on services and on the length of use that the department has so far offered only in general terms.
