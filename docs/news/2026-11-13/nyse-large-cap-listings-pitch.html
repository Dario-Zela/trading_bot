NYSE pitches large caps on switching listings as London debates keeping its blue chips

In one sentence
The NYSE says large caps do better on its board, but Hilton, GM and Walmart have chosen Nasdaq, and its valuation claim rests on 21 index joiners.

The New York Stock Exchange is making a public case that large companies should list with it rather than its rival Nasdaq, and that pitch lands as London argues over how to hold on to its own blue chips. The pitch comes from the exchange itself, through its spokespeople, so it is best read against what companies have actually done.

The NYSE's most recent flow data is not flattering. This autumn Hilton is moving from the NYSE to Nasdaq on 5 November, and General Motors follows on 17 November, according to Fortune's 28 October report. Walmart made the same switch late last year and, as that report notes, rose 3% on 12 January, the day its Nasdaq 100 inclusion was announced. CAE and Culp have also moved to Nasdaq.

What the exchange says

An NYSE Group spokesperson told Fortune that companies joining the Nasdaq 100 "experienced compressed market valuations and increased share volatility", and that about $500 billion of market capitalisation has moved to the NYSE this year. Fortune notes that the first claim rests on 21 companies that joined the index over 40 years. That is a thin sample for a claim about valuations, and it does not show cause and effect.

The NYSE does have some counter-evidence. Etsy, Virtu Financial and Trinity Capital have announced moves from Nasdaq to the NYSE, and the exchange says 347 companies have transferred to it from Nasdaq since 2000. Those are mostly mid-sized names, and most of the dates come from press releases. I could not locate the remarks from the NYSE's Chris Bergan on which this piece is based, so his argument is not quoted here.

Why companies are moving

The reasons given by executives point mainly in Nasdaq's direction. Hilton's chief executive, Chris Nassetta, said Nasdaq offers a "platform aligned with our innovation and growth ambitions". The Nasdaq 100 is the recurring draw. Only Nasdaq-listed companies qualify, and funds tracking it must buy the constituents. Fees may matter too: the maximum annual listing fee is reported at $500,000 at the NYSE against $193,000 at Nasdaq. Nasdaq says 500 companies, worth $2.7 trillion, have switched to it over time.

The NYSE's defence is brand. Brad Jacobs called it "the most recognized stock exchange in the world". That is a real asset, but it is harder to put a number on than index inclusion.

The London angle

For London the contest is mostly in another league. Ferguson has cancelled its secondary London listing to trade only in North America, and Flutter has delisted from London while keeping its primary NYSE listing. AstraZeneca has added a direct US listing alongside its London one. In those cases the alternative to London was New York, not London against a second US exchange. The reasons for them, deeper capital pools and higher valuations, are the ones the NYSE is invoking. The detail of the London debate was not in the material available, so this piece does not set out who is arguing what.

What to watch

The next tests are GM's move on 17 November and any Nasdaq 100 inclusion decisions that follow the Hilton and GM transfers. If the NYSE's valuation argument holds, it should show in how those shares trade against peers. So far the evidence from actual listing decisions favours the exchange's rival.
