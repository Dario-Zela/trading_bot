Luxury slump: investors look to the ultra-rich, as L'Oréal valuation draws questions

In one sentence
Barron's argues the top end of luxury is holding up better than aspirational brands, raising questions about valuations such as L'Oréal, which one model puts about 14% overvalued.

Luxury stocks have had a poor stretch, and the usual explanation is that shoppers have stopped spending. A line of argument in Barron's offers a narrower diagnosis: demand has not so much fallen as divided. The very wealthy, it suggests, are still buying. The customers who stretch to afford a handbag or a premium skincare range are the ones pulling back.

The distinction matters because the two groups are not equally important to the companies that serve them. This piece rests on a single published argument, and the figures behind it, including sales splits by brand tier or customer segment, could not be checked here. What follows is therefore the shape of the claim, not a verified dataset.

Two customers, two outcomes

Barron's case is that the top end of the luxury market has proved more resilient than the aspirational tier. The logic is familiar. Households at the top of the wealth distribution are cushioned by asset values and tend to adjust spending slowly. Aspirational buyers are more exposed to prices, credit and confidence, and they are more likely to trade down or postpone.

For investors, the split changes what a sector-wide slump means. A fall in luxury shares that reflects weakness only among aspirational customers is a different problem from one that reflects the whole client base retreating. It would also imply that earnings are becoming more concentrated in the top-tier customer, and in the brands able to hold that customer's loyalty. Without the underlying data, the size of that concentration is unknown.

L'Oréal and the valuation question

The second thread is a valuation one. A Simply Wall St model estimates that L'Oréal shares are about 14% overvalued. That figure comes from a automated discounted-cash-flow style screen, and its inputs, date and underlying share price were not verified. It is a view on price, not a fresh company announcement, and it should be read as one.

Its relevance to the luxury debate is indirect but real. L'Oréal sells across price tiers, from mass-market products to prestige lines, so its mix of aspirational and high-end customers is the kind of detail the Barron's thesis would turn on. A premium rating is easier to defend if the premium end is carrying growth, and harder if the aspirational buyer is the swing factor. The discovery summary also linked the valuation discussion to AI-generated imagery and premium pricing, though no company statement or dated source for either point was available.

What would settle it

The argument is testable. The next round of company results should show whether growth at the top end is offsetting softer aspirational sales, and whether groups are reporting that split in any detail. Brands that disclose sales by customer tier, or whose results diverge sharply between their flagship and entry-level lines, would offer the clearest evidence.

Until then, the sensible reading is cautious. If the ultra-rich are indeed the proxy for where luxury earnings come from, then weakness among aspirational shoppers matters more than a headline slump implies, because it points to a narrower base underpinning valuations. If the premium for owning these shares assumed that base was broad, the 14% gap the screen identifies for L'Oréal is the sort of question the next reporting season will start to answer.
