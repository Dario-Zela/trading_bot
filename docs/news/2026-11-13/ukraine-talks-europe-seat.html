Zelensky welcomes US move to bring Europe into Russia-Ukraine peace talks

In one sentence
Zelensky has welcomed a US push to give European governments a place in Russia-Ukraine talks, but Moscow has only said it will consider the idea and no format is agreed.

Washington has said it wants European governments at the table in the effort to end the war in Ukraine, and President Volodymyr Zelensky has welcomed the change. Until now the diplomacy has run mainly through a US-led channel, with American envoys shuttling between Kyiv and Moscow. A formal European role would turn that channel into something closer to a multi-party negotiation. Accounts of the move differ in detail, and the Kremlin has not yet agreed to it.

What was said

Two strands of reporting describe the shift. Both are secondhand: the original BBC report could not be read directly, and the accounts below come from wire and syndicated copies.

The first is an AFP report from Wednesday 11 November. Speaking to reporters in Copenhagen, Zelensky said the inclusion of Europe was "not simple", and named it as the first of the changes to the US approach that he was "happy" about. He repeated a long-standing Ukrainian line that decisions on European security cannot be taken "without Europe". The day before, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio had said the format should include European partners, because they are part of solving the problem over the long term.

The context was a round of US-mediated talks in Abu Dhabi, the first trilateral session of the US, Russia and Ukraine since February, when discussions ended in stalemate. According to AFP, several European representatives were present in the city. A separate meeting of Ukrainian, US and European officials followed the formal session. Washington also met key European nations, the EU and NATO to ease concerns about being sidelined. AFP did not say which countries were involved, and the Abu Dhabi talks ended without an agreement.

The second strand came on Thursday 12 November. US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff met Vladimir Putin in Moscow, then met Zelensky in Dublin that evening, where Irish Taoiseach Micheál Martin was hosting European leaders. Kushner said Washington had pressed Putin to accept European participation, and that the Russian leader was prepared to "give the proposal a try". Zelensky said Europe needs a voice in decisions about its own security.

The sources do not fully agree on the sequence. One treats Europe's presence as already visible in Abu Dhabi, and another treats a formal European seat as a proposal still awaiting Moscow's answer. The safest reading is that Europeans have been near the process, while their formal standing at the table has not been settled.

Why Moscow is the obstacle

Russia has traditionally resisted a major European role. It argues that the substance of any settlement is a matter for Moscow and Washington, with Kyiv consulted, and that European governments are parties to the conflict rather than neutral brokers. Putin is reported to have left Russia's stance on the war and its territorial demands unchanged in Moscow. No Kremlin statement on the European proposal appears in the accounts reviewed. "Willing to consider" is therefore a long way from agreement.

EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas, speaking to the BBC, read Russian openness as a sign that Moscow recognises Europe's influence. She doubted, however, that Russia is genuinely moving toward peace. Martin told the leaders in Dublin that the war has changed the region's security environment, and that several EU states regard it as an existential threat, in part from fear that a Russian victory would encourage further aggression.

What a European seat would change

The practical question is what Europe would bring that Washington cannot supply alone. There are at least three answers.

  • Security guarantees. Any ceasefire would depend on assurances that deter a second invasion. European governments are the likeliest to supply troops, money or air cover for such a arrangement, so their terms and Washington's would have to match.
  • Sanctions. Much of the sanctions architecture on Russia, including frozen assets, is held in European jurisdictions. Relief that the US might offer in a deal would require European consent to be real.
  • Ukraine's future. Ukraine's EU ambitions, which Zelensky cites in making his case, sit in European hands rather than American ones.

Each of these also constrains Kyiv, since a larger table means more governments whose interests must be reconciled before an offer can be made. For Moscow, a broader format could mean facing a united Western position on sanctions and guarantees, rather than a US channel it can negotiate with separately.

Other strains

The diplomacy is moving alongside other difficulties. About a fifth of Ukraine's territory remains under Russian control, and cross-border attacks continue. Russia and Ukraine agreed another prisoner exchange, described by Kushner as the largest since the full-scale invasion began in February 2022, though no numbers or timing were given. Zelensky also said Ukraine's military faces increasingly systematic limits on access to Starlink's military-grade services, including Starshield, which he said hampers the interception of Russian jet-powered drones. The dispute adds strain to US-Ukraine relations. AFP noted that Washington's attention shifted to its war with Iran after the February talks collapsed.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the Kremlin formally accepts European delegations in the next round, and in what capacity: observers in a side room, or parties to a negotiation. Also to watch are which European governments are named, and whether the EU, NATO and national capitals send separate delegations or a single one. Until Moscow answers, the widened format remains an American proposal that Kyiv supports and Europe has asked for.
