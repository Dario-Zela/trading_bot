Some childhood offenders may no longer have to reveal convictions to employers

In one sentence
England and Wales are consulting on ending lifelong employer disclosure of some childhood convictions, with 5.5- and 11-year limits for certain offences; serious violent and sexual crimes are excluded.

The government wants to stop childhood convictions following people into the job market for life. Ministers in England and Wales are consulting on changes that would let some people who offended before 18 leave those convictions off criminal record checks once a fixed period has passed. The convictions would stay on file but would not be shown to employers.

Justice minister Jake Richards, speaking at a roundtable with employers and campaigners, described the exercise as "just a consultation looking at maybe how we can differentiate the system". Many young people who offend, he argued, do not go on to lives of crime. No closing date or implementation timetable has been published.

What would change

At present, about 770 "specified offences" trigger lifelong disclosure on standard and enhanced checks, whatever the person's age when they committed them. The proposals would loosen that for childhood convictions in three ways, according to LBC's account of the announcement:

  • A childhood conviction for offences such as violent disorder, supplying drugs to a child, robbery, affray or actual bodily harm would stop being disclosed after 5.5 years, if the sentence was non-custodial.
  • A childhood conviction carrying a custodial sentence of two years or less would be disclosed for 11 years rather than for life.
  • A custodial sentence of more than two years would still be disclosed for life.

The government also plans to end mandatory disclosure of out-of-court resolutions, and to reduce or remove disclosure of childhood convictions that ended in a fine. Serious violent and sexual offences are excluded, and the government says sensitive roles would be treated differently. The age of criminal responsibility stays at 10; Richards said "at the moment we don't think there is a case for making that change", despite campaigners' calls for 12 to 14.

The existing rules

Under the Rehabilitation of Offenders Act 1974, a conviction for an offence committed under 18 becomes "spent" in half the time that applies to an adult with the same sentence. Once spent, it generally need not be declared. But for many roles, and for a long list of listed offences, checks still reveal spent convictions, and some stay visible permanently. The proposal targets that second layer.

The policy has a legal backdrop. In 2019 the UK Supreme Court, in the P and Gallagher cases, held that blanket, automatic disclosure of old and minor convictions and warnings was disproportionate and incompatible with the right to private life under Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. The coverage reviewed here does not link the new plan to those rulings, so whether this is a response to them, or simply a fresh policy choice, is not established. Earlier reforms, including 2020 proposals to shorten disclosure for minor and youth convictions, followed the same judgment and reviews such as the Taylor Review on youth justice.

The employment case

The stated motive is work. Ministry of Justice data put roughly one in four working-age people in England and Wales, some 12 million, as having a criminal conviction. A criminal record is the second most common barrier to hiring disadvantaged people, cited by 47% of employers. Ministers believe England and Wales have some of the strictest disclosure rules among comparable countries, and want to avoid entrenching inequalities among groups over-represented in youth justice: Black and ethnic minority children, children with special educational needs and children who have been in care.

Employers including KPMG, Timpson and Greene King attended the roundtable. Campaigners have previously welcomed such changes as help into work while saying they stop short of wiping the slate clean.

What to watch

The consultation's publication and closing date will show how firm the proposals are, and whether the 5.5- and 11-year thresholds survive. The related review by Alan Milburn into young people not in education, employment or training is due next week; it covers benefits for under-25s, and the two are likely to be read together as one agenda on getting young people into work.
