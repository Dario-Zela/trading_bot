North Korean hackers used fake video calls in $400m crypto hack, FBI says

In one sentence
The FBI says North Korean hackers stole over $400m in crypto by luring 900-plus victims into fake video calls, a claim reported secondhand and not yet independently verified.

The FBI says North Korean hackers stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over about 18 months. They did it with a method that needed no malware at the first step. Victims were invited to video calls that looked routine, and the calls were the way in.

The account comes from the FBI as relayed in BBC-based reports published on 12–13 November. It has not been independently verified, and the original BBC and FBI texts were not reviewed directly for this article. Some details below differ between outlets and are flagged where they do.

How the scheme worked

According to those reports, the FBI calls the operation "FaceStealer". The hackers used fake or compromised social-media accounts to invite targets to meetings. The meetings were hosted on fraudulent look-alikes of Zoom, Microsoft Teams and Google Meet.

The FBI says more than 900 people have been victims in the past 18 months. It also says it seized more than 900 webcam recordings that the hackers had secretly captured. Those recordings were then reused: on a later call, footage of a previous victim made it look as though a trusted contact was present and talking.

The FBI says the operation still relies mainly on genuine recordings. It says the hackers have begun adding AI-generated video of prominent crypto figures. That detail matters for the framing. This is closer to a puppet show built from stolen footage than to a flawless deepfake. It works because people tend to trust a familiar face on a screen.

Patience was part of the method. The FBI says some thefts came months, or more than a year, after the first compromise. The hackers appear to have waited for victims' wallet balances to grow.

What is and is not established

The headline numbers are the $400 million total and the 900-plus victims. Both are the FBI's, as reported secondhand. The reports do not agree on everything. The BBC-based coverage also refers to the attribution of a Binance-related hack to North Korea-linked actors. One outlet gives a figure for it of $6.3 billion, and another cites a $1.5 billion Bybit hack. Those figures conflict, and the Bybit theft was an earlier, separate case that the FBI attributed to North Korea in February 2025. This article does not rely on the Binance claim. Pyongyang has denied involvement, according to one report.

The pattern is consistent with earlier findings. Google's Mandiant team reported that North Korean operators used deepfake video, including a fake company chief executive, against a crypto-sector target. Chainalysis estimated that North Korean hackers stole about $2.02 billion in crypto in 2025, up 51% on the year before. A $400 million theft fits that trend.

Why it matters

FBI Cyber Director Brett Leatherman said the regime relies on proceeds from large-scale crypto theft to fund its illicit weapons programme. If that holds, theft of this kind works as a steady source of revenue for the weapons programme, and not as an occasional windfall.

For firms, the implication is practical. Many treat a live video call as proof that a person is who they appear to be, especially for approving large transfers. This campaign attacks exactly that assumption. The defence the FBI recommends is low-tech: confirm unexpected meeting invitations through a separate channel, and join meetings through the official app using a meeting code, not a link.

Two things are worth watching. One is whether the FBI publishes the technical details and indicators behind FaceStealer, so that other firms can check their own logs. The other is whether the original BBC report and the FBI notice settle the discrepancies in the secondary coverage, including the loss total and the Binance link.
