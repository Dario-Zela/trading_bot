UK economy grows at its fastest rate in over two years, complicating the Bank of England's next move

In one sentence
UK GDP grew 0.7% in Q3, driven by AI-linked services, but the figure matched forecasts, so its effect on the Bank of England's rate path is far from clear.

Britain's economy grew by 0.7% in the third quarter, according to the Office for National Statistics, up from 0.5% in the second. That is a firm number. Whether it should move the Bank of England is less clear, because the headline quarterly figure was largely what forecasters and the Bank itself had expected.

That matters for how the release is read. A strong print only reprices rate-sensitive assets if it surprises, and on the quarterly measure it did not. The ONS published the data on 12 November. The Reuters poll consensus was for 0.7%, and the Bank's own projection was reportedly the same.

What the ONS reported

Output in the July–September period was 1.9% higher than a year earlier. The expansion came almost entirely from services, which rose 0.9% on the quarter. Production edged up 0.1%, and construction fell 0.7%.

The standout was information and communication, up 2.1% in the quarter. The ONS tied much of that to AI and cloud-computing work. Computer programming, consultancy and related activities, the largest contributor, rose 4.4%. The pattern is narrow. One fast-growing corner of the services sector accounts for a disproportionate share of the headline.

The monthly data held a small surprise. September output grew 0.2% against a forecast of roughly zero growth. The ONS also revised August down to 0.0% from 0.1%. Taken together, the revisions and the September beat leave the quarter's momentum a little stronger at its end than the headline implies, though the change is modest.

The headline's claim that this is the fastest growth in more than two years could not be confirmed against the ONS series in the sources reviewed, and no comparison with earlier quarters is made here. The ONS did report a monthly 0.5% rise in February, the fastest monthly expansion since January 2024, but that is a different measure over a different period.

Why the rate path is the real question

The Bank of England sets Bank Rate through its Monetary Policy Committee, and gilt yields move on what traders think the committee will do next. Growth that arrives in line with the Bank's own forecast confirms its existing view and gives it no new reason to change course. Growth that beats it argues for keeping rates higher for longer, because stronger demand tends to keep price pressures alive.

On the quarterly figure, the first case applies. The September monthly beat is a mild nudge towards the second. This reporting found no data on current Bank Rate, on the market's pricing of the next move, or on gilt yields after the release. Any claim that the print has pushed back expected cuts would therefore be speculation, and none is made here.

The composition of growth complicates the picture further. Spending on AI infrastructure and cloud services adds to output, but it is not obviously the kind of broad household demand that most worries rate-setters about inflation. A policymaker who sees a capital-spending boom concentrated in a few large firms may judge it less inflationary than a consumer-led surge of the same size.

Durability, and a two-speed economy

The resilience also invites the question of how long it lasts. Suren Thiru of the ICAEW said growth was projected to slow in the final quarter of this year and into 2027. That reading treats the third quarter as a peak.

The British Chambers of Commerce struck a similar note, warning of an "emerging two-speed economy". A small number of larger firms are investing heavily, while many smaller businesses hold back. The aggregate number can look healthy while the median business sees little of it. That split is a reason for the Bank to be cautious about reading the headline as evidence of broad strength.

Earlier this year, the ONS's chief economist, Grant Fitzner, said stronger growth made it more likely that the Bank would eventually raise rates, though a hold was the likeliest near-term outcome at that time. Those comments concerned the February data, before the Iran war, and they should not be taken as a view on the current release.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The first is the Bank's next Monetary Policy Committee decision and the minutes of the vote split, which will show whether any member cites the September strength. The second is the next monthly GDP release, which will show whether October continues the 0.2% pace or reverts to the flat August reading. The third is the next inflation figure, which decides whether services-led growth feeds into prices. Until then, a quarterly print that matched forecasts is more likely to leave the rate outlook where it was than to move it.
