Two men found guilty of helping Iran try to 'eliminate' UK-based journalists

In one sentence
Two men were convicted in London of helping Iranian intelligence surveil Iran International journalists and a barrister, in a case adding to evidence of Iranian transnational repression.

Two Iranian men have been convicted at a London court of helping Iran's intelligence apparatus watch and identify people in Britain it regards as enemies, among them journalists at the Persian-language broadcaster Iran International. The verdicts, returned on 12 November, make state-directed targeting of critics on British soil a matter of proven fact rather than official warning.

The verdicts

Mostafa Sepahvand, 41, and Farhad Javadi Manesh, 46, were found guilty at Woolwich Crown Court after a six-week trial. Both were unanimously convicted of engaging in conduct likely to assist a foreign intelligence service under the National Security Act. Sepahvand was also convicted of carrying out surveillance and reconnaissance intended to commit serious violence against a person in Britain. Javadi Manesh was acquitted of that second charge. A third defendant, Shapoor Qalehali Khani Noori, 57, was cleared of all charges and discharged.

Both convicted men are due to be sentenced at the Old Bailey on 17 December.

What the jury heard

Prosecutors said the men carried out surveillance for Iranian intelligence from August 2024 to early 2025, gathering information on people in Britain whom Tehran viewed as opponents. The targets included Iran International journalists Kambiz Hosseini and Mojtaba Pourmohsen, and the barrister Jonathan Turner.

Prosecutor Bill Emlyn Jones told the court the men visited addresses, photographed buildings and vehicles, and recorded videos "to help Iran's Revolutionary Guards identify potential targets." Sepahvand, the court heard, was meant to carry out an attack and recruited the other two to find and monitor the targets. Evidence included communications with contacts in Iran about surveillance and payments, and Sepahvand's attempts to resolve a criminal case against him in Iran. Press reports of the trial also say he hoped to earn £150,000 and used an AI chatbot to look up a journalist's home address; those details come from secondary coverage and not from the court record.

The defences differed. Sepahvand admitted conducting surveillance but said threats against him and his family forced him to act, and that he deliberately tried to undermine the operation. Prosecutors answered that his repeated requests for more accurate target information showed he was helping to locate the targets. Javadi Manesh denied knowing that his activities assisted Iranian intelligence and did not give evidence. Noori also denied any knowledge of an Iranian link.

A pattern, and a denial

The case follows the March 2024 stabbing of Iran International presenter Pouria Zeraati outside his home in south-west London. In that case two Romanian men were convicted of the attack, and a judge said it was carried out for the benefit of a foreign power. Counter-terrorism police say they have disrupted some 20 Iran-linked plots in the UK since 2022. Posters bearing the faces of London-based journalists, Zeraati among them, appeared in Tehran in 2022 under the heading "Wanted: dead or alive."

The Iranian embassy in London has "categorically" rejected allegations that Tehran is involved in violent activities in Britain, and Tehran has consistently denied directing plots against critics abroad. Police said hostile states are increasingly working through proxies, which is the pattern this case fits: hired individuals with weak or no formal ties, rather than officers of the state themselves.

What to watch

The sentences on 17 December will show how seriously the court weighs the surveillance charges, given that no attack took place. The wider diplomatic question is whether the government answers with new measures against Iran, such as further sanctions or restrictions on its diplomatic presence. Reports also say two other men face charges in Britain and three have been indicted in the United States over related activity. Those proceedings will test how far the evidence reaches up the chain from the hired watchers to those who directed them.
