Nvidia's Huang reported to have resigned over spy-affair allegations, as Congress seeks his testimony

In one sentence
Nvidia's 8-K confirms Jensen Huang resigned as CEO and Colette Kress is interim chief; it gives no reason, and the spy-affair allegations behind the stock drop remain unproven.

Nvidia said late on 12 November that Jensen Huang, its co-founder and chief executive for more than three decades, had resigned as president, CEO and board member, effective immediately. The company's own filing gives no reason. The reason people are asking is a different set of reports, in which Huang is said to have reported interactions with a woman suspected of working for Chinese intelligence. The two accounts have not been tied together by anything on the public record.

What the filing says, and what it leaves out

The 8-K that Nvidia filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission on 12 November is short. Colette Kress, the chief financial officer since 2013, becomes interim CEO and keeps her finance job. Harvey C. Jones becomes the board's first-ever chair. The board has started a search for a permanent chief executive and says it will consider internal and external candidates.

The release thanks Huang for his contributions and says nothing about why he is leaving. It does not mention an investigation, an affair or Congress. Jones said the board had "full confidence in Colette and the leadership team" and expected operations and product plans to continue without interruption. Kress said "NVIDIA's strength has always come from its people."

So the resignation is confirmed by a primary source. The affair allegation is not. Nothing in the filing supports it, and the reporting that does carry it, in the BBC, Benzinga and trade press, does not name who made the claims. The headline's "reported" is doing real work: this is an allegation that has not been proven, and Huang has not been shown to have done anything improper.

How the story reached the market

The sequence matters more than any single claim. Earlier this week, reports said Huang had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation and had reported contact with a woman suspected of working for Chinese intelligence. On Sunday the board said its independent directors would investigate. Nvidia also said it had no reason to believe US national security or its corporate secrets had been compromised, and said, in a statement to Business Insider, that "Jensen is committed to continuing to lead Nvidia as CEO without interruption."

That commitment lasted about three days. Between the Sunday statement and the Wednesday filing, the company went from backing its chief executive to replacing him.

The market reaction was fast. Reports put the fall in Nvidia shares at more than 5% in a day, roughly $200 billion of market value for a company worth more than $4 trillion. The brief also says the Nasdaq 100 recovered after an initial drop. The exact intraday low and the closing figures have not been checked against exchange data, so those numbers should be treated as approximate. The arithmetic is at least consistent: 5% of $4 trillion is $200 billion.

On the evidence so far, the share price was reacting to uncertainty, not to a known fact. Investors did not know what the allegation was, whether it involved any compromise of technology, or whether the board had found anything. A drop of that size in a stock this large makes the point that a counterintelligence allegation about one person can move a market in hours, well before any investigation reports.

Congress

Washington moved as well. According to one report, three Republican House committee chairs asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on 18 November about his reported interactions and Nvidia's security protocols. That is a request for a briefing, not a subpoena, and the candidate description of a "summons to testify" goes further than anything this reporting supports. Whether the request still stands now that Huang has left the company is not clear. The committees involved have not been confirmed here, and neither has any public statement from them.

The link to policy is the reason Congress cares. Nvidia's accelerators sit at the centre of the US-China debate over export controls, and the company's chief executive has been one of the most visible lobbyists on that subject. An allegation that he was a target of Chinese intelligence, however it resolves, gives the people who want tighter controls a talking point.

What to watch

Three dates and questions will settle most of this. The first is 17 November, when Nvidia reports third-quarter fiscal 2027 results, with a call at 2 p.m. Pacific. Kress will presumably lead it, and analysts will ask about the investigation, the succession timetable and whether the board expects any customer or regulatory fallout. The second is 18 November, the date of the requested House briefing. The third is the board's investigation: if it finds nothing, the question becomes why Huang left; if it finds something, the question becomes what was compromised.

For now the facts are thin. A chief executive has gone, the company has not said why, and the market has priced in a lot of doubt. The way this episode plays out will show how well a board can handle a security allegation when its stock is the biggest weight in many portfolios.
