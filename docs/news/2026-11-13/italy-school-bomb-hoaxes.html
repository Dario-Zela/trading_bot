Fresh bomb hoax threats force evacuations of Italian schools

In one sentence
A new wave of suspected bomb hoaxes has again forced Italian school evacuations, part of a repeating pattern of mass emails whose source investigators have yet to establish.

Bomb threats have emptied Italian classrooms again, the latest entry in a run of false alarms that has stretched across weeks and several countries. As far as reporting shows, none of the threats has turned out to be real. What has changed is how ordinary the disruption has become. Pupils are marched into the street, carabinieri sweep the building, nothing is found, and lessons resume the next day or the day after.

The BBC reported the new round of suspected hoaxes on 13 November. This account draws on that report and on earlier Italian coverage. The BBC item was the only source for the latest wave, and no school count or police statement for it was available. The figures below therefore come from the earlier wave.

A pattern, not an incident

The clearest picture comes from 19 October, when Italian outlet Quotidiano.net reported a cluster of evacuations. Eleven schools in the Lucca area were emptied in late morning, several more in Arezzo, and at least three in the province of Padua: the Rodari school in Padua, the Newton in Camposampiero and the Vivaldi in Montegrotto Terme. Carabinieri checks found nothing at any of them.

The threats arrived by email. They claimed bombs were inside the buildings and, in several cases, invoked Allah. Italian reports from early November described a larger tally still, one citing 279 institutions targeted, though that figure comes from secondary reporting and should be treated with caution.

Not only Italy

Italy was not alone. The same October reporting described a far bigger effort elsewhere:

  • Northern Ireland: up to 300 schools received what appeared to be the same email.
  • Serbia: more than 100 schools.
  • Hungary: more than 70 schools, with threats sent to local media rather than to the schools directly.
  • Scotland: several schools, with police calling the messages generic false alarms.

The scale and the identical wording point to automated, bulk sending. That is an inference from the reports, not a finding by investigators.

Who is behind it

Attribution is unsettled. Police in Northern Ireland said the messages originated outside the UK. Education Minister Paul Givan said the threat "seemed to come from Islamist circles" and that it demanded sharia be imposed. Italian authorities are investigating, but the 19 October reporting said the incidents were not yet clearly linked to one another or to a single source. Hungary's interior minister, Gábor Pósfai, said investigations so far showed the threats to be unfounded, and no explosives were found.

The religious language may be a disguise. Mass hoax emails of this kind often borrow the vocabulary of terrorism to guarantee a response, whatever their real motive. Nothing published so far settles that question.

The cost of caution

Each evacuation is cheap to cause and costly to answer. A single email can pull police, bomb-disposal teams and teachers away from their normal work, and it can turn a school day into a lost one. Officials cannot treat any threat as a hoax in advance, which is exactly what makes the tactic work. Repetition adds a second cost: the risk that routine response breeds fatigue.

What to watch is whether Italian prosecutors, or Europol if the case goes cross-border, can trace the sending infrastructure and tie the Italian emails to those sent elsewhere. A confirmed common source would turn this from a nuisance into a coordinated campaign. Until then, each new wave is likely to be handled school by school, one empty classroom at a time.
