FTSE 100 has worst day since May as 30-year gilt yield tops 6% for the first time since 1998

In one sentence
The FTSE 100 fell 1.7% to 10,428, its worst day since May, as the 30-year gilt yield topped 6% for the first time since 1998 and the 10-year briefly passed 5.5%.

The cost of lending to the British government for three decades rose above 6% on Friday, a level last seen in 1998. UK shares fell with it. The FTSE 100 closed down 1.7% at 10,428, its worst session since May, and the mid-cap FTSE 250 lost 1.6%. The 10-year gilt yield briefly passed 5.5%, its highest since 2007.

The equity move was large by recent standards, but the bond market was where the day's news happened. Equities were repricing a discount rate that had shifted under them.

The numbers

The 30-year gilt yield is the benchmark for the cost of very long-dated sterling borrowing. Pension funds and insurers are its natural buyers, because their liabilities run for decades. A print above 6% is the first since 1998, a 28-year high. The 10-year yield's brief break above 5.5% is the highest since 2007, before the financial crisis reshaped the rate environment.

The equity declines were broad rather than confined to rate-sensitive pockets. The FTSE 100 at 10,428 had its sharpest one-day fall in about five months. The FTSE 250 fell 1.6%. That index is more domestically exposed and more dependent on borrowing, so it is usually the more direct read on UK financing conditions. The two indices fell by almost the same amount. That suggests a market-wide repricing, not a sector story.

A yield is a price in inverse form. A move of this size at the long end means holders of existing long gilts are sitting on large mark-to-market losses. Long-duration bonds lose far more per percentage point of yield than short-dated ones. That is why a rise at the 30-year point is more painful for portfolios than the same rise at two years.

Not only a British problem

The obvious reading is a domestic one: investors demanding more compensation for UK inflation risk or for fiscal sustainability. Both are plausible, and the triage material points to both. But gilts did not move alone. The US 10-year Treasury yield was near 5.2%, and UK yields moved in tandem with it.

That co-movement matters for interpretation. If the shock were purely British, gilts would be expected to underperform Treasuries, and sterling would likely carry much of the strain. A synchronised rise points instead to a shared driver: a global reassessment of how high policy rates must stay, or of how much term premium long-dated debt should carry. The inputs are the same either way. Markets are pricing either higher-for-longer rates or weaker growth, and the two have different implications for equities.

The evidence available does not settle which dominates. Higher yields alongside falling equities fit a story in which rates are expected to stay restrictive. A growth scare would typically pull yields down. Friday's pattern, with long yields rising and shares falling together, is the first of those. It is also the pattern that is hardest on stocks, because it raises the discount rate without raising the earnings forecast.

The US data flow complicates the picture. US payrolls rose just 29,000, and the odds of an October Fed hike collapsed. A weak jobs number would normally pull yields lower. That the long end sold off regardless suggests that the pressure is coming from term premium and supply, not from the near-term path of policy.

How equities are being repriced

The channel from bonds to shares runs through several routes. A higher risk-free rate lowers the present value of future earnings, and the effect is largest for companies whose profits lie furthest out. It raises refinancing costs for leveraged firms. And it gives investors an alternative: a 6% yield on a UK government bond, with no credit risk, is a competitive benchmark against an equity dividend yield.

The FTSE 100 is more exposed to global earnings and commodities than to UK borrowing costs, which is why the FTSE 250's matching fall is notable. Domestic-facing companies, housebuilders and highly indebted businesses are the usual casualties when gilt yields rise this quickly. The article's sources do not break the day down by stock, so the pattern within the indices cannot be described with confidence.

Other pressure points are already in view. Oil and fuel prices remain a live inflation risk: a G7 release pulled crude down as much as 5%, yet UK diesel has passed £2 a litre. On the fiscal side, a leak suggests Conservative spending cuts go beyond the £47bn pledged, which shows how close fiscal arithmetic is to the centre of the political argument. And the Bank of England governor's warning on AI valuations is a reminder that equity prices are being asked to carry a great deal.

What the model inputs now say

For systematic strategies, the move changes several inputs at once. The yield curve at the long end is steeper and higher, which feeds directly into discount-rate assumptions and into equity risk premium estimates. The UK risk premium is no longer a stable parameter. Currency dynamics also need attention. If gilt yields rise because of fiscal worries, sterling usually weakens, whereas if they rise on a global move, the currency effect is smaller. Any model that treats gilt yields and sterling as moving together should be checked against that distinction.

The next tests are specific. The first is whether the 30-year yield holds above 6% when the market reopens, or whether Friday's close proves to be a spike. The second is whether the 10-year finishes at or above its intraday high. The third is the next round of UK inflation data and the Bank of England's response, which will show whether the market's concern is inflation or the fiscal position. In the US, the next Treasury moves will show whether the 5.2% 10-year yield is a ceiling or a waypoint. If Treasuries stabilise and gilts do not, the problem is a British one. If both keep rising, the repricing is global, and UK equities are one casualty among many.
