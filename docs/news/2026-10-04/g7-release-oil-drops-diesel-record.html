G7 oil and diesel release pulls crude down as much as 5%, but UK diesel passes £2 a litre

In one sentence
A G7 release of 100 million barrels, with diesel frontloaded, pushed crude down as much as 5%, yet UK diesel passed £2 a litre, suggesting the squeeze sits in refined products.

Oil fell by as much as 5% in US trading after the Group of Seven agreed to release 100 million barrels of crude and refined fuel from emergency stocks. The same day, the RAC reported that UK diesel had passed £2 a litre at the pump for the first time. The two facts sit awkwardly together. Crude is the input and diesel is the product, and the gap between them is where this story sits.

What the G7 agreed

The plan is coordinated through the International Energy Agency and runs for four months. It totals 100 million barrels, covering crude and products. The distribution matters more than the headline figure. Diesel is frontloaded into the first 20 days instead of being spread in proportion across the programme.

That design implies something about the problem policymakers think they are solving. A general crude shortage would call for an even release of crude. Pushing diesel out first points to a shortage in the middle distillates that run lorries, ships, farm equipment and freight. The disruption to energy markets from the US-Iran war, and the political pressure that followed Donald Trump's threats on energy policy, are the stated backdrop. The release is a stopgap built around the tightest part of the system.

The arithmetic is also modest. A programme of 100 million barrels over four months is roughly 0.8 million barrels a day, if spread evenly. Frontloading raises the early rate, but it cannot change the fact that strategic stocks are a finite buffer. They buy time, and they do not replace lost supply.

A market that wanted a reason to fall

Crude's drop of up to 5% was not driven by the release alone. US labour data came in soft the same session, which fed concerns about demand. Payrolls rose by just 29,000, a figure that also wiped out expectations of an October Fed rate hike (see our coverage of the payrolls report). Traders were pricing a supply boost and weaker demand together, a combination that suits an oil bear.

That is also why the move deserves caution. A fall that rests on one policy announcement and one data print can reverse as quickly as it arrived. If the jobs figure turns out to be an outlier, or if the release proves too small against the underlying disruption, the demand argument fades and the supply argument has to carry the price on its own.

The pump price disagrees

The RAC figure shows the limit of what the release can do in the short run. Diesel at more than £2 a litre in the UK came despite crude easing from its recent peaks. Pump prices lag wholesale prices, and retailers rebuild margins slowly on the way down. More important, the diesel price reflects the tightness in refined products that the G7 plan is aimed at. Cheaper crude does not help much if diesel supply is the constraint.

For households and businesses the effect is direct. Diesel feeds into haulage, food distribution and construction costs, and it is felt in consumer prices with a delay. It also reaches the Bank of England's problem. Inflation that stays high because of energy gives the Monetary Policy Committee little room, and it arrives as the labour market softens and as gilt markets are already under strain (the 30-year yield has topped 6% for the first time since 1998). Energy suppliers are meanwhile lobbying ministers over household bills, with wholesale prices still elevated.

What to watch

The test comes in the first 20 days, when the diesel releases are scheduled. If wholesale diesel prices and the pump price both start to turn down by the end of that window, the stockpile has done its job. If crude holds its lower level while diesel stays near £2, the constraint is in refining or logistics, and a release of stocks will not fix it. The RAC's weekly pump data and the next US labour report are the near-term markers. If crude is cheaper, diesel is still dear and growth is slowing, central banks face the squeeze that the policy was meant to avoid.
