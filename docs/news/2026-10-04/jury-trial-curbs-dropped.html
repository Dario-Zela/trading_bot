Government drops plans to curb jury trials in England and Wales

In one sentence
Ministers have dropped plans to end jury trials for cases carrying under three years' jail after a backlash, though judge-alone trials for some fraud cases remain.

A government that spent most of a year defending a plan to shrink the role of juries in England and Wales has dropped it. On Sunday the Ministry of Justice said it would no longer pursue the proposal to remove jury trials for cases in which a jail term of under three years was likely. Justice Secretary Alex Norris said: "I have heard the strength of feeling on the proposals for increasing the number of judge-alone trials in the Crown Court and have made the decision to change course."

The policy belonged to Keir Starmer's government and was first set out in December 2025, as a way to clear a growing court backlog. Andy Burnham became Prime Minister in July. Ditching it is among the first visible breaks with his predecessor's domestic agenda.

What was proposed, and what survives

The original package did three things. It would have ended the right to a jury in less serious Crown Court cases. It would have increased the sentencing powers of magistrates, who hear lower-level offences without a jury. And it would have removed a defendant's right to choose trial by jury in cases that can be heard either way.

The retreat is partial. Judge-alone trials will still be available for some fraud and financial offences, the complex cases where ministers argue that jurors struggle most. Lawyers have also kept up their objections to the changes affecting magistrates and to the loss of the right of election. Andrew Thomas, chair of the Criminal Bar Association, welcomed the change of course but warned that "a severe threat to our trial by jury remains". The Bar Council also broadly welcomed the move.

The backlog has not gone away

The reason for the original plan has not changed. At the end of June, 80,900 cases were waiting for a Crown Court trial, nearly double the figure in 2020. Ministers argued that jury trials were slow and that sending fewer cases to juries would free up court time.

The opposition from lawyers, MPs and civil liberties groups rested on a different diagnosis. Critics said years of under-investment and neglect had caused the delays, not the jury system. They also questioned whether the changes would shorten the queue at all. That argument carried particular weight with Labour MPs, since jury trial is a principle many of them regard as constitutional rather than administrative. A restriction plan had cleared an early parliamentary hurdle despite Labour dissent, but that dissent did not go away.

What it signals

The lesson is narrower than it might look. This was a new prime minister with no personal stake in the policy, who could drop it at little cost. The reversal says less about how fragile governments are than about how costly it is to change an institution that the legal profession, backbenchers and the public all treat as untouchable.

For anyone tracking policy risk in the UK, the signal is mild but real. Plans that touch long-standing constitutional safeguards appear to have a lower ceiling than fiscal or regulatory changes do. The reversal also leaves the backlog unresolved without the savings the original plan promised.

The next test is what replaces it. Watch for the Ministry of Justice's detail on the fraud carve-out, and for whether the magistrates' sentencing powers and the election right survive in the revised package. The backlog figures due after the summer will show how much pressure remains on the courts.
