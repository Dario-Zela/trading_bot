Suspect charged with planning a terror act against Nigel Farage

In one sentence
Joshua Kerry, already charged with murdering Ann Widdecombe, now faces a terrorism-preparation charge that includes alleged targeting of Nigel Farage, and appears in court Wednesday.

A 28-year-old man already accused of murdering the former Conservative minister Ann Widdecombe has now been charged with preparing acts of terrorism, including against Reform UK leader Nigel Farage. The new charge moves a killing that had been treated as a single crime into a wider case about a suspected campaign against figures on the populist right.

Joshua Kerry, from Rotherham, faces one count of engaging in conduct in preparation of terrorist acts. Counter Terrorism Policing says the activity is alleged to have taken place between 31 May 2024 and 8 July 2026. Kerry is due to appear at Westminster Magistrates' Court in London on Wednesday. He is a defendant, and none of the allegations has been tested in court.

What the charge adds

The date range matters most. The window starts in the weeks before the 2024 general election, when Farage was campaigning and was hit by a thrown milkshake and had objects hurled at his campaign bus. It ends the day before Widdecombe, 78, was found dead at her home in south-west England on 9 July. Prosecutors say she was struck 21 times with a hammer, and Kerry was charged with her murder in late July.

The terrorism charge covers the preparation of acts, not an attack that took place. Public reporting so far does not say how the alleged plan against Farage was to be carried out, or whether Widdecombe's killing is treated as part of the same plan. The police have not set out the evidence behind either point. Those gaps will probably be filled only at later hearings, and reporting restrictions will limit what can be published until trial.

Vicki Evans, the senior national coordinator for Counter Terrorism Policing, said officers had been running "a hugely intensive and complex investigation for some time". That phrase, and the length of the alleged offence period, suggest a case built up over months, not a quick response to a single event.

Politics and security

Reform's reaction has already turned to protection. Richard Tice, the party's MP, said on X that the charge shows why Farage "needs money for security". That refers to a recent controversy over a £5 million gift to Farage to fund his security. The charge gives that argument a concrete case to point to, though the court has yet to decide whether the allegations are true.

Farage, 62, leads a party that has run high in the polls, and his public events draw large crowds. The charging decision does not change that exposure. It does show the authorities treating the threat against him as a matter for terrorism law, not ordinary public-order policing.

What to watch

The first marker is Wednesday's appearance at Westminster Magistrates' Court, which should show how the two cases are being handled and whether they will be tried together. The Crown Prosecution Service's later filings, and any detail about the alleged preparations, will show whether the Farage count and the Widdecombe murder rest on the same evidence. Until then the case is a serious allegation that is only partly set out.
