US payrolls rise just 29,000 and October Fed hike odds collapse

In one sentence
September payrolls grew by only 29,000, with earlier months revised down by 60,000, and futures odds of an October Fed hike fell from 64% to under 21%.

The US economy added 29,000 jobs in September, a fraction of the pace needed to absorb new entrants to the labour force, and the unemployment rate rose to 4.2%. Earlier months were also marked down. Futures markets, which had been leaning towards another Federal Reserve rate rise this month, reversed within hours.

The report is the first in some time to land on both counts at once: a weak current reading and a weaker past. It arrives about a month before the midterm elections, and it gives the Fed's rate-setters a harder decision than the one they were expecting to make at the end of the month.

A thin month on a thinner base

America typically needs something like 100,000 to 150,000 new jobs a month to keep pace with labour-force growth. September's 29,000 is well short of that range. Taken alone, a number like that might be written off as noise, since monthly payroll figures are volatile and subject to sampling error.

The revisions make that harder. August and July combined were cut by a net 60,000 jobs, so the deterioration reaches back through the summer rather than starting in September. The uptick in unemployment to 4.2% points the same way. Payrolls and the jobless rate come from separate surveys, one of employers and one of households, and when both soften together the case for a statistical blip weakens.

None of this amounts to a collapse. A 4.2% jobless rate is still low by long-run standards. But direction matters more than level to a central bank that has been tightening, and the direction here is clear: hiring is slowing faster than forecasters had assumed, and the data they had been relying on was somewhat too generous.

Markets reprice in hours

The sharpest reaction came in rates. Futures pricing for a hike at the Federal Open Market Committee meeting on 27–28 October fell from 64% to under 21% after the release. A two-in-three bet on tightening became roughly a one-in-five bet within a trading session.

The size of the move says something about positioning. A repricing that large, that quickly, implies that traders had been set for a hike and were caught leaning the wrong way. It was a surprise to them, not a gradual change of mind.

Bonds moved in the expected direction. The yield on the 10-year Treasury slipped below 5.2%, as investors bought longer-dated debt on the view that policy rates would stay lower than previously assumed. The level is worth noting. A yield above 5% leaves little doubt that borrowing costs have already been rising, and a fall to just under it is a modest retreat, not a regime change.

The usual playbook for weak labour data is a move into safer assets and a lowering of growth assumptions. The bond market followed it. Equities have a more ambiguous reading: softer rates support valuations, while softer hiring undermines the earnings that justify them.

The Fed's narrower path

The hike that markets had been pricing is now the minority outcome, not the central case. Under 21% is not zero, however, and the committee has two more labour reports before it next meets in December and one more before the October gathering is over, if the schedule holds. A single weak month, even one with downward revisions, rarely settles a policy debate by itself.

What the data does is shift the burden of proof. Officials who favoured another increase now have to argue it against a labour market that is visibly cooling, and the revisions mean they cannot easily point to underlying strength that the headline missed. Officials leaning towards a pause can point to three consecutive months of weaker evidence.

The political backdrop adds noise rather than signal. The Fed insists its decisions are made without regard to elections, and nothing in the data release changes that. But a jobs number this soft ahead of the midterms will be read for its political implications by everyone else.

The global picture is not helping the committee's task. Elsewhere, long-dated borrowing costs have been rising, and governments are coordinating on energy supply. Those pressures cut in different directions for inflation and growth, and they complicate any simple reading of the American data.

The next catalysts are specific. The October jobs report, due in early November, arrives after the FOMC meeting, so the committee will decide on 27–28 October with only intervening data on inflation, claims and activity. If those readings echo September's softness, a pause becomes the likely outcome. If they do not, the 21% could rise again as quickly as the 64% fell.
