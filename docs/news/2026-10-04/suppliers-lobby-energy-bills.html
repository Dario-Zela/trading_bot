Energy suppliers lobby ministers over bills as wholesale prices stay elevated

In one sentence
Energy suppliers are pressing ministers for relief as wholesale prices, driven by the Middle East conflict, point to a 16% rise in the price cap in January.

Britain's energy suppliers are pressing ministers for help with household bills, warning that wholesale prices driven up by the Middle East conflict will push costs higher again within months. The lobbying poses a question for the government. It can shield households directly, or it can leave suppliers and customers to absorb the cost.

The trade body Energy UK said this week that domestic gas prices, which rose on Thursday, and forecasts of steep rises in January mean inaction would lead to a "longer-term, more costly and deeper crisis". Its chief executive, Dhara Vyas, said: "We cannot afford to wait for the same scale of crisis before acting again."

The EDF Energy chief executive, Simone Rossi, went further. He warned that the UK was "walking into a second energy crisis".

The numbers

Bills for households on the regulator Ofgem's price cap rose 4% in October, about £60 a year or £5 a month. That takes the typical annual bill to £1,723. Forecasters cited by the BBC expect a 16% rise in the new year, which would lift the figure to about £1,999. The increase would fall on around 20 million households on variable tariffs.

The underlying pressure is wholesale cost. Energy UK says the Strait of Hormuz has effectively been closed to shipping and the wider Middle East conflict has lifted international prices. In its account, the cuts to VAT on electricity and the levies removed earlier in the year have been wiped out by the rise in wholesale costs.

The cap passes wholesale costs through to bills with a lag, so suppliers carry the gap in between. Customer debt adds another burden. Energy UK puts the cost of unpaid bills at an average of £67 a year on every account.

What suppliers want

The asks are specific and, for the most part, aimed at households rather than at supplier balance sheets:

  • Targeted support beyond the existing £150 Warm Home Discount.
  • A debt relief scheme for the worst-affected households.
  • The removal of more levies from electricity bills, with the cost shifted to general taxation.

The third item is the largest in fiscal terms, because it moves the cost from bill-payers to the Treasury. It also follows the logic of the earlier measures, which the industry says have been overwhelmed by market prices.

The government's position

Prime Minister Andy Burnham said ministers are "looking at any measure that can give people breathing space". That is a commitment to look, not to act. The Treasury is also under pressure from gilt markets, where the 30-year yield has passed 6%, which limits how much borrowing-funded relief it can offer without a market reaction.

The broader effect runs through inflation and spending. Another 16% on bills would feed directly into the consumer price index and squeeze disposable income, at a point when diesel has passed £2 a litre and households are already cutting back, including on pension contributions.

What to watch

The next marker is Ofgem's announcement of the January price cap, which will confirm whether the forecast 16% rise, to roughly £1,999, holds. Any government package would be expected to land before then. The form it takes, whether levy removal funded by the Treasury or targeted help for debt, will show which side ministers are choosing to protect.
