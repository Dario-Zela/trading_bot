Tens of thousands protest in Spain over the housing crisis

In one sentence
Tens of thousands marched across Spain after parliament rejected the government's tenant-protection package, adding pressure on Pedro Sánchez's fragile coalition.

Spain's housing crisis has moved from the rental listings to the streets. On Saturday, tens of thousands of people marched in Madrid and other cities. They demanded action on rents and evictions a day after parliament threw out the government's relief package. For Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez, whose coalition has no comfortable majority, the timing is awkward.

A defeat and an eviction

The immediate trigger was personal rather than legislative. On 23 September a pensioner was forcibly expelled from her Madrid flat. The case became a symbol of the squeeze on tenants, and protests built over more than a week. The Tenants' Union, which has coordinated the demonstrations, called rallies in more than 50 cities for Saturday.

The government had a legislative answer ready. Its package would have shielded vulnerable tenants from eviction until 2030. It also included curbs on property purchases by investment funds and tax breaks for landlords who cut rents. On Friday parliament rejected it. Opponents argued it would shrink the supply of rental housing, which is the standard objection to tenant-protection measures.

The defeat matters as much as the bill. The coalition can pass housing measures only with support from outside its own benches, and that support was not there when it counted.

Counting the crowds

Turnout figures differ sharply, as they usually do in Spain. In Madrid, government officials put attendance at no less than 70,000. Another account said 150,000. The Tenants' Union claimed about 500,000. The range is wide enough that it is safest to say only that this was a large national mobilisation. Reports from Valencia said police fired rubber bullets and tear gas to disperse demonstrators, some of whom got into the City of Arts and Sciences complex before being removed. Most of the protests were otherwise reported as peaceful.

Saturday's marches also follow earlier ones. Housing rallies drew crowds in Spanish cities in April 2025, which suggests a grievance that has been building for a long time. Rents and purchase prices have risen faster than wages in many cities, and younger Spaniards in particular describe independent living as out of reach.

Why the markets may care

The political risk is the main concern. A coalition that cannot deliver on an issue this visible invites questions about its staying power. If unrest persists, ministers face a choice. They could offer costly concessions, such as subsidies or tax relief, or they could risk further parliamentary defeats on a topic where public anger is running high.

Spain is a eurozone member with a large debt stock, so fiscal credibility matters beyond its borders. Nothing in the reported facts points to an immediate fiscal shock. The exposure is indirect: a weaker government has less room to hold a budget line, and investors in euro-area debt tend to notice when that room narrows. Gilt-market turbulence elsewhere in Europe this week shows how little patience bond buyers currently have for fiscal uncertainty (see our report on the 30-year gilt yield).

What to watch

The next test is whether the government reworks the package into something that can win a majority, or whether it lets the issue run. The Tenants' Union has shown it can fill squares in dozens of cities within days, so further action is likely if no new proposal appears. Any fresh bill, and the vote on it, will show how much room the coalition has left.
