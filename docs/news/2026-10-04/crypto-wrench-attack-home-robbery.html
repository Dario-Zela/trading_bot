Crypto 'wrench attack' on a family at home is the latest in a wave of violent robberies

In one sentence
A hammer attack on a Solihull family for their crypto is the latest wrench attack, and it shows self-custody carries physical-security costs that custody comparisons rarely price.

A businessman was beaten with hammers in his own home in Solihull by masked intruders who threatened to kill his heavily pregnant wife unless he moved hundreds of thousands of pounds of cryptocurrency to them. The couple, who asked to remain anonymous, described the ordeal to the BBC as "horrific". It is the latest case in a category of crime that has acquired its own name.

The attack

According to the BBC's account, the victim — called "James" in its report — was at home when three men forced their way in. The assault reportedly lasted about 45 minutes. His wife was pinned to a sofa and a pillow was held over her face. One of the attackers is said to have directed the others over a video call. James transferred the funds under duress, a sum the BBC puts at hundreds of thousands of pounds.

The method matters as much as the violence. Nothing was forced open except the front door: the thieves needed the victim's cooperation, because a crypto balance held in a private wallet cannot be taken without the keys or the holder's consent.

A named category

The crime is known as a "wrench attack", after the idea that the cheapest way past strong cryptography is a physical threat to the person holding the key. The BBC cites Chainalysis research recording about $30m (£22.6m) stolen in violent robberies of this kind through June 2026, and says 2026 could become the worst year on record. The figure covers only what has been tracked on-chain and attributed. Many victims do not report, so it is likely a floor rather than a total.

Why it reaches portfolios

Self-custody has been sold on removing counterparty risk: no exchange to fail, freeze or be hacked. Wrench attacks show that the risk has not vanished; it has moved from a trading venue to the holder's front door. That has costs which rarely appear in a comparison of custody options:

  • Physical security. Alarms, secure entrances and vetted staff for holders with large balances, and the attention that high-value holders now give to their public profile.
  • Operational design. Multi-signature setups, time-locked withdrawals and wallets that a single person under duress cannot empty are all ways to make coercion pointless.
  • Insurance. Custody and exchange cover is priced against hacking and insolvency. Whether, and at what price, a policy would respond to coercion of an individual at home is a question wealth managers will increasingly be asked.

The interpretive step here is ours rather than the BBC's: if these attacks become routine, the choice between self-custody, an exchange and a regulated cold-storage provider becomes a physical-risk decision as well as a tax and counterparty one. A regulated custodian carries its own failure risks, but it does not put a family in the same room as the keys.

What to watch

Chainalysis's full-year figures, due after the year closes, will show whether 2026 does set a record. Police data on how many attacks are reported in the UK, and whether insurers begin to offer explicit coercion cover, would show whether this is a cluster of cases or a cost the industry has to price.
