Wealthy Cuban Americans position for Havana's possible fall

In one sentence
Cuban-American businesspeople in Miami are drawing up multibillion-dollar plans for a post-regime Cuba, but the reporting shows intent and estimates rather than committed capital.

A group of wealthy Cuban Americans is drawing up business plans for an island whose government has not yet changed. They are doing it in the open, over dinner, in Miami. The Cuban-American National Chamber of Commerce (CANCC) was founded in April 2026. It has since held four meetings, the latest at a waterfront mansion overlooking Biscayne Bay, according to a BBC report by Will Grant, as relayed by CiberCuba.

The stated aim of its president, the 82-year-old real estate developer Juan Omar Sixto, is to be in Cuba "once the regime falls". Sixto says the intention is "to gather under one roof the most prominent Cuban-American businesspeople".

What is actually on the table

The figures are large, but they are plans and estimates. They are not committed capital.

  • An $8bn project to renovate eight Cuban airports, led by the 75-year-old architect Willy Bermello.
  • A proposal to create a Cuban stock exchange.
  • Working committees on energy infrastructure and food security.
  • A transition-funding estimate of $90bn for the first three years, within a total reconstruction bill put at $275bn–$375bn over 10–15 years.

The group has not published fund names, pledged amounts or purchase prices. The brief's premise, that money is quietly buying land and property, rests on stated intent rather than disclosed transactions. Nothing reported so far shows a closed deal in agricultural land, real estate or tourism assets, and no valuation benchmarks exist for a market that is closed to US investors.

The obstacles

Property is the hardest problem. Sixto told the BBC his father lost five properties and a farm in Pinar del Río after 1959. "I want restitution or compensation," he said. Claims like his compete with the claims of those who stayed and with any state that wants to hold on to land. The Cuban government has told exiles that "the doors are open" to invest, according to Al Jazeera in March. That is a different proposition from investing in a post-transition Cuba.

The legal exposure is also growing. Secretary of State Marco Rubio has announced direct sanctions on GAESA, the military conglomerate that controls an estimated 40–70% of Cuba's formal economy. An investor who buys the wrong asset today could end up dealing with a sanctioned entity. The economy itself is in poor shape. A US oil blockade and sanctions have brought long blackouts and shortages of fuel, food and medicine.

The timeline is the other weak point. Hugo Orizondo, an agricultural expert at the meeting, described the island as "like a black hole; we don't know what's happening". Mike Bustamante, a University of Miami historian, said expectations of an immediate transition have been "deferred".

Signal or noise

This is a diaspora lobbying and networking exercise with a capital-raising purpose. It is not yet a capital flow. It does show that prominent exiles now treat a transition as a scenario to plan around. That is a change in tone from past decades. It is not evidence of any new signal from Washington on sanctions relief, and the sanctions direction in the reporting is toward tightening.

The things to watch are concrete ones: whether the CANCC names funds or discloses commitments, whether the US Treasury issues licences or guidance that would make any investment lawful, and whether Havana makes any move on property restitution. Until one of those happens, the exile investors are positioned for an outcome that no one can date.
