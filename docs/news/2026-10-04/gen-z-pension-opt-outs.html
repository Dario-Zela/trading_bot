Cost-of-living pressure is pushing Gen Z out of workplace pensions

In one sentence
More young UK workers are opting out of workplace pensions to cope with living costs, trimming future inflows to asset managers and raising the eventual state pension burden.

A growing share of Britain's youngest workers are declining to join, or are leaving, the workplace pension schemes they are enrolled in automatically. The reason most often given is the cost of living. The trend is small beside the £ trillions already in UK pension pots, but it falls on the cohort that depends most on decades of compounding.

The numbers

The figures come from several surveys and a government series, and they measure different things. They agree on the direction.

  • In the three months to December 2025, 11.5% of eligible 22-to-29-year-olds who had recently started a job opted out. That compares with 6.6% in the same period of 2020, as reported in coverage of the BBC's analysis.
  • Separate survey work puts opt-outs among 18-to-24-year-olds at about 17%, the highest of any age group.
  • Industry research cited in the same coverage says 16.5% of twenty-somethings have cut their contributions, more than any other generation. It also says 38% of young workers have considered pausing payments, and that 42% are on track for retirement poverty.

These come from secondary reports of surveys, not from a single audited dataset. The opt-out series is the firmest of them.

One worker quoted by the BBC put the trade-off plainly: leaving "could cost me £10k but I need the money now".

Why the arithmetic is unforgiving

Automatic enrolment was designed to make saving the default and to rely on inertia. It requires a minimum 8% total contribution on qualifying earnings, of which employers pay at least 3%. An opt-out therefore forfeits the employer's money as well as the worker's own. It also forfeits tax relief.

The cost compounds with time. Industry estimates suggest that delaying saving from 22 to 27 could cost around £40,000 in retirement funds. That is an illustration, not a forecast, since it depends on assumed returns and wages. But the mechanism is not in dispute. Early contributions have the longest to grow, so a few years of missed saving costs more than the same years missed later.

What it means for asset managers

The direct hit to UK asset managers is modest for now. Young workers' salaries are low, so the missed contributions are small in absolute terms. Pension inflows are also dominated by older, higher-earning savers. Managers' revenue from workplace schemes comes mostly from a basis-point charge on assets, so the exposure grows slowly.

The longer-run effect is on the pipeline. Defined-contribution assets rely on a steady stream of new contributors to keep growing in the years when market returns are less generous. A decade-long equity rally has flattered balances. A cohort that saves less will build smaller pots from the same returns. That weakens the growth of assets under management that providers have assumed. I could not verify a reliable estimate of the AUM lost to Gen Z non-participation, and none is offered here.

The larger exposure may be fiscal. Savers with thin private pensions will lean harder on the state pension and on means-tested support. That is a cost for the Treasury decades from now, not a revenue problem for fund managers today.

What to watch

The next data points are the Department for Work and Pensions' opt-out figures and the Pensions Commission-style adequacy reviews the government has signalled. Policy options already under discussion include extending auto-enrolment to under-22s and lowering the earnings trigger. Any move on contribution levels would have to weigh the squeeze on take-home pay that is driving the opt-outs in the first place.
