Leak suggests Tory spending cuts go beyond the £47bn pledged, as Badenoch offers childcare to high earners

In one sentence
A leak suggests Conservative spending cuts exceed the published £47bn, as Badenoch pledges childcare for high earners, handing Labour an attack line and giving gilt investors a fiscal signal.

The Conservative Party has told voters it would cut £47bn from public spending. A leak now suggests the figure is a floor rather than a ceiling. Reported comments from shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith point to savings "in every part of government" beyond the published total. The same weekend, Kemi Badenoch pledged to extend free childcare to families earning over £100,000. Together the two stories show a party promising a costly new benefit while privately sketching a bigger squeeze than it has put its name to.

For gilt investors the politics is secondary. The question is whether a plausible alternative government is pencilling in a faster path to lower borrowing. If it is, that shifts expectations for gilt supply and growth.

What the leak says

The leaked remarks, reported by the Guardian and syndicated by several outlets, have Griffith discussing further savings across Whitehall once the £47bn is delivered. The recordings and the wording have not been independently verified here, and the party has not published a costed plan covering the extra cuts. No second headline figure has emerged. What is known is that the public number is described internally as a starting point.

The discrepancy matters because the £47bn is the figure against which every Conservative fiscal claim will be scored. A larger unstated total means the stated one understates the intended consolidation. It also hands Labour an easy line: that the opposition is hiding the scale of its cuts.

The childcare pledge

Under current rules, parents lose eligibility for free and tax-free childcare support once either earns more than £100,000. That creates a cliff edge, where a marginal pound of income can cost thousands in lost help. Badenoch's proposal would remove the ceiling, so any parent working 16 or more hours a week could claim.

The Conservatives put the cost at about £700m a year. Party officials say it would be funded by cutting jobs at arm's-length bodies such as Arts Council England and the Environment Agency. The sum is small against a £47bn headline, and small against the gilt market's concerns. But it sets the tone. A pledge that benefits higher earners, paid for by cuts elsewhere, sits awkwardly beside a leak describing deeper cuts than disclosed. Labour's counter is predictable: the party that would spend on high earners would cut everywhere else.

What it means for gilts

The backdrop is a market already unsettled by fiscal risk. The 30-year gilt yield has just topped 6% for the first time since 1998 (see our coverage of the selloff). Long-dated yields have been rising partly on concern about borrowing and supply. In that setting, a signal that one party would cut harder is a small but real input into deficit paths.

The mechanism is straightforward. Deeper cuts imply lower future issuance, which is supportive for prices. They also imply weaker nominal growth and a larger drag on activity, which cuts the other way for the economy but, for duration, usually helps. Against that, the opposition is not in government and the pledge is not policy. Traders will weigh credibility more than the leak itself. A party that publishes a £47bn plan, then is overheard describing more, invites doubt about every number it issues. Credibility discounts on fiscal rules were the lesson of 2022.

The practical read is that the leak changes little for the next Budget path, which is set by the current government's forecasts, and more for the political risk premium attached to the next election. The Bank of England's governor has separately flagged valuation risk in AI-linked assets (see the warning), a reminder that gilt investors are juggling several uncertainties at once.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this matters. First, whether Griffith or the Treasury team publish a costed plan for the additional savings, or disown the remarks. Second, whether the Office for Budget Responsibility is asked to scrutinise the £700m childcare costing and the arm's-length-body savings funding it. Third, the next gilt auction and the next set of public-finance figures, which will show whether long-end demand is holding at current yields.
