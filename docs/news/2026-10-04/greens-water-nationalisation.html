Greens would nationalise Thames and South East Water without compensation

In one sentence
The Greens' pledge to take Thames and South East Water into public ownership with no payout to shareholders or creditors is not policy yet, but it sets a harsh reference point for water-sector investors.

The Green Party has said it would take Thames Water and South East Water into public ownership before Christmas and pay nothing to shareholders or creditors. The pledge came from deputy leader Rachel Millward at the party's annual conference. The Greens are not in government, so nothing changes legally. The claim they are testing is a different one: that insolvent water companies have no equity value and no entitlement to a bailout.

The pledge

As reported by Left Foot Forward, Millward said the party would act immediately rather than over a decade. Her argument was that compensation should be dropped because these are "insolvent companies". She also said the two firms are failing to deliver their statutory duties, and that the failure is clear enough for the Greens to be confident about putting them into special administration.

The government's stated position is the opposite. It says it is not looking at nationalisation, because the cost would run to more than £100bn of public money. The Greens' answer is that the cost to the taxpayer would be nil.

One correction to the framing: neither company is a listed stock. Thames Water, which serves roughly 16 million customers, and South East Water are privately held, with their exposure carried largely by private shareholders and by creditors holding the debt. The direct market footprint is therefore in bonds and in the owners' balance sheets, not in a share price.

Why creditors are the audience

The distinctive feature of the pledge is that it covers creditors as well as equity holders. Special administration is an existing legal route for a failing water company. In that route, the usual question is how much of the debt survives. A stated policy of zero compensation for creditors moves the reference point for that question, even from opposition.

The effect is probably small for now. A party that is not in office cannot impose terms. Pricing in a regulated sector does, however, depend on how credible the regulatory and political settlement looks. A no-compensation marker from a party that is gaining attention adds a tail risk to the sector's valuations, and that tail applies to the other water groups as well as to these two.

What to watch

The test is whether the other parties move. A shift in the government's language on special administration, or on how creditors would be treated in one, would matter more than the Greens' arithmetic. Ofwat's decisions on Thames Water's recapitalisation and on the sector's price controls are the next hard data on how much of the loss falls on investors.
