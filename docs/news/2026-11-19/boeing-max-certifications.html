Boeing's 737 MAX certifications could speed its recovery

In one sentence
Boeing's MAX recovery now hinges on FAA approval of the MAX 7 and MAX 10 and on the production cap, which would lift deliveries and cash flow, but no new date has been given.

Boeing's 737 MAX spent 21 months grounded, and the FAA cleared it to fly again on 18 November 2020, six years ago this week. The aircraft is no longer the problem it was then. What holds back Boeing's recovery now is narrower and more bureaucratic: whether regulators let it certify the two variants it has been waiting on, the MAX 7 and MAX 10, and whether they let it build more aircraft each month. An approval would be a production and cash-flow story, not a safety one.

From grounding to bottleneck

The MAX was grounded worldwide in March 2019, after the Lion Air crash in October 2018 and the Ethiopian Airlines crash in March 2019. The FAA lifted its grounding on 18 November 2020. Deliveries resumed, and the large MAX 8 and the MAX 9 became the workhorses of the programme.

The second shock came in January 2024, when a door plug blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 in flight. The FAA responded by capping Boeing's 737 output at 38 aircraft a month. The triage notes for this piece date the cap to October 2024; the regulator's restriction followed the January incident, and readers should treat the exact date of any later change to it as something to confirm against the FAA's own statements.

The cap matters because Boeing earns most of its cash on delivery. Airlines pay the bulk of an aircraft's price when they take it, so every jet held back by a production ceiling or a missing certificate is cash that stays off the balance sheet.

Why the variants matter

The MAX 7 and MAX 10 sit at the two ends of the family: the smaller one suited to thinner routes, the larger one a rival to the Airbus A321neo. Both have been waiting on certification, and airlines have ordered them in volume. Until they are approved, Boeing cannot deliver them, and customers that planned fleets around them have had to keep older aircraft flying or lean on the variants that are available.

There are three channels through which an approval would show up:

  • Deliveries. Undelivered MAX 7 and MAX 10 orders could convert to cash once certificates arrive.
  • Production mix. A wider range of certified models gives Boeing more freedom in what it builds and sells in a given month.
  • Airline planning. Carriers could firm up fleet and route decisions that have been left open.

The cap works the other way. Easing it lifts the ceiling on every variant at once, which is why analysts tend to watch it as closely as any single approval.

What is not known

The source for this story, a Zacks note, gives no new certification date, and none of the facts above amounts to a regulatory decision. The case for a faster recovery is conditional: it depends on the FAA acting, and the agency has been slower and more cautious with Boeing since the door-plug incident. Equity investors have a habit of pricing approvals before they arrive, so the risk runs both ways: a delay disappoints, but an approval may already be partly in the share price.

What to watch is specific. The first marker is any FAA announcement on MAX 7 or MAX 10 certification. The second is any change to the monthly production ceiling. The third is Boeing's next quarterly report, where delivery counts and free cash flow will show whether the constraint is loosening in practice rather than on paper.
