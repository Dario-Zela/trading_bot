Weight-loss drugs reach nearly one in 10 British homes, and are changing what people buy

In one sentence
A study says weight-loss drugs reach nearly one in 10 British homes, but panel data show 6.3% of households, and shoppers' habits are shifting either way.

Weight-loss injections have moved from the specialist clinic to the weekly shop. A study reported by the Guardian and Just Drinks says GLP-1 drugs, the class that includes Novo Nordisk's Wegovy and Eli Lilly's Mounjaro, are now present in nearly one in 10 homes in Great Britain. If that is right, they are less a niche medical intervention than a mass-market habit, with consequences for what households put in the trolley.

The headline figure needs care, though. The two studies that can be checked do not agree with it.

What the numbers say, and what they do not

The full text of the new study was not available when this was written, so its method and sample could not be confirmed. Two published sources use similar language, and they measure different things.

The first is Worldpanel by Numerator, a consumer-panel firm. Its data, drawn from more than 11,500 households, put the share of Great Britain households with at least one current GLP-1 user at 6.3% (Grocery Gazette, 11 June 2026). That is up from 4.1% in 2025 and 2.3% in 2024. It is roughly one household in 16, not one in 10, though the trajectory is steep: the share has nearly tripled in two years.

The second is an academic study by researchers at University College London, published in BMC Medicine. It estimated that 4.9 million adults in Great Britain, nearly one in 10, had recently used a weight-loss GLP-1 or were interested in starting one. That combines two groups: about 1.6 million who had used one in the past year and about 3.3 million who were merely interested. The survey was fielded in early 2025. Among those using the drugs for weight loss, 15% were using products not licensed for the purpose, and the authors noted that the survey did not record BMI or health conditions.

So "nearly one in 10" can describe adults who use or want the drugs, or, if the new study is a fresh household count, a later and higher reading than Worldpanel's 6.3%. Whether it reflects actual use, intended use or a newer household measure is the first thing to establish. Until then the figure is best read as a direction of travel, not a precise count.

The shopping basket

The demand-side evidence is firmer than the prevalence figures. Worldpanel found that GLP-1 households spent about £780m less on groceries over its analysis period, buying 299m fewer packs, a gap of £418 per household against non-user households. Chocolate confectionery spending fell 18% more among users than among non-users since they started treatment. Three-quarters of users said they were cutting back on chocolate and 72% on crisps. Mouthwash and chewing gum rose, which the firm links to side effects such as dry mouth. More than half of users reported fewer cravings and less "food noise".

The pattern is similar in the Americas. Just Drinks reported a survey claim that GLP-1s shape the diets of about one in five adults there. The brief gives no sample, fieldwork dates or methodology for that claim, and it could not be traced to a primary source. A separate survey of 1,955 people by Oklahoma State University researchers, a different study, found current users consumed fewer calories and were most likely to cut processed foods, sugary drinks, refined grains and beef. Fruit, leafy greens and water rose. Such figures are self-reported, and the headline shares in the American numbers vary widely depending on whether they count households, lifetime use or current use.

Implications for the drugmakers and food groups

For Novo Nordisk and Lilly, wide use is a revenue signal, but a qualified one. Worldpanel reported that four in 10 people who stopped treatment in 2026 said it was too expensive, and much UK use is privately paid for. Prevalence that depends on out-of-pocket spending is vulnerable to price changes, and to any shift in NHS supply.

For food and drink makers, the evidence points to reallocation more than collapse. Snacks, confectionery and sugary drinks bear the cut; water, fruit and some oral-care and gum products gain. Even at 6.3% of households, a spending gap of £418 each is material for categories with thin margins. It also means that a headline share nearer 10% would imply a considerably larger effect.

What to watch next: the study's own methodology, which should show whether it counts use or interest and which households it samples; the next Worldpanel update, which will test whether the 6.3% share is still climbing; and the drugmakers' next quarterly results, where UK volumes and pricing will show whether private demand is holding.
