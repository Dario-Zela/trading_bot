Leasehold to be scrapped in England and Wales, with ground rent capped from 2028

In one sentence
Ministers say they will end 'feudal' leasehold in England and Wales, with ground rent capped from 2028, though the bill is not yet law and the cap figure needs confirming.

The government has confirmed, again, that it intends to end what it calls the "feudal" leasehold system in England and Wales. The two facts that matter most to the five million or so leaseholders affected are when the changes start and what ground rent will cost until they do. The date is 2028. The figure is less settled than the headline suggests, and the reform is also less complete than it sounds.

The evidence available for this article is the published record up to now, not a fresh statement. The most recent detailed text is the draft Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill, published on 27 January 2026 for pre-legislative scrutiny. Triage notes suggested that the cap announced this week differs from the £250 previously proposed. That could not be confirmed against a government statement, and nothing found shows a different figure. Until the statement is checked, £250 a year is the only cap on the public record.

What the draft bill does

Leasehold lets a person own a home, usually a flat, for a fixed term while the land beneath it belongs to someone else, the freeholder. The leaseholder typically pays ground rent and service charges, and needs permission for changes. Ministers present the system as a relic of land tenure, and the "feudal" label is theirs rather than a neutral description. It echoes the manifesto commitment to bring the "feudal leasehold system to an end".

The draft bill, as reported by law firms and trade press, would do several things:

  • cap ground rent on existing residential leases at £250 a year, then reduce it to a peppercorn, effectively zero, after 40 years;
  • ban the sale of new leasehold flats, with commonhold, in which owners collectively own the building, as the default;
  • abolish forfeiture, the power of a landlord to end a lease over unpaid sums, and replace it with a new enforcement regime.

The Leasehold and Freehold Reform Act 2024, passed under the previous government, did not include a ground rent cap. The cap is the main new element.

The 2028 date, and what it does not mean

The cap is expected to take effect in late 2028, subject to Parliament passing the bill. The draft gives no firm implementation timetable for the wider provisions. One campaigner has argued that a start at the end of 2028 sits uncomfortably close to the next general election, which leaves room for delay or reversal.

The phrase "ending leasehold" also needs care. Existing leases do not disappear. Phasing means millions of homes stay leasehold for years, and the practical route out is conversion to commonhold or lease extension. The headline describes a direction of travel more than an event.

Who gains and who pays

For leaseholders on rents that double every 10 or 25 years, a cap is worth real money, and it removes a feature that has made some homes hard to mortgage. Campaign groups have welcomed the £250 proposal.

Freeholders and investors take the other side. The Residential Freeholders Association has called the cap a wholly unjustified interference with existing property rights. Ground rent income is bought and sold as an asset class, so any cap on existing leases reprices those portfolios. Lenders and valuers will have to reassess properties where onerous ground rent has depressed prices. Legal analysts have noted that a cap applied to existing contracts invites challenge on property-rights grounds.

What to watch

The next test is parliamentary. The draft goes to committee scrutiny, and a bill must then be introduced and pass both Houses before 2028 means anything in law. The government statement itself should settle whether the cap figure has moved from £250. The later detail of commonhold conversion rules and any compensation for freeholders will show how much of the "end of leasehold" arrives, and how fast.
