UK firms owed thousands as Saudi-backed LIV paid golfers millions

In one sentence
Suppliers to the once Saudi-funded LIV Golf say they are unpaid even as executives received multimillion-dollar payouts, and the league is now in Chapter 11.

A breakaway golf league bankrolled by Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund has spent the year paying its stars and senior executives large sums while outside suppliers chased it for invoices. The suppliers' complaints began as a trickle of small claims and have become lawsuits and, since September, a US bankruptcy filing. The BBC Sport report behind this piece could not be opened for this article, so its detail on UK firms is not reproduced here. Everything below comes from US court filings and the golf trade press.

What suppliers say they are owed

LIV Golf launched in 2022 and was financed by the Public Investment Fund (PIF), which in April 2026 said it would stop funding the league. By mid-August, Front Office Sports reported that outside contractors, as opposed to full-time staff, had not been paid for work done earlier in the year. The work covered video production, data collection, merchandising and food and beverage. Individual claims ran from a few thousand dollars to almost $100,000. Some contractors said they had waited months and that legal demand letters went unanswered. LIV declined to comment at the time.

The larger claims have reached court:

  • Fresh Tape Media, which produced the league's January pre-season media event, is seeking $1.23m.
  • Mobii Systems, which supplied the "Any Shot, Any Time" streaming feature, claims about $1.1m. It sued in July and says LIV paid all of its 2025 invoices.
  • Fantasy Interactive, which built and ran the league's app and website, claims $992,870.75.
  • Deltatre, the sports technology provider, has sued in New York for roughly $935,000 for technical infrastructure, according to one report.

None of the sources reviewed names a UK-based supplier. Mobii is Canadian, and the one London firm in the story, BC Partners, is a potential backer, not a creditor. Whether British small businesses are among the owed is the central question, and it can only be settled from the BBC's reporting or the creditor lists.

Where the money went

The contrast the story rests on is in the filings. Court documents reported by GolfMagic show that former commissioner Greg Norman received more than $9.4m in September 2025, including a settlement of about $5.8m and a $3.6m bonus. His successor, Scott O'Neil, received more than $13.6m in pay and benefits between September 2025 and August 2026. That included a $6.375m retention bonus on 24 June. About $15.6m in retention bonuses went to several senior executives.

O'Neil said in August the league was "doing everything we can to make sure we can do right by them and the work they committed." Those payments went to executives, not players, so they do not show what the stars are paid. GolfMagic has separately reported that LIV owes millions to its biggest names, including Jon Rahm and Bryson DeChambeau, but those figures were not checked here. The point stands without them: money was available for retention bonuses while invoices went unpaid.

Bankruptcy and what suppliers can expect

LIV and its affiliates filed for Chapter 11 protection on 8 September. The filing lists assets of $100m–$500m against liabilities of $500m–$1bn, and the league laid off more than 90% of its staff. Under Chapter 11, suppliers are generally unsecured creditors. That puts them behind secured lenders and means they usually recover only part of what they are owed, and slowly. BBC Sport has reported 27 creditors among the 30 largest unsecured claims, with stated claims of $64.2m. That figure was not independently confirmed.

The proposed rescue is a "LIV 2.0" backed by BC Partners, with up to $300m of funding, including an initial $4m loan. The wider money depends on enough star players staying. The player commitment deadline was pushed from 13 October to 25 October. Under the proposal players could eventually gain majority ownership.

What to watch

The test of whether this is a pattern rather than one dispute is the creditor matrix in the bankruptcy docket. It would show how many suppliers are owed, where they are based and how much each is due. A reorganisation plan that pays suppliers cents on the dollar while the rescue keeps the stars would settle the question of who ranks where. For small UK firms that work with sports events, the lesson is already practical: ask for payment upfront or in stages when the client's funder has stepped back.
