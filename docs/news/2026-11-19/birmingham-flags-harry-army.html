Birmingham wins interim flag injunction; Prince Harry says he would rejoin the army

In one sentence
Birmingham council has won an interim injunction over flags on lamp-posts, a pre-trial holding order that leaves the merits, and any wider test for councils, undecided.

Birmingham City Council has been granted an interim injunction in its legal fight over union flags attached to lamp-posts. The order is a holding measure, not a verdict. It settles what may happen on the city's streets until a full hearing, and it does not decide whether the flags, or the council's powers over them, are lawful in the end.

The detail available is thin. The court's reasoning, the exact terms and the date of the order were not available for checking, and the account here rests on triage notes sourced to the BBC and the Guardian. The background is better documented.

What the council asked for

The council applied to the High Court in late August for an interim injunction and a power of arrest. The aim was to stop flags and other items being attached to lamp-posts and other highway infrastructure without permission. The defendants named in reports include Ryan Bridge of the Raise the Colours campaign, six others, and "Persons Unknown". A first hearing was listed for 8 September, and one report said a judge adjourned it.

Bridge contested the application. He argued that an injunction would infringe free expression and the right to protest under the European Convention on Human Rights, and asked for the claim to be dismissed as "Totally Without Merit". A report of the hearing carried the defence's warning of a "chilling effect" on rights.

The council has said the application "is not based on the nature of type of flags raised by the defendants". Its case is about street furniture and unauthorised attachments, not about which flag is flown. Reform UK, which holds 22 seats on the council, opposed the move. The case has become a political flashpoint, but the framing as a culture-war fight is the editors' shorthand, and the legal argument is narrower.

Why an interim order is not a precedent

An interim injunction is granted before trial, usually on the basis that the applicant has a serious case and that the balance of convenience favours holding the position until the merits are decided. It does not amount to a finding that the flags are unlawful, or that the council's rights over its lamp-posts defeat the defendants' free-expression arguments. Those questions belong to the final hearing, if the case reaches one.

The council is also not the first to try this route. Oxfordshire County Council secured a High Court injunction earlier this year against unauthorised flags on highway infrastructure across that county. Whether the Birmingham ruling sets a wider test for how far councils can police street furniture will depend on the written reasoning, which was not available at the time of writing.

Prince Harry and the army

A separate story ran alongside this one in the day's headlines. The BBC reported that Prince Harry said he would rejoin the army, linking the remark to a warning about the threat from Russia. A search turned up no direct quote, date or context for the remark, so it cannot be described in more detail here. It is a hypothetical from a former serving officer, not a change in defence policy. He has said before that he lost part of his "purpose and identity" on leaving the army, and he has visited Ukraine three times since Russia's full-scale invasion. The remark would matter to the European readiness debate only if it moved officials, and nothing seen so far suggests it has.

What to watch

The next marker in the Birmingham case is the full judgment, or a date for a final hearing. Those will show whether the court treated the council's highways powers as strong enough to override the protest arguments, and whether other councils can cite the order.
