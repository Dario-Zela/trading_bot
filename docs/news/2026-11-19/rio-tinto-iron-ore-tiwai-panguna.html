Rio Tinto warns on iron ore, restarts Tiwai Point capacity and pledges $250m for Panguna

In one sentence
Rio Tinto will restart Tiwai Point's Line 4 under a 50MW Contact Energy deal and put US$250m into a Panguna legacy fund, as iron ore's share of its earnings shrinks.

Rio Tinto said this week it will restart an idle aluminium potline in southern New Zealand. It also agreed to put US$250m into a fund for the legacy of its abandoned Panguna copper mine in Bougainville. The two announcements came a day apart and have little in common operationally. Taken together, they show a company with a large iron ore business tending to its other obligations and its other metals.

One caveat on the framing. Aggregator summaries said Rio also flagged price pressure in iron ore in the same update. Rio's own releases on Tiwai Point and Panguna, which are the primary documents for both announcements, say nothing about iron ore. No Rio statement with specific price levels or guidance language turned up. The iron ore softness described below comes from third-party reporting and analyst estimates, not from a Rio warning that could be quoted here.

The restart

On 17 November Rio Tinto said it had signed an electricity agreement with Contact Energy for 50MW of power, predominantly renewable, to bring Line 4 at the Tiwai Point smelter back into production. The line has been idle since April 2020. The deal is a bridging arrangement of three and a half years.

The terms, as Rio set them out:

  • Timing: restart is expected to begin in October 2027, with full production by December 2027.
  • Output: about 30,000 tonnes a year of additional aluminium, taking the smelter back to roughly 360,000 tonnes a year.
  • Revenue: around NZ$100m a year in extra export revenue at current spot aluminium prices, according to Rio.
  • Winter: supply drops to 25MW in the winter months (the second and third quarters), which Rio says protects New Zealand's energy security.
  • Longer term: Rio and Contact are negotiating a power purchase agreement that could keep Line 4 running for at least two more decades.

Rio did not disclose the capital cost of the restart or any jobs figure. Michelle Henderson, general manager of the New Zealand Aluminium Smelters, called it "great news for the Southland and wider New Zealand economies". Armando Torres, who runs Rio's Pacific aluminium operations, said demand for low-carbon aluminium was at a high.

The counterparty matters. Meridian Energy remains the main supplier under the wider 572MW arrangement that runs to 2030, and an earlier 50MW Line 4 contract with Meridian dates from 2018. The new deal is with Contact, so the extra load is covered by a second generator rather than by Meridian alone. Tiwai is New Zealand's only aluminium smelter and one of its largest single electricity users. Adding Line 4 returns a meaningful block of demand to the grid, and the winter step-down to 25MW shows how much of the arrangement is shaped by hydro-lake risk. A restart or a closure at Tiwai moves national demand and industrial policy in a way few other sites can.

The softer core

The more interesting question is why Rio is adding aluminium now. Iron ore remains the profit engine: the company's Pilbara operations generated US$6.8bn of EBITDA in the first half of 2026, according to a September analysis. But its share of group earnings is shrinking. Citi's Ephrem Ravi estimated last year that iron ore would account for 48% of Rio's 2026 EBITDA, down from 81% in 2023.

The pricing environment has also been unsettled. In August China's state-backed buyer, China Mineral Resources Group, told some mills to pause purchases of Rio's Pilbara Blend while contract talks continued. Kpler reports that the standoff has reportedly ended with a multiyear contract running to May 2028, though volumes and pricing have not been published and the report has not been confirmed against a primary source. Physical flows were largely unaffected: Rio shipped 83.9 million tonnes of iron ore from Western Australia in the third quarter, up 2% on the year.

So the case for a hedge is plausible, but it rests on a trend and a negotiating dispute. It does not rest on a disclosed warning. A 30,000-tonne line is also small next to Rio's iron ore volumes. At roughly NZ$100m of annual revenue, Line 4 is a rounding error against iron ore's EBITDA. It is better read as a use of power that Tiwai has secured, and a bet on premium low-carbon metal, than as a strategic pivot.

Panguna

The day before the Tiwai announcement, on 16 November, Rio, Bougainville Copper Limited and the Autonomous Bougainville Government agreed with community leaders to set up a fund responding to the Panguna Mine Legacy Impact Assessment. Rio will commit US$250m, about 1.1 billion kina, "over the coming years" as a voluntary contribution.

Panguna was closed in 1989 after landowner opposition over environmental and land damage, which fed into the Bougainville civil war of 1988-1998. The assessment, published in December 2024, followed a complaint by affected communities represented by the Human Rights Law Centre. The fund is meant to cover practical responses in the river and delta areas, plus social programmes across the mine, port and town areas.

The release leaves much open. It says remedy priorities, scope, structure, governance and delivery arrangements are still to be developed with communities, the ABG, BCL and Rio. The release does not say whether the money is staged or paid as a lump sum, nor whether any of it overlaps with provisions Rio already holds. It also separates the fund from decisions on redevelopment of the mine and port, which it says rest with the ABG and the people of Bougainville.

Reaction was cautious. Bougainville's president, Ishmael Toroama, called the fund "an important step forward". Sir Mel Togolo of BCL said "there is still much work ahead". Keren Adams of the Human Rights Law Centre said it "creates a real opportunity to now address an environmental disaster that has remained unresolved for decades".

What ties it together

Both announcements are about the same thing: capital allocation by a miner whose largest business is no longer growing its share of earnings. US$250m for a liability dating from 1989 and a power contract for a 30,000-tonne line are both modest by Rio's standards. They do show where management attention goes when iron ore is a source of negotiation risk rather than upside.

The next data points are specific. Governance of the Panguna fund is the first test of whether US$250m turns into delivery, and community consultations are due to set priorities. At Tiwai, the longer-term power purchase agreement with Contact will decide whether Line 4 is a bridge or a two-decade commitment, and the restart date of October 2027 is the first check on whether the schedule holds. On iron ore, the terms of the CMRG contract, if they emerge, will say more about price pressure than any aggregator summary.
