Outages: hacks, AI or just overdue updates?

In one sentence
Recent big cloud outages mostly trace to providers' own configuration and update errors, not attacks, which makes concentration risk the issue for markets.

Every few weeks, it seems, a large part of the internet stops working. Banks' apps hang, airlines cannot check in passengers, and websites return error pages. The BBC's technology desk has put a question to the pattern: are these outages the work of hackers, the result of AI, or simply the cost of putting off routine maintenance? The question matters because the answer decides what firms should defend against. That includes the exchanges, brokers and trading firms that now run much of their plumbing on a handful of providers.

The BBC's own reporting was not available to check for this piece, so what follows relies on the public record of recent failures rather than on that article's conclusions.

What the record shows

The best-documented recent outages point mostly in one direction. In July 2024, a faulty content update from the security firm CrowdStrike crashed about 8.5 million Windows machines, grounding flights and disrupting hospitals. No attacker was involved. In October 2025, an outage at Amazon Web Services' US-East-1 region was traced to a DNS fault tied to its DynamoDB database service, and it knocked out apps and services around the world. Later that month, Microsoft's Azure Front Door service failed after an inadvertent configuration change. In November 2025, Cloudflare said a database permissions change had caused a file used by its bot-management system to double in size, which led to widespread errors. Cloudflare said at the time that it had found no sign of an attack.

The pattern is consistent. Each of these failures began with a change made by the provider, not with an intruder. The causes were a configuration push, a software update, a database permissions edit. That is ordinary change management failing at very large scale.

Why the three explanations differ

The three candidate causes call for different responses.

Attacks. Hostile action is real, and denial-of-service floods and ransomware do cause outages. But the headline failures above were self-inflicted. Planning only for attackers would miss the more frequent threat.

AI. This is the least documented of the three. Providers increasingly use automation, and in some cases AI tools, to write and ship code. Whether that has measurably raised the failure rate is not established in the public record. Without incident reports that name AI-generated changes as a cause, the claim is a hypothesis.

Deferred maintenance. Ageing systems and tangled dependencies do raise risk. Yet several of the failures above came from new changes, not neglected old ones. The tidier reading is that complexity and concentration are the problem: when a single configuration file or DNS record sits under thousands of businesses, a small slip spreads very far.

The market angle

For trading firms, the concentration matters more than the cause. A broker whose order system, market-data feed and authentication all depend on one cloud region has a single point of failure, whatever triggered it. Regulators in the UK and EU have already moved towards oversight of critical third-party technology providers, partly for this reason. Whether any provider is formally designated as critical is a separate question from whether firms have tested running without it.

The practical test is whether a firm can keep trading, or at least close positions safely, when a provider is down for several hours. That is a question about backups, fallback venues and manual procedures. It is not a question about attackers.

The next data points will be the providers' own post-incident reports, which usually appear within days and state a root cause. If they keep citing internal changes, the evidence will favour the maintenance-and-change-control reading over the attack and AI explanations.
