Tulip Siddiq considering legal action against Sheikh Hasina

In one sentence
Labour MP Tulip Siddiq is reported to be weighing legal action against her aunt, Sheikh Hasina, over corruption allegations, though the details remain unconfirmed.

A British Labour MP is reported to be weighing legal action against her own aunt. Tulip Siddiq, who sits for Hampstead and Kilburn, is said to be considering a case against Sheikh Hasina, the former Bangladeshi prime minister, over corruption allegations that have followed her from Dhaka to Westminster. The details of any claim, including the court, the cause of action and the timing, have not been independently confirmed, and the account here rests on the initial report.

A family at the centre of two countries' politics

Siddiq is Hasina's niece. Hasina led Bangladesh for some 15 years until she was driven from office and fled to India in August 2024 after weeks of student-led protests. Siddiq, a Labour MP since 2015, was a Treasury minister with responsibility for anti-corruption when her family connections drew scrutiny. She resigned from that post in January 2025, saying she did not want to be a distraction, though she denied wrongdoing.

The Bangladeshi authorities have since pursued both women. In November 2025 the country's International Crimes Tribunal sentenced Hasina to death in absentia over the crackdown on the protests. That is a separate matter from the corruption allegations, and Hasina has rejected the tribunal's authority. Later in 2025 a Dhaka court sentenced Siddiq in absentia in a case over the allocation of state-owned plots of land. She has contested that verdict and the process behind it.

Why a legal move would be unusual

The case is awkward in several ways. Siddiq is one of the people the Dhaka prosecutions have targeted, so a legal step aimed at Hasina would put her on the opposite side of an allegation from the one in which she is named. It would also be a notable break within a family whose political fortunes have long been intertwined. The reported intention does not establish what any claim would allege, or who it would be brought against beyond Hasina.

The broader question is how far accusations made in a home-country court can travel. British politicians with diaspora roots are not subject to Bangladeshi judgments, and Dhaka has no power to compel a British MP to appear. Convictions in absentia carry little direct legal force in the UK. Their weight is political and reputational, and that is where much of the pressure on Siddiq has fallen. A civil or criminal step in a British forum would test the opposite direction of travel: whether an MP can use UK courts to answer allegations that originated abroad.

What to watch

The first marker is whether Siddiq or her representatives set out a claim publicly, including the legal basis and the forum. The second is how Labour and the parliamentary authorities respond, given the earlier questions over her ministerial position. A third is whether Dhaka's interim authorities, who are seeking Hasina's return from India, treat any such move as relevant to their own cases. Until a filing or formal statement appears, the story remains a stated intention rather than a legal proceeding.
