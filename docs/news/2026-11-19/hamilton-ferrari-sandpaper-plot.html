Hamilton denies trust problem at Ferrari; cricket revisits the 'sandpaper plot'

In one sentence
Lewis Hamilton says there is no trust problem at Ferrari, while BBC Sport revisits cricket's 2018 Cape Town ball-tampering scandal without changing the established account.

Lewis Hamilton has rejected the suggestion that there is a trust problem between him and Ferrari, answering questions about his relationship with the team in comments reported by BBC Sport. It is the kind of denial drivers deliver routinely when a rough spell invites talk of friction. Nothing in the material available points to a contract dispute, a management row or a specific falling-out. Without one, the story is a driver asked about a rift and saying there isn't one.

A denial, not a development

Hamilton has driven for Ferrari since the 2025 season, when he left Mercedes after 12 years. A move of that size comes with expectations, and when results fall short of them, questions about how driver and team get along tend to follow. Teams and drivers almost always answer in the same way: the relationship is fine, the focus is on the car.

A denial is only as informative as the evidence it answers. The reporting so far does not set out any concrete sign of strain, such as a public disagreement over strategy, a dispute over his future or a change in how the team is run around him. Until something of that kind appears, the useful reading is the plain one. Hamilton was asked, and he said no.

The next real tests are practical ones: whether Ferrari's performance improves, and whether any talk about his future at the team moves from speculation to detail.

Cricket revisits Cape Town

Separately, BBC Sport has returned to the 2018 ball-tampering affair, the so-called sandpaper plot, which remains one of the most damaging episodes in Australian cricket's recent history.

The facts are well established. During the third Test against South Africa in Cape Town in March 2018, Cameron Bancroft was caught on camera using sandpaper on the ball. Cricket Australia banned captain Steve Smith and vice-captain David Warner for 12 months each, and Bancroft for nine. Smith and Warner were also barred from leadership roles, and Smith stepped down as captain.

What keeps the episode alive is the question of who knew what, and why. A retrospective earns its place only if the accounts it gathers change that picture. The material available here does not show that they do, and the established account of the bans and the roles of the three men stands as it did.

The common thread

The two stories have little in common beyond the sport pages. One is a present-tense denial, the other an old affair re-examined. Both turn on the same test: whether anyone offers evidence that changes what was already known. For Hamilton, that would be a sign of real friction at Ferrari. For cricket, it would be an account that shifts the blame or the chain of knowledge in Cape Town. Absent either, both remain on the record as they stood.
