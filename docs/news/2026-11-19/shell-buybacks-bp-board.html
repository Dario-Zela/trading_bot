Shell extends buybacks as BP reshuffles its board

In one sentence
Shell started gas output at Malaysia's Rosmari-Marjoram fields and continued its buyback, while BP began a board rebuild; none of it signals a shift in Shell's capital allocation.

Shell has begun producing gas from the Rosmari-Marjoram fields off Sarawak, Malaysia, while a continued share buyback keeps cash flowing back to its shareholders. Neither item changes how the company allocates capital. Both fit the pattern of a major that funds new supply and shareholder returns at once.

The start-up

Sarawak Shell Berhad, a Shell subsidiary, began producing from the Rosmari and Marjoram fields on 17 November, according to Shell and trade reports. The fields lie about 220 kilometres offshore and feed the PETRONAS LNG Complex in Bintulu. Design capacity is up to 800 million standard cubic feet a day.

Shell holds a 71% operating interest. Its partners are PETRONAS Carigali, INPEX Malaysia and SEARAH (Malaysia). Shell took the final investment decision in September 2022, so first gas arrives a little over four years later. The company says the project supports production growth into the 2030s. The offshore platform is partly solar-powered, with diesel generators as back-up.

For Malaysia, the feed gas matters because it supports LNG output at a complex that exports cargoes. For Shell, 800 million cubic feet a day is meaningful but not large against its overall portfolio, and the project is a continuation of existing Malaysian operations rather than a new market.

The buyback

The buyback is the thinner half of the story. The reports that prompted this piece, from Simply Wall St. and Zacks, say Shell has extended its programme. They do not give the size of the latest tranche, its length or how it compares with earlier ones, and those figures could not be checked against a Shell release. Without them there is no basis for calling the extension a change in policy. Continuing a buyback in tranches is what Shell has done routinely, and the reasonable reading is that this is more of the same.

BP's board

BP, meanwhile, has begun rebuilding its board. It named Andrew Bonfield, the former Caterpillar finance chief, and Stuart Chambers, chair of Anglo American, as independent non-executive directors. Bonfield joins on 1 December and Chambers on 1 January 2027. Dave Hager becomes senior independent director on 1 December, succeeding Dame Amanda Blanc, who leaves the board then. BP says the changes are the first stage of a wider overhaul led by chair Ian Tyler, with a near-term emphasis on oil and gas expertise among the non-executives.

The framing in the aggregator coverage, that the reshuffle is a verdict on BP's returns against European peers such as Eni, is not supported by figures in the material available. The more concrete point is that BP is changing its board after a turbulent year for its leadership, while Shell is presenting continuity.

What to watch

The useful data points are Shell's fourth-quarter results, where the buyback size and any ramp-up profile for Rosmari-Marjoram should appear, and the arrival of BP's new directors on 1 December and 1 January. Until then, the contrast is one of tone, not of numbers.
