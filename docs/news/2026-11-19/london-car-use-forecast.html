Car use in London forecast to rise 10% in the next decade

In one sentence
A reported forecast of 10% more London car use over a decade would undercut congestion and net-zero goals and revive the case for road pricing, though its source and model are unconfirmed.

A forecast that car use in London will rise by about 10% over the next decade, reported by the Guardian, cuts against the direction the capital has set for itself. City Hall's transport strategy aims for most journeys to be made on foot, by bike or on public transport. Its climate plans assume fewer vehicle miles, not more. A rise on that scale would widen the distance between policy and what happens on the roads.

The underlying figures were not available for this article. The forecast's author, model and baseline year have not been independently confirmed, and the 10% figure rests on the Guardian's reporting alone. A search turned up no separate publication of it. That matters because transport forecasts are sensitive to their assumptions about population, housing growth, road pricing and fuel costs. A projection of "car use" could mean vehicle-kilometres, trips or time spent in traffic, and those measures can move in different directions.

Why the number would matter

The stakes are clear even without the detail. London's transport emissions are dominated by road vehicles. Electrification will cut tailpipe carbon, but it does nothing for congestion, and it only partly offsets emissions from tyres, brakes and vehicle manufacture. Earlier analysis reported in 2022 put the reduction in London car traffic needed to meet the city's climate targets at 27% by the end of this decade. A forecast rise runs in the opposite direction from that.

There is a caveat. The strategic modelling behind the draft London Plan projects overall travel demand growing by more than 11%. The same document projects car ownership falling, particularly in inner boroughs where housing growth is concentrated. Growth in total travel and growth in driving are different claims, and which one the new forecast describes is the first thing to establish.

History also gives reason for caution about extrapolation. Department for Transport figures cited in 2022 showed traffic flows in London down roughly 10% over the preceding decade. Credit for that went partly to the congestion charge, the ultra-low emission zone and the low-emission zone, the city's three road-pricing schemes.

Pressure on road pricing

That record is what gives the forecast its political edge. If the policies that restrained traffic are now being outrun by growth in population and in trips, the options narrow. Sadiq Khan has said London will eventually need a new kind of road-user charging system, and City Hall advisers have pointed to distance-based charging as a longer-term step beyond today's zone-based schemes. It is politically fraught. Charging by distance would reach outer-London drivers, who have the fewest alternatives and have fought expansion of the ultra-low emission zone.

The alternatives are not painless either. Holding traffic flat without pricing means more bus priority, more road space reallocated and tighter parking controls, each of which draws its own opposition. Doing nothing means the congestion the forecast implies.

What to watch

The first test is the document itself: once the model, baseline and scenarios are published, it will show whether 10% is a central case or the outcome if current policy stays as it is. The second is the mayor's response, in particular whether City Hall treats it as a reason to bring forward work on distance-based charging. Transport for London's next travel-demand and traffic data will show whether the trend is already visible on the roads.
