Mining giants' big dividends come with a catch

In one sentence
Commodity prices and heavy capital spending have already squeezed payouts at Rio Tinto, Anglo American, Glencore and BHP, and the same pressures could return.

The dividend is the main thing many investors want from a big miner. It is also the line most exposed when prices turn. The sector's recent record shows how that works. Payouts follow the commodity cycle, and they are increasingly competing with the cost of replacing and expanding mines.

The themes are not new, and this piece does not rest on a new trigger. The most recent company-level evidence available is from the 2025 reporting season, which is more than a year old. Figures below are dated to that period and should not be read as current guidance.

Rio Tinto: iron ore and a big replacement bill

Rio Tinto is the clearest case of price and spending pulling in opposite directions. In August 2025 it reported its smallest first-half underlying profit since 2020 and its lowest interim dividend in seven years, as iron ore prices fell and costs rose at its Australian business. It also said it planned to spend more than $13bn over three years replacing depleting iron ore assets in Western Australia.

That is the catch in its simplest form. Iron ore pays for the dividend, and iron ore mines in Western Australia need replacing at great expense. Rio's more recent iron ore warning is covered separately in this edition.

Anglo, Glencore and BHP: three different exposures

Anglo American reported a $1.9bn loss for the first half of 2025 and cut its dividend to the lowest in at least five years, while continuing to shed coal and diamond assets. A payout that depends on a restructuring is hard to forecast.

Glencore has the most explicit formula. It pays a fixed $1bn base plus a variable element equal to at least 25% of the free cash flow of its industrial businesses in the prior year. It held its 2025 dividend flat, at its lowest since 2021. The structure cushions the floor but makes the upper part of the payout follow cash flow directly.

BHP is the more conservative case. Analysts ahead of its 2025 results expected a full-year payout of $1.02 a share, its lowest in eight years. UBS expected a cautious stance because capital expenditure was set to rise materially in 2025-26.

What the pattern says

The common thread is that the largest miners are choosing between shareholder cash and growth capital, and weak prices make the choice sharper. The evidence is the sequence of cuts and flat payouts above, not a forecast. Payout policies expressed as a share of free cash flow, as Glencore's is, will move down with prices almost automatically. Fixed-dollar or progressive policies are less flexible, and are the ones that tend to be cut in a downturn.

The next data points are the full-year results due in early 2027, when each company will set out its payout against its capex guidance. The test will be whether any of them lets capital spending crowd out the dividend again, or holds the dividend and slows the spending.
