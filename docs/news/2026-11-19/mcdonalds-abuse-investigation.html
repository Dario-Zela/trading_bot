McDonald's workers describe being targeted in BBC abuse investigation

In one sentence
A BBC investigation into McDonald's abuse allegations revives the question of whether franchise-run restaurants give young, low-paid staff any reliable way to report mistreatment.

A BBC investigation into abuse at McDonald's has again put workers' accounts of being targeted at the centre of a question the company has faced before. The question is whether a workforce that is young, low-paid and spread across hundreds of separately run restaurants has any dependable way to report mistreatment, and what head office knew before reporters began asking.

The details of the latest BBC report, including how many workers spoke to it, where they worked and over what period, could not be independently confirmed for this article. Nor is it clear how McDonald's responded to the BBC or what disciplinary action followed. The analysis below therefore leans on the public record of earlier investigations and on what the company has committed to since. Where the new accounts add to that record, the text says so only in general terms.

A pattern already on the record

This is not the first time the BBC has examined the subject. In July 2023 it reported that it had spoken to more than 100 current and former McDonald's staff in the UK, some as young as 17. According to coverage of that investigation, 31 of the allegations concerned sexual assault and 78 concerned sexual harassment. Workers also described racism and bullying.

The company's response at the time was an apology. Alistair Macrow, head of McDonald's UK, said there was "simply no place for harassment, abuse, or discrimination" at the company. The BBC's report followed a legally binding agreement McDonald's had signed earlier that year with the Equality and Human Rights Commission (EHRC), the statutory equality regulator, and the EHRC later set up a confidential email line for workers to report harassment.

A January 2025 BBC follow-up found that workers said the problem persisted. Their accounts included inappropriate touching by managers, harassment by customers and verbal abuse by colleagues. Coverage of that report said the EHRC had received 300 reported incidents since the 2023 investigation. The chair of a parliamentary committee called the situation "appalling", and a law firm was pursuing a case alleging that McDonald's failed to protect its staff. Union estimates date the concern back further: in 2019 the Bakers, Food and Allied Workers Union put the number of female McDonald's workers who had experienced sexual harassment at more than 1,000.

The franchise problem

The structural point is that a large share of McDonald's UK restaurants are run by independent franchisees, who are the legal employers of the staff in them. A worker facing abuse from a shift manager is therefore usually reporting to the franchisee, who may also be the manager's employer and sometimes the person who hired them. The brand sets standards and can inspect or sanction, but it does not employ the people at the counter.

That arrangement is common in fast food and is lawful. It does leave an open question about where responsibility sits. If a complaint goes to a franchisee who does nothing, the sensible next step for a 17-year-old on a part-time contract is unclear. The EHRC hotline can be read as a regulator-backed answer to that gap. It is also an implicit admission that the internal routes were not enough.

What the company knew

The governance test is a chronological one. If the workers' latest accounts describe events after the 2023 agreement with the EHRC, the issue is not one of awareness. McDonald's had publicly acknowledged the problem and committed to fix it. The question becomes whether those commitments reached restaurant level.

The legal exposure follows from that. UK employers owe a duty to take reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment, and that duty was strengthened in law in late 2024. A pattern of complaints that went unaddressed after a binding agreement with the regulator is harder to describe as isolated misconduct. It also carries reputational cost for a consumer brand whose workforce skews young.

The caveat is that the governance argument holds only if the reporting is systemic. A handful of accounts, even serious ones, would point to individual failures and to individual franchisees. A spread across many sites and several years would point to something in the model itself.

What to watch

Three things will settle the point. The first is whether McDonald's and the franchisees it works with respond to the BBC's specific claims and what disciplinary outcomes they report. The second is whether the EHRC, which monitors the 2023 agreement, comments on the latest accounts or takes further steps. The third is the outcome of the legal action over alleged failure to protect staff, which would test where a franchisor's duty of care ends.
