Van Gogh painting sells for record $190.4m despite Nazi-era ownership claim

In one sentence
Van Gogh's Châtaigniers en fleurs made a record $190.4m at Sotheby's a day after a deal with the heirs of a Jewish former owner, who claimed a Nazi-era forced sale, cleared the way.

A small canvas of flowering chestnut trees, painted in the last weeks of Vincent van Gogh's life, sold at Sotheby's in New York on Wednesday for $190.4m including fees, the highest price ever paid at auction for one of his works. The sale nearly didn't happen. A day earlier, the heirs of a Jewish publisher had asked for the painting to be pulled, saying their family lost it to Nazi persecution.

One point of framing needs correcting. The claim was not still open when the hammer fell. Sotheby's announced on Tuesday that the consignors and the claimants had reached an agreement, the terms undisclosed, and the auction went ahead on that basis. What the sale tests is less whether the market will sell over an open claim than how a dispute gets closed: privately, in the final hours, with the price still to be set.

The painting

Châtaigniers en fleurs (Chestnut Trees in Blossom) is an oil on canvas, 70 by 58 centimetres, painted at Auvers-sur-Oise in May 1890, about two months before Van Gogh's death. It is the only one of his chestnut-tree pictures of that period in which figures appear. After his death it passed to his brother Theo, and later through collectors including Prince Alexandre Berthier and the songwriter and producer George "Buddy" DeSylva.

In 1909 it was acquired by Samuel Fischer, the German-Jewish publisher. His family kept it after his death in 1934. In 1936 it was sold through an Amsterdam gallery to the French collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer. The Fischer descendants say the family was forced to part with it because of Nazi persecution. The Blaquier-Arrieta family of Argentina bought it in 1975, and it has been in their collection since. Sotheby's says it was not publicly shown for decades until this year's pre-sale tour, which included a stop in Hong Kong where visitors queued to see it.

The settlement

The timing is the story. Estimated at around $180m and backed by a Sotheby's guarantee, the painting was the centrepiece of a roughly $450m Impressionist and modern evening. The Fischer heirs' request that it be withdrawn arrived days before the sale. The agreement between the two families was announced on Tuesday, hours before the auction. Neither side has said what was agreed, though settlements of this kind often involve a division of proceeds or an acknowledgement of the claimants' history.

The pattern is familiar in the restitution world. Sales under duress in the 1930s, particularly of families fleeing Germany, sit in a grey zone. The 1936 sale was a transaction through a dealer, not a seizure, and whether it counts as a loss to persecution is exactly the kind of question that courts and advisory panels have taken years to settle. A negotiated deal can dispose of that question in days, but it also keeps the outcome private. A buyer gets clean title; the public does not get a ruling.

The price

The hammer price was $165m; fees took the total to $190.4m. The previous Van Gogh record was $117.2m, paid for Verger avec cyprès (Orchard with Cypresses) at Christie's in New York in 2022. The new figure is about 62% higher than that and makes the work the fourth most expensive painting ever sold at auction, according to The Art Newspaper. The buyer was not named in the reports.

The record claim holds, though it depends on counting fees, which is how auction houses report prices. On hammer price alone, the $165m is still well above the 2022 sale's total.

What to watch

The open questions are the terms and the buyer. If the settlement's outline emerges, it will show how much a last-minute claim is worth to a consigner with a guaranteed lot. The next test comes with the auction houses' own practice: whether they push claimants and sellers to talk well before a catalogue is printed, rather than on the eve of the sale.
