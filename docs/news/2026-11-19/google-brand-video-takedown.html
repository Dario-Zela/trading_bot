Google rejects UK request to remove Russell Brand YouTube video

In one sentence
Google reportedly refused an MoJ request to remove a YouTube video of Russell Brand's message from prison; the government says removal rests on the platform's own terms.

Google has declined a request from the UK government to take down a YouTube video in which Russell Brand, now serving a prison sentence for rape and sexual assault, says he is not guilty. The refusal was reported by the BBC, citing unnamed sources, and republished by the Independent on 19 November. Google had not commented when approached, and the details below rest on that reporting rather than on any on-record statement from the company.

The episode is narrower than the headline suggests. It is not, on the evidence so far, a ruling under the Online Safety Act. It is a request made to a company that was free to say no, and it said no.

What happened

The video is a message from Brand, who is serving a 10.5-year sentence. His wife, Laura Gallacher, read it aloud on the couple's YouTube podcast on a Sunday. In it he maintains he is innocent.

The Ministry of Justice then formally asked Google, which owns YouTube, to remove the video. According to the BBC's sources, Google concluded that the content had not broken its rules and left it up. The video and the couple's channel remain available.

The government's position was blunt. An MoJ spokesperson said: "we strongly believe YouTube should remove this content." Downing Street called the comments "clearly appalling" and said the matter was being urgently investigated. A Prime Minister's spokesperson said: "Prisoners are strictly banned from making statements that cause distress to victims," and added that "prisoners cannot get around these rules by having someone else publish their comments."

The same MoJ spokesperson also conceded the limit of its own leverage: "Removal ultimately depends on the platform's own terms."

A request, not an order

That last sentence matters most. The Online Safety Act puts duties on platforms to tackle illegal content and to protect children, and gives Ofcom the power to enforce them. It does not give ministers a general power to order the removal of lawful speech by a named person. Nothing in the reporting says the MoJ invoked a legal power, or that the video is illegal. The request appears to have gone through the informal channel the prison service has with technology companies. The BBC also reported a discrepancy: it was first told a minister had met Google, but the MoJ later said no minister had, and that the request went through that established mechanism.

So the legal question is thin. The request rested on a prison-rules argument: prisoners may not make statements that distress victims, and generally may not speak to the media except in exceptional circumstances. Those rules bind the prisoner. They do not bind a third party, or a platform, that publishes his words. The reading of the message prompted an investigation into whether Brand breached them, and the outcome of that has not been reported.

Google's reported reasoning, that its own rules were not broken, is the usual test. YouTube set its own terms and applied them. It has acted against Brand before on those terms: in September 2023 it suspended his ability to earn advertising revenue from his channel after sexual assault allegations, citing its creator responsibility policy. That was demonetisation, not removal, and it came before any conviction. Meta has since disabled his Instagram and Facebook accounts after the conviction, which shows platforms can reach different conclusions on the same person.

What is unverified

Several things are not in the public record. The reporting does not say which YouTube policy Google assessed the video against, when the episode aired, or whether the request carried any threat of regulatory consequence. Google's own account is missing. The BBC's description of Google's reasoning comes from unnamed sources, so it should be treated as probable rather than established.

There is also a precedent worth keeping in view. In 2023, a parliamentary committee asked Rumble to stop Brand earning money on its platform, and Rumble refused, citing a free internet. Brand then claimed the government was pressing platforms to censor him. Platforms turning down official requests about him is not new; what is new is that the person is now a convicted prisoner and the request came from the department that runs prisons.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is a one-off or a test. The first is whether Google confirms its decision and names the policy it applied. The second is the outcome of the prison service investigation, which will say whether the rules were breached and by whom. The third is whether ministers respond by seeking a firmer legal footing, such as Ofcom guidance or new rules on prisoners' speech published by proxy, or by accepting that the decision rests with the platform. The MoJ's own wording suggests it expects the latter.
