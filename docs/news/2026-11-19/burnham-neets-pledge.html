Burnham promises to 'move quickly' on Neets report

In one sentence
Burnham's pledge to 'move quickly' on young people not in education, employment or training has no stated plan, so the test is whether funded action follows the Milburn review.

A new prime minister has said he will "move quickly" on young people who are not in education, employment or training. The pledge, reported by the BBC, is about the group known as NEETs. It came without a timetable, a costing or a named policy, which makes it a statement of intent and not yet a programme.

The subject is not new. Nearly one million people aged 16 to 24 in the UK are classed as NEET, according to recent analysis. Roughly 613,000 of them are described as economically inactive, meaning they are not looking for work at all. That distinction matters. A young person between jobs is a different policy problem from one who has dropped out of the labour market altogether.

What is already on the table

The pledge arrives alongside a review of youth inactivity led by Alan Milburn, whose interim findings have been published and whose final recommendations are still awaited. Commentators such as Reed in Partnership have argued that the current system is fragmented and too reactive, and that government should be ready to act fast once the review concludes. A UCL commentary this month notes that researchers know which young people are most likely to end up NEET in England but not why. It also records Burnham's earlier pledge of a "whole-childhood approach" in response to the review.

This piece could not confirm the exact wording or occasion of the "move quickly" remark beyond the BBC headline. It also could not confirm which report the pledge responds to, or whether any dates or figures came with it. Those are the details that decide whether the line means anything.

Why the money matters

NEET numbers are not only a social concern. Each young person outside work or study is a potential long-term welfare claimant and a missing contributor to labour supply. Employers in sectors short of workers have an obvious interest, and so does the Treasury. Research suggests that a spell out of work or education in the early years can leave lasting scars on later earnings and employment.

That is why speed carries a price. Practical interventions, such as guaranteed placements, expanded apprenticeships, mentoring or reformed benefit conditions, cost money up front and pay back slowly. A government promising quick action must say whether it will fund it from new spending, from existing budgets or from reallocated welfare funds.

The causes are also contested. Youth Futures Foundation and others point to weak recruitment for entry-level jobs and too few routes into training. Others stress health, particularly mental health, as a driver of inactivity. A fast response that targets only one cause could miss much of the problem.

What to watch

The first test is the publication of the Milburn review's final recommendations and the government's formal response. If the response names policies, a budget and dates, the pledge will have become action. If it defers to further consultation, "quickly" will have meant something less. The next fiscal statement will show whether any money has been set aside.
