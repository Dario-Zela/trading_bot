Myanmar faces a genocide verdict at the UN court over the Rohingya massacres

In one sentence
The ICJ is due to rule today on Gambia's genocide case against Myanmar, with its findings on standing and intent likely to shape South Africa's case against Israel.

The International Court of Justice is due to deliver its judgment today in The Gambia v. Myanmar, the first time the UN's principal judicial body has ruled on the merits of a genocide case over the Rohingya. The judgment is final and cannot be appealed. At the time of writing no ruling had been reported, so what follows sets out what the court has to decide and why the outcome will be read well beyond Myanmar.

How the case got here

The Gambia, a small West African state with no border or population connection to the dispute, filed its application in November 2019. It was backed by the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. It argued that Myanmar's military-led campaign against the Rohingya, a Muslim minority in Rakhine state, breached the 1948 Genocide Convention. UN figures put the number driven into Bangladesh by the 2017 clearance operations at more than 700,000. That figure was not re-verified for this article against UN OCHA.

The court has already spoken on the case twice. In January 2020 it ordered Myanmar to take provisional measures to prevent genocidal acts and to preserve evidence. Those orders were interim findings only: the court needed to see only that the rights claimed were plausible, not to decide that genocide had occurred. In July 2022 it rejected Myanmar's preliminary objections, including the argument that The Gambia lacked standing, and let the case proceed. Merits hearings opened in January 2026, and the court announced that month that it had begun deliberating.

Two questions the judges must answer

The first is intent. Genocide under the Convention requires acts such as killing or causing serious harm to be carried out with intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. Proving that a state held that intent is notoriously hard. The court's 2007 judgment in the Bosnia case found a failure to prevent genocide at Srebrenica but did not hold Serbia responsible for committing it. Its 2015 ruling in Croatia v. Serbia set a demanding bar, requiring that genocidal intent be the only reasonable inference from the pattern of conduct. Myanmar has denied any such intent and described its operations as counter-terrorism against militants.

The second is standing. The 2022 ruling accepted that every party to the Convention has an interest in compliance, so a state need not be a victim to sue. The merits judgment will show how far that principle carries when it comes to remedies and findings, and how the court treats evidence gathered largely by others, including UN investigators.

Why Gaza is watching

South Africa brought its own case against Israel in December 2023, also as a state not directly affected, also invoking the Genocide Convention. Israel rejects the allegation and says it acts in self-defence. That case is far earlier in its procedure, and the facts differ. But lawyers on both sides will read The Gambia v. Myanmar for how the court weighs inference of intent from official statements and patterns of operations, and for what it says about the scope of states' obligations to prevent genocide.

A finding against Myanmar would be the first ICJ determination that a state committed genocide since the Convention's drafting, since the earlier Bosnia ruling stopped short of that. A finding that the intent standard was not met would be read as a high bar for the pending cases.

What to watch

The operative paragraphs matter more than the summary. Watch whether the court finds Myanmar responsible for genocidal acts or only for violations of the duty to prevent and punish; what reparations or guarantees of non-repetition it orders; and how Myanmar's ruling military authorities, which have little record of complying with the court, respond. The ICJ has no enforcement power of its own. Any follow-up would pass through the UN Security Council, where Myanmar's allies hold vetoes.
