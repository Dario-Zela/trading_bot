Phil the bar-tailed godwit tracked on 6,800-mile round trip to Siberia

In one sentence
A satellite-tagged bar-tailed godwit named Phil has reportedly flown a 6,800-mile round trip to Siberia, a pleasing anecdote from a species famed for extreme flights.

A bar-tailed godwit called Phil has been followed by satellite on a round trip to Siberia of about 6,800 miles, according to a report in the Guardian. The bird, tagged so that its movements could be traced from orbit, flew out to the breeding grounds and came back. The tale is small and pleasing. The Guardian item is the only source for it. It does not name the researchers or the institution behind the tag, and no dataset accompanies it, so the details here rest on that single account.

A bird built for distance

The species is the reason the story has any sparkle. The bar-tailed godwit is a long-legged, long-billed wader that holds the record for the longest known non-stop migratory flight of any bird. In 2021 a male tagged 4BBRW flew about 8,100 miles (roughly 13,000 km) from Alaska to New South Wales without landing, according to published tracking records.

Against that, Phil's 6,800 miles, out and back, is a modest outing. It does not set any record that I could find. Nor does it say which route he took or how long he spent at either end.

Siberia is the destination of one of the species' subspecies, menzbieri. It breeds there and winters in northern and western Australia. Whether Phil belongs to that population is not clear from the report, and I could not confirm it.

How the tracking works

Satellite tags became small enough to carry on a bird's back, or to be implanted, around 2007. That let scientists follow shorebirds across oceans for the first time. A US Geological Survey study of a fully tracked Alaska-breeding godwit recorded a round trip of 29,280 km over 174 days, with only about 20 of those days spent in major migratory flight. The rest was feeding, resting and breeding.

The individual-bird approach has a practical use. Tracking shows which coastal stopover sites a species depends on, and so which ones are worth protecting. Nothing in the report on Phil suggests it was aimed at that kind of question, or that it adds to it.

What to watch

The obvious next data point is the return leg, or next season's departure, if the tag keeps transmitting. A named researcher or a published track map would also settle the open questions about route and subspecies. Until then Phil stays a charming anecdote: a wader with a name, a transmitter and a very long way to go.
