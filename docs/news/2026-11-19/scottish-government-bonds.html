Gilts v kilts: how Scottish government borrowing really works

In one sentence
Holyrood has no gilt-style bond market: its capped borrowing comes mainly from the UK Treasury, so its costs track gilt yields rather than any investor view of Scotland.

Scottish government debt is not a market in the way UK gilts are. No Holyrood bond trades on any screen, and no investor sets a price for Scottish credit risk. The cost of what the Scottish government borrows is decided mostly by what the UK Treasury charges it, which means that cost follows gilt yields rather than any view of Scotland's finances.

A loan book, not a bond market

Under the Scotland Act 2016 and the fiscal framework agreed alongside it, Holyrood has borrowing powers, but they are tightly bounded. Capital borrowing, for roads, hospitals and the like, is limited to an annual ceiling and a cumulative one, set in the low hundreds of millions of pounds a year and a few billion in total. Resource borrowing, for day-to-day spending, is narrower still. It exists mainly to smooth forecasting errors in tax receipts and to cover specific economic shocks, not to fund a standing deficit.

The limits matter because Scotland's annual budget runs to tens of billions of pounds. Borrowing on this scale could not fund a budget of that size, let alone support a deep and liquid bond market. Most of the money is block grant from Westminster, plus devolved taxes. The borrowing is a margin on top of that.

What borrowing there is has mainly come from the UK National Loans Fund, the Treasury's lending arm. The rate is therefore anchored to the UK government's own cost of funds. The Scottish government pays something close to gilt-linked rates because the lender is the UK government itself. No lender is weighing a Scottish credit spread against the gilt curve.

What 'Scottish bonds' actually means

Talk of Scottish bonds is therefore about possible future issuance, not an existing market. The Scotland Act does allow Holyrood to borrow from other sources, including by issuing bonds, but within the same statutory limits. A small issue would struggle to build a following of investors, because liquidity is what makes a bond market work, and small or infrequent issues tend to carry a premium for being hard to trade.

That is where the independence argument comes in. A sovereign Scotland would have to issue its own debt, and investors would price it on its own merits: its deficit, its oil and gas receipts, its currency arrangements, its track record. The current arrangement cannot show how that would go, because there is no price to observe. What it does show is how much of Holyrood's financing is, for now, a function of Treasury decisions.

The point cuts in two directions. Backers of independence can argue that the present limits say little about what a sovereign Scotland could raise. Sceptics can argue that the absence of any market signal means the claim is untested. Neither side has a Scottish yield to point to.

What to watch

The practical tests are the ones that already exist: how much of its borrowing allowance Holyrood uses in each budget, and whether the limits are revisited in a future review of the fiscal framework. A change to the caps, or a first market issue, would be the first real price signal for Scottish debt. Neither is currently scheduled.
