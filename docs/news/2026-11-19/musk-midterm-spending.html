Musk calls his record $446m midterm spending 'pretty foolish'

In one sentence
Musk says he feels "pretty foolish" about record midterm spending of about $442m–$446m that failed to deliver Republican wins, and insists he is "not Maga".

Elon Musk has described the money he poured into the US midterm elections as a mistake. Speaking to the journalist Andrew Ross Sorkin on Wednesday, the world's richest man said: "I feel pretty foolish about having spent so much money and not seeing much for it." He added: "So probably if I could rewind the clock, I would have spent less money."

A public retreat of this kind is unusual. Major political donors rarely concede that their cash was wasted, and Musk's outlay was a record. The headline figure is reported as $446m, though outlets differ: The Hill and CBS News cite $442m, an Associated Press analysis put it at at least $445m, and ABC News said nearly $500m.

What the money bought

The results were poor for his side. According to the AP analysis, Democrats won all five of the Senate races in which Musk's America Pac spent the most backing Republican candidates. More than $80m, about 93% of the committee's spending, went to races Republicans lost. Separately, filings reported by Bloomberg showed a $158m gift to the Senate Leadership Fund in September.

The midterms were held on 3 November, and the president's party traditionally loses ground in them. Some conservative commentators have attributed the Republican losses to that pattern and to national conditions, not to any failure of Musk's own spending. Even so, his remarks suggest he does not see the outlay as having bought influence.

Distancing from Maga

Musk also said he is "not Maga", distancing himself from the movement around Donald Trump. He described himself as "ideologically quite moderate" and said his aim had been to keep one of the House, the Senate or the White House under Republican control, but without a supermajority for either party, so that some "checks and balances" remained.

That framing is notable for a man who spent heavily on Mr Trump's 2024 campaign and then ran the administration's cost-cutting effort. In May 2025 he said he would spend far less on future political campaigns. The midterm total shows how far that pledge fell short, and his latest comments read partly as a second attempt at withdrawal.

The Tesla question

The commercial stakes explain why the remarks matter beyond Washington. BBC coverage noted that Musk spoke while under pressure over the effect of his political involvement. Tesla and SpaceX both have major operations in Texas, and investors have long worried that a chief executive who devotes months and hundreds of millions of dollars to partisan contests has less attention for his companies, and risks alienating customers and officials of either party.

There is no evidence in the reporting so far of a formal change in his standing with the administration, and the interview itself does not say what he thinks it cost him at Tesla. The remarks may be read as an attempt to reset: to tell investors the spending has stopped, and to tell Washington he is not tied to one faction.

What to watch next is whether the words are matched by filings. Federal Election Commission reports in the coming weeks will show whether further money is going to party committees, and Tesla's next investor communications will show whether shareholders press the board on how much of Musk's time politics is taking.
