Reform fences off immigration policy from ex-Tories; home secretary targets 'two-tier' policing

In one sentence
Reform UK's Zia Yusuf says ex-Tory MPs will not shape its immigration policy, while Shabana Mahmood backs a Pride uniform ban to counter 'corrosive' two-tier policing claims.

Reform UK is drawing a line around its immigration policy, and the line runs between its long-standing leadership and the Conservatives who have come over to it. Zia Yusuf, the party's home affairs spokesman, has said former Tory MPs will have no part in shaping that policy, according to the Guardian's report of his remarks. Separately, the home secretary, Shabana Mahmood, has set out plans on policing, describing the perception of "two-tier" policing as "corrosive" and backing a ban on officers marching in uniform at Pride events. Both are bids to control a story about trust: who speaks for a party, and whom the police can be seen to serve.

A platform with a gatekeeper

The detail available so far is thin. It rests on the Guardian's account of Yusuf's remarks, and it has not been possible to read the original here, so no direct quotes are given. The substance is clear enough: defectors are welcome as members and candidates, but immigration, the party's central issue, is not theirs to write.

The context makes that unsurprising. Yusuf was himself a Conservative member until 2024 and became Reform chairman that July. He has since taken the home affairs brief, and he has been openly hostile to the Tory record on migration. He has called Boris Johnson one of the most damaging prime ministers in British history, blaming post-Brexit visa changes for a surge in legal migration, and he has suggested defectors could be called before an inquiry into the non-EU migration peak under the last government.

There has also been friction in public. Reports in 2026 described a clash between Yusuf and Robert Jenrick, a former immigration minister who defected, over whether foreign nationals in social housing would be deported. Yusuf said Jenrick's answer was not Reform policy. Labour's immigration minister said it showed Reform was "making it up as they go along", a line Reform will want to retire.

The logic is plain. Reform's pitch is that the Conservatives had power over immigration and failed. Letting those same figures redraft its policy would blur that message. Fencing them off protects it, and keeps authority over the platform with a small group around Nigel Farage. The cost is a possible ceiling on what defectors can contribute, and on how many will want to come.

Policing and the trust problem

Mahmood's intervention addresses the same terrain from the other side. "Two-tier policing", the claim that forces treat some groups more leniently than others, has become a staple of the right's attack on Labour, and Jenrick has pressed the home secretary on related "two-tier" charges in the Commons. Her answer is to call the perception corrosive and to act on a visible symbol.

The Pride question has a legal backdrop. A High Court ruling in 2025 found that Northumbria Police were wrong to wear uniform while taking part in a Pride event in Newcastle, stressing impartiality as a core constitutional principle of policing. Earlier, Suella Braverman had ordered a review of police involvement in Pride. I could not find the home secretary's own wording or the full plans, so the scope of the proposed ban, and whether it would be statutory or guidance, remains unconfirmed.

The gesture invites an obvious criticism: that a rule about marching in uniform is a culture-war signal, not a remedy for whatever distrust exists. Mahmood is also steering a wider police reform white paper through Parliament, which is where any substantive answer would have to come.

What ties them together

Both stories are about who gets to define a party's or an institution's neutrality. Reform is narrowing the range of voices on its signature policy as its poll position makes it a threat to both Labour and the Conservatives. Labour is trying to show that the police are not aligned with any cause. The next tests are practical: whether Reform publishes a costed immigration platform under Yusuf's name alone, and whether Mahmood's police reform legislation puts the Pride rule into law.
