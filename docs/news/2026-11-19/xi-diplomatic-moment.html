A remarkable diplomatic moment for Xi, with pitfalls

In one sentence
A planned Trump-Xi-Putin meeting in Shenzhen would be a diplomatic coup for Xi, but it was unconfirmed by official readouts at writing, and it carries real risks for Beijing.

China is preparing to host something no recent Chinese leader has managed: the presidents of the United States and Russia in the same room, on Chinese soil, at the same time. The White House said last week that Donald Trump, Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin plan a joint meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, which runs on 18 and 19 November. As this piece was written, no official readout from Beijing, Washington or Moscow confirming that the three-way meeting had taken place was available. What follows therefore assesses a planned event, not a completed one.

What has been announced

The plan was reported by Al Jazeera and others on 14 November, citing the White House. It would be the first three-way summit of its kind. Mr Trump has said he wants to raise nuclear arms control, the wars in Ukraine and Iran, and trade. Reporting differs on how much Ukraine will feature: one account quotes a senior official saying Mr Trump will steer away from it. A separate Trump-Xi bilateral is also expected, and Mr Trump is reportedly not scheduled for a one-on-one with Mr Putin.

The idea is not new. The Kremlin said in September that Mr Putin and Mr Trump had discussed a trilateral with Mr Xi, and Russian officials have since declined to rule one out.

Why it flatters Xi

The appeal for Mr Xi is plain. A host who can seat both leaders at one table presents China as the indispensable broker of great-power relations, not merely one party to them. It also lets him show a domestic and regional audience that Beijing sets the terms of the conversation. Shenzhen, China's showcase of technology and manufacturing, makes a deliberate backdrop. The APEC summit itself is billed as a forum on trade rules, AI and security, and Mr Xi has used such platforms to cast China as the defender of open trade.

The Trump-Xi track has also had a stabilising effect. Their first in-person meeting of Mr Trump's second term, in Busan on 30 October 2025, produced a truce on trade restrictions that both sides have since extended. Preview coverage suggested the Shenzhen bilateral would yield modest results, with larger deliverables left for the G20.

The pitfalls

The risks are as real as the optics. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky has warned that the gathering could amount to a Yalta-style carve-up of spheres of influence. The Kremlin's spokesman, Dmitry Peskov, rejected the comparison, saying any agreements reached in Shenzhen could not be imposed on others. The comparison still points at a genuine problem for Beijing: being seen to host a conversation about Ukraine without Ukraine invites criticism in Europe, where China is trying to keep its markets open.

There is a narrower risk too. Three leaders with different agendas can easily produce a photograph without a substance behind it, and a summit that ends in visible disagreement, or in nothing, would reflect on the host. Moscow wants recognition and relief from isolation, Washington wants leverage over both, and Beijing wants to be seen as the fulcrum. Those aims do not obviously align.

What to watch

The first test is whether the meeting happens as announced. After that, the official readouts matter: the wording from the Chinese foreign ministry, the White House and the Kremlin will show whether the three sides describe the same event. Any language on Ukraine, or on nuclear arms control, would be the first concrete sign of whether Shenzhen is a breakthrough or a photo opportunity.
