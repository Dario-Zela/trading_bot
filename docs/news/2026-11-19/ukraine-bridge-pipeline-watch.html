Ukraine war: Crimean Bridge reportedly hit again, and fighting moves into a gas pipeline

In one sentence
Ukraine hit the Crimean Bridge two days running and Moscow admits damage, but the logistical effect depends on repair time, while soldiers fight inside old gas pipelines.

The Crimean Bridge, the 19-kilometre road and rail link between Russia's Krasnodar region and the Crimean peninsula, has been hit twice in two days. Reuters reported on 17 November that Ukraine struck it on Monday and again on Tuesday. The attacks came as part of what Russian state news agencies called the largest Ukrainian drone assault on Russia since 2022.

The triage note for this item framed it as a claim resting on a Russian official's account. The reporting has moved beyond that. Ukraine has claimed the strike, and Moscow has acknowledged damage and traffic disruption. What remains unverified is the scale of the damage.

What is known, and what is not

President Volodymyr Zelensky called the attack retaliation for Russian strikes on Ukrainian infrastructure. He posted footage that appeared to show the main road arch collapsed into the Kerch Strait. Reuters could not immediately verify that footage. Ukraine's general staff gave a more measured account: an arch and a span of the road section had fallen, while the rail section was still standing with a damaged arch.

Russian officials have confirmed part of this. Sergei Aksyonov, Crimea's Russian-installed governor, said two people were killed and that traffic across the bridge had been suspended. Russian investigators said the structure was damaged. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said a repair plan would be prepared. Russia has not officially confirmed the extent of the damage.

Train travel resumed on Tuesday after a brief pause, according to Reuters. Motorists were told to use car ferries across the strait. A shipping analyst at Sovecon said clearing debris could substantially delay resumption of shipping out of the Sea of Azov, since a fallen arch can block the navigation channel as well as the roadway.

The pattern fits earlier history. On 8 October 2022 a truck bomb damaged a road span and destroyed sections of the rail span; Russian officials blamed Ukraine. The road reopened within days, and the crossing has since been repaired and re-attacked more than once. Repair speed, rather than the first photographs, has tended to decide whether a strike matters for logistics.

The logistics question

The bridge matters because it is a supply route for Russian forces in Crimea and southern Ukraine. Its military weight is partly offset by alternatives. Rail resuming within a day suggests the most important freight link is not severed. The ferries are slower and more exposed. Heavy equipment moves by rail rather than by road, so the state of the rail arch is the figure to watch, and the footage does not settle it.

On present evidence this is a serious strike with an unproven operational effect. A collapsed road arch is a real loss. Whether it changes Russian logistics depends on how long the road stays shut and whether rail traffic holds.

Fighting underground

A separate strand of the war shows how front-line tactics are adapting. BBC World has reported on fighting inside a disused gas pipeline. The BBC piece itself could not be checked for this article, so location, unit and casualty figures are not given here. Other reporting shows the practice is not new.

Near Kupiansk, in Kharkiv region, Ukrainian troops reportedly fought firefights inside a Soviet-era pipe about 1.2 metres wide and advanced several kilometres through it in an operation called Veles. Ukrainian commanders from the Khartiia brigade describe a "whack-a-mole" routine: monitor the pipes for exit holes, kill the Russians who emerge, fill the holes in. In March 2025 Ukraine's General Staff said Russian troops had crawled through a 1.4-metre pipeline to attack its positions at Sudzha in Kursk region.

The common reason is drones. Their density makes crossing open ground in the "kill zone" close to suicidal, so both armies use the old gas network, which once carried Russian gas to Europe, as a covered route. The accounts come mainly from military statements and partisan bloggers, so casualty and distance figures are best read cautiously.

What ties it together

Both stories point to a war where mass and mobility are being fought over by cheap, precise weapons. Drones strike a 19-kilometre bridge from the air and force infantry beneath the ground. The next signals are concrete: Russia's repair timetable for the road arch, whether the rail line stays open, and whether Azov shipping resumes. Satellite imagery of the arch would settle the question quickly.
