Miliband: British consulate in East Jerusalem stays open as a UK mission

In one sentence
Ed Miliband says Britain's East Jerusalem consulate stays open as the 'UK Mission, Jerusalem' after Israel ordered it shut in retaliation for UK sanctions on West Bank settlements.

Britain has said its consulate in East Jerusalem will stay open, but under a new name and in much reduced circumstances, after Israel ordered it shut. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband said on 8 October that the building would continue as an independent, operational "UK Mission, Jerusalem", serving the Palestinian people. He added that the UK does not accept that Israel has a legal right to close it.

The decision is less a staffing matter than a statement about Jerusalem. London is declining to treat the city's eastern half as Israeli territory, and it is keeping a diplomatic channel to Palestinian representatives that it has maintained, in one form or another, since the 19th century.

How the dispute began

The trigger was the UK's September sanctions package. On 8 September Miliband announced measures against violent settlers and illegal settlements in the occupied West Bank, including a ban on imports of settlement-produced goods. He spoke of "ethnic cleansing of Palestinians in areas of the West Bank" carried out by "settler terrorists". Israel called the measures a "series of anti-Israel decisions", and Foreign Minister Gideon Saar called the accusations "outrageous lies".

Israel's response, as Saar set it out, had four parts:

  • removing British representatives from the International Assistance Centre for Gaza;
  • ending British training of Palestinian Authority forces in the West Bank;
  • closing the British consulate in Jerusalem;
  • barring entry to 12 elected officials and other British citizens over alleged "anti-Semitic and anti-Israeli activity".

Saar said the consulate had "ended its duties", that its signage had been removed, and that the consul general and 20 diplomats must leave. Israel has allowed a seven-person task force to remain, with what he described as a very limited scope of activity.

Closed or renamed?

The two governments describe the same building differently. Saar says the consulate is finished. London says only the label has changed. The premises are British government property, and Stephen Doughty, the Foreign Office minister for the Middle East, said: "Our building remains open, our staff remain doing important work."

Miliband said the UK had already withdrawn some staff and families in recent weeks for safety reasons and would now work to build its presence back up. He also said Britain would not take reciprocal action "while such discussions are underway and credible". Ynet reported, citing a message to Israeli officials, that London could expel one Israeli diplomat for each British one forced out. Doughty did not confirm that.

Al Jazeera noted that the legal position is awkward for Israel. Under the international view, East Jerusalem is occupied territory and Israel has no right to shut a mission there. But occupation gives it practical control over what can operate on the ground. A mission that exists on paper and in a building, with a handful of staff, is the result of that gap.

What it signals

The consulate-general has a long history. The consulate opened in 1839, closed in 1914 at the start of the First World War, and was reopened as a consulate-general in 1948 after the British Mandate ended. It has traditionally dealt with the Palestinian Authority, separately from the embassy in Tel Aviv. Keeping it as a distinct mission, rather than folding it into the embassy, preserves that separation, which matters for how London treats Palestinian representation and the status of the city.

The retaliation also reaches beyond symbolism. Ending British training of Palestinian security forces and removing UK staff from the Gaza assistance centre cut practical cooperation.

What to watch

The near-term test is whether London and Jerusalem can negotiate the mission's staffing, and whether the reciprocal expulsions reported by Ynet materialise. Vincent Fean, a former consul general, told Al Jazeera that Britain is likely to wait for Israel's election on 27 October before dealing with the next government. He pointed to E1, the planned settlement that would link Ma'ale Adumim to East Jerusalem and divide the West Bank, as the central issue.

The reports on which this account rests are all dated 8 October, and the facts on the ground may move quickly.
