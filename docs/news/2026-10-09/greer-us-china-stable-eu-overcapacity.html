Greer calls US-China situation 'stable' and says EU states agree on Chinese overcapacity

In one sentence
Greer calls US-China relations 'stable' and says EU states agree on Chinese exports, but the claims are unverified beyond a headline and no formal EU position or US policy change is shown.

Two sentences attributed to the US trade representative, Jamieson Greer, went round the wires on 9 October. The first: relations with China are "stable". The second: every EU member state agrees that Chinese exports are a problem. The first is a familiar line from Greer. The second would be news if it can be shown to be a settled position, and the headline alone does not show that. The source for both is a CNBC headline carried by Benzinga. The full context and wording of the remarks were not available for this report.

A familiar word

Greer has used "stable" before. At the Hudson Institute in April he said: "Right now, the United States and China, I would characterise our economic and trade relationship as stable." He added that Washington was not seeking a confrontation. In December 2025 he said many allies wanted coordinated action against Beijing, but that the US preferred stability for now.

That calm sits alongside substantial tariffs on Chinese goods, which remain in place. Greer has also acknowledged that Chinese products still reach the US through third countries. So "stable" describes a truce that has held. It does not announce a change in policy. A markets reader would want to see a tariff decision or a negotiated concession behind it, and the headline points to neither.

What 'all EU members agree' can and cannot mean

The EU claim needs more care. The headline does not say which exports are meant, or whether the agreement is a formal Council or Commission position or a statement by ministers. Those are different things. A shared view that Chinese industrial overcapacity is a problem is cheap to hold and was already common in Brussels. A shared willingness to act on it is a different matter, and the EU's members have historically split over trade defence. Export-heavy economies wary of Chinese retaliation have pulled one way, and those with exposed manufacturers have pulled the other.

The wider context points the same direction without settling the question. At the G20 finance ministers' meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, in early September, every member except China agreed to take action against subsidy-driven overcapacity, according to Reuters. China's Ministry of Commerce disputes any necessary link between industrial subsidies and excess capacity.

The US is also running its own Section 301 investigation into excess capacity, opened in March. It covers 16 economies, the EU among them. USTR has cited large EU surpluses in chemicals, machinery and vehicles in Germany and in pharmaceuticals in Ireland. On 30 September Greer said the administration would take trade-deal tariff caps into account when acting on the report. EU, Japanese and South Korean deals cap US tariffs on their goods at 15%. Seen from Washington, then, the EU is both a prospective ally against Chinese overcapacity and a target of the same probe.

What to watch

The test is whether the EU position shows up in a document or a vote. That could be Council conclusions, a new Commission trade-defence case or safeguard measures on Chinese goods. On the American side, the 301 report and any proposed action are the point at which "stable" will either hold or not. Neither had been published at the time of writing, and no October finding was located.
