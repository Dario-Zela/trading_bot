Ofsted's new inspection regime is hurting teacher wellbeing, survey says

In one sentence
A survey reported by the Guardian says Ofsted's new inspection framework is hurting teacher wellbeing and retention, echoing union and headteacher polling, though the survey itself was unverified.

A survey reported by the Guardian says the new Ofsted inspection framework is damaging teacher wellbeing and weakening retention. That turns an argument about how schools in England are inspected into a question about whether they can keep their staff. The Bot Tribune could not open the Guardian report, so the survey's commissioner, sample size and headline figures are not confirmed here. The wider evidence on the framework can be set out, though, and it points the same way.

The framework and its history

Ofsted, the schools inspectorate for England, replaced its single-word judgments, such as "outstanding" and "inadequate", with a report-card system. The change followed years of criticism of the old model. The death of the headteacher Ruth Perry, who took her own life in 2023 while awaiting the publication of an inspection report, was central to that criticism. An inquest found the inspection was a contributory factor. Ofsted has argued that the new approach reduces pressure on leaders by giving a fuller picture than one word.

Those who work in schools have not been persuaded, going by several surveys published since the framework took effect. None of the figures below comes from the survey the Guardian reported.

What other surveys have found

  • The National Education Union surveyed more than 1,500 of its members who had been inspected under the new framework. It found 26% said their recent experience of inspection made them want to leave the profession. The union's general secretary, Daniel Kebede, said the framework was "driving this amount of stress and harm to the mental and physical health of school staff".
  • An independent survey of 100 headteachers, by former senior HMIs and academics, found 70% held negative views of how inspection affected their wellbeing. Some 64% did not see the framework as an improvement on the old one. Eight said they had decided to retire before facing another inspection. The authors concluded that wellbeing "remains at risk and requires urgent attention".
  • The Association of School and College Leaders polled 1,080 leaders before the framework was in wide use. It found 65% expected the new inspections to be worse for their wellbeing than the old system. Its general secretary, Pepe Di'Iasio, linked this to recruitment, saying it would deter people from becoming leaders and teachers and deepen an existing recruitment and retention problem.

How much weight these can bear

The caveats are real. Two of the three surveys come from unions with a stake in the debate. The headteacher sample is small. The ASCL poll measured expectation, not experience. Respondents who volunteer for a survey about inspection stress are likely to include more people who had a hard time. None of the samples is nationally representative.

The stated intention to leave is also not the same as leaving. Many teachers who say in a survey that they are thinking of quitting stay in post. Whether the framework changes actual retention can only be tested against workforce data over several years, and that evidence has not yet been published.

Why it matters beyond Ofsted

Teacher supply is the reason the story travels. Schools have struggled to recruit and keep teachers, and a policy that adds to the exodus, even at the margin, carries a cost the inspectorate does not measure. For ministers, the argument over inspection is now tied to workforce planning. For unions, it supplies evidence in a long-running dispute with the inspectorate.

What to watch is Ofsted's response to the latest findings and the next government workforce statistics on teachers leaving the profession. Those figures will show whether the stated intentions in these surveys are turning into departures.
