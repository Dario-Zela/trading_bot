US agencies warn China-based cybersecurity firm is enabling attacks on critical infrastructure

In one sentence
CISA, the FBI, NSA and partners say China-based Integrity Technology Group supplies tools and infrastructure used to hit critical infrastructure in several sectors worldwide.

US and allied cyber agencies have named a Chinese security company as an enabler of attacks on critical infrastructure. In a joint advisory published on 8 October, CISA, the FBI and the NSA said Integrity Technology Group, known as Integrity Tech, is helping threat actors target multiple sectors around the world. The charge is unusual because Integrity Tech sells cybersecurity services, so the accusation puts a security vendor on the list of threats.

What the agencies say

The advisory describes Integrity Tech as a China-based company with ties to the Chinese government. It says the firm acquires or develops cyber tools, hosts attack infrastructure and compromises networks. The tools include large-scale botnets, which are networks of hijacked devices, along with VPN infrastructure and "living off the land" techniques. Those techniques use legitimate software already on a victim's systems, which makes intrusions harder to spot.

The agencies tie the activity to clusters that security researchers track as Flax Typhoon, Ethereal Panda and Red Juliett. They say it was observed in North America, Southeast Asia and Africa. The advisory says it rests on real-world investigations, not inference from the company's public profile.

The targets named in the CISA release include government, critical manufacturing and healthcare organisations, as well as US law enforcement and education bodies. The actors are said to go after operational technology, the systems that run physical processes. They often enter through edge devices, such as routers, VPN appliances and firewalls, that victims do not closely monitor. That gives them a quiet foothold that can last a long time.

Who signed

The US agencies were joined by international partners. Search reporting lists the UK's NCSC, Australia's ASD, the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security, Japan's NPA and NCO, New Zealand's NCSC and Spain's CNI. I could not confirm that list on the CISA page itself, which does not name the partners, so it should be treated as provisional.

Chris Butera, CISA's acting executive assistant director for cybersecurity, said: "Chinese government-affiliated actors continue to position themselves within critical infrastructure networks." Brett Leatherman, who leads the FBI's Cyber Division, said: "We urge every organization to apply this advisory's mitigations."

What is not public

The CISA release is a summary. It does not, in the portion I could read, name specific victims or intrusions. It also gives no botnet size. The full advisory, hosted by the FBI's IC3, carries the technical detail, including the known exploited vulnerabilities that defenders are told to patch. Beijing's response was not available at the time of writing. Governments have publicly accused a named Chinese vendor before, but the allegation here is that the company itself is an active enabler, and that is a stronger claim than a customer misusing a product.

For operators the guidance is practical. They should read the advisory, hunt for signs of compromise, patch the listed vulnerabilities, harden edge devices and report suspicious activity to the local FBI field office.

What to watch

The open questions are whether Washington backs the advisory with sanctions or a trade-list designation against Integrity Tech, and whether allies tell their own operators to review any dealings with the firm. A company named in a joint advisory from this many governments will find it hard to keep selling to Western customers. The advisory also lands as US-China relations are described as "stable" by trade officials, which makes the next government response worth watching.
