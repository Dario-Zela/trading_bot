Burnham tells Merz UK and Germany 'will face threats together' as Paris urges Britain to rejoin the EU

In one sentence
Burnham met Merz in Berlin on 8 October to exchange ratification instruments for the Kensington Treaty, promising the two will 'face threats together' as Paris urges Britain back into the EU.

Britain and Germany are due to formalise their closest security relationship in decades this week, and the formality is being delivered by a prime minister who has barely settled into Downing Street. Andy Burnham was received by Chancellor Friedrich Merz at the Federal Chancellery in Berlin on 8 October. It was his first visit to Germany since taking office. On the agenda was the exchange of ratification instruments for the bilateral friendship treaty known as the Kensington Treaty. Burnham stressed joint defence and security, and said the two countries "will face threats together", according to BBC Politics.

The framing in the original brief was that the treaty was "up for ratification". The German government's own notice suggests something more advanced. The Chancellery described the swap of ratification instruments as a highlight of the visit, which is the step that completes the process. Neither parliament is being asked to vote this week. The open question is what the treaty does once it is in force.

A treaty signed under another prime minister

The Kensington Treaty was signed in London on 17 July 2025 by Keir Starmer and Merz. It was meant to give a bilateral frame to a relationship that had no formal one after Brexit. Berlin and London have worked together through NATO, the Ukraine support coalition and the E3 format with France, but they lacked a treaty of their own. Germany had already signed comparable agreements with France and Poland.

The text has not been supplied in the material available for this article, and the Chancellery notice does not describe its contents. Coverage at the time of signing emphasised defence and security cooperation, along with commitments on economic ties and migration. Readers should treat the detail of any binding clauses as something to check against the treaty itself, not against the summaries in circulation.

That gap matters, because "will face threats together" is a political sentence, not a legal one. A treaty can say that two states will consult, coordinate procurement and share planning. It can also say that one will come to the other's aid. These are very different undertakings, and Britain and Germany already owe each other the strongest of them through NATO's Article 5. The test of a bilateral pact is therefore whether it adds anything the alliance does not: joint equipment programmes, intelligence arrangements, shared industrial capacity, or a faster consultation mechanism in a crisis.

What the visit shows

The Chancellery programme was ceremonial in its first half: military honours at 12:30, then an exchange with students from an English advanced course at a Berlin school. The talks that followed were set to cover bilateral, foreign-policy, European and economic questions. No joint statement or readout of any new commitment was available at the time of writing, and no figures for defence spending or procurement have been published in connection with the visit.

What the visit does establish is continuity. Burnham took over a treaty negotiated by his predecessor and chose to make Berlin an early stop, rather than reopening the terms. A telephone call between the two leaders shortly after he took office, reported by ANSA, had already pointed that way: both said they wanted to press on with implementing the treaty and to keep supporting Ukraine while increasing pressure on Russia.

Paris raises the stakes

The visit coincided with a louder intervention from across the Channel. BBC Politics reported that France's finance minister told the UK to "come all the way" back to the EU. That is a headline claim from a single source. The speaker's name, the date and the full context could not be checked independently, and the quote should be read with that caveat.

There is precedent for French ministers saying such things, though usually with more hedging. Bruno Le Maire, then finance minister, suggested in 2018 that Britain might want to return within 10 to 15 years. In March 2026, the foreign minister Jean-Noël Barrot said the EU would welcome Britain with "open arms" if it chose to rejoin the single market, as Chancellor Rachel Reeves spoke of aligning with many EU business rules. A rejoin call from a finance minister, if confirmed, would fit that pattern while moving it onto economic ground.

The two stories point in different directions. A bilateral treaty with Germany is a way of building European ties without re-entering EU institutions. A French invitation to rejoin tests how far Burnham wants to go. Neither the brief nor the available sources show that his government has proposed rejoining, and the claim that it is "resetting European ties" has not been verified beyond the BBC's framing.

What to watch

The first marker is the treaty text: whether the instruments exchanged in Berlin bring into force a mutual-assistance clause, or a framework for consultation and joint projects. The second is delivery, in concrete programmes and funding that can be counted. The third is domestic. The 28 October Budget will show how much room the new government gives defence, and any response from Burnham to Paris will show whether bilateral ties are meant to complement the EU relationship or replace the idea of closer membership.
