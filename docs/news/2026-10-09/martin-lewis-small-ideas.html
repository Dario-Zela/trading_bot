Martin Lewis asks the public for 'small ideas' to improve UK life

In one sentence
Martin Lewis has launched a public call for small, low-cost fixes to everyday problems, open until 12 November, with about 50 ideas to be taken to government by a cross-party panel.

Martin Lewis, the consumer campaigner who founded MoneySavingExpert, has asked the public to send in "small ideas" for fixing the everyday irritations of British life. Suggestions go to a new initiative that will pick the best and take them to government, councils and regulators. The call is open until 12 November.

The project is called the Small Ideas Initiative. Submissions are taken through its website, instituteforsmallideas.org, and anyone can contribute. Lewis says he wants "smaller fixes across a huge range of areas that could quickly improve daily life". Reports put the likely topics at health, schools, policing, technology and money.

What he is asking for

Lewis calls the targets the "potholes" of daily life: clunky systems, unintended consequences and badly designed rules that make things harder than they need to be. Ideas should be practical, low-cost and uncontroversial. Suggestions that are too local, overtly party-political, illegal or harmful are out.

Coverage cites four examples:

  • A universal allergy symbol on restaurant menus.
  • Real-time sewage alerts at every beach, not only those monitored by water companies.
  • Fairer car-parking charges, since drivers must pay for a set period before they know how long they need.
  • Clearer naming for tax-free childcare. The scheme gives parents a 20% top-up on childcare costs but does not reduce the tax they pay, which Lewis finds confusing.

Lewis says the idea grew out of his campaign on the council tax reduction scheme. Hundreds of thousands of people miss out on discounts of up to 100% for people with conditions such as Alzheimer's or dementia. Awareness is low and rules differ between councils. "We shouldn't underestimate the impact of small stuff on people's lives," he said.

How it will work

Two charities are organising the project: Nesta's Centre for Collective Intelligence and Involve. According to Brit Brief, Lewis has personally given them £175,000. A cross-party panel, said to run from Reform UK to the Greens, will sift the submissions and choose about 50 ideas. No panel members are named in the coverage I could check, though one report names Polly Mackenzie, who helped develop the idea, as a member. That claim rests on a single secondary summary and should be treated with caution.

The cross-party panel is the part that matters politically. Lewis has a large public following and a record of getting people to act. A call from him is less likely to be dismissed as one party's pitch. The model is crowdsourcing with a filter, and the filter is where the influence sits.

Where it fits

There is no formal government response. The coverage notes that the new prime minister, Andy Burnham, is already working on similar long-standing grievances. A ban on "subscription traps", first announced by Sir Keir Starmer, would make contracts easier to cancel. Earlier this week ministers also said they would scrap some extra airline charges, such as fees to sit with children. Those measures suggest the government is receptive to consumer-friction fixes. Nothing yet commits it to taking up whatever the initiative produces.

The initiative is better read as agenda-setting than as policy. Lewis is not proposing a measure. He is asking for a list, and the value of that list will depend on whether ministers, councils or regulators adopt items from it. Small fixes are also the cheapest kind of reform to announce, which may make them easy for ministers to welcome and just as easy to leave unimplemented.

What to watch

The first marker is the 12 November close, followed by publication of the panel's shortlist of roughly 50 ideas. The test is how many of them are taken up by government, local authorities or regulators, and how quickly.
