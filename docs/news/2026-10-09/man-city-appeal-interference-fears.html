Rival clubs fear political interference in Manchester City's 115-charges appeal

In one sentence
Rivals reportedly fear political pressure on City's appeal, but the claim traces to unnamed sources in 2024 reporting and no on-record evidence of government involvement has emerged.

A claim is circulating in English football that rival clubs fear the government could lean on the outcome of Manchester City's appeal. It is attributed to no club, official or on-record voice. Until someone puts their name to it, it is a worry reported at second hand, and the evidence for it is thinner than its force suggests.

Where the claim comes from

The fear is not new. The earliest version this paper could trace is a report in The Times from June 2024, which Goal summarised at the time. It said unnamed rivals believed the 115 charges could be dropped under political pressure. The reasoning was that the UK government might step in to protect its trading relationship with the United Arab Emirates. City are majority-owned by Sheikh Mansour, a member of Abu Dhabi's ruling family and the UAE's vice-president.

No individual was quoted in that account. The rivals were unidentified, and the view was passed on through another outlet's reporting. Goal compared the situation with the 2020 Newcastle United takeover, when government correspondence reportedly showed ministers weighing the diplomatic consequences of the Saudi-backed bid. That precedent shows politics can enter commercial decisions. It does not show it entering a disciplinary tribunal.

The story has resurfaced because the case has moved. Reports around 25 September, including from The Athletic, said an independent commission found City guilty of all but one of the 115 charges. The charges cover alleged breaches of financial rules between 2009-10 and 2022-23. The Premier League had not commented at the time of those reports, and the verdict rests on that reporting, not on a published judgment. City deny wrongdoing. They are understood to be appealing, and say the process still has "significant elements to be completed".

What is on the record

The documented political involvement is much narrower than the rumour. Caroline Dinenage, chair of the Commons Culture, Media and Sport Committee, has urged that due process be followed. She has also asked for the case to conclude as quickly as possible, saying its length and cost raise serious concerns about financial fairness across the football pyramid. That is a parliamentarian pressing for speed and rigour. It is not a minister pressing for a particular result.

No source reviewed for this article shows a government department contacting the Premier League, the commission or the appeal panel about the City case. The reporting found describes no ministerial approach, and no club has put its name to the suggestion that one is coming. Several of the surrounding details were also inconsistent between outlets. One aggregator gave a different charge count from the 115 used by most. Claims that clubs were briefed on the ruling before it became public could not be confirmed.

Two features of the process also cut against the fear. Disciplinary cases under Premier League rules are heard by independent panels, and appeals go to a separate panel, not to a minister or to the league board. Politicians can comment on that process. They have no formal route into it. A rival's anxiety that informal pressure might still work is understandable. It is a different thing from evidence that it has been applied.

The reported guilty verdict also sits awkwardly with the old premise. The fear was that charges would be quietly dropped. If the reports are right, a panel has already ruled against City on almost every count, and any attempt to soften the outcome would have to work through an appeal that is yet to be heard.

Maresca and the dressing room

On the pitch, Enzo Maresca, who succeeded Pep Guardiola, has tried to keep the noise at a distance. At his first press conference he was asked whether the charges concerned him. "No, to be honest," he said, pointing out that he was at the club for the treble season three years earlier. "In three years, it's still the same, so nothing happened." After the verdict reports he posted that the club needed "Time to breathe. Time to analyse. Time to learn." This paper found no instance of Maresca addressing the political-interference claim itself, and nothing suggests he has been asked to.

City's squad calm matters because the sanctions are open. Ian Hargreaves of Quillon Law has set the range from a transfer ban at the mild end to expulsion from the league at the severe end. The precedents are points deductions: eight for Everton and four for Nottingham Forest, both for breaching the same profit-and-sustainability rules. Any penalty would be settled only after the appeal.

What would settle it

The claim becomes a story about the league's independence only if someone with standing says it on the record. That could be a club executive, a league official or a minister's office confirming or denying contact. Failing that, the appeal itself is the test. A published judgment, with its reasoning and any sanction, would let rivals and the public judge whether the outcome follows from the evidence. The date of the appeal hearing has not been announced. Until it is, the political-interference story stays a rumour with a two-year history and no named source.
