Pentagon to livestream firing-squad execution, officials say

In one sentence
The Pentagon says Nidal Hasan's firing-squad execution at Fort Hood on 3 December will be livestreamed, in what would be the Army's first such execution since 1945.

The Pentagon says it will livestream the execution by firing squad of Nidal Hasan, the former Army psychiatrist who killed 13 people at Fort Hood in 2009. A Pentagon official confirmed the plan on Wednesday. If it goes ahead, it will be the first Army firing-squad execution since 1945 and an unusual case of a US government broadcasting a state killing.

What has been announced

The decision has come out in stages. Chief Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell said on Monday that Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth had recommended death by firing squad and that President Donald Trump had approved it. Acting Army Secretary Adam Telle then set the date: Thursday 3 December, at 1pm local time, at Fort Hood, Texas.

Hegseth chose the place and time to match the attack. He called that a "no-brainer" on the Human Events Daily podcast, where he said the execution would be public. "We'll make sure that people are able to watch it, that it's public," he said. The anonymous Pentagon official confirmed the livestream but gave no detail on how it would work. Officials have said more will follow.

The case

Hasan, now 56, was convicted by a military jury in August 2013 and sentenced to death. He is held on death row at Fort Leavenworth, Kansas. He admitted the shooting at his court-martial and said he acted in support of the Taliban. The attack on 5 November 2009 killed 13 people and wounded more than 30.

Military capital punishment has been dormant for decades. The last sentence the US military carried out was a hanging at Fort Leavenworth in 1961. The last Army firing-squad execution was that of Pvt. Eddie Slovik, shot in France in January 1945 for desertion.

What is not yet clear

The questions are largely legal and procedural. None of the reports seen cites a specific legal authority for broadcasting the execution, or says how it squares with Army regulations on military capital punishment. Some reports say it could conflict with those rules, and that Hasan could file a habeas petition in civilian court, which could delay the date. The reports seen do not say whether any appeal is pending. Those points come from secondary reports rather than the Pentagon.

The plan has also drawn objections across party lines. Republican Senator Thom Tillis is reported to have called it "beyond disgusting". Capital punishment in the US is usually carried out away from public view, with a small number of official witnesses. Public executions are rare in democracies, and a livestream would go well beyond the limited witness access typical of state executions.

The BBC, whose report first flagged the story to this desk, was the only source on the initial item. Military Times and other US outlets have since reported the same announcement, including the date and place.

What to watch

The next markers are the "details to follow" the Pentagon has promised, covering who would see the stream, and any court filing from Hasan's lawyers. Lawmakers may also ask the Army to explain its authority for a public broadcast before 3 December.
