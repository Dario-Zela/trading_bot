'Add me to WhatsApp' scam calls to be investigated

In one sentence
UK authorities will investigate scam calls urging people to 'add me to WhatsApp', but the lead agency and the scale of losses have not been made clear.

A scam that starts with a stranger's phone call and ends in a WhatsApp chat is now the subject of an investigation by UK authorities, according to the BBC. The pitch is simple. A caller, or increasingly a recorded voice, asks the person who answers to add a number on WhatsApp. Once the target does, the conversation leaves the phone network and moves to a platform where the usual call-screening tools do not reach.

The BBC's report, as relayed to this desk, gives no figures for how many people have been called or how much has been lost. It also does not make clear which agency is leading the work. Those gaps matter, because they decide whether this is a platform-scale fraud inquiry or a collection of individual rip-offs. Until they are filled, the investigation's significance is a matter of inference.

How the scam works

The tactic has been circulating for some time. LBC has reported a rising run of unsolicited calls telling recipients to add a number on WhatsApp, with fraudsters reportedly using AI and bots to place the calls at scale. The security firm Bitdefender describes how an automated opener can turn into account hijacking, financial fraud, malware or identity theft once the chat begins. It also notes that scammers may ask for a WhatsApp verification code under a false pretext. Sharing that code can hand over the account.

TechRadar makes the structural point. Moving a victim onto WhatsApp lets the caller sidestep spam filtering, and it makes it easier to send malicious links or files. There is no suggestion that WhatsApp itself has been hacked. The weakness is the hand-off between two systems, each of which sees only part of the fraud.

Platform problem or pile of cases?

That hand-off is why the framing matters. A voice call crosses the telecoms networks, which Ofcom regulates. The chat that follows runs on a messaging service, which falls under the Online Safety Act's fraud duties, also overseen by Ofcom. A probe that looks only at the calls would treat the scam as a telecoms nuisance. A probe that also asks what the messaging platform could detect, such as bursts of new contacts from numbers that have just been reported, would treat it as a fraud pipeline.

Ofcom's recent record points to the telecoms side first. In September it opened investigations into the cloud phone providers Vonage and Voxbone over concerns that their numbers were being misused to carry out scams. Neither inquiry concerned WhatsApp. Ofcom's separate January probe of WhatsApp was about the accuracy of information it supplied for a bulk-SMS market review, not about fraud.

So the precedent is for enforcement aimed at the companies that supply numbers. Whether the new inquiry reaches Meta, which owns WhatsApp, is the open question. This desk could not independently confirm which body is running it, and Meta had not commented in the coverage reviewed.

What to watch

The first test is simple: which agency is named, and whether its terms of reference cover the messaging platform as well as the carriers. The second is whether the investigation produces loss figures, since without them the scale argument cannot be made. In the meantime, the advice from Ofcom and the security firms is unchanged. Hang up on an unexpected caller, never share a WhatsApp verification code, and report suspicious calls to the phone provider or forward suspicious texts to 7726.
