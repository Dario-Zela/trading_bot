Gilts: 30-year yields stay near 6% ahead of the 28 October Budget

In one sentence
With 30-year gilt yields near 6%, the highest since 1998, the 28 October Budget is a market test of fiscal credibility, and the BoE is reported to be easing long-gilt sales.

Long-dated British government borrowing costs are sitting close to 6%, and the Budget on 28 October is now being read as a test of whether the Treasury can hold that line. Yields on 30-year gilts have hovered near that level in recent sessions, according to tradingeconomics.com. That is the highest since 1998, and it leaves little room for a Budget that investors read as loosening fiscal discipline.

The numbers

Reports on the exact level differ, which is normal for a market moving in small increments across venues. One account has the 30-year yield at roughly 5.89–5.93% in mid-September. Another says it touched 5.95%. A third, from an automated news site, puts a brief peak at 6.029% on 1 October. That last figure is the least reliable of the three. Treated together, they put the 30-year yield within a few basis points of 6%, with the exact print depending on the day.

The pressure is not confined to the long end. The 10-year gilt has been quoted at about 5.25% to above 5.4%, depending on the source and date. By comparison, 10-year yields are roughly 4.79% in the US, 3.36% in Germany and 3.01% in Japan, per the same September account.

The analysis cited in coverage points to four drivers: energy-driven inflation risk, expectations of tighter Bank of England policy, heavy gilt supply and fiscal uncertainty. The government plans to sell £252.1bn of gilts in 2026-27, including £23bn of long conventional gilts, so supply is not a small factor.

The Budget as a test

The Budget is scheduled for 28 October. Coverage names Chancellor John Healey as delivering it, with Andy Burnham as prime minister. That rests on a small number of sources, and the name should be checked against Reuters before it is relied on.

The logic is straightforward. Every extra basis point on long gilts raises the cost of financing new and refinanced debt, so the Chancellor's room for manoeuvre shrinks as yields rise. Investors have warned against loosening discipline, and a Budget that reads as a loosening would be tested immediately in the gilt market. The yield level is the market's running estimate of that credibility.

The Bank of England

The central bank is a second variable. The Daily Telegraph first reported, and other outlets repeated, that the Bank would stop active sales of 20- and 30-year gilts from its Asset Purchase Facility. The reports tied the move to the Monetary Policy Committee decision on 18 September, with the 12-month quantitative tightening target falling from £70bn to about £50bn for the year to September 2027.

This is reported, not confirmed. The searches behind this piece found no Bank of England publication that settles it, and some accounts conflict on whether the Bank is merely skipping long-dated sales or buying. The Bank's own stated direction is clearer. In September 2025 it voted 7-2 to slow the pace of sales to £70bn and to skew them away from long maturities, with a 40:40:20 split across short, medium and long gilts.

One estimate puts the saving to the government at about £2.5bn a year by the end of the decade. About £22bn of losses crystallised since 2022 would remain. Reform UK's deputy leader Richard Tice said: "We shouldn't be doing quantitative tightening, we shouldn't be paying voluntary interest on printed money." Governor Andrew Bailey has defended the programme as preserving the Bank's ability to intervene in markets.

What to watch

Two dates matter. The Bank's published schedule and minutes will show whether long-dated sales have in fact stopped. The Budget on 28 October will show whether the Chancellor's plans leave the 30-year yield below 6% or push it through. A clean move below 5.9% would suggest relief. A sustained print above 6% would suggest the market has priced in more borrowing than the Treasury admits to.
