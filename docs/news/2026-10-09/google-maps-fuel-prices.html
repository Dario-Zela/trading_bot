Google Maps adds live fuel prices as UK pump costs climb

In one sentence
Google Maps and Waze now show near-real-time UK petrol and diesel prices from the government's Fuel Finder data, hiding any price Google cannot verify.

Drivers in the UK can now see petrol and diesel prices inside Google Maps and Waze, with the figure shown under each forecourt's name. The prices arrive as pump costs keep rising. Google says the feature began rolling out on 8 October. The data is not Google's own. It comes from the government's Fuel Finder scheme.

To use it, a driver searches for "petrol" or "fuel" and nearby stations appear with their latest prices. The routes and the map are the same as before. What has changed is that the price comparison now happens in an app many drivers already have open.

Where the numbers come from

Fuel Finder launched in March 2026. Retailers are legally required to submit a new price within 30 minutes of changing it. The government says the scheme covers around 99% of the fuel sold on UK roads. Third-party sites, such as the extension FuelHop and the Fuelsave tool, were already built on this open data. Google has now plugged the same feed into a product with a very large user base.

Strictly, "live" is the wrong word. A 30-minute reporting window makes the data near-real-time at best, and it is only as good as retailers' compliance.

The accuracy question

Google says it will hold back prices it cannot trust. Sarah-Jayne Williams, Google's director of product partnerships for Maps and Waze, told the BBC that a price is shown only if there is high confidence that it is the right one.

Industry voices say the gaps are real. Clare Lafferty of PetrolPrices has warned that some sites have not registered with the scheme at all. Others have registered but do not update consistently. In practice, some forecourts may show no price, or a price that is slightly stale. A missing figure is better than a wrong one, but it makes the tool less useful in exactly the places where a driver might want it.

Why the timing matters

Pump prices have been climbing with oil. One report put diesel at a record peak of 201.01p a litre, though that figure comes from a single secondary source and I have not checked it independently. The government presents the feature as a cost-saving tool and estimates the average saving at £40 a year. That is a modest sum, and it assumes drivers change where they fill up. It is an official estimate and has not been independently tested.

The feature is a convenience and does not move markets. The commercial effect is more subtle. Price-comparison apps have until now been the main way to use the open data. Putting prices in the default navigation app narrows what a separate app can offer. Retailers also lose some room to hold a higher price near a rival's cheaper one, since the comparison now sits one tap away.

What to watch

The test is coverage. If the share of forecourts that Google judges reliable stays high, the feature will matter. If many stations are left blank, drivers will keep using the dedicated apps. Any government compliance figures on retailers' reporting under Fuel Finder would show which way it is going.
