Second UK health body drops Israeli drugmaker after activist pressure

In one sentence
The Royal College of Nursing has dropped Teva UK as an event sponsor after activist pressure; a second completed cut-off is unconfirmed and no procurement change is reported.

A British nursing body has ended a commercial relationship with the UK arm of an Israeli drugmaker after pressure from pro-Palestinian campaigners. The case is the clearest evidence so far of activist pressure reaching health-sector sponsorship. Whether it is the second such case, as the Guardian-sourced brief behind this piece describes it, could not be confirmed. Whether it is the start of a pattern is a separate question, and the evidence does not yet answer it.

What is established

The Royal College of Nursing (RCN), which represents more than 500,000 nursing staff, has dropped Teva UK as a sponsor of an event on migraine and headache services. The event is scheduled for 30 October at the RCN's London headquarters and will go ahead as planned, according to the union's letter.

The decision was set out in a letter dated 23 September from Carmel O'Boyle, chair of the RCN Council, to the campaign group Nurses for Palestine. Reporting that relays the Guardian's account says the group, together with UK Healthcare Petitions to Royal Colleges, had pressed for the move. The Guardian article itself could not be accessed for this piece, so details below come from secondary outlets and should be read with that caveat.

The union also announced a review intended to tighten its commercial partnership policy, according to the same reports.

The stated reasons

The RCN's explanation, as reported, rests on corporate conduct rather than on the campaign. The letter says further due diligence showed that a number of Teva Pharmaceuticals' operations have been the subject of legal sanction and public scrutiny. The Israeli outlet Ynet, relaying the Guardian, put the sum Teva has been required to pay European Commission and US authorities at about £1bn, in connection with bribery, price-fixing and abuse of market power. That figure is second-hand and has not been checked against the underlying settlements.

Campaigners framed it differently. They called the decision a "major breakthrough for grassroots healthcare organising and the international boycott, divestment and sanctions movement". Teva, for its part, expressed disappointment and criticised what it called the politicisation of the British healthcare system, according to a company statement cited in the coverage.

The two accounts are not the same, and it matters which one the RCN adopts in practice. A policy anchored in legal sanctions would, in principle, apply to any supplier with a comparable record. One anchored in a company's country of origin would not.

Is there a second body?

The brief's headline refers to a "second" UK health body. A follow-up search found no report of an earlier or later completed cut-off. It did find campaigners calling on a pharmacy professional body to drop Teva UK as a sponsor of its annual conference next month and to cancel an exhibition stand the firm has booked. That is a demand, not a decision, and nothing found shows the body has responded. The body's name is also given inconsistently across sources, so it is not named here.

The brief's claim of "read-across for the sector" is the discovery editor's inference. No other organisation's move has been cited.

Why it matters, and what would change that

Teva is described in coverage as one of the larger suppliers of medicines to the NHS, though that characterisation was not independently verified here. Sponsorship of a college event is a long way from NHS procurement, which runs on tendering rules and price. A professional body choosing its event sponsors is a reputational decision; a hospital trust choosing a generic-drug supplier is not, and nothing reported suggests any buyer has changed supply arrangements.

Three developments would turn a single case into a pattern: a named third organisation completing a cut-off, a formal ethical-sourcing policy emerging from the RCN review, or any purchaser, rather than a sponsorship recipient, acting on supply. None has been reported.

The nearest checkpoints are the pharmacy body's conference next month, where the pending demand will be answered one way or the other, and the RCN's 30 October event, which will show whether the sponsorship change draws further challenge.
