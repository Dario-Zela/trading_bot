Asos hackers took more personal data than first disclosed, BBC finds

In one sentence
Asos first said hackers may have taken basic contact details; the BBC saw a sample including birth dates and search histories, and Asos now concedes detailed profiles were taken.

Asos told shareholders on Tuesday that hackers may have reached "basic personal information including name and contact details". By Thursday, the BBC had seen a sample of the stolen data that suggested the haul was wider than that. The gap between those two descriptions is now the main question for the retailer. It is also the main question for the customers who received the first reassurance.

What Asos said, and what the BBC saw

The incident became public on Tuesday, when an unauthorised message appeared as a pop-up notification in the Asos app, reading "ASOS HACKED". Asos told the London Stock Exchange that the notification had been sent by an "unauthorised third party". It emailed customers with similar wording about basic contact details.

On Wednesday evening the people behind the attack contacted the BBC's cyber correspondent, Joe Tidy, and supplied a sample of the data. According to the BBC's account, the sample showed more than contact details. It contained:

  • names, addresses, phone numbers and email addresses
  • Asos customer numbers
  • dates of birth
  • customers' searches on the Asos site, such as "reclaimed vintage", "glamorous wide fit" and "Asos petite"

The BBC held back its report so that Asos could contact customers first. After the BBC published on Thursday, Asos sent an update confirming that hackers held detailed customer profiles, potentially for millions of users. It maintained that no bank details or passwords had been accessed.

Asos has not said how many customers are affected. It did not respond to the BBC's questions about the scale of the breach. The sample is the hackers' own, so the full volume and contents remain unverified. The BBC's reporting nonetheless shows the first notice understated what was taken, and Asos's own follow-up message effectively accepts this.

How the attackers got in

Asos says the attackers "gained access to an Asos employee account by impersonating a trusted contact to obtain log in credentials". They then used those credentials to log in to a service, which Asos has not named, and download customer data. The company says it is still investigating.

The pop-up claimed the hackers had "compromised the Snowflake instance". Snowflake, the cloud data platform, has previously said its own platform was not breached. The group, which calls itself Xuanyewen, told the BBC it got in through Simon AI, a marketing platform built on Snowflake. Simon AI has been approached for comment. The account is the attackers' claim and has not been confirmed.

If the account is accurate, the weak point was a person and a login. It was not a flaw in the underlying software. That pattern is common in retail breaches, where an employee is tricked into handing over credentials.

Why the difference matters

Security specialists quoted by the BBC say the combination is what makes the data useful to criminals. A name, number and address is a standard phishing list. Add a birth date, a customer number and a record of what someone has been shopping for, and a scam message can sound like genuine customer service. Asos says it will never ask for passwords, security codes or payment details in unsolicited messages or calls. Trevor Dearing of the security firm Illumio notes that passwords were not stolen, so customers should treat any unprompted request to change or share one with suspicion.

The regulatory consequences are not yet known. Neither the BBC's report nor Asos's statements mention the Information Commissioner's Office. Under UK data-protection law, companies must tell the regulator about a qualifying breach within 72 hours of becoming aware of it. They must also tell affected individuals without undue delay when the risk to them is high. Whether the original notice was an honest first read or a framing that undersold the risk is a question regulators would ask. No such inquiry has been announced.

Asos says customers are not being asked to take any action, and that its website and app are safe to use. It says it has "already taken additional steps" to strengthen its security controls and will contact people directly where they need more support.

What to watch

Three things will show how serious this is. The first is the number: Asos has yet to say how many customers were affected, and "potentially millions" is not a figure. The second is whether it publishes a fuller account of the data involved than the one the BBC extracted from the attackers. The third is any acknowledgement from the ICO. If the attackers release more of the data, the picture could change again.
