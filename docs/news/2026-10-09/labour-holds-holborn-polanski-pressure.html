Labour holds Holborn and St Pancras as Polanski vows to stay on after Green defeat

In one sentence
Labour held Keir Starmer's old Holborn and St Pancras seat, a setback for Green leader Zack Polanski, who says he will stay on; the full figures are still to be confirmed.

Labour has held Holborn and St Pancras, the north London seat Keir Starmer represented from 2015. The result is a setback for Zack Polanski, whose Greens had hoped to show they can take Labour territory in inner London. It is also the first electoral read on the government of Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

The full declaration (majority, vote shares and turnout) was not available to this report, and the numbers matter more than the headline. A Labour hold in a seat this safe is the expected outcome. The question is how far the Green vote moved, and that cannot be answered until the figures are checked against the official count.

A safe seat, and a test of something else

Starmer won the constituency at the 2015 general election and held it through his years as Labour leader and then as prime minister. By-elections in seats a party has held for a long time rarely produce a change of hands. They are read for direction: whether the governing party's vote has held, and whether a challenger has gained enough to threaten it at the next general election.

That is the Greens' problem here. Polanski became party leader in September 2025 with a pitch aimed squarely at younger, urban and left-leaning voters who might otherwise vote Labour. Inner London, with its renters, graduates and students, is the territory where that pitch should work best. A seat that Labour holds without much strain suggests the Green surge can stall at the margins. A narrow Labour hold on a sharply reduced share would point the other way.

Which of those it was will decide how the result is remembered. A comfortable hold gives Burnham an early, cheap piece of good news. A thin one would give the Greens a basis to say that Labour's inner-London seats are within reach, even though they lost.

Polanski's position

Polanski says he "absolutely" intends to remain Green leader after the defeat, according to BBC Politics and Guardian reporting. A leader saying he will stay on is not the same as a challenge to him, and nothing in the reporting available suggests a formal move against him.

The defeat arrives alongside a row over Zionism that is running in parallel. The reporting available does not set out the remarks at issue or who has criticised them, so the details are left out here rather than guessed at. The two episodes are linked mainly by timing: a poor result tends to widen the audience for internal criticism that was already circulating.

The Greens are in an awkward position. They have built their recent growth around Polanski's profile, which makes him hard to replace and also makes him the obvious target when something goes wrong. The party's internal rules and its history of resisting a dominant leader will shape how far any dissent goes.

What comes next

For Burnham, one by-election hold settles very little. His government faces larger tests, including the 28 October Budget, with 30-year gilt yields hovering near 6%. The Budget will say far more about his standing than a seat Labour was always likely to keep. His diplomacy has also started to take shape: he has told Germany's Friedrich Merz that the two countries "will face threats together".

For Polanski, the immediate task is to confirm that the Zionism row stays a row and does not become a leadership question. The things to watch are the full by-election figures, particularly the Green share compared with the last general election, and whether any senior party figure moves from criticism to a formal challenge. If the Green vote in Holborn and St Pancras proves modest, the case that inner London is ready to switch from Labour will have to wait for the next general election.
