Russian strike on buses kills at least 30 as attacks on Ukraine surge

In one sentence
A Russian glide bomb killed at least 30 people on buses in Kramatorsk, one of several deadly strikes this week as Ukraine braces for winter.

A Russian glide bomb struck two passenger buses in Kramatorsk, in Ukraine's eastern Donetsk region, on Thursday morning. The Donetsk regional prosecutor's office said the death toll had risen to 30, with 18 people wounded. The figure comes from Ukrainian officials and has not been independently confirmed. Reports differ on the vehicles, with some outlets describing minibuses and others city buses. President Volodymyr Zelensky said the bomb hit "an ordinary public transport stop". He added that some of the dead had not been identified because of the severity of their injuries.

The strike

Ukraine's national police attributed the attack to a glide bomb, a Soviet-era bomb fitted with wings and guidance kits that Russia launches from aircraft beyond the reach of most Ukrainian air defences. Vadym Filashkin, the Donetsk regional official, said those killed were civilians. He ordered Kramatorsk and a neighbouring city to suspend public transport because of the risk of further attacks. The Kramatorsk city council called the strike "yet another horrific crime committed by the Russian army against the civilian population".

The reports reviewed for this article carried no Russian official response on the bus strike. Moscow has consistently said it targets only military objects and denies deliberately striking civilians. Ukrainian and UN monitors have repeatedly documented civilian deaths from such attacks.

Kramatorsk matters beyond the casualty count. It is a logistics hub and part of the "fortress belt" of cities that Russia has not yet captured in Donetsk. Russian forces have moved close to its eastern edge, so the city is both a battlefield and a place where tens of thousands of civilians still live and travel by bus.

A surge, not an isolated blow

The bus attack followed a day after a Russian missile hit an apartment block in the Chernihiv region. France 24 reported 22 dead, including five children. NPR located that strike in Pryluky, a town in the same region, and the two accounts probably describe the same attack. A separate strike on Kyiv killed three more people, according to the city's mayor. NPR also reported worsening blackouts in the capital. Taken together, that is more than 55 deaths in two days.

The question this raises is whether the Kramatorsk attack marks a change in targeting. The evidence so far does not settle it. Russia has hit buses before: Bloomberg reported that a drone strike on a bus killed 15 civilians in February. Frontline cities like Kramatorsk have long been within reach of guided bombs. The difference this week is the combination of scale and timing. Strikes on residential buildings, a bus stop and the capital's power supply have come within days of each other, as the cold season approaches. Whether that amounts to a deliberate campaign against civilian life, or the cumulative effect of a higher tempo of attacks, would need a longer run of data on weapons used and sites chosen. No such analysis was available in the coverage reviewed.

Retaliation and winter

Ukraine is also striking deep inside Russia. A drone started a fire at a Yandex data centre in Sasovo, in the Ryazan region about 300 km from Moscow. Yandex said no one was hurt but the facility had stopped operating. Ryazan's governor, Pavel Malkov, said two Ukrainian drones had been shot down overnight and a business roof had caught fire. Ukraine's General Staff said it hit the Gazprom Neftekhim Salavat refinery in Bashkortostan. Andriy Kovalenko, a former head of Ukraine's Centre for Countering Disinformation, wrote on Telegram: "Two can play at this game." He did not speak for the government, and Kyiv has not formally claimed the data-centre strike.

For Ukrainian cities, the practical consequences come first. Suspended bus services in Donetsk strip residents of one of the few remaining ways to leave or reach shelter. Blackouts in Kyiv add to the strain before heating season. Municipal authorities have to plan evacuation routes and warming points while assuming that transport, power and apartment blocks are all liable to be hit.

The next markers are practical ones. They include whether Filashkin's transport suspension is extended beyond Kramatorsk, whether prosecutors open a formal war-crimes case and publish a final toll, and whether Russia's glide-bomb strikes on towns near the front continue at this pace into the winter.
