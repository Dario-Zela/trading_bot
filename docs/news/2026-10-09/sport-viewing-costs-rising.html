Ticket prices and TV packages: why the cost of watching sport keeps rising

In one sentence
With Premier League rights split between Sky and TNT, and streaming added on top, following one league in Britain can now take several subscriptions.

A fan who wants to watch one league in Britain now has to work out which of several accounts carries which match. The Premier League shows the pattern in its plainest form. Live games are split between two pay-TV rivals, with a streaming service in the mix at various points. Anyone who wants the whole season has to assemble it piece by piece.

Who holds what

The current domestic cycle runs from 2025-26 to 2028-29. Sky Sports holds the larger share, 215 live matches a season, in a deal reported at about £6.7bn over the four years. TNT Sport holds 52. A supporter who wants every televised game of their club therefore needs both services, and the clash between the two schedules is often the deciding factor in which subscriptions they keep.

Streaming has added to the sprawl instead of replacing the old arrangements. Amazon Prime Video has carried Premier League packages since the 2019-20 season, but only as a bolt-on. The usual route to a game is still a dedicated sports subscription, and the streaming services have mostly added another bill alongside it.

The free-to-air option has shrunk to highlights. BBC coverage of top-flight English football is largely Match of the Day, which shows the games after they have been played, not live league rights. A household that cannot or will not pay is left with the edited version.

Why the bill keeps climbing

The cause is the auction itself. Rights fees are set by competing bidders who need subscribers to recoup their outlay, and they do that by raising prices or by holding back content from cheaper tiers. The cost lands on the viewer. The fragmentation is partly deliberate, because exclusive content is what persuades a household to take out a second subscription.

Ticket prices work the same way. Clubs that depend on broadcast money for much of their income still face pressure to raise match-day prices as wage and operating costs rise. Fans therefore pay twice, once for the stadium seat and again for the screens at home.

The specific monthly prices of the current packages are not confirmed here. Figures change with promotions and contract terms, so they should be checked against each broadcaster's own listings before anyone relies on them.

What to watch

The pressure will not ease before the cycle ends in 2029, and the next rights auction will test whether bidders still want to pay at this level. The regulatory question is whether ministers will treat the cost of watching live sport as a consumer issue as well as a sporting one. Until that happens, the main choice a fan has is how many of the bills to pay.
