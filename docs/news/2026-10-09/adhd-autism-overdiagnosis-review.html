Government review warns of ADHD and autism over-diagnosis and 'system failures' in NHS services

In one sentence
A government review says it cannot conclude ADHD and autism are over-diagnosed at scale but warns the risk is growing, blaming system failures that make diagnosis the gateway to support.

A government-commissioned review of mental health, ADHD and autism has concluded that Britain may be approaching a point where the risk of over-diagnosis is real. It stops short of saying that over-diagnosis is happening. Its sharper finding concerns the NHS itself. Families are pushed towards diagnosis because help is locked behind it, and the review calls that a system failure.

The distinction matters, and some headlines have blurred it. The review's wording, as reported, is that "on the basis of evidence available to us we cannot conclude that overdiagnosis is occurring at scale", while trends suggest the risk is growing. This article relies on press accounts of the report, which is reported to run to 607 pages. The GOV.UK collection page, when checked, listed only the interim report (10 June 2026) and the terms of reference (4 December 2025), not the final text. Exact wording below should be read with that caveat.

What the review found

The review was led by Professor Peter Fonagy of University College London, with Professor Sir Simon Wessely and Professor Gillian Baird, both of King's College London, as vice-chairs. It was reported to draw on 120 experts.

Its central observation is a gap between measured prevalence and recorded diagnosis. The best evidence, as reported, puts ADHD at an estimated 3.8% of 5–15-year-olds and autism at 1.2% of young people. NHS records show 3.88% of 12–17-year-olds diagnosed with ADHD, a 15-fold rise since 2000. For autism, 4.6% of 6–11-year-olds are diagnosed, a 20-fold rise. The review is reported to say underlying prevalence of both conditions has not risen in step. Anxiety, by contrast, has surged, and genuine mental illness among younger people has increased, while severe conditions such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder have not.

Fonagy is quoted as saying the rise "cannot be explained by greater awareness alone", and also that "diagnosis remains essential and must be protected". Those two statements sit together uneasily, and the review appears to resolve them through incentives. In its account, a diagnosis has become the main way into support in schools and in employment. People therefore seek one even where it may not describe their needs. Baird is quoted as saying a diagnosis "doesn't tell you very much about a person's needs".

A system, or the lack of one

The demand figures explain why the review reaches for system language. About 560,000 people had an open ADHD referral at the end of 2025, more than 17 times the 2019 figure. More than 254,000 had an open autism referral in December 2025. The NHS outsources about half of ADHD assessments and over a third of autism assessments to private firms. Baird is quoted as saying only 14% of people receiving an NHS diagnosis get any follow-up care.

Taken together, those numbers describe a pipeline that spends heavily on assessment and thinly on what follows. Wessely, asked about blame, said: "It's system failures we're talking about." The review is also reported to say that around 20% of school pupils identify as neurodivergent, many without a diagnosis. Whether that figure reflects unmet need or a widening of the category is the question the review itself says it cannot settle.

The cost context is large. The review is reported to put the annual cost of mental ill health in England at about £300bn, including £110bn to the economy and £60bn to the NHS. Mental illness is reported to affect 23% of working-age adults, and about one in four 16–24-year-olds, up from roughly one in six. These are the review's estimates as reported, and the method behind them has not been checked here.

Recommendations and the dispute

The review is reported to call for "a new model of care and radical reform", so that support for daily living, education and employment does not depend on a medical diagnosis. It also proposes reviewing assessment waiting lists to prioritise higher need, support for people with moderate needs while they wait, and earlier help for symptoms such as anxiety, impulse control and poor concentration. The government response, as reported, would bring private ADHD and autism assessment providers under Care Quality Commission regulation. Health Secretary Yvette Cooper said services "need to change to focus more on the early support young people need". NHS England's mental health director, Dr Nick Broughton, said the NHS "is ready to play its part".

The politics are contested. The review was commissioned in December 2025 amid concern over rising sickness and incapacity benefit claims, and critics, including the charity Contact, have argued that rising numbers reflect better recognition rather than over-diagnosis. Benefits and Work reported fears that the review's timetable was tied to a separate review of disability benefits. An NHS-backed ADHD taskforce report in 2025 described ADHD as "under-recognised, under-diagnosed and under-treated". The two claims can both be true, since a system can over-assess some people while leaving others unserved. Mind's chief executive, Dr Sarah Hughes, called the report "a watershed moment for change".

The review therefore moves the argument from access to appropriateness without winning it. The measurable finding is structural: diagnosis has become the gate to support, and the gate is jammed. Whether the government delivers needs-based support outside it, and on what budget, is the point to watch. The detail will come in the formal response, any change to disability benefit rules, and the CQC's approach to private assessors.
