'Black box' wastewater trade earns UK water utilities millions, Guardian reports

In one sentence
A Guardian investigation says UK water companies earned over £340m last year taking industrial wastewater, in a trade with no independent regulator or central register.

Water companies in the UK are paid to take industrial and commercial wastewater into sewage works that were mostly built to treat household sewage. A Guardian investigation published on 9 October says that trade earned the sector more than £340m last year. It also says almost nothing about the trade is visible to the public.

The reporting, by Rachel Salvidge with Watershed Investigations and the campaign group River Action, was read here through a syndicated copy. The original Guardian page was not accessed. Figures and responses below are as reported there.

What the investigation found

The waste comes from power stations, pharmaceutical plants, food factories, hospitals, abattoirs and waste facilities. The Guardian puts the volume entering sewage works at about 608bn litres a year.

No independent regulator oversees the trade-effluent process, and there is no central register of who sends what to which works. The investigators therefore built their own database of 26,000 consents. An unnamed industry expert called the system "a bit of a black box" and said companies have a financial incentive to accept too much toxic material.

By company, the reporting says:

  • Revenue: United Utilities earned the most from industrial waste, at £61.6m, followed by Yorkshire Water at £48.8m. Anglian Water's income from the trade rose 62% over five years.
  • Volume: Thames Water accepts the most permitted industrial sewage, at 113bn litres, ahead of United Utilities (105bn) and Severn Trent (79bn).
  • Caveat on comparisons: Welsh Water, Scottish Water and Wessex Water said they route tankered commercial waste through separate group companies, so their figures are not directly comparable.

Why treatment is the concern

Most sewage works have only biological treatment. The investigation says that cannot remove industrial chemicals such as PFAS, flame retardants, solvents and pharmaceuticals. Some permits list cyanide, chromium, cadmium and mercury.

What survives treatment goes into rivers, lakes and seas. What is captured in sludge is spread on farmland. Industrial flows also add to the load on sewers, which the Guardian links to raw sewage spills.

An Environment Agency officer cited in the report named landfill leachate as the agency's greatest concern, saying checks often miss contaminants. Davyhulme in Manchester (United Utilities) and Avonmouth (Wessex Water) are named as major receivers of tankered waste, including leachate.

Responses

The responses disagree about where the fault lies.

  • Water UK said companies are legally obliged to accept trade effluent and that Ofwat sets the charges. It argued the Environment Agency's permit conditions ignore sewers' inability to treat toxic substances, and called for a ban on such substances and for manufacturers to pay removal costs.
  • United Utilities said it has strict consent and control processes and takes tankered waste, including leachate, only at permitted sites.
  • The Environment Agency said the framework is more than 30 years old and that it is working to improve how contaminants are managed.
  • Defra said water companies should not profit from pollution and pointed to continuing reform of wastewater disposal.
  • River Action's Amy Fairman said industrial pollution should not be a profitable business for water companies. She urged the government to address the trade through the Clean Water Bill.

What to watch

The trade is lawful and companies are obliged to accept it. The open questions are whether the revenue creates an incentive to take waste the works cannot treat, and who should police that. The Clean Water Bill is the vehicle River Action is pressing. Whether ministers add a register or independent oversight of trade effluent to it is the next test. The investigation reports no finding of wrongdoing by any named company.
