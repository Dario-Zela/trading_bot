Court rules warrants used to search Prince Andrew's homes were unlawful

In one sentence
The High Court quashed the warrants used to search Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor's homes, which police conceded were unlawfully issued, but the judge said the investigation itself continues.

The High Court has quashed the search warrants that Thames Valley Police used on properties linked to Andrew Mountbatten-Windsor, the former Duke of York. The police did not fight the point: according to reports, the force and Mountbatten-Windsor agreed the warrants had been unlawfully issued. The ruling does not end the criminal investigation.

What the court decided

Mr Justice Hilliard formally quashed the warrants on Thursday 8 October. They had been issued by a judge at the Central Criminal Court on 18 February 2026 and were used to search Royal Lodge, near Windsor Castle, and Wood Farm on the Sandringham estate. Police carried out the searches after arresting Mountbatten-Windsor on suspicion of misconduct in public office. The arrest came on his 66th birthday. He was released the same day and remains under investigation. He denies wrongdoing.

Reports say the force accepted that the legislation it relied on to obtain the warrants was the wrong basis, and that the Old Bailey judge's decision to grant them under that provision was therefore mistaken. The full written reasoning has not been seen by this newspaper, and accounts of the concession differ in detail.

The challenge was filed in early October. His lawyers argued that officers had taken private material unconnected to the criminal case. His attorney, Mark Stephens, said earlier this week that a finding in his client's favour could stop authorities using evidence found in the searches in any prosecution. He described Thursday's private hearing as a preliminary skirmish in a wider legal battle.

What it does not do

The judge was careful about the limits of his ruling. "The fact that the search warrants were quashed does not bring an investigation to an end or mean that the whole of the investigation was unlawful," he said. He also indicated that some of the seized material could stay with investigators, and the ruling does not automatically require everything taken to be returned.

This matters because quashing a warrant and excluding evidence are separate questions. A warrant that was unlawfully issued makes the search itself unlawful. But English courts have discretion over whether material obtained that way can still be used, and a trial judge would decide that if charges were ever brought. No charge has been brought.

Thames Valley Police said it is working to find a legal route to keep the material it seized. A spokesperson said the decision on the warrants is not a verdict on the merits of the investigation.

What comes next

The immediate contest is over the seized material. Mountbatten-Windsor's side will want it returned or excluded, while police will look for another lawful basis to hold on to it. The outcome will indicate how much of the case built on the February searches survives. The allegation under investigation is that he passed sensitive documents to the late financier Jeffrey Epstein while working as a UK trade envoy. Whether the Crown Prosecution Service decides to charge will depend partly on that evidence.
