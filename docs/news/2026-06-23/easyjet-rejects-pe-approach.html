easyJet rejects private equity approach, questioning LBO economics for budget airlines

An easyJet Airbus A320-214 on approach — the carrier's fleet renewal programme is central to management's case for rejecting Castlelake's bids. — Anna Zvereva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
easyJet rejected three Castlelake bids totalling up to £4.74bn, citing opportunistic timing and structural concerns that expose the thin LBO economics of budget-airline deals.

easyJet's board has rejected three escalating bids from US firm Castlelake, calling the 625p-a-share offer an attempt to acquire the airline "on the cheap" while its valuation is temporarily depressed.

easyJet's board has given Castlelake LP three chances to make its numbers work, and declined each one. The British budget carrier on 21 June turned down the US alternative investment firm's third and highest proposal — 625 pence a share, valuing easyJet at roughly £4.74 billion ($6.26 billion) — calling it "an opportunistic attempt to acquire easyJet on the cheap."

The progression of bids — 560p, then 600p, then 625p, submitted across barely a week — carries a 59 per cent premium to easyJet's undisturbed share price of 394.2p on 28 May, the day before Castlelake first surfaced its interest. Yet on Monday, shares were trading at 521p — well below the ceiling. The gap signals that markets do not believe a formal offer is coming. Dan Coatsworth of AJ Bell noted the price reaction showed investors doubting Castlelake will succeed.

Where the economics break down

Budget airlines are operationally lean and capital-intensive in equal measure, a combination that makes LBO arithmetic difficult even at depressed valuations. Castlelake — a Minneapolis-based alternatives manager with roughly $38 billion under management, built around aviation finance and specialty credit — cannot itself hold controlling interest in an EU carrier. Its proposed acquisition structure would give Castlelake 49%, with the remaining 51% held by European investors whose identities easyJet's board said were insufficiently disclosed. That opacity, alongside the leverage levels and attached conditions, drew explicit concern from the board, which also cited "considerable regulatory, financial and other execution challenges."

A secondary complication: the share price Castlelake is targeting reflects near-term headwinds — jet fuel elevated by Middle East conflict, softer consumer sentiment, geopolitical disruption — not the earnings trajectory easyJet's management believes is visible from here.

The company has outlined a medium-term profit target of more than £1 billion before tax, underpinned by a fleet replacement that is already under way. Seventeen new Airbus A320neo and A321neo aircraft arrive in fiscal 2026, with 73 more scheduled through 2028 as 79 older A319s retire. The seat-capacity and fuel-efficiency numbers shift materially as the mix changes. The holidays division — capital-light, already past its £250 million profit target — carries a new bar of £450 million by 2030.

If PE cannot get the economics to work at a 59 per cent premium to a depressed print for a carrier with an investment-grade balance sheet, net cash, and a scaling ancillary business, the implied floor for sector entry is uncomfortably high. The leverage required to justify the bid premium into acceptable returns runs directly into the same fuel volatility and thin-margin dynamics that suppressed the valuation in the first place.

Castlelake faces a "put up or shut up" deadline of 5pm Thursday, 26 June, under UK takeover rules. It must either announce a formal firm intention to make an offer or stand down for six months. If it walks, the next test of PE appetite in European short-haul aviation will need a materially different entry price — or a different macro environment entirely.
