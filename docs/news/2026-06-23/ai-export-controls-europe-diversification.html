US AI export curbs accelerate Europe's push to escape hyperscaler dependency

The European Commission's Brussels headquarters: EU officials proposed the Cloud and AI Development Act on 3 June, a sovereignty-oriented procurement framework that would restrict non-EU providers from higher-tier public-sector AI contracts — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US export controls on frontier AI models are accelerating European corporate diversification away from US hyperscalers, giving sovereign-AI challengers their clearest structural opening yet.

On the evening of 12 June, a US government directive arrived at Anthropic's San Francisco offices. By the following morning, two of the most capable AI models in commercial deployment — Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5 — were unavailable to customers worldwide.

The directive, the first known use of US export control authorities to regulate a specific frontier AI model on national security grounds, cited official concern that a method had been found to bypass Fable 5's cybersecurity guardrails. Unable to segment users by nationality in real time, Anthropic disabled both models globally rather than selectively. Access was restored within days. The argument the shutdown triggered will take considerably longer to settle.

"Sovereignty often gets confused with autarky," said Cedrik Neike, chief executive of Siemens' Digital Industries division, speaking at VivaTech in Paris five days later. "Autarky is absolutely not the way to do it." He was not arguing against independence from US providers. He was arguing for something more systematic: a portfolio.

Dependency made visible

Neike was not alone at VivaTech. Executives from Renault Group, Orange, ChapsVision, SAP, Sopra Steria and Capgemini told Reuters at last week's conference that they already run AI workloads across multiple providers — American, European and Chinese — specifically to hedge against precisely what had just occurred.

Renault's approach is representative. The carmaker runs models from Google, Microsoft, Mistral AI, DeepSeek and Dataiku concurrently. A company spokesman cited "a sharp rise in AI token costs" alongside access risk as motivation to spread the load. Christel Heydemann, chief executive of Orange, called on Europe to build AI it could "access, govern and challenge on its own terms" — services that would not be "switched off on a whim." ChapsVision put it more plainly: the company defines sovereignty as having "a credible alternative ready if a key service is cut," and maintains parallel pipelines from Mistral, Anthropic, OpenAI and Qwen accordingly.

Capgemini noted that most AI providers are already adapting their offerings beyond remote-access-only models to address European dependency concerns — though characterised this as "a work in progress."

The structural position underlying these concerns is stark. US hyperscalers control roughly 70% of European cloud infrastructure revenue. European data-centre capacity sits below 10 gigawatts against roughly 60GW in the United States. For most of the past decade, that asymmetry was categorised as strategic dependency — a concern for policy papers. The Anthropic shutdown moved it into the operational risk column.

Brussels responds with rules

The European Commission had already moved. On 3 June — nine days before the directive arrived — it proposed the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), a sovereignty-oriented procurement framework for EU public institutions that introduces a four-tier classification system for cloud providers.

At higher tiers, providers must demonstrate EU ownership, EU operational control and independence from third-country interference. The most stringent level demands full software supply-chain transparency with no third-country override capability. Non-EU providers have been direct about the implications: the Computer & Communications Industry Association called the draft "discriminatory" and warned of severe market fragmentation. The Commission frames it as a sovereignty baseline.

CADA must still clear European Parliament and Council — a trilogue process that typically runs 12 to 36 months. But the sequence of events — sovereignty framework proposed, frontier-model export control applied, European boardrooms visibly shaken — has given the legislation a rhetorical momentum its architects could not have planned. Retaliatory trade measures have been discussed on the margins; none have moved to formal proposal. The nearer-term consequence is the signal: EU public procurement is adjusting, and adjustment on the demand side tends to reshape supplier decisions faster than legislation alone.

The market that emerges

Mistral AI, the French large-model company, tripled its enterprise business in May 2025 and has since secured an €830 million debt facility to fund a 44-megawatt data centre at Bruyères-le-Châtel, south of Paris. At VivaTech this week, Mistral and Nvidia confirmed Mistral Compute — a sovereign GPU cloud platform for European enterprises, backed by 18,000 Grace Blackwell Superchips already partially operational. The framing was deliberate: sovereign infrastructure, not merely European software running on American compute.

In April, Cohere and Germany's Aleph Alpha announced a merger implying a combined valuation of roughly $20 billion, anchored by a $600 million commitment from Schwarz Group, the retail conglomerate behind Lidl and Kaufland. The combined entity explicitly targets "sovereign AI" workloads, where enterprises and governments prioritise data residency and auditability over frontier performance. McKinsey's March estimate put sovereign AI demand at $600 billion of the projected $1 trillion global enterprise AI market by 2030; Europe is expected to account for $180–200 billion of that figure.

These remain asymmetric comparisons. The four major US hyperscalers are committing a combined $725 billion in AI infrastructure capital expenditure this year alone. But the moat those investments build is partly a remotely delivered dependency — and European enterprises have just received a demonstration of what that dependency costs when policy decisions intervene.

European sovereign cloud infrastructure spending is forecast at roughly $12.6 billion in 2026, up 83% year-on-year by Gartner's estimate — still an order of magnitude below US hyperscaler CapEx, but the direction of travel is the point. The CADA trilogue and Mistral Compute's commercial ramp are the near-term catalysts to watch. If CADA survives negotiation with its sovereignty tiers substantively intact, the procurement reallocation it drives will be structural rather than cyclical — and the cloud sector valuations that assume durable European lock-in will need revisiting.
