House committee advances bipartisan Kids Internet and Digital Safety Act

Rep. Brett Guthrie (R-KY), Chair of the House Energy and Commerce Committee, co-authored the bipartisan KIDS Act deal with ranking member Frank Pallone Jr. — U.S. House of Representatives / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
House Energy and Commerce Committee strikes bipartisan deal on omnibus kids' safety bill, imposing algorithmic opt-outs and COPPA extension to age 17.

The House Energy and Commerce Committee announced a bipartisan deal on Monday on its omnibus kids' internet safety package — a development that breaks a months-long partisan impasse and puts federal restrictions on how platforms monetize minors meaningfully closer to becoming law.

From Party Lines to a Joint Statement

The bill that emerged on June 22 is a revised version of the Kids Internet and Digital Safety Act (H.R. 7757), a sprawling package of more than a dozen measures that the committee had advanced in March on a strictly party-line vote. Republicans passed it without Democratic support then; Monday's announcement represents a genuine negotiated settlement.

Committee Chair Brett Guthrie (R-Ky.) and ranking member Frank Pallone Jr. (D-N.J.) said they "have now found common ground on policies to significantly improve the digital environment for kids." The language is anodyne, but the act of joint authorship carries procedural weight: bills that reach the floor under suspension of the rules — the path Guthrie has indicated — require a two-thirds majority. A party-line bill cannot clear that bar.

The price of Pallone's signature was visible in the revised text. The new version incorporates COPPA 2.0 — an update to the 1998 children's privacy law — extending its protections from children under 13 to users under 17. It adds a federal data broker registry, a Pallone priority: data brokers must register with the Federal Trade Commission, pay at least $22,500 annually, and disclose what categories of data they sell and whether they have suffered recent breaches. The preemption language was also revised to set a federal floor rather than a ceiling, preserving the ability of states that have already moved aggressively on minors' privacy to maintain standards above the federal baseline.

What the Package Would Actually Do

The KIDS Act consolidates more than a dozen separate bills into a single legislative vehicle. The Kids Online Safety Act, incorporated as Title 2, would require social media platforms to default minors' accounts to maximum privacy settings and dismantle the addictive design features — infinite scroll, auto-play video, notification nudges — that drive compulsive engagement. Algorithmic recommendation feeds would be opt-out by default for children. A tiered knowledge standard governs compliance: smaller companies are held to an "actual knowledge" test, while larger platforms must obtain parental consent if they "willfully disregarded information that would lead a reasonable and prudent person" to identify a user as a minor.

Separate titles address direct and ephemeral messaging — disappearing-message features restricted for users under 17 — online gaming, and AI chatbots that interact with minors. The App Store Accountability Act, also included, would require parental consent for under-18s to download age-restricted applications.

What the bill does not do matters as much as what it does. Unlike the Senate version of KOSA, the House package omits a "duty of care" provision that would have legally required platforms to exercise reasonable care to prevent harms to minors. That omission was enough to bring Pallone aboard — and it ensures that the House and Senate versions remain substantively different, guaranteeing a conference negotiation if both chambers act.

Quantifiable Risk, Uncertain Timeline

For the platforms most exposed — Meta, Alphabet's YouTube, Snap, and TikTok — the bill's passage would impose structural costs: compliance infrastructure for age verification and parental consent flows, forced changes to recommendation algorithms for a substantial user segment, and restricted behavioral advertising. Minors generate a disproportionate share of engagement time on short-video and social platforms even where they do not constitute the majority of declared advertising revenue, precisely because platforms have never been legally required to differentiate them at the design layer.

The bipartisan imprimatur matters for risk pricing. The March markup produced a bill that could plausibly die in partisan gridlock; Monday's agreement narrows that escape route. A suspension vote with broad Democratic backing also reduces the political cover available for Senate inaction. That said, the Senate's retained preference for a duty-of-care standard — and the absence of any scheduled Senate floor action — means the realistic timeline for any enacted version likely extends beyond the current session.

The KIDS Act has now survived committee and, as of this week, the more durable test of bipartisan negotiation. The next measurable checkpoint is a House floor suspension vote. If it clears the two-thirds threshold, pressure on the Senate to advance a reconcilable version will be considerably harder to defer.
