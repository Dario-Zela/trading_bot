STANDING WATCHES · IRAN-HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-06-23 · 3 min read

Iran: $12bn unfreezes, inspection claim surfaces, crude timeline firms

The Strait of Hormuz from 35,000 feet — the waterway at the centre of the US-Iran diplomatic framework carries roughly a fifth of global seaborne oil supply — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Iran's $12bn frozen asset release — signed in Switzerland — is the first cash yield of the US-Iran thaw, with a 60-day crude waiver and Hormuz hotline now live.

The most concrete financial fruit of the US-Iran diplomatic thaw materialised Tuesday, when Tehran's chief negotiator Abbas Araghchi confirmed the formal signing ceremony releasing $12 billion in previously frozen Iranian assets — two tranches of $6 billion each — following high-level talks at Switzerland's Bürgenstock resort. The money, immobilised under successive rounds of sanctions, begins flowing as part of a broader memorandum of understanding signed by President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian, with the asset release ceremony marking the first hard cash to clear the ice since US-Iranian hostilities opened in late February.

The asset release arrived alongside a 60-day US Treasury licence permitting the production, sale, and delivery of Iranian crude in US dollars, effective until 21 August. Energy consultancy Rystad Energy estimates Iran could push exports to roughly two million barrels per day under the window — about one-third above pre-conflict output. Brent crude slipped 5.1 per cent to $78.92 a barrel and WTI fell 6 per cent to $75.94 as markets absorbed the prospect of incremental supply. Whether that repricing is complete is an open question: ramp-up timelines for dormant wells are notoriously difficult to forecast, and the licence is explicitly temporary, pending a final agreement.

The Strait and the Clock

A direct communication channel between Washington and Tehran — announced by mediating parties Qatar and Pakistan after last weekend's talks — is now operational to coordinate commercial vessel movements through the Strait of Hormuz. The waterway carries roughly a fifth of global seaborne oil supply; the channel's stated purpose is to prevent accidental military escalation during the interim 60 days, and commercial traffic is already transiting.

The clock carries a conditional threat attached. Trump has said that if no final deal materialises, the United States may begin collecting tolls at Hormuz, with Washington claiming the right to levy a transit charge of up to 20 per cent of oil value. The statement briefly lifted S&P 500 and Dow futures and pushed energy sector equities higher, with BP shares outperforming a broadly flat FTSE 100 as traders interpreted the Iran thaw as a net positive for energy names even as crude prices declined.

Inspections, Gaps, and Regional Fault Lines

Trump posted on Truth Social that Iran had agreed to "major weapons inspections" extending "long into the future." The claim has found only partial corroboration from the US side itself: Vice President JD Vance, who led the American delegation in Bürgenstock, said IAEA inspectors would enter Iran "this week." Iranian officials have confirmed the principle of inspections in broad terms without affirming the specific US framing. That gap — between Washington's characterisation and Tehran's — is not unusual at this stage of complex negotiations, but it leaves markets pricing off imprecise inputs when assessing the durability of any eventual settlement.

Regional responses split along familiar lines. Gulf Arab states, whose economies bore the heaviest collateral damage from the Hormuz blockade, welcomed de-escalation while remaining privately unnerved by what Iran's survival of the conflict signals about Washington as a security guarantor. Israel's position is sharper: the deal permits Iran to continue enriching uranium at some level, and by linking the agreement's architecture to events in Lebanon, Tehran has effectively constrained Israeli freedom of action in the north at a moment of domestic political sensitivity for Prime Minister Netanyahu.

The 21 August expiry of the Treasury licence is the next hard deadline. A permanent settlement would need to resolve enrichment caps, the inspection architecture Trump has claimed, and a broader sanctions rollback — all within a 60-day technical negotiating track that has barely begun.
