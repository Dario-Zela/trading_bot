BofA and Wells Fargo both lift ASML targets in the same session

ASML's corporate headquarters in Veldhoven, Netherlands — the nerve centre of the world's only EUV lithography business. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BofA ($2,345) and Wells Fargo ($2,200) both raised ASML targets on June 22, betting AI-driven equipment demand outweighs the company's export-control exposure.

Two of Wall Street's larger research desks raised their ASML price targets on the same trading session Monday, an unusual show of unison that sent the Dutch chipmaking-equipment company's shares up more than 3 percent on the day. Bank of America lifted its target to $2,345 — implying roughly 22 percent upside from Thursday's close — while Wells Fargo moved its own figure to $2,200, up from $1,750, a jump of 26 percent in a single revision.

The pair of upgrades landed just days after a Bloomberg report, published June 19, that Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had met with ASML's senior leadership to express concern that one of the company's extreme ultraviolet machines may have found its way into China in violation of US-led export controls. ASML flatly denied it — "ASML has never shipped an EUV machine to China nor have we shipped to China any component, module or equipment specially designed to be used in an EUV machine" — but the episode is a reminder that the company remains a live target for geopolitical pressure. That both banks moved their targets higher in the immediate aftermath says something about how the institutional view of ASML's risk profile has shifted.

Fundamentals doing the heavy lifting

Bank of America's rationale leaned on production economics. The firm cited higher EUV unit forecasts and growing confidence in ASML's earnings power through 2028, and expects the company's order book to be fully committed for 2027 by the time second-quarter results land on July 15. The implication is that demand is running so far ahead of supply that near-term policy noise does not materially alter the long-run trajectory.

Wells Fargo analyst Joe Quatrochi framed it more broadly: another quarter of positive results in the semiconductor capital equipment industry, reinforcing that demand trends among leading chipmakers remain intact. The subtext is AI infrastructure spending, which has pushed ASML's customers — TSMC, Samsung, Intel — to commit to capacity expansion plans that require more EUV tools than the company can currently manufacture.

ASML lifted its own 2026 revenue guidance to between €36 billion and €40 billion in April, up from an earlier range of €34 to €39 billion. The company says it can ship at least 60 low-NA EUV systems this year, up roughly 25 percent on 2025 volumes, and expects to reach 80 units in 2027 — and still expects supply to lag demand. Shares have risen around 80 percent year to date; the 12-month return sits near 155 percent.

What the targets concede — and what they don't

Neither bank is dismissing the export-control overhang. China, once ASML's largest single market at roughly 36 percent of net system sales in the fourth quarter of 2025, has shrunk to about 19 percent in the first quarter of 2026, and ASML itself guides for China to account for around 20 percent of 2026 revenue — down from 33 percent last year. The revenue mix has already been repriced.

What BofA and Wells Fargo appear to be betting on is that the non-China demand — from AI buildouts in the United States, Europe, and Taiwan — is large enough and durable enough to absorb that loss and then some. On current order-book evidence, that reading is difficult to argue against.

The next hard data point comes July 15, when ASML reports second-quarter results. If the order book does arrive fully booked for 2027, as BofA expects, the targets set Monday may look conservative before long.
