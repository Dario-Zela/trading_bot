Fed Chair Warsh faces Congress on 14 July

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, official portrait, June 2026 — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Kevin Warsh testifies to the House Financial Services Committee on 14 July — his first statutory appearance as Fed Chair — with no disclosed agenda and no forward guidance to deploy.

Kevin Warsh makes his first appearance before Congress as Federal Reserve Chair on 14 July, when he testifies before the House Financial Services Committee at 10 a.m. Eastern. The Senate Banking Committee follows the next day. Both are required by law — the biannual Humphrey-Hawkins ritual that every Fed chair endures and every trading desk circles on the calendar, hoping for a slip.

There is no announced agenda, no disclosed line of questioning, no signal from either chamber about what it intends to press him on. That will change.

The backdrop he's walking into

The hearing matters because of what Warsh did — and pointedly did not do — at his first FOMC meeting on 17 June. The committee held rates for the fourth consecutive time at the 3.5%–3.75% target, but the drift inside the room was unmistakably hawkish: nine of eighteen FOMC participants now pencil in at least one rate hike before year-end, a sharp reversal from March, when no policymaker had forecast a hike and the committee still leaned toward a cut. Stock markets fell; short-term rates jumped.

Warsh then declined, explicitly, to offer forward guidance. Pressed on the outlook, he demurred — citing the danger of financial markets simply "reflecting back what we've said," which he described as the Fed blinding itself to its most important information source. He also mused aloud about scaling back press conferences. He declined to participate in the dot plot at all.

All of which means that when Warsh takes his seat before French Hill's committee, he arrives without the standard central-banker toolkit: no guidance to dispense, no forecasts to shade, no carefully calibrated phrase to run out the clock with. The committee room is an unforgiving environment for a chair who has declared deliberate silence to be strategy.

The institutional backdrop adds texture. Hill did not schedule Warsh's predecessor, Jerome Powell, for the February 2026 testimony slot — an unusual omission that followed a since-suspended Department of Justice inquiry into statements Powell had made in his July 2025 Senate appearance. A federal judge later deemed that investigation "an inappropriate effort to pressure him to lower rates or resign." The memory sits in that room.

What makes this a story

The testimony hardens into news if Congress presses on specifics: the five task forces Warsh announced to rethink the Fed's communications, balance sheet, data sourcing, and inflation framework; or the question raised by Citadel Securities in a note published the week prior, which flagged second-round inflation effects as making a September hike a genuine live risk. Any inflation data landing between now and mid-July will sharpen the backdrop further.

Absent a catalyst, 14 July is prelude rather than event — a first formal accounting before Congress, and Warsh's first open-mic moment since his FOMC press conference established that saying nothing was, for now, the plan.
