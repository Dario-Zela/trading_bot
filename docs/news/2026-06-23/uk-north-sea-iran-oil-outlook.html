Iranian crude rebound prompts North Sea strategy rethink in Westminster

A North Sea drilling rig — the policy case for expanding UK offshore production has shifted sharply as Iranian crude returns to global markets. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Iranian crude flowing again after the US-Iran deal upends North Sea expansion logic, with Brent at $77 and BoE rate guidance hanging on energy's next move.

The interim US-Iran peace deal that cracked the Strait of Hormuz open last week has done more than move a price chart. It has thrown the working logic of UK North Sea policy into question — and Westminster has not yet caught up.

Throughout the spring, as Brent crude spiked toward $144 a barrel and petrol prices returned to their 2022 peaks, the case for expanding North Sea production had a certain political gravity. The oil lobby, union officials and a faction of Labour backbenchers all argued that Rosebank and Jackdaw — two offshore fields awaiting ministerial approval following a January court ruling that their original consents were unlawful — were both an energy-security imperative and a cost-of-living gesture. Chancellor Rachel Reeves declared herself "very happy" to back both fields. Energy Secretary Ed Miliband was markedly less enthusiastic, particularly about Rosebank.

Then the market turned. The US Treasury issued a 60-day sanctions waiver on Iranian crude on Monday, and ships carrying roughly 10 million barrels have since emerged from the Strait. Brent fell more than 3.5% in a single session to $77 a barrel. Goldman Sachs puts its fourth-quarter Brent target at $60, against a structural 2026 oversupply it estimates at around two million barrels per day.

Output versus asset value

That backdrop makes the Rosebank-Jackdaw decision considerably less straightforward. The energy-security argument was built for a tight market. In a world of expected oversupply, maximizing North Sea output means adding supply into a price environment already under downward pressure, compressing the margin on every barrel and eroding the tax revenue the Treasury was counting on. North Sea investment is already expected to fall below $3.5 billion this year — its lowest real-terms level since the 1970s. A sustained Brent decline would test the economics of fields with long development lead times and high extraction costs even further.

The counter-argument has a mercantilist logic: if global supply is rising and prices are going to fall regardless, UK producers may as well take their share. Falling energy costs also work directly into UK inflation dynamics. The MPC voted 7-2 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% at its June 17 meeting — the two dissenters pushed for a hike to 4% — and the Bank's central forecast has CPI reaching 3.25% in the fourth quarter, up from 2.8% in May. The MPC noted that global energy prices had fallen since its previous meeting but remain elevated and volatile. A sustained Iranian supply revival would speed the downward leg of that trajectory, and with it the arguments for an earlier cut rather than a further rise.

The 60-day US-Iran memorandum of understanding expires in the third week of August. Miliband is expected to rule on Jackdaw before the summer recess. Both decisions will be made against a backdrop of continued oil price volatility and an OPEC group yet to signal how aggressively it intends to defend market share against returning Iranian barrels. The August MPC meeting is the next fixed point on the monetary policy calendar.
