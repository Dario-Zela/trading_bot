L'Oréal: historical data is a deeper AI moat than speed

L'Oréal operates across more than 150 countries — and the proprietary data accumulated through those operations is central to its AI strategy. — Brejnev / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
L'Oréal's innovation chief says 40 years of proprietary beauty data gives incumbents a structural AI edge over faster-moving startups.

L'Oréal's innovation chief Delphine Viguier-Hovasse argues that AI tilts the competitive balance toward legacy companies — not because they move faster, but because they've spent decades building data sets that new entrants simply cannot replicate overnight.

The argument is almost heretical by the standards of the tech industry's past two decades. Speed, disruption, the relentless iteration of the young challenger — these have been the accepted axioms of competitive strategy since at least 2010. Then last Sunday, in a Fortune interview, Viguier-Hovasse — L'Oréal's Chief Innovation and Prospective Officer — offered a different theory: "AI is fantastic for old companies."

The logic runs as follows. AI systems improve with data volume and quality. Established enterprises have been generating proprietary data — on customers, products, and operations — for decades. That data, cleaned and organised, becomes a feedstock that newcomers cannot purchase or shortcut their way to. L'Oréal has been accumulating usable data "not necessarily over the past 120 years, but over the past 40 years," Viguier-Hovasse said. New brands, by contrast, can launch quickly but arrive at the AI table with shallow histories.

What 40 years of beauty data actually looks like

Viguier-Hovasse offered specifics that are worth taking seriously. L'Oréal maintains a global database on women's skin colour, an "atlas on wrinkles," and data on how hair behaves across climates and environments — assets built through clinical trials, consumer panels, and retail feedback accumulated over generations of product development. Last year alone, the company ran 40,000 stability tests on formulations and 44,000 consumer-reaction tests on a single skin cream.

The practical payoff, she argued, is already measurable. Testing 20 molecules on hair once took several years; AI-assisted screening now compresses that to three months. Speed of execution still matters, but it is now the incumbent that is accelerating — not the startup racing to gain a foothold.

L'Oréal registered 725 patents this year and ranked sixth on Fortune's 2026 list of Europe's Most Innovative Companies. Research absorbs roughly 3% of annual turnover — a figure that, across €43 billion in revenue, funds a formidable discovery pipeline.

The investment question

For capital allocators, the claim matters most if it holds across sectors beyond beauty — financials, healthcare, and consumer packaged goods all sit on decades of proprietary transaction and operational data. The incumbent-as-AI-winner thesis would justify a rotation away from high-multiple AI-native startups toward legacy enterprises with deep, clean data libraries.

The counterargument is that model generalisation and agent-native architectures may reduce the advantage of proprietary data over time, with foundation models trained on broad corpora closing the gap faster than Viguier-Hovasse's thesis assumes.

The test will be quantitative: whether L'Oréal's AI-accelerated pipeline generates measurably faster product cycles and margin expansion over the next two to three years. The patent count and research spend provide baseline comparisons. If the data moat thesis is real, the numbers will show it.
