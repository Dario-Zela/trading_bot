America's cottage cheese shortage is a private equity story

Cottage cheese, once a grocery afterthought, has become the unexpected test case for supply resilience in consolidated food manufacturing. — Rlsheehan / Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
A TikTok-driven protein craze has exposed how decades of private equity-driven dairy consolidation left the cottage cheese market with almost no slack capacity.

Dairy aisles at supermarkets across the United States have been running short of cottage cheese for the better part of two years. The tubs that do appear are often the wrong size, the wrong fat percentage, or the wrong brand. For a food that spent most of the last four decades sitting unloved beside the sour cream, it is a peculiar kind of crisis — but one that says something worth understanding about how American food manufacturing has been organised, and who benefits when that organisation starts to creak.

The protein boom nobody planned for

Cottage cheese's rehabilitation began on TikTok sometime in 2022, when influencers started blending it into protein-rich alternatives to ice cream, folding it into pasta sauces, and baking it into flatbreads. The results were not always photogenic but they were, apparently, persuasive. U.S. retail sales rose 11% in 2022, then 17% in each of 2023 and 2024, before climbing another 20% in the 52 weeks to mid-June 2025, according to Circana data. Average American consumption hit 2.4 pounds per person in 2024, the highest since 2009.

The brands positioned for exactly this kind of moment found themselves overwhelmed rather than triumphant. Good Culture, an Austin-based startup founded in 2014 that turned clean-label cultured dairy into a category of its own, saw revenues roughly double in a single year to around $200 million by the end of 2024. Andrew Westrich, marketing manager at Organic Valley, said the cooperative's cottage cheese was "selling faster than we can make it." Westby Cooperative Creamery in Wisconsin reported orders exceeding output by more than 30%.

None of this demand arrived invisibly. But the supply response has been slow in a way that reflects something structural rather than simply a momentary bottleneck. Building a new dairy processing facility, or retooling an existing one, takes at least 12 months — often longer, once food safety certification, equipment sourcing, and milk supply contract negotiations are factored in. When the category spike came, the industry had little slack to absorb it.

A consolidated kitchen

The dairy processing infrastructure that received the cottage cheese boom had spent the previous two decades consolidating, and not always through the most durable means.

Dean Foods, once the largest dairy processor in the United States, filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in November 2019. The company had spent years accumulating smaller fluid milk processors across the country, building market share through acquisition rather than organic efficiency. A shifting consumer landscape, structural declines in fluid milk consumption, and the weight of accumulated debt eventually overcame a business that had grown large but not particularly resilient. Dean Foods had already sold its Morningstar Foods division — maker of Friendship Cottage Cheese — to Saputo in 2013 for $1.45 billion, a sale that compressed the number of independent operators in the category well before the bankruptcy. Saputo consolidated Morningstar's ten manufacturing facilities into its broader North American network.

Borden Dairy, another American stalwart, filed for bankruptcy in January 2020, two years after KKR extended significant debt financing to the company. The debt load made it difficult for management to adapt the product mix as consumer preferences shifted. The pattern across both companies was consistent: leveraged capital entered dairy, promised scale efficiencies, and found those efficiencies difficult to deliver when the underlying market moved. The result has been fewer plants, less geographic redundancy, and a processing infrastructure with less flexibility than existed 20 years ago.

The top five producers in cottage cheese now account for roughly 42% of total sales volume — a concentrated structure in a category that not long ago was spread across many regional dairies. When demand exceeds expectation, there are fewer alternative suppliers to absorb the overflow.

The L Catterton bet

Into this supply-constrained environment stepped L Catterton, the private equity firm backed by LVMH and the Arnault family, managing roughly $34 billion in assets. In January 2026, L Catterton announced a majority investment in Good Culture at a valuation exceeding $500 million. The deal made Good Culture the most prominent cottage cheese brand under PE majority ownership, and the capital is being directed, at least in part, at the very shortage that made the brand attractive to investors in the first place. In June, Good Culture announced a new manufacturing partnership with the Michigan Milk Producers Association's facility in Remus — adding cottage cheese production capacity that had not previously existed there.

Critics of private equity in food manufacturing often focus on the debt-loading playbook — the kind that contributed to Borden's and Dean Foods' failures. The L Catterton thesis with Good Culture is different on the surface: growth investment rather than financial engineering, with the existing management team retained. CEO Jesse Merrill, who built Good Culture into a $500 million business from scratch, has said the firm is "just getting started."

But the broader question the cottage cheese shortage raises has not gone away. Daisy Brand, the largest cottage cheese producer in the United States by volume, has remained family-owned through five generations and is currently breaking ground on a new Iowa facility. That expansion is proceeding on roughly the same timeline as Good Culture's PE-funded capacity push. The difference is that Daisy's investment decisions have never had to clear a returns committee with a five-to-seven-year exit horizon — a structure that is, by design, less inclined to build reserve capacity against demand curves that haven't materialised yet.

New cottage cheese facilities are expected to come online across several producers by late 2026, which should relieve the worst of the shortages. Whether the category's increasingly PE-touched structure builds in the redundancy to handle the next unexpected demand shock is a question that will only be answered when one arrives.
