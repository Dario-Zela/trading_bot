Citadel Securities calls a September Fed hike, sending dividend strategists back to the drawing board

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC held rates steady on June 17 — but signalled a hawkish tilt. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Citadel Securities warns second-round wage and services inflation will force a September Fed rate hike, putting $22bn of Q1 dividend-stock inflows at risk.

The June pause that markets greeted as the beginning of an easing cycle may be nothing of the sort. That, at least, is the view from Citadel Securities, whose macro strategy team published a note this week arguing that the Federal Reserve is likely to resume raising interest rates in September — not cut them.

The call runs directly against the dominant post-pause narrative: that Chair Kevin Warsh, sworn in just weeks before his first FOMC meeting on June 17, would use the hold as cover to let inflation drift back toward target before pivoting to accommodation. Citadel Securities sees the sequence differently — and the data, it argues, are on its side.

The Persistence Problem

The firm's thesis centres on what it calls hysteresis — the idea that temporary shocks can leave durable marks on an economy long after the initial impulse fades. In the note, titled Hysteresis May Set Up a September Hike, Citadel macro strategist Frank Flight argues that the energy and supply-chain disruptions driving the first wave of price increases are now colliding with a set of structural amplifiers: easy financial conditions, a reaccelerating labour market, and a generational AI capital expenditure cycle the firm estimates at roughly $750 billion in 2026 alone, rising to $1.25 trillion in 2027.

The result, the note contends, is that the transmission of price pressure into wages and services — the so-called second-round effects — is still running. "The near-term boost to headline inflation from higher energy prices, together with renewed supply-chain disruption, is likely to catalyse second-round effects at a particularly vulnerable point in the cycle," Flight writes. Citadel already sees evidence of inflation expectations spilling over into the most cyclical parts of the labour market, where wages are accelerating meaningfully faster than the aggregate.

The data supply the context. Headline CPI printed at 4.2% year-on-year in May. Core CPI came in at 2.9%, with transportation services up 4.1% and shelter up 3.4% on the same basis. Producer prices were running at 6.5% annually. The Fed itself acknowledged the pressure: at the June meeting, policymakers raised their 2026 PCE inflation forecast from 2.7% to 3.6%, and 17 of 18 officials judged the risks to inflation to be tilted upward.

The dot plot, in other words, already told part of the Citadel story. The median official now expects the federal funds rate to end 2026 at 3.8%, a quarter-point above the current 3.50%–3.75% range and up from 3.4% in the March projections. Citadel goes further: the firm's Taylor Rule analysis suggests 75 basis points of hikes are warranted in 2026, implying September, December, and March moves. CME FedWatch data put the probability of a September increase at 63.3%.

A Crowded Trade Reconsidered

The rate outlook matters well beyond the bond market. In the first quarter of 2026, dividend-focused equity strategies attracted nearly $22 billion in net flows — the heaviest pace since the second quarter of 2022. The rationale was straightforward: as growth stocks wobbled on tariff uncertainty, utilities, consumer staples, and real-estate investment trusts offered yield and relative stability. Defensive rotation has a logic in an uncertain macro environment, and dividend ETFs absorbed the demand.

A confirmed hiking cycle would test that positioning hard. When Treasury yields rise, the premium that dividend stocks command over risk-free instruments compresses. Utilities and REITs — the sectors that dominate high-yield dividend ETFs — carry the longest duration and the highest sensitivity to rate moves. The 2022 experience is instructive and not reassuring: investors piled into dividend strategies at exactly the moment rate hikes began to erode their relative attractiveness, and the trade lagged through 2023 and 2024.

The parallel is imperfect — dividend yields are not a reason to sell on their own, and cash flows from quality companies remain real regardless of rate level. But the arithmetic of a September hike arriving when positioning is stretched is not comfortable for strategists who built defensive books on the assumption that rates had peaked. A 25-basis-point hike would push the two-year Treasury yield higher, tightening the spread that makes high-yield equity income look attractive by comparison.

What to Watch Next

Three dates now anchor the macro calendar. On 14 July, Warsh faces the House Financial Services Committee in his first congressional testimony as Chair — a venue where any signal about the July FOMC statement's tone will move rate futures. Then in late August, the PCE inflation release for July will offer the cleanest forward-looking read on whether services disinflation is materialising or stalling; given that shelter and transportation services are still printing above 3%, the latter looks more likely on current trends. Finally, the September 16–17 FOMC meeting is when Citadel Securities' call either resolves or does not.

If the firm is right — if wages stay sticky, services prices keep printing above 3%, and the Taylor Rule gap stays open — the question for dividend strategists will not be whether to reposition. It will be how much time remains to do it.
