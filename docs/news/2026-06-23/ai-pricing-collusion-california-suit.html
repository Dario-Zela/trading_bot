BP, Marathon, 7-Eleven and Walmart sued over AI-driven petrol price coordination

California gasoline prices near Sonoma in April 2026 — weeks before regulators issued subpoenas to station owners over elevated pump costs — Sarah Stierch / CC0
In one sentence
California drivers sue BP, Marathon, 7-Eleven, and Walmart, alleging Kalibrate's shared AI pricing tool coordinated pump prices at 1,700+ stations — inflating gasoline by up to 22¢/gallon.

Four of the largest names in American fuel retail — BP, Marathon Petroleum, 7-Eleven, and Walmart — were named as defendants Monday in a proposed class action filed in Sacramento federal court, accused of using a shared artificial-intelligence pricing tool to coordinate gasoline prices across more than 1,700 California filling stations. The lawsuit, brought by a group of California drivers, also names Kalibrate Fuel Systems, the UK-based software company whose algorithm sits at the centre of the alleged scheme. If the legal theory holds, the case could force courts and regulators to confront a question that antitrust law has never directly resolved: when an autonomous system makes pricing decisions on behalf of competing companies, who is responsible?

"While families struggle to afford the commute to work, defendants have conspired to put an end to competition, joining an AI-powered trust to ensure that no matter where a driver turns, the price for gasoline is artificially high."

The complaint alleges that the Kalibrate tool inflated gasoline prices by as much as 22 cents a gallon and diesel by up to 33 cents a gallon — against a baseline that had already climbed above $7 a gallon in some areas of California during the disruption to crude supply caused by the US-Iran conflict. Every additional penny per gallon, according to figures cited in the complaint, costs California motorists roughly $134 million a year.

The Algorithm in Question

Kalibrate's product is designed to help fuel retailers respond dynamically to competitor pricing. The system ingests data from subscribing stations — including non-public, commercially sensitive information about prices and supply — and uses it to generate pricing recommendations that subscribing retailers then implement. The complaint says the tool does not merely observe the market; it actively shapes it, feeding each user's data back into a model that all subscribers rely on.

This structure is what plaintiffs' lawyers are framing as a textbook hub-and-spoke conspiracy. The legal concept, long used in antitrust enforcement, describes a market arrangement in which a central actor — here, Kalibrate — coordinates the behaviour of competitors who never need to communicate directly with one another. The US Department of Justice has been sharpening this theory in recent years, arguing that competitors routing pricing decisions through a shared algorithm can constitute illegal coordination even in the absence of boardroom conversations or explicit price-fixing agreements.

The key distinction the complaint draws is between single-firm systems that use only a company's own data — which California law leaves untouched — and shared platforms that pool competitor information. Kalibrate, the suit alleges, sits squarely in the latter category.

A New Law, Tested Early

The filing is among the first brought under Assembly Bill 325, a California statute that took effect on 1 January 2026. AB 325 amends the state's principal antitrust statute, the Cartwright Act, to explicitly prohibit the use of "common pricing algorithms" — defined as any methodology used by two or more companies that draws on competitor data to recommend, align, stabilise, or otherwise influence a price.

The law also makes a procedural change that could prove as consequential as the substantive prohibition. It lowered the pleading standard for Cartwright Act conspiracy claims: plaintiffs must now only allege facts that make their claims plausible, and courts cannot dismiss cases at the pleading stage simply because independent action by competitors is an alternative explanation. That shift matters because algorithmic price coordination tends to leave thin evidentiary trails — no emails, no meetings, no price-fixing handshake. The algorithm is the agreement.

California Governor Gavin Newsom signed AB 325 in October 2025, and lawmakers were explicit that the rental-housing market had informed their thinking. The RealPage case — in which the DOJ sued the property-management software company for allegedly enabling landlords to push apartment rents above competitive levels through a shared algorithmic tool — provided a near-identical template. That case settled earlier this year, with RealPage agreeing to guardrails on how it collects and uses competing landlords' non-public data. The California fuel lawsuit arrives as legal practitioners are still digesting what that settlement signals about the survivability of algorithm-enabled conspiracies under existing law.

Regulatory Pressure Was Already Building


A BP filling station. BP, Marathon, 7-Eleven, and Walmart collectively operate more than 1,700 stations in California. Photo: Wikimedia Commons

The lawsuit did not arrive in isolation. California's Division of Petroleum Market Oversight, the state's fuel watchdog, issued subpoenas to a number of station owners last month over elevated pump prices — a sign that state agencies were already probing whether market concentration and pricing practices were contributing to costs that had been running well above the national average.

The broader context is a California retail fuel market under structural pressure. The combination of state-specific fuel blending requirements, refinery capacity constraints, and supply disruptions linked to the Iran situation has given operators unusual pricing power in recent months. Plaintiffs argue the Kalibrate tool allowed that structural advantage to be systematically extracted rather than eroded by competition among the 1,700-plus participating stations.

That number matters legally. The suit does not allege that BP and Walmart sat in a room together and agreed to raise prices. It argues that by subscribing to the same algorithm and feeding it their confidential data, they produced the economic equivalent of that outcome — coordinated, parallel pricing that no individual competitive incentive would have generated independently.

What the Case Will Test

The practical liability question the court will eventually face is novel: does a company bear antitrust responsibility for coordination its systems execute autonomously, without explicit human direction at each pricing interval? Defendants can be expected to argue that subscribing to a third-party software tool is a unilateral business decision, not a conspiracy, and that the algorithm's outputs reflect independent market responses rather than coordination. That defence has never been squarely adjudicated under California's amended Cartwright Act.

For companies using algorithmic pricing tools across retail, energy, and financial services, the answer has implications beyond the pump. If the Sacramento court finds that subscribing to a shared pricing platform with competitor-data inputs can constitute illegal price coordination, the burden on compliance teams at every company running similar systems will shift materially. The DOJ's RealPage settlement suggested the federal enforcement apparatus had reached a working view; a California court judgment would give private plaintiffs a far more accessible enforcement route.

The next procedural waypoint is a motion-to-dismiss ruling — the first test of whether AB 325's lower pleading standard changes the calculus enough to keep the case alive through early litigation. Defendants have not yet responded publicly to the filing.
