Brexit at ten: Britain marks a decade since the vote that reshaped Europe

A DAERA border inspection post for goods entering Northern Ireland from Great Britain — among the most tangible pieces of post-Brexit infrastructure to emerge from the 2021 Trade and Cooperation Agreement — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Ten years on from the 52-48 Leave vote, a Bank of England-backed study puts the GDP cost at 6%, sterling sits 12% below 2016 levels, and Britain prepares for its seventh PM in a decade.

The United Kingdom marks ten years today since 52 per cent of its voters chose to leave the European Union — a date that lands, with some irony, the morning after Prime Minister Keir Starmer announced his resignation, handing the country a sixth change of leadership in a decade.

The coincidence is not incidental to the story. Brexit's most durable economic consequence may not be the trade barriers visible in customs queues or the twelve-point collapse in sterling. It is the political instability that has shadowed British policymaking ever since, corroding the investor confidence and long-term planning that underpin capital formation.

The ten-year scorecard

The freshest and most rigorous estimate comes from a paper co-authored by Stanford University's Nicholas Bloom using the Bank of England's Decision Maker Panel — a survey commissioned in 2016 specifically to track Brexit's corporate footprint year by year. The conclusion: Brexit has cost the UK approximately 6 per cent of GDP, with roughly half of that damage attributable to the uncertainty of the prolonged exit process and the remainder to the higher trade barriers that came into force in January 2021. A synthesis of five parallel methodologies puts the average closer to 8 per cent. The Office for Budget Responsibility's official figure is a permanent 4 per cent reduction in long-run productivity — more conservative, but pointing the same direction.

The goods-versus-services split has become the defining structural story. UK goods exports are estimated 10–15 per cent below their counterfactual. Five consecutive years of investment contraction between 2016 and 2021 hollowed out capacity that has proven slow to rebuild. Services have held up rather better: the UK is now the third-fastest growing services exporter in the G7, a shift that reflects deliberate repositioning toward finance, insurance, and professional services as much as residual strength.

Capital markets encode the same divergence. The FTSE 100 — dominated by multinationals that earn in dollars and euros, and whose revenues are flattered by a cheaper pound — is up 62 per cent since the referendum. The more domestically oriented FTSE 250 has significantly underperformed. Sterling tells the bluntest story: a pound bought €1.31 on referendum eve; this week it buys roughly €1.15, a 12 per cent loss in European purchasing power that has not been recovered in a decade. In 2025, UK exports of goods and services to the EU stood at £384 billion — 41 per cent of all UK exports — against imports of £472 billion, half the UK total.

What comes next

The Starmer government's "EU reset" produced one concrete gain — a May 2025 agreement giving British defence contractors preferential access to the EU's €150 billion rearmament fund — but that work is now interrupted. Andy Burnham, the former Manchester mayor widely expected to succeed Starmer, has signalled intent to deepen economic ties with Brussels without re-entering the single market or customs union, a formulation that keeps the political ceiling largely where Starmer left it.

The more immediate catalyst is structural rather than electoral: the fisheries provisions of the Trade and Cooperation Agreement come up for formal renegotiation this year, and Brussels has made clear that progress on that dossier is the price of concessions elsewhere. The anniversary arrives not at a moment of resolution, but at the start of another round.
