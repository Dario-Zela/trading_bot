G20 finance chiefs clash over trade-imbalance language in draft communique

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent chaired the G20 finance meeting in Asheville and announced the fallback chair's statement after China declined to join a joint communiqué. — US Department of the Treasury
In one sentence
G20 finance chiefs settled for a US chair's statement, not a joint communiqué, after China alone declined to sign language on trade imbalances and sovereign debt.

The Group of 20's finance chiefs wrapped up two days of talks in Asheville, North Carolina, on Tuesday the way Washington had hoped to avoid: without a joint communiqué. Instead, the United States, as chair, issued a statement backed by 19 of the 20 members, with China declining to sign on to the paragraphs dealing with trade imbalances and sovereign debt.

"I'd hoped to be able to announce a unanimous joint communiqué today," Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told reporters, before framing the near-unanimous outcome as evidence of consensus rather than failure. The document is not nothing — it commits members to financial stability and continued work on debt restructuring for developing economies — but it is a step down from the binding joint text Washington had been pushing for, and a marker of how far apart surplus and deficit economies remain on the diagnosis, let alone the remedy.

The line China wouldn't sign

The sticking point was language declaring that "excessive and persistent imbalances pose risks and can generate economic distortions," and a companion clause urging "countries with excessive and persistent external surpluses" to remove distortions that suppress domestic consumption. Beijing also objected to a paragraph praising the IMF's "surveillance of global imbalances" and to wording that singled out "countries where a meaningful share of external debt is owed to G20 members" — a pointed reference to China's role as the largest bilateral creditor to developing economies.

"The country with the world's largest and unsustainable current account surplus, the People's Republic of China, was the dissenter," Bessent said. Erin Browne, the Treasury's under secretary for international affairs, was blunter about the trade-off Washington had accepted going in: "We've been working really hard on a communiqué, but we want to make sure that it reflects U.S. interests and America First priorities, and if we are unable to come to a conclusion, we're comfortable with that." Browne described China as "widely viewed as the biggest source of imbalances around the world," citing an undervalued yuan and export subsidies that pushed China's July exports up 23.9% year-on-year and widened its goods surplus with the EU to €360.6bn, up 15%.

A meeting already short on unity

The imbalance fight was not the only fracture. European ministers pushed for tougher language on Russia's war in Ukraine and were unsettled to find Russian Finance Minister Anton Siluanov at the table — his first in-person G20 appearance since the 2022 invasion. Canada's finance minister, François-Philippe Champagne, said the presence "created discomfort, no doubt about that." Germany's Lars Klingbeil offered the meeting's most quotable diagnosis of the mood: "Uncertainty is poison for economic growth." The chair's statement also endorsed "free, safe, and predictable navigation through the Strait of Hormuz" — a line added after last week's escalation between the US and Iran — which China likewise declined to endorse.

None of this derails the G20's formal machinery; a leaders' summit later in the cycle will revisit the same imbalance language, and the IMF's next Article IV surveillance round will test whether "surveillance," absent Chinese buy-in, has any teeth. But a chair's statement is a weaker instrument than a communiqué — it records disagreement rather than resolving it — and with the world's largest surplus economy declining to sign even a description of the problem, coordinated action on trade imbalances looks further away than the Asheville gathering was meant to suggest.
