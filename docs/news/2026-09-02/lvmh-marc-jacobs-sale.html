LVMH completes sale of Marc Jacobs to WHP Global and G-III

Marc Jacobs, who remains the label's creative director under its new owners. — Ståle Grut/NRKbeta, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH has sold Marc Jacobs to WHP Global and G-III for roughly $925m, ending 29 years of ownership as it trims its portfolio.

LVMH has completed the sale of Marc Jacobs, closing the books on nearly three decades of ownership and handing the fashion house to a consortium of WHP Global and G-III Apparel Group in a deal reported by WWD at roughly $925 million.

The structure is unusual enough to be worth spelling out. WHP Global and G-III each take a 50 percent stake in a joint venture that will hold the Marc Jacobs and Marc by Marc Jacobs brands, with WHP — a New York brand-management firm that already owns Vera Wang, rag & bone and G-Star — leading strategy. G-III, better known as a manufacturer and licensee for names like Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger, separately bought the operating business outright: the US and European retail network of more than 100 stores, plus wholesale and e-commerce. Marc Jacobs himself, who founded the label in 1984 and sold a majority stake to LVMH in 1997, stays on as founder and creative director. Bernard Arnault called him "a designer of rare creativity and unique vision" in a statement that read more like a eulogy for the relationship than a deal announcement.

Not an isolated exit

Marc Jacobs joins a lengthening list. LVMH sold Off-White in 2024 and Stella McCartney's namesake label in 2025, and Fenty Beauty — one of the group's actual growth stories — is reportedly under review as well. The common thread is brands that never achieved the scale of Louis Vuitton or Dior but still absorbed management attention and balance-sheet space. LVMH's fashion and leather goods division, its largest, posted an organic revenue decline of roughly 5 percent across 2025 before finally returning to growth — up 1 percent — in the second quarter of 2026, snapping seven straight quarterly declines. Group revenue slipped 1 percent to €80.8 billion in 2025. Operating margin has held near 22.5 percent, which is the more telling number: LVMH is not retrenching because the group is unprofitable, it is retrenching because smaller houses no longer justify the overhead relative to what they return.

That calculus is precisely the bet WHP Global and G-III are taking the other side of. Yehuda Shmidman, WHP's founder and chief executive, described the acquisition as a "defining moment," and the arithmetic behind that confidence is straightforward: combined with its existing roster, WHP now oversees a portfolio generating more than $9.5 billion in retail sales, monetized largely through licensing rather than owned retail — a leaner model than LVMH ever ran for a brand this size.

The test now shifts from deal terms to execution. WHP and G-III have signalled ambitions to expand Marc Jacobs' global footprint, but licensing-driven growth has diluted brand equity before at other houses that took the same path. The next marker is LVMH's third-quarter trading update, due in October, which will show whether the fashion and leather goods division's return to growth was a real inflection or a one-quarter blip — and whether Fenty Beauty follows Marc Jacobs out the door.
