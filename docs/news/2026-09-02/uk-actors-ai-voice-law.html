UK actors demand law against AI voice cloning

Actor Hugh Bonneville is among more than 80 performers who signed the Save Our Voices Now letter. — Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 80 UK performers, led by Hugh Bonneville and Nicola Coughlan, want a law giving people legal ownership of their own voice against AI cloning.

It takes about three seconds of audio, campaigners say, for modern AI tools to produce a convincing copy of someone's voice — enough to make them say anything, in any tone, with no further input from the real person. More than 80 UK performers, including Hugh Bonneville, Nicola Coughlan, Matt Lucas and Siobhán McSweeney, have now signed an open letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham demanding legislation to stop it happening without consent.

The letter, organised under the banner Save Our Voices Now, wants the government to give every person in the UK a clear, enforceable legal ownership of their own voice — the same kind of protection that already exists for a person's name or image. A companion petition has gone up on the government's petitions site, and campaign co-founder Peter Caulfield put the urgency plainly: "In just three seconds, with the right AI system, your voice can be cloned, stolen and reinterpreted without your knowledge." Fellow co-founder Alice Sockett, an audiobook narrator, called it "an existential threat to our entire industry," adding that voice theft "increases every week."

Beyond luvvies' pay cheques

What makes this more than a trade dispute over dubbing and audiobook fees is the framing. The letter leans heavily on the risk to ordinary consumers, not just working performers — the same cloning tools used to lift a line reading from a drama series can just as easily fabricate a phone call from a relative in distress, or a voicemail from a bank. That reframing, from IP grievance to consumer-protection issue, is precisely what tends to move governments faster than industry lobbying alone.

The campaigners point to Denmark, which is already legislating to give citizens ownership of their face, body and voice, with rights to demand removal of unauthorised AI content and to claim compensation. A government spokesperson responded that ministers "value and protect human creators" and confirmed a forthcoming consultation on how to address the harms — while, in the same breath, promising to protect "legitimate innovation," the standard hedge that precedes most tech legislation and the clause worth watching most closely.

Nothing here compels anyone yet. A consultation is a long way from a statute, and the UK has a recent history of announcing AI reviews that move slowly. But a G7 government being pushed to define legal ownership of a voice — with scams, not royalties, as the headline justification — is the kind of early marker that shapes how AI voice and dubbing startups price their compliance risk long before any bill reaches the Commons. The consultation's terms of reference, once published, will show whether Westminster is aiming at Hollywood-style licensing disputes or at the wider fraud problem the letter actually leads with.
