Burnham blames Brexit and Thatcherism for growth slump, pledges utility nationalisation

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, official portrait taken at 10 Downing Street, July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham pledged devolution and vaguer "public control" of utilities, having already retreated from nationalising Thames Water outright.

Andy Burnham used his first set-piece appearance at the despatch box as prime minister to tell the House of Commons that the last 40 years of British economic policy amount to a series of wrong turns — and to promise, without quite committing to, a reversal of the biggest one: the sale of the country's water and energy utilities.

"Political power was centralised, economic power privatised, the country de-industrialised," Burnham told MPs on Tuesday, in a statement that ran three hours and 23 minutes with questions. "Austerity followed, hollowing out councils." He added that Brexit had since "compounded the damage," leaving Britain with what he called a decade of low growth and stalled regeneration. "Unless we are blunt about what went wrong," he said, "we won't turn things around."

The headline commitments were two: a devolution drive that would let more of England set its own economic priorities, and a plan — due before the end of the year — for "stronger public control" of water, energy and transport. Markets, still digesting a separate jump in gilt yields to their highest level in 28 years, treated the two announcements very differently.

From nationalisation to "public control"

The distinction between full public ownership and Burnham's preferred phrase, "public control," is not academic, and it has already cost him some credibility on the left of his own party. As a candidate for the Labour leadership in June, Burnham said explicitly of Thames Water — the heavily indebted utility serving 16 million customers in and around London — that "public ownership is absolutely an option… I would say for Thames Water, that is what should be done." By late August, weeks into the job, he had backed away, telling allies that nationalising the company outright would be "too costly," "too complex" and "too disruptive."

That retreat drew sharp criticism from campaigners who had taken the earlier pledge at face value. Cat Hobbs of the campaign group We Own It said that if Burnham did not bring Thames Water into public ownership, "he'll go down in history as a same-old prime minister delivering sewage." James Wallace of River Action called for immediate special administration of the company to halt further asset-stripping.

In the Commons, Burnham stuck to the softer formulation. He called the water industry "a leaking monument" to an approach that ran Britain "in the private rather than the public interest," and said a ten-year plan would set out "stronger public control over these essentials, making them work for people and places again" — but he declined to specify whether that meant renationalisation, a regulatory overhaul, or something closer to the mutualised model long favoured by some Labour figures for Welsh Water. The plan itself will not appear until later in the year, folded into the government's broader ten-year strategy.

The ambiguity was enough to move share prices. Deutsche Bank cut National Grid from "buy" to "hold" and trimmed its price target from 1,370p to 1,250p, citing the political overhang. United Utilities shares nonetheless rose 1.1% to 1,329p on the day, a sign that investors are, for now, pricing in a slow-moving regulatory story rather than an imminent expropriation. Whether that calm survives contact with an actual white paper is a separate question — one for which the Budget, on 28 October, is the next scheduled marker.

Devolution: more concrete, less contested

Where the utilities pledge was long on rhetoric and short on mechanics, the devolution announcement had teeth. Burnham confirmed that Number 10 will begin devolution talks with Cornwall Council, with or without an elected mayor, and extended the same offer to every part of England that currently lacks devolved power — an implicit target of a strategic authority in every region by the end of 2028. English mayors are also promised the ability to retain a share of locally raised business rates from April 2027, alongside new powers over post-16 technical education and employment support, replacing grant funding with a more durable local revenue stream.

The Institute for Government has described the devolution package as a genuinely strong start relative to the rest of Burnham's stated priorities — a rare compliment for a government seven weeks old. Unlike the utilities question, there is little market read-through here beyond a slow reweighting of infrastructure spending decisions toward mayoral combined authorities, but for regional development funds and construction firms with exposure to local transport and housing schemes, it is the more investable of the two announcements.

Badenoch, borrowing costs and the budget constraint

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch used her response to accuse Burnham of offering "more government control, more taxes and more politicians," arguing that his diagnosis of Britain's growth problem was simply wrong, and warning that the premiership would prove "very different" to running Greater Manchester, where he "did not have to make difficult trade-offs." Some of that critique will write itself over the coming weeks regardless of what Badenoch says: 10-year gilt yields rose to 5.22% on the day of the statement, having touched an intraday high of 5.25%, their highest since 2008; 30-year yields hit 5.89%, a level last seen in 1998. One market analyst estimated the government's annual debt-interest bill has risen by up to £6bn since spring as yields have climbed — a squeeze that has as much to do with a global bond sell-off, sharpened by the Iran conflict, as with anything Burnham said.

That backdrop matters for how much of the utilities plan survives the transition from statement to legislation. Burnham would not confirm whether the 28 October Budget will include tax rises, though he signalled defence spending could reach 3% of GDP by 2030 — itself a call on fiscal headroom that competes directly with any serious public-ownership programme. A government facing 28-year-high borrowing costs and a defence spending ratchet has less room to fund utility buybacks than one delivering its pledges in a calmer bond market.

What to watch

The substantive test of Burnham's utilities pledge will not come until the ten-year plan is published later in the year, expected around the 28 October Budget alongside the devolution white paper. Until then, the gap between "public ownership is absolutely an option" and "stronger public control" is doing a great deal of work — and utility investors, judging by National Grid's downgrade and United Utilities' steady share price, are still deciding which version of Burnham to believe.
