US strikes on Iran spark missile retaliation as oil surges past $90

The Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint between Iran and Oman through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade passes. — NASA/MODIS via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US strikes on IRGC minelayers near Bandar Abbas drew Iranian missile retaliation and pushed Brent crude above $90, with Trump warning of harsher strikes to come.

The Strait of Hormuz has gone from a simmering flashpoint to an active battlefield again. On Monday, US forces struck Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps sites in and around Bandar Abbas, Iran's main naval hub on the strait, after Washington said it caught the IRGC preparing to lay sea mines in the world's busiest oil corridor. Within a day, Iran hit back with missiles and drones against American positions across the Gulf, and Brent crude tore through $90 a barrel for the first time in months. Oil traders, airline executives and — improbably — crypto investors are now all watching the same 33-kilometre stretch of water.

Strikes, mines, and a wedding party

US Central Command said the operation targeted IRGC minelaying forces that were "preparing to launch rockets with sea mines" into the strait, hitting sites near Bandar Abbas along with Konarak, Chabahar, Qeshm and Sirik. Satellite imagery showed fires and heavy black smoke over Iran's naval headquarters, with at least one vessel ablaze. CENTCOM tied the action to what it called recent IRGC attacks on commercial shipping and on American service members in the region — the first acknowledged US strike in the strait in roughly a month, part of a campaign that traces back to February's Operation Epic Fury, the Israeli-American strike that killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei and touched off months of tanker seizures, mined shipping lanes and a de facto Iranian toll system on Gulf traffic.

The strikes were not bloodless. A separate US missile attack reportedly hit a wedding celebration in Kuhestak, in Iran's Hormozgan province near the strait, killing four people — including a child — and wounding more than 50, according to Iranian state media. Tehran cited that strike, and an earlier one, as justification for what the IRGC called a "decisive" retaliatory campaign: coordinated missile-and-drone attacks on American bases and interests in Jordan, Iraq and Bahrain. Iran's armed forces general staff vowed, in a Telegram statement, to "impose a heavy cost on the American enemy." Jordan's army said it intercepted ten ballistic missiles aimed at two US bases; Iranian state media separately reported projectiles striking areas around Chabahar and Konarak on Iran's own southeastern coast, among the murkier details of an exchange still short on independently verified damage assessments.

Trump's warning, and the island still on the table

President Trump confirmed the US strikes personally, calling them "very justified" and linking them to Iran's "failed" mining attempt — which he said had been "removed and detonated" — as well as an earlier Iranian missile launch at a US base in Jordan. His warning to Tehran was blunt: retaliation would be met with force "at a much harder and higher level, but it will not be the biggest attack of them all, that is waiting in the wings," adding that "there will be very little left" of the country if Iran pushes further.

That rhetoric is not new, and that is precisely what makes it hard to read. Trump has cycled through similar ultimatums against Kharg Island — the terminal handling roughly 90% of Iran's crude exports — since March, when US bombers hit more than 90 Iranian military sites while pointedly sparing oil infrastructure. He threatened in June to "take" the island outright, then stood down citing progress in back-channel talks; by July he had declared an earlier ceasefire "over" and revived the threat. As recently as this week he posted a one-line warning — "Kharg Island being blown to smithereens" — accompanied by an AI-generated explosion video. Markets have learned to treat the Kharg threat as the real tripwire: strikes on IRGC vessels and minelayers are now almost routine; an actual hit on Iran's main export terminal would be a different order of event, and traders are pricing the probability of that happening, not just the news of the day.

Oil, equities and a chokepoint that won't stay closed

Brent crude climbed above $90 a barrel and traded as high as roughly $91-92 through the session, its firmest level in three months, as maritime intelligence firm Windward AI reported Hormuz tanker traffic had fallen to just six vessels in a single day — a sharp drop for a waterway that normally carries around a fifth of global seaborne oil trade and a comparable share of global LNG exports, most of it from Qatar. Energy majors Shell and BP rallied on the prospect of sustained higher prices, while airline stocks slid as carriers face the prospect of costlier jet fuel just as travel demand heads into the autumn. Russia's decision to extend its diesel export ban through the end of September added a second layer of pressure to refined product markets already strained by the Gulf disruption.

The mechanics are straightforward even if the politics are not: Hormuz has no meaningful bypass for the volumes involved, so every mine, every intercepted missile and every burning tanker gets priced almost immediately into the front of the crude curve. What is harder to price is duration — whether this is another flare-up in a nine-month pattern of strike, threat and stand-down, or the start of something that actually closes the strait.

Crypto's growing beta to Gulf headlines

The clearest sign that this is no longer a niche oil-market story came from bitcoin, which slid roughly 3% to trade near $77,000–78,000 as the strikes landed, with ether down a comparable 3.6% and XRP and dogecoin also retreating. Analysts framed the move as a straightforward risk-off reaction — investors rotating out of volatile assets in tandem with the broader flight from equities — rather than anything crypto-specific, and several noted the sell-off was milder than during earlier flashpoints in this same nine-month conflict, when bitcoin briefly crashed below $76,000. That muted-but-real reaction is itself the story: bitcoin's advocates have long argued it behaves as an uncorrelated or even safe-haven asset. Its tendency to fall in lockstep with equities the moment Trump posts an Iran threat suggests otherwise, at least in the acute phase of a geopolitical shock.

What to watch

The through-line across oil, equities and crypto is the same: markets are no longer treating Hormuz strikes as one-off headlines but as recurring inputs to a risk premium that hasn't been fully unwound since February. The next marker is whether Iran's response stays confined to intercepted missiles and minor projectile incidents, or escalates toward the kind of infrastructure hit — Kharg Island chief among them — that Trump has threatened and pulled back from three times already this year. Also worth watching: whether Hormuz tanker traffic recovers from its six-vessel low in the coming days, which would signal shippers see this as another contained flare-up, or stays depressed, which would keep upward pressure on Brent regardless of what happens next on the battlefield.
