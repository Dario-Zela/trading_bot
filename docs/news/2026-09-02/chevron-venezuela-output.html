Chevron to grow Venezuelan oil output, distinct from Trump's broader bet

In one sentence
Chevron plans to grow Venezuelan output up to 50% via its existing PDVSA license, separate from the Pentagon-backed NABEP deal Trump announced days earlier.

Chevron is preparing to expand its Venezuelan crude production by as much as 50% over the next two years, a move that puts the only major U.S. oil company still operating in the country on a distinctly different track from the Trump administration's own, far more elaborate bet on Venezuelan oil.

The company already pumps roughly 280,000 barrels a day through its joint venture with state firm PDVSA — about a quarter of Venezuela's total output — and is expected to formalize the expansion this week, with Energy Secretary Chris Wright and Chevron officials traveling to Caracas to announce new investment in the Orinoco Belt, home to some of the heaviest crude reserves on the planet.

Two tracks, one country

What makes Chevron's plan notable is what it isn't. Days earlier, the White House unveiled a far more unusual arrangement: a new entity built around North American Blue Energy Partners, a firm owned by Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt, granted 100-year rights to 17 oil fields holding an estimated 65 billion barrels of reserves. The Pentagon would take a 35% equity stake in that venture, with the State Department holding an option to buy 20% of the oil produced at cost — terms unheard of in a private-sector license.

Chevron's expansion, by contrast, runs through the conventional channel: an existing operating license with the U.S. Treasury and a joint venture with PDVSA that predates the current administration by more than a decade. Francisco Monaldi, who directs the Latin America energy program at Rice University's Baker Institute, put the distinction plainly, noting Chevron is "the best positioned among US oil companies — by far," precisely because it already has infrastructure, contracts and cash flow on the ground rather than a freshly minted 100-year concession.

Why the daylight matters

The divergence tests how far Trump's Venezuela opening extends. His own framing — "we have Exxon going in, we have Chevron going in," as he put it in announcing the NABEP deal — casts both moves as parts of one strategy. But Chevron's path relies on Treasury sanctions relief that has been granted and revoked by successive administrations depending on political conditions in Caracas, a licensing regime built on executive discretion rather than statute. Analysts have been more confident in Chevron's ability to deliver the barrels than in the NABEP structure, which still needs to clear questions about Venezuela's National Assembly and the durability of any deal struck with an acting government.

For crude markets, the near-term signal is supply: Chevron's incremental barrels are real and coming, layered on top of a Venezuela oil sector that Washington is now actively trying to unlock through two separate mechanisms. Wednesday's Caracas announcement, and the fine print of whatever license underpins it, will be the next marker of which track — corporate license or Pentagon-backed concession — Washington actually intends to prioritize.
