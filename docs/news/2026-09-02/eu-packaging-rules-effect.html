New EU packaging-waste rules take effect across the single market

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which drafted the packaging regulation now in force across the bloc. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Three weeks into the EU's new packaging-waste regime, compliance costs are shifting onto producers and retailers ahead of a 2028 recyclability-grading deadline.

The European Union's harmonised packaging-waste rules have been in force for three weeks, and the bill is starting to arrive. Regulation (EU) 2025/40 — the Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, or PPWR — entered into application on 12 August, replacing a patchwork of national packaging laws with a single EU-wide rulebook. For retailers, manufacturers and logistics firms trading into the bloc, that means new design standards, new reporting duties, and new fees, all landing at once.

The most immediate change is narrow but absolute: food-contact packaging containing PFAS — the "forever chemicals" once used to grease-proof pizza boxes, bakery bags and microwave popcorn sleeves — above strict limits can no longer be placed on the EU market. E-commerce parcels face a separate, more mundane constraint: empty space cannot exceed 40% of a package's volume unless technically unavoidable, a rule aimed squarely at the oversized boxes familiar to anyone who has ordered a single cable online.

Who pays, and how much

The regulation's deeper cost sits in extended producer responsibility. Under the new regime, the cost of collecting, sorting and recycling packaging waste — historically borne by taxpayers through local authorities — shifts onto the businesses that put the packaging on the market. Fees are "eco-modulated": packaging that is easy to recycle qualifies for a lower rate, while material that gums up sorting lines pays a surcharge. A harmonised A/B/C recyclability grading system, due by January 2028, will formalise that scale across all 27 member states; national EPR fee rates already vary by a factor of twenty or more, from under €50 a tonne in parts of central Europe to over €1,000 in the Nordics.

The European Commission's own accounting puts the near-term pain at roughly €5.9bn in additional compliance spending — largely for reuse schemes, deposit-return systems and recyclability certification — against a projected €47.2bn in savings for the packaging sector by 2030, once fragmented national rules stop duplicating costs. That trade sits at the centre of the political argument: industry groups have flagged the up-front burden as disproportionate for smaller operators and hospitality businesses already absorbing other cost pressures, while Brussels frames it as the price of ending 27 separate compliance regimes.

Enforcement, notably, is not yet uniform. The PPWR requires member states to set penalties that are "effective, proportionate and dissuasive" but leaves the amounts to national legislation, due by 12 February 2027. Germany has moved first, setting fines of up to €200,000 per breach under its Packaging Law Implementation Act; other capitals have yet to publish figures, leaving multinational compliance teams working against a moving target.

The next fixed points on the calendar are the harmonised labelling system for waste sorting in 2028, followed by the heavier lift in 2030: binding recycled-content minimums, recyclability-by-design mandates, and the first of the regulation's phased waste-reduction targets — a 5% cut versus 2018 levels, rising to 15% by 2040.
