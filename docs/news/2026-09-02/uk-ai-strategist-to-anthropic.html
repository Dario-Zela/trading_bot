Architect of UK government's AI strategy departs for Anthropic

10 Downing Street, where Matt Clifford served as the Prime Minister's AI adviser until June 2025. — Sergeant Tom Robinson RLC / MOD, Wikimedia Commons (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Matt Clifford, who wrote the UK's AI Opportunities Action Plan, has left government for a senior international role at Anthropic.

Matt Clifford spent three years building the scaffolding for how Britain thinks about artificial intelligence — the safety summit, the AI Security Institute, the growth-zone plan meant to keep Britain in the AI race. On Tuesday, Anthropic announced it has hired him.

Clifford will join the ChatGPT rival — maker of the Claude chatbot, and reportedly preparing for a future IPO at a valuation approaching $2 trillion — as managing director for international affairs. The brief is broad: engagement with every government outside the United States that Anthropic deals with, from Westminster to Brussels to Delhi. He left Downing Street just over a year ago.

In one sentence: The tech investor who wrote Britain's AI playbook has taken a senior job at Anthropic, reviving worries about how easily Whitehall's AI expertise walks out the door into industry.

From Sunak's summit to Starmer's action plan

Clifford, a co-founder of the startup accelerator Entrepreneur First, first entered government orbit under Rishi Sunak, helping run the 2023 AI Safety Summit at Bletchley Park and shape the Frontier AI Taskforce that became the UK's AI Security Institute. When Labour took over, he stayed on: Keir Starmer's government asked him to chair a review of AI opportunities, and the resulting AI Opportunities Action Plan — published in January 2025, covering everything from domestic compute buildout to AI growth zones — carries his fingerprints throughout. He was rewarded with the title of Prime Minister's AI adviser, a role he held until leaving in June 2025 for family reasons. He remains chair of ARIA, the government's blue-sky research funding body, and of Entrepreneur First — both of which he'll keep alongside the Anthropic job.

The revolving door, again

The reaction in Westminster was less alarm than weary familiarity. Chi Onwurah, the Labour MP who chairs the Commons science, innovation and technology committee, credited Clifford with a "significant contribution" to AI policy and said movement between the public and private sector shouldn't be barred outright — but added a pointed hope that he'd "use his skills and experience to promote responsible AI to Anthropic rather than promoting irresponsible AI to the UK government." Tom Brake of Unlock Democracy was blunter, calling it evidence that "the revolving door is a problem for advisers as much as it is for ministers," and warning that a company gains "a huge advantage" from hiring someone who carries detailed knowledge of government thinking on a policy area as sensitive as AI. Anthropic's line was that expertise is the point: "We hire people with expertise and who understand how government works because our work requires it."

Clifford is not the first senior figure to make this exact move. Nick Clegg went to Meta in 2018, George Osborne joined OpenAI's board this year, and Rishi Sunak himself took advisory roles with Microsoft and Anthropic after leaving No 10. What's different this time is the gap: Clifford wasn't a former minister years removed from office but the government's own AI adviser until fourteen months ago, hired specifically because Whitehall didn't have his expertise in-house.

Why it's worth watching

The practical policy risk isn't that Clifford breaks any rules — these moves go through the standard business appointment rules process — but that each departure widens the gap between what government understands about frontier AI and what the companies building it understand about government, at exactly the moment the UK is trying to decide how hard to regulate the technology. That asymmetry matters more as Britain heads toward decisions on AI liability and testing regimes that will shape how firms like Anthropic, OpenAI and DeepMind operate in the country — a live fight, given DeepMind has been pushing for binding testing rules while Meta resists them at the G20 level. A government that keeps losing its AI-literate staff to the companies it's meant to oversee has less capacity to hold its own in that argument.

The next marker to watch is whether Clifford's replacement — Downing Street has not yet named a new AI adviser — gets the same access and mandate he had, or whether the role is quietly downgraded now that the Action Plan is already written.
