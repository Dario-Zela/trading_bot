Farage's standing wobbles as polls diverge and standards claim unravels

Nigel Farage, whose satisfaction ratings have fallen to the lowest of any UK opposition leader amid polling volatility and a disputed standards claim. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK's polling swung from 23% to 35% in a fortnight as Farage posts the worst satisfaction ratings of any opposition leader and Rupert Lowe's breakaway party bites into his base.

Nigel Farage has spent the past two years as the most consequential man in British opposition politics, the figure both main parties built their strategies around. That certainty is now cracking. A run of polls released in the past fortnight has put Reform UK's vote share anywhere between 23% and 35% — a 12-point spread that would have been unthinkable a year ago, when the party moved in a tight, predictable band. Something has come loose.

The most jarring number came from More in Common, whose fieldwork on 21–23 August put Reform on just 23%, four points behind Labour on 27% and the party's lowest reading since January 2025. Barely a week later, BMG's survey for The i put Reform at a record 35%. PollCheck's rolling seven-poll average, a smoother measure, has Reform at 24.1% as of 1 September, edging out the Conservatives but trailing Labour's 26.6% — a picture that looks nothing like either extreme. Pollsters disagree publicly now about methodology, about how to weight shy Reform voters, and about whether the party's 2025 peak of nearly 32% was ever as solid as it looked. For a party whose entire pitch rests on being the inevitable alternative to a discredited establishment, that kind of noise is its own kind of damage.

A leader losing his own voters

The polling wobble has a personal counterpart. Ipsos's most recent leader-satisfaction tracker found just 23% of Britons satisfied with Farage's performance against 61% dissatisfied — the worst net score of any opposition leader tested, behind Kemi Badenoch's -22 and Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey's -22. Dissatisfaction with Farage has climbed steadily, from 49% in June 2025 to 63% a year later, tracking almost exactly the period in which scrutiny of his finances intensified.

That scrutiny includes an unresolved dispute over the Reform leader's own account of his standards troubles. When Farage briefly resigned his Clacton seat in July, he said the parliamentary standards system had become "a political tool" because "yet another standards investigation is under way" into his finances. Westminster reporting since has found no evidence that a second inquiry exists. The Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards' published list shows only one live investigation, opened in May over an undisclosed £5 million gift from the donor Christopher Harborne. A separate complaint concerning payments from Farage's friend, the convicted fraudster George Cottrell, was lodged in July, but the commissioner has not confirmed folding it into a formal inquiry, and had still not decided whether to widen the probe as of Farage's return to the Commons. The gap between Farage's public framing — a coordinated pile-on — and the commissioner's actual paper trail is, on the available record, unexplained.

The Lowe problem

Both trends converge on the same rival: Rupert Lowe, the former Reform MP for Great Yarmouth who had the party whip withdrawn in March 2025 after a bitter falling-out with Farage over internal governance. Lowe launched Restore Britain in February, positioning it to Reform's right and picking up an endorsement from Elon Musk along the way.

Restore Britain is not, on current numbers, a serious electoral force — it has settled at 3–4% in most trackers. But that modest figure understates its bite: More in Common's analysts linked Reform's slide to 23% directly to Restore's rise, and YouGov's polling of Restore's own supporters found roughly one in ten had backed Reform at the 2024 general election. A party drawing single-digit support can still be the difference between a Reform lead and a Reform slump if it is drawing from exactly the segment of voters most invested in the project's purity.

What it means

None of this moves markets today — Reform sits in opposition, with no vote scheduled and no policy about to change hands. But the combination of a volatile topline, a leader with the weakest personal ratings in opposition politics, and a breakaway faction eating at his flank is the kind of instability that compounds. If Reform's ceiling turns out to be nearer 24% than 35%, and its right flank keeps fragmenting, the calculus around any future Reform-adjacent government — on tax, on regulation, on Britain's fiscal trajectory — gets harder to model, not easier. Traders pricing UK political risk over a multi-year horizon have one more variable to carry. The next test is whether the Cottrell complaint formally becomes a second investigation; the commissioner's decision, whenever it lands, will settle at least one of the two disputes hanging over Farage this month.
