Fed's Kevin Warsh marks 100 days as chair ahead of September FOMC

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as the 17th Chair of the Federal Reserve on May 22, 2026. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Fed chair Kevin Warsh's 100th day is a footnote next to his Jackson Hole speech, which pushed September rate-hike odds from 35% to roughly 60%.

One hundred days is not usually a policy event. For Kevin Warsh, the Federal Reserve's 17th chair, the milestone falls this week almost by coincidence — the number that actually moved markets landed five days ago, in Jackson Hole, Wyoming.

Warsh was sworn in on May 22, 2026, after a 54-45 Senate confirmation vote, the narrowest in the position's history. His first policy meeting came weeks later, on June 16-17. Between then and his August 28 keynote at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium, he had said relatively little in public — deliberately. But Jackson Hole was the one appearance he could not skip, and what he said there reset how traders price the Fed's September 15-16 meeting.

The speech that moved the odds

Warsh's core message was that inflation progress has stalled, not reversed. He pointed to a personal consumption expenditures reading of 3.7 percent over the past 12 months, but a hotter 4.1 percent over the past six — a sign, in his telling, that underlying price pressure has not eased despite two years of restrictive policy. "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed," he said. "Otherwise, we have work to do."

That phrase — sufficient speed — did the damage. It was the clearest signal yet that Warsh sees current policy as not restrictive enough, and traders read it as an opening for a hike rather than a cut. The CME FedWatch tool showed the odds of a quarter-point increase at the September meeting jump to roughly 60 percent from about 35 percent before he spoke. The two-year Treasury yield rose nearly 8 basis points to 4.31 percent, its highest since late July, and the dollar index gained 0.6 percent to 99.66.

Warsh was careful not to commit outright. He noted the economy looks resilient by most measures — capital expenditure growth near 9 percent, corporate profits up roughly 20 percent, consumer spending growing above 2 percent, unemployment steady at 4.1 percent — which gives the Fed room to act without fear of tipping the economy into recession. But on inflation itself, he was blunt: "The responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank."

A quieter Fed, on his terms

The speech doubled as a manifesto for how Warsh wants the institution to communicate. He has trimmed FOMC policy statements to 132 words, dropped the dot-plot rate projections that governors had published quarterly since 2012, and used Jackson Hole to explain why: he considers routine forward guidance a source of false certainty. "In normal times, the role of forward guidance should be limited and circumscribed," he said. "Otherwise, it risks creating ambiguity in the name of clarity." Asked to describe what remains, he offered a workaround rather than a name: "Call it an outline, you can call it a trail map, just don't call it forward guidance."

That stripped-down approach is itself the story of Warsh's first hundred days — a Fed chair betting that saying less, and meaning more when he does speak, is a more credible way to run policy than Powell-era press conferences and projection charts. Jackson Hole tested that bet under real market conditions, and it worked in the sense that traders moved. Whether the underlying diagnosis is correct is a separate question the September meeting will start to answer.

The FOMC meets September 15-16. Between now and then, the August employment report and the next CPI print are the data points most likely to tip the committee's decision — and confirm or complicate the read markets took from Jackson Hole.
