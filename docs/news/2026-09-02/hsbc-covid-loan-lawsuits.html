HSBC faces stepped-up Covid-era loan default lawsuits

HSBC's headquarters at 8 Canada Square in Canary Wharf, London. — Matt Brown / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
HSBC is chasing more small-business borrowers through the courts as 2020's Bounce Back Loans hit their six-year repayment deadline.

HSBC is chasing a growing number of small businesses through the courts over unpaid pandemic-era loans, as the first tranche of emergency lending handed out in 2020 hits the end of its repayment term. The bank has instructed debt-recovery solicitors to pursue Bounce Back Loan borrowers who have stopped paying, according to loan-tracking site Mr Bounce Back, which compiles quarterly lender data — a shift that lands awkwardly next to a UK government pledge, made in June, to write off the same debts for anyone deemed a genuine casualty of the pandemic rather than a fraudster.

The numbers involved are modest next to HSBC's balance sheet but telling in direction. As of its most recent quarterly disclosure, the bank's defaulted Bounce Back Loan balance stood at £16.76m, with a further £53.31m in arrears. Suspected fraud across its BBL book was put at £276.74m, of which £80.72m has already been claimed back from the state-backed guarantee. Six years on from the scheme's launch, those figures are no longer static: loans issued in 2020 are now reaching their contractual maturity, forcing a reckoning that was always going to arrive on a calendar, not a whim.

Why the guarantee isn't a free pass

The pursuit is partly mechanical. Bounce Back Loans carried a 100% government guarantee, but that cover is withdrawn once a lender or the loan itself is flagged for fraud or processing error — at which point the bank must chase the borrower directly, through default notices, county court judgments and, if needed, winding-up petitions. Britain's Public Sector Fraud Authority has been running data-matching exercises with HMRC and lenders to identify exactly such cases, with enforcement expected to intensify by autumn. On the sister scheme, the Coronavirus Business Interruption Loan Scheme, the guarantee only ever covered 80% of a loan, and lenders were contractually obliged to pursue genuine recovery — including calling in personal guarantees on facilities above £250,000 — rather than simply writing the loss off to the taxpayer.

Seen that way, HSBC's litigation is less a change of heart than an overdue accounting exercise, arriving years late because forbearance and loan terms delayed it. But it sits alongside a broader, harder-to-explain wariness on credit. HSBC lifted its 2026 group credit-charge guidance to 45 basis points of average gross loans from 40bps, citing "ongoing uncertainty," after a first-quarter expected-credit-loss charge that jumped by $400m to $1.3bn — driven chiefly by a fraud loss on a UK financial sponsor's securitised exposure, unrelated to small-business pandemic lending but a reminder that the bank's underwriting judgment is under scrutiny on more than one front simultaneously.

None of this proves HSBC's Covid-era loan book is rotten beneath the surface. It is equally consistent with a bank finally doing the unglamorous work the original scheme rules required all along. What would change the read is if defaults keep climbing after the government's fraud-only write-off carve-out takes effect this autumn — at which point forbearance will no longer be a plausible explanation, and the numbers will have to speak for themselves. HSBC's next quarterly results, due in late October, are the first checkpoint.
