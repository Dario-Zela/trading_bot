Broadcom's earnings face a high bar after Nvidia's blowout quarter

Hock Tan, Broadcom's president and CEO, whose custom AI silicon business has become a bellwether for AI-infrastructure spending beyond Nvidia. — Greg Bezat / Bezat Video Group, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Broadcom reports Tuesday after Nvidia's beat-but-sold-off quarter, with its own custom-chip and networking numbers standing in as a test of how broad-based the AI spending boom really is.

Broadcom reports fiscal third-quarter earnings after markets close on Tuesday, and for once the number that matters most isn't really about Broadcom. It's about whether Nvidia's blowout quarter was a company story or an industry one.

Nvidia posted $96.2bn in revenue for its second fiscal quarter on August 26, beating Wall Street's $92.1bn estimate, with data-center sales alone up 117% year-on-year to $89bn. Chief executive Jensen Huang forecast 70% revenue growth for fiscal 2028, well above what analysts had penciled in. By any normal measure, that is a spectacular quarter. Nvidia shares fell 4.6% anyway, because guidance for the current quarter — $108bn, in line with rather than crushing expectations — left investors wanting more. That reaction is the backdrop Broadcom now reports into: a market so primed for AI-infrastructure growth that even a historic beat can read as merely adequate.

A different piece of the stack

Broadcom doesn't compete with Nvidia head-on. Where Nvidia sells general-purpose GPUs, Broadcom co-designs custom AI accelerators — application-specific chips, or ASICs — with the hyperscalers themselves, then arranges fabrication through TSMC. Its customer list includes Google, Meta, ByteDance, Anthropic and OpenAI, and chief executive Hock Tan has said the company added a fifth custom-silicon customer this year, a $1bn order for delivery in late 2026. Broadcom also supplies the Ethernet switches and networking silicon that stitch thousands of GPUs and accelerators together into a single training cluster — plumbing that is agnostic to whose chip sits at the center of the rack.

That positioning is precisely why the stock has become a proxy for the AI trade's breadth. Wall Street expects Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue to hit roughly $16bn this quarter, more than 200% higher than a year ago, on total revenue near $29.4bn — an 85% increase. Tan has guided toward $56bn in AI-chip revenue for all of fiscal 2026 and has talked up a $73bn AI order backlog on the way to a $100bn annual AI-revenue target by 2027. If those numbers hold up, or move higher, it argues that AI capital spending is diversifying across a real ecosystem of suppliers rather than pooling in one vendor's order book.

The market's low tolerance for merely good news

The complication is that Broadcom's own recent history shows results don't have to disappoint to punish the stock. After its June earnings report — which beat estimates — shares fell 13%. The stock hit an all-time closing high of $480.77 on June 2 and has since drifted down to around $370, even as the AI narrative around the company has, if anything, strengthened. Broadcom now carries a $1.76tn market capitalization, making it the world's eighth-most-valuable company, and trades near 31 times forward earnings — a multiple that prices in sustained hypergrowth and leaves little room for hedged language on an earnings call.

That sets up Tuesday's report as a test of nerve as much as of demand. Analysts flagged four things to watch beyond the headline beat: whether revenue guidance clears the roughly $29.25bn bar rather than just meeting it, whether gross margins — which have expanded from 73.9% in fiscal 2021 to 77.3% in fiscal 2025 — hold as AI programs scale against constrained manufacturing capacity, whether management signals new custom-chip customers beyond the current five, and whether commentary on customer concentration and 2027 capacity gives any indication that the backlog is more lumpy than durable.

The stakes extend past one stock. Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey, in his capacity as chair of the Financial Stability Board, warned G20 finance ministers this week that stretched AI valuations combined with rising leverage and concentrated exposure to a handful of technology companies could amplify any future market correction. A Broadcom quarter that confirms broad-based hyperscaler capex — Google, Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI all still building — cuts against that concentration warning. A Broadcom quarter that disappoints, even on strong absolute numbers, would feed it: evidence that the AI capex boom rests more narrowly on Nvidia's order book than the market has been pricing.

What comes next

Results land after Tuesday's close, with the earnings call to follow. The single figure likely to move the stock most is not the reported quarter but the forward revenue guide and any update to the $100bn 2027 AI-revenue target — the same asymmetry that hit Nvidia's stock despite its own beat. The next major data point after that is Nvidia's fiscal third-quarter report in November, alongside continuing capex disclosures from Google, Meta and Microsoft, which will show whether hyperscaler AI spending is still accelerating or beginning to plateau.
