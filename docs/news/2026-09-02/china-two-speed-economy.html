China's economy stays 'two-speed' as tech strength offsets housing slump

Unitree's G1 humanoid robot, emblematic of the tech and robotics rally driving Chinese equities even as property and consumption lag. — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Chinese tech and robotics stocks are surging even as retail sales stall and home prices fall for a 37th straight month — a bet that growth now comes from automation, not consumers.

Two charts, laid side by side, would tell you everything you need to know about China's economy in the late summer of 2026. One shows the CSI 300 up 17 percent since the start of last year and Hong Kong's Hang Seng climbing almost 30 percent over the same stretch, powered by a run of AI and robotics names that has made Shanghai's STAR Market feel, on its more excitable days, like Nasdaq in 1999. The other shows new-home prices falling for a 37th consecutive month, retail sales growth stuck near zero, and real-estate investment down by nearly a fifth year on year. Both charts are true. Neither is describing the same country, exactly — and the gap between them has become the story investors are now trying to price.

A market that has decided what it likes

The clearest evidence of where capital wants to go arrived on August 19, when Unitree Robotics — the Hangzhou-based maker of humanoid and quadruped robots — made its trading debut on the Shanghai Stock Exchange's STAR Market. Priced at 150.80 yuan a share, the stock traded as much as 629 percent higher intraday before closing up 460 percent. Unitree raised roughly 906 million dollars in the offering; DeepSeek, the AI lab that rattled Silicon Valley in January, was among the early backers, alongside Tencent. Unitree booked about 1.7 billion yuan in revenue last year, with more than 40 percent from overseas sales of its robot dogs and, increasingly, its bipedal machines.

It was the loudest single event of a broader move. Over the same window, China's CSI Artificial Intelligence Index rose 5.9 percent, the CSI Semiconductor Index climbed 4.9 percent, and the CSI 5G Communication Index surged more than 8 percent, while the ChiNext growth board — Shenzhen's answer to Nasdaq — jumped 5.64 percent. Goldman Sachs, which correctly called the 2025 rally, is telling clients to expect more of the same, if slower: "We expect the bull run to continue, but at a slower pace," the bank wrote, pointing partly to a structural shift in where Chinese households park their money. With deposit rates down to around 1.5 percent and property no longer a reliable store of value, an estimated 163 trillion yuan in household savings is migrating toward equities — a pool of money large enough, on its own, to keep bidding up whatever sector Beijing has decided to champion.

The economy the rally is ignoring

Set against that is a consumer economy that keeps missing its own low bar. Retail sales grew just 0.6 percent year on year in July, according to China's National Bureau of Statistics — down 0.4 percentage points from June and well short of the 1.8 percent economists in a Caixin survey had expected. Some of the softness is a hangover from Beijing's own stimulus: trade-in subsidies and car-purchase tax cuts pulled spending forward earlier in the year, leaving less to show for July. Industrial output slowed alongside it.

Property remains the deeper wound. New-home prices in July were down 3.2 percent year on year and 0.1 percent month on month — the 37th straight month of declines. Real estate investment fell 16.2 percent in the January–May period, with new construction starts down 22.6 percent and completions down 23.4 percent; for the first half of the year as a whole, investment was off 18 percent. Only 16 of 70 major cities recorded a monthly rise in new-home prices as of the most recent reading, and for the first time in three decades, more than half of residential transactions nationally now involve second-hand homes rather than new construction — a sign that buyers no longer trust developers to deliver what they presell. Beijing has responded with what amounts to a managed retreat rather than a rescue: officials describe a "new development model" in which property becomes a source of steady baseline demand rather than the growth engine it was for two decades, a framing that all but concedes there will be no return to the old boom.

What the divergence is betting on

Economists have taken to calling this a "two-speed" economy, and the numbers back the label up starkly: high-tech manufacturing output grew 13.8 percent year on year in the first seven months of 2026, comfortably outpacing the broader industrial average, even as fixed-asset investment overall fell 6.7 percent. Coastal manufacturing hubs are humming; large parts of the inland economy, still tied to property and construction, are not. The Shanghai municipal economy beat its own GDP forecasts this year even as the national property overhang persisted — a microcosm of the pattern playing out across the country.

What the stock market is doing, in effect, is placing a bet that this split is permanent rather than cyclical — that Beijing has chosen automation, semiconductors and robotics as its next growth engine precisely because it has given up on reflating property or engineering a consumption-led recovery. That is a defensible read of policy: state support has flowed unmistakably toward advanced manufacturing and AI since 2020, the same year authorities began squeezing developer financing. It is also a risky one for equity investors, because a market pricing in a decoupled tech recovery is a market with very little cushion if that recovery disappoints — Unitree's post-debut valuation, for instance, now implies growth assumptions that would be aggressive for a company still built substantially on niche industrial and research sales.

The next test comes with August's retail sales and property data, due from the National Bureau of Statistics in mid-September, alongside any signal from Beijing on further household-facing stimulus — the kind of direct consumption support policymakers have so far avoided in favour of supply-side bets on manufacturing and technology. Until that changes, the market's answer to China's growth problem will keep looking like more robots, not more shoppers.
