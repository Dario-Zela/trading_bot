BEYOND THE TAPE · UK FISCAL · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-02 · 4 min read

Thinktank urges Healey to fund defence spending via tax rise on middle earners

John Healey, pictured as Defence Secretary at the 2024 NATO summit, is now the Chancellor tasked with funding Britain's defence pledge. — U.S. Navy Petty Officer 1st Class Alexander Kubitza / DoD, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Resolution Foundation says Chancellor John Healey cannot fund Labour's 3.5%-of-GDP defence pledge without raising taxes on middle earners.

There is a particular kind of political silence that says more than a denial would. Asked, in effect, how he intends to pay for a defence budget that is meant to swell to 3.5% of GDP by 2035, John Healey — barely six weeks into the job of Chancellor of the Exchequer — has so far offered none. Into that silence has stepped the Resolution Foundation, a think tank with a habit of saying the quiet part first.

In a report published Tuesday titled Thin End of the Wedge, the Foundation's economists conclude that Healey cannot raise the roughly £28bn a year required to hit Labour's defence pledge without dipping into the pay packets of people who consider themselves comfortably, unglamorously middle-income. Not the wealthy, not big business — the £33,000-a-year median earner.

The arithmetic of a bigger state

The Foundation's case rests on international comparison rather than ideology. It notes that Labour has already pushed through roughly £70bn a year in tax rises since taking office in 2024 — a 2.4 percentage point jump in Britain's "tax wedge" (the gap between what an employer pays and what an employee takes home) that was, by the OECD's reckoning, the largest single-year increase of any rich country last year.

And yet, even after all that, the wedge sits at 32.4% for a single earner on average pay — still below the OECD and G7 average. The Foundation's chief economist, James Smith, draws the conclusion bluntly: "No other OECD rich country has a bigger state and a lower burden on average workers, so any politician promising both is not being realistic." Countries that spend more, his team found, tend to fund it by asking ordinary workers to contribute more directly, rather than leaning on wealth or business taxes alone. "Any benefits of increased defence spending will be broadly shared," Smith argues, "so the tax rises needed to fund this should be too, including higher rates on middle earners."

An awkward inheritance

The report lands with a certain irony, given who it is addressed to. Healey spent the first half of this year as Defence Secretary under Keir Starmer, and resigned from that post accusing the Treasury — then run by Rachel Reeves — of refusing to properly fund the military buildup he was demanding. When Andy Burnham replaced Starmer as prime minister in July and rebuilt his cabinet, he handed Healey the very job whose occupant Healey had spent months criticising. The defence hawk is now the one who has to find the money.

He inherits the post at an uncomfortable moment. Ten-year gilt yields touched their highest level since 2008 this week, and 30-year borrowing costs hit their highest since 1998, squeezing the fiscal headroom Healey will have to play with when he delivers his first Budget on October 28. Bloomberg Economics estimates the combination of higher borrowing costs and inflation has already eaten around £12bn of a £23.6bn cushion. Money that might once have absorbed a defence spending increase quietly is now visibly, uncomfortably scarce.

No commitment, no denial

Nothing here amounts to policy. The Resolution Foundation is an influential voice in Westminster's pre-Budget conversation, not a branch of government, and Healey has made no public commitment to raising taxes on middle earners specifically — nor has he ruled it out. Treasury officials have pointed only to the promised spending review next year as the moment fuller detail will emerge.

That non-answer is itself a data point. A government under fiscal pressure, led by a Chancellor who built his reputation demanding more for defence, is now the target of a well-timed report making the case that his own logic requires a tax rise he has not yet been willing to name. Whether he embraces that logic, or spends the coming weeks trying to find £28bn a year somewhere else, should become clear well before the gavel falls on 28 October.
