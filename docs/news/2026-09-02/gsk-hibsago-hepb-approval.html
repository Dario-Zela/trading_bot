GSK's Hibsago wins first global approval as a functional cure for hepatitis B

GSK's head office in Brentford, London; the company's bepirovirsen (Hibsago) is the first drug approved anywhere as a functional cure for chronic hepatitis B. — Ian Wilson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
GSK's Hibsago won Japan's approval as the first functional cure for chronic hepatitis B, with an FDA decision due October 26.

GSK has secured the world's first regulatory approval for a drug that puts chronic hepatitis B into lasting remission without a lifetime of pills — a milestone that has eluded the field for three decades of antiviral medicine. Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare cleared Hibsago (bepirovirsen) on August 24, and GSK is now waiting on the US Food and Drug Administration, which has set an October 26 decision date under a priority review.

The distinction between this and existing hepatitis B drugs is not cosmetic. Current nucleos(t)ide analogues — tenofovir, entecavir and their relatives — suppress the virus for as long as a patient keeps taking them, but stopping treatment typically lets the infection rebound. Bepirovirsen, a six-month course, aims for something closer to a ceasefire the immune system can hold on its own: undetectable virus and undetectable surface antigen for at least 24 weeks after the drug is stopped. GSK and outside researchers have taken to calling that outcome a "functional cure," distinct from full eradication of the virus, which nothing currently achieves.

What the trial data actually show

The Japanese approval rests on the B-Well Phase III programme, which enrolled patients who had already been on antiviral therapy for at least six months but remained chronically infected. Across the pooled trials, 19% of the 1,220 patients given bepirovirsen alongside standard care met the functional-cure definition after 24 weeks off treatment, against 0% of 614 patients on standard care alone (p<0.001) — GSK and independent commentary both put existing background cure rates on antivirals alone at roughly 1%, making a 19-point gap clinically meaningful rather than a statistical footnote. In patients who started with lower viral antigen levels (HBsAg of 1,000 IU/ml or below), the cure rate rose to 26%, against zero in that subgroup's placebo arm.

The mechanism explains why patient selection matters so much. Bepirovirsen is an antisense oligonucleotide — a short synthetic strand of genetic material, licensed from Ionis Pharmaceuticals in 2019, that binds to the hepatitis B virus's RNA and blocks it from being translated into the viral proteins that suppress the immune response. Knock those proteins down for long enough, the theory goes, and a patient's own immune system can start clearing the infection rather than merely being held at bay by drugs. That is a different intervention than the antivirals, which block the enzyme the virus uses to replicate but do nothing to help the immune system recognise it. Ionis stands to collect up to $150 million in remaining milestone payments plus tiered royalties of 10–12% on net sales, a structure typical of the licensing deals through which large pharmaceutical companies now source much of their novel biology.

Safety-wise, the most common adverse events were injection-site reactions and transient rises in liver enzymes — the kind of signal regulators watch closely in liver disease but that did not derail the Japanese review. Patients require a set of six subcutaneous injections over the treatment course.

Why the FDA date matters more than the Japan approval

Japan carries real epidemiological weight on its own — roughly one million people live with chronic hepatitis B there, and the disease contributes to some 4,000 deaths a year, mostly from cirrhosis and liver cancer. But Japan's regulator moved bepirovirsen through under its SAKIGAKE-style expedited pathway for medicines addressing unmet need, and Tokyo's approval, while historic as a first, is not the market that determines whether this becomes a mainstream standard of care.

That test comes on October 26, when the FDA's priority-review clock runs out. The drug already holds Breakthrough Therapy designation in the US, granted after the B-Well data read out, which is itself a strong signal of regulatory receptivity — the designation is reserved for drugs showing substantial improvement over existing therapy on a preliminary evidence basis. An approval would put bepirovirsen in front of American hepatologists and, more consequentially, set a template other regulators — the European Medicines Agency, and eventually authorities across Asia and Africa — are likely to follow. GlobalData has forecast Hibsago could approach blockbuster status by 2034, with global sales near $926 million in a hepatitis B therapeutics market it projects will reach $3.2 billion that year.

Markets so far have shrugged. GSK shares actually edged lower in the days after the Japan announcement, even paired with a separate oncology readout — a reminder that a single approval, however novel the mechanism, does not by itself move a company the size of GSK, and that investors are waiting on the FDA's answer before repricing the hepatitis B franchise.

The gap between a cure and access to it

The scale of unmet need is the reason this approval is being watched well beyond pharmaceutical circles. The World Health Organization estimates more than 250 million people live with chronic hepatitis B worldwide, causing around 1.1 million deaths a year, mostly from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma — hepatitis B remains a leading cause of liver cancer globally. GSK cites data showing that clearing surface antigen is associated with an 89% reduction in liver cancer risk and a 62% reduction in all-cause mortality relative to patients who remain antigen-positive, which is the clinical rationale for chasing a functional cure rather than settling for suppression.

The geography of the disease does not match the geography of where a $900-million-a-year drug is likely to sell first. Sub-Saharan Africa carries a disproportionate share of the global burden — WHO data put hepatitis B surface antigen prevalence above 1% in 26 African countries, with an estimated regional seroprevalence around 6% — yet as of the most recent WHO figures, only about 13% of people with the infection there have been diagnosed and just 3% are on treatment, far below the WHO's 2025 targets of 60% diagnosed and 50% treated. A six-injection biologic requiring monitoring and cold-chain handling is, for now, a far harder proposition to deploy in a low-resource setting than the generic oral antivirals that already struggle to reach patients there. GSK's press materials talk about broadening access over time; whether that translates into differentiated pricing or licensing for high-burden, low-income countries is not yet specified, and is the detail worth pressing the company on as the drug rolls out.

The next fixed point is October 26, the FDA's decision date. A US approval would be the moment this stops being a single-market breakthrough and starts becoming a global treatment-guideline question — and the moment the harder conversation, about who actually gets access to a functional cure, begins in earnest.
