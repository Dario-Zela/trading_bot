FTC and 22 states sue Amazon over alleged $20bn ad-auction rigging

The Federal Trade Commission's Apex Building headquarters in Washington, D.C. — Harrison Keely / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
FTC and 22 states allege Amazon secretly overrode its own second-price ad auctions to overcharge advertisers by $20bn since 2019; Amazon calls the case misguided.

Amazon told advertisers for years that its ad auctions worked on a simple, honest principle: the highest bidder wins, but pays only a cent more than whoever came second. On Monday, the Federal Trade Commission and attorneys general from 22 states said that promise was false — and that the gap between what Amazon claimed and what it actually charged added up to roughly $20 billion since 2019.

The lawsuit, filed in federal court in Seattle, targets the advertising engine that has become Amazon's most profitable business line, generating tens of billions of dollars a year from sellers bidding for placement in front of shoppers. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton filed a separate, parallel suit over the same conduct the same day. Amazon shares fell roughly 2.5–3% on the news and continued sliding into Tuesday, though at least one bank — Citi — called the pullback a buying opportunity rather than a red flag.

How the auction was allegedly rigged

Second-price auctions are a well-understood mechanism, used across digital advertising precisely because they're supposed to be self-policing: bidders have no incentive to lowball, since they'll only ever pay one increment above the runner-up, not their own number. Amazon marketed its Sponsored Products, Sponsored Brands and Sponsored Display formats on that basis.

The complaint alleges the company quietly abandoned the mechanism it advertised. According to the FTC, Amazon overrode actual auction outcomes with higher, Amazon-set prices in 70 to 80% of auctions, and charged advertisers something very close to their own full bid — rather than one cent above the next-highest — in roughly four out of five Sponsored Products auctions. Regulators also allege Amazon used an "invented auction participant" — a phantom bidder with no real ad to run — to push prices higher than genuine competition would allow. A former employee is quoted in the complaint saying the setup let Amazon charge "beyond what [can] be achieved through advertiser competition."

The FTC says the effect compounded on Amazon's busiest shopping days, with pay-per-click costs rising as much as 50% during peak events, and that advertisers passed at least some of that cost through to shoppers in the form of higher retail prices. "Amazon has millions of advertising customers who were misled into paying significantly higher prices," FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson said in a statement announcing the case. "These higher costs were largely passed on to American consumers."

Amazon's numbers versus the FTC's numbers

Amazon's public rebuttal, posted the same day, is built almost entirely on a competing set of statistics. The company says average winning bids actually fell 50% between 2019 and 2025, that cost-per-click stayed flat in inflation-adjusted terms from 2019 to 2024, and that its auction design — which it describes as a standard generalized second-price mechanism used industry-wide for decades — delivered advertisers more than $8 billion in savings between 2021 and 2025 while lifting conversion rates for individual sellers by over 24%. The company also points out that 92% of placed ads were not, in fact, the highest bid submitted, which it presents as evidence the system rewards relevance rather than raw price.

On the consumer-harm claim specifically, Amazon argues the FTC's complaint contains no direct evidence that shoppers paid more, and notes that its own customers saved an average of $230 a year through its deals and loyalty programs. It has also gone after the FTC's evidentiary basis, calling the case "misguided" and accusing regulators of leaning on outdated internal training material — the company says the specific courses cited in the complaint had a combined 1,849 enrollments over their entire lifetime, and one instructional video was watched by just 928 people over two and a half years.

Why the ad business is the one that matters

Amazon's retail arm runs on thin margins; its advertising business does not. It is the closest thing the company has to a pure-margin software product, sold on top of a marketplace where sellers have little choice but to buy visibility if they want to be found. That is exactly why the auction's integrity is the load-bearing question here: if Amazon was quietly setting a price floor above what competitive bidding would produce, the effect is indistinguishable from a hidden tax on every seller who wanted a spot on the results page — a tax collected by the platform that also decides who ranks where.

This is the FTC's third major action against Amazon in recent years, following a $2.5 billion settlement last September over Prime's sign-up and cancellation practices. That case ended in a payout without an admission of wrongdoing; this one is shaping up differently, with two separate government plaintiffs, a detailed evidentiary complaint citing internal documents and former employees, and no early indication Amazon intends to settle quickly. A defeat on the merits would put pressure on Amazon to refund advertisers, disclose auction mechanics in more granular form, or restructure how Sponsored Products pricing works — any of which cuts into the margin that makes the ad segment so valuable to the group's earnings.

What comes next

The case now heads into pretrial discovery in the Western District of Washington, where the FTC's internal-document citations and the "invented bidder" allegation will face their first real test. Watch for Amazon's formal motion to dismiss, which will likely lean on the same statistical defense — falling bids, flat CPC, savings passed to advertisers — laid out in its public statement. Paxton's parallel Texas suit adds a second track that could move on a different timeline. For now, the market's initial verdict was a shrug rather than a selloff: a few points off the share price, not a repricing of the ad segment's earnings power.
