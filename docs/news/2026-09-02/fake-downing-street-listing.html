Fake 10 Downing Street Booking.com listing exposes lax platform checks

The door of 10 Downing Street, which Which? listed as a one-bedroom holiday apartment on Booking.com. — robertsharp / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Which? kept a fake 10 Downing Street listing live on Booking.com for two months, saying it exposes systemic gaps in the platform's verification checks.

It took a researcher at Which? about twenty minutes to turn the British prime minister's front door into a holiday let. On 18 June, the consumer group listed "10 Downing Street" on Booking.com as a "1 bedroom apartment in the heart of London," complete with the real address and a photograph of the famous black door with its brass lion knocker. Booking.com did not take the listing down until 27 August — more than two months later.

In one sentence: A consumer watchdog says a bogus Downing Street listing sat live on Booking.com for two months, exposing how thin the platform's verification checks really are.

In the twenty minutes Which? left the listing open to bookings, fourteen people tried to reserve a stay. The group accepted one, paid for a week's stay through the platform's normal checkout, and says the money still has not been refunded. A second researcher, posing as a guest, later left a five-star review under the name "Trevor," praising the location and singling out a highlight of the stay: "hanging out" with Larry, the Downing Street cat, official holder of the title Chief Mouser to the Cabinet Office since 2011. That review also went live without challenge.

Checks that check nothing

The finding matters less for its punchline — nobody actually got to sleep in the prime minister's flat — than for what it says about the plumbing underneath one of the world's largest travel marketplaces. Booking.com's own rules do not require a host to submit photo ID or proof of ownership until three months after a listing first appears, which is longer than Which?'s fake entry survived even after being flagged. Rory Boland, Which?'s travel editor, put it bluntly: "If Booking.com's so-called sophisticated AI systems can't spot that 10 Downing Street is not a holiday rental, then it's no wonder scammers can exploit the platform so easily." Which? says the wider investigation, of which the Downing Street stunt was one test case, "uncovered systemic security failures across the platform," including other listings using addresses of properties their supposed hosts did not own.

Booking.com's response leaned on the artificiality of the test rather than disputing its outcome. A spokesperson told the BBC that the listing was never "live" in the sense of being publicly bookable across the full two months, and that "some of our automatic fraud controls were not triggered to completely remove the closed listing" — an admission, in effect, that a listing can sit in limbo, neither fully active nor fully purged, for weeks. The company separately maintains it uses a mix of automated and manual checks, including AI systems, that catch "the majority of fraudulent listings within 24 hours," and cautioned that a single limited test "is not a true reflection of the experience of millions of listings or reviews published on our platform."

Why a stunt like this lands

The stunt works precisely because it is legible without any specialist knowledge — everyone recognises that door — but the underlying complaint is one that has dogged marketplace platforms of every stripe for years: identity and ownership verification is expensive and friction-inducing, so platforms tend to backload it, verifying after a listing goes live rather than before. That trade-off is invisible until someone demonstrates it with the world's most photographed front door, at which point it becomes, briefly, everyone's problem. Airbnb, eBay and various online marketplaces have faced near-identical criticism over the years for accepting listings first and asking questions later.

What happens next is mostly regulatory theatre unless something forces Booking.com's hand. Which? has called for tighter identity verification before a listing can go live at all, rather than the current three-month grace period, and for platforms to be held liable when fraudulent listings cause direct financial harm to guests. The UK's Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, which gives the Competition and Markets Authority stronger powers over misleading online listings, is the mechanism most likely to be tested if further cases surface. For now, the more durable damage may be reputational: a watchdog has shown, in public, that the checks meant to stop fraud on one of the internet's biggest booking platforms did not so much as blink at an address every person in Britain can recognise.
