Nestlé sells mainstream vitamins brands for $1bn, a steep markdown

Nestlé's administrative headquarters in Vevey, Switzerland. — Jmh2o / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nestlé sold seven mainstream vitamin brands, including Nature's Bounty, for $1bn — an 82% markdown on the $5.75bn it paid in 2021.

Nestlé has agreed to sell seven mainstream vitamin and supplement brands, including Nature's Bounty, to US private equity firm Yellow Wood Partners for $1 billion — a little over five years after paying $5.75 billion for the core of that same business.

The deal, announced Tuesday, hands Yellow Wood a portfolio built around Nature's Bounty — still the number two vitamins, minerals and supplements (VMS) brand in the US — alongside Osteo Bi-Flex, Ester-C, Gard, Nuun, Puritan's Pride and Sisu, plus Nestlé's US private-label supplements business and the manufacturing and distribution operations that support it. The unit posted $1.2 billion in sales last year. Nestlé bought the core of it from KKR-owned The Bountiful Company in 2021 for $5.75 billion, when those same brands were generating $1.87 billion in trailing sales. On a price-to-sales basis, Nestlé paid roughly 3x then and is selling at roughly 0.8x now.

A carve-out, not a fire sale

Nestlé is keeping the parts of its supplements business it considers defensible: Solgar and Pure Encapsulations, both positioned as premium, clinically backed brands sold partly through practitioners rather than pharmacy shelves. "This is another important step in the strategic transformation of our portfolio," said chief executive Philipp Navratil. "We are focusing our resources where we have the strongest competitive advantage." Navratil, who took over a year ago after his predecessor Laurent Freixe was dismissed over an undisclosed relationship with a subordinate, has since used joint ventures to cut Nestlé's exposure to ice cream and bottled water and is now narrowing the group around coffee, pet care and packaged food.

For Yellow Wood, this is the sixth sizeable carve-out it has bought from a global consumer giant, following purchases of Q-tips, Chapstick and Suave from larger owners looking to declutter. Its partners framed the deal as a bet on underinvested but still-growing brands. "VMS adoption continues to increase among a wide range of consumers and demand for benefit-specific solutions expands," said partner Tad Yanagi, while colleague Dana Schmaltz argued that running the brands as a standalone company would "accelerate the growth of all of these brands" — the standard private-equity thesis that a corporate parent's overhead and inattention were suppressing value a leaner owner can unlock. The deal needs regulatory clearance and is not expected to close until the first half of 2027.

Category split, not just a bad bet

The more interesting comparison than Nestlé's own purchase price is a deal struck barely a month earlier: Procter & Gamble agreed to pay $3.8 billion for Thorne, a supplements maker with a fraction of the revenue Nestlé is now offloading. Thorne sells through clinical and practitioner channels and has leaned into the market for products that complement GLP-1 weight-loss drugs — protein, electrolytes, micronutrient support for patients on Wegovy or Zepbound. Nature's Bounty and Puritan's Pride, by contrast, compete on drugstore shelf space and price promotions, the segment of the vitamins market most exposed as GLP-1 use has climbed from roughly 9% of US households in January 2025 to an estimated 21% now, denting demand for the general multivitamins that built brands like Nature's Bounty in the first place.

That contrast — one supplements business fetching nearly four times what Nestlé is accepting, despite smaller revenue — points to a market that has split cleanly along the same premium-versus-mass lines reshaping skincare, pet food and infant formula: scientific credibility and specialist distribution command a premium; broad-market shelf brands increasingly don't. Nestlé's 2021 purchase, made near the peak of pandemic-era home health spending, didn't anticipate either the GLP-1 shift or how quickly private-label and discount competitors would erode mainstream vitamin pricing. That is a forecasting failure as much as an operational one, and it means the 82% write-down says less about Nestlé mishandling these brands than about a market repricing itself out from under an acquisition that assumed the old growth math still applied.

What to watch

Nestlé's near-term share reaction is likely to be muted — the 2027 closing date pushes any earnings benefit well into the future, and the write-down itself will show up in restructuring charges before then. The more useful signal is what Yellow Wood does with the brands: if it can grow Nature's Bounty and Puritan's Pride as an independent operator, that would suggest Nestlé's execution, not the category, was the problem. If the brands keep losing shelf share to store labels and GLP-1-adjacent challengers, it confirms the mainstream vitamin aisle is shrinking regardless of who owns it. Regulatory clearance and the deal's actual close in H1 2027 will be the next formal checkpoints; Nestlé's full-year results in February will show how large the associated write-down lands on the balance sheet.
