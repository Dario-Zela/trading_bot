Shell buys out US retail partner as it weighs $8bn chemicals sale

A Shell-branded fuel station forecourt in the US, where Shell is expanding its company-owned retail network. — Michael Rivera (Mjrmtg) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Shell is buying full control of US retailer Tri Star Energy while shopping its loss-making US chemicals unit for up to $8bn — cash cow in, cyclical asset out.

Shell is buying out its remaining partners in Tri Star Energy, the Nashville-based fuel retailer, taking its stake from 33% to full ownership in a deal that will more than double the company's US-owned convenience-store footprint. It is a modest transaction by Shell's standards — financial terms were not disclosed — but it lands the same week the company is fielding bids of up to $8bn for its US chemicals business, and the pairing says something sharper about where Shell wants its capital than either deal does alone.

"Tri Star has built a strong business with high-quality assets, a dedicated team and a loyal customer base." — Machteld de Haan, Shell's president of downstream, renewables and energy solutions

Buying more of what already works

Equilon Enterprises, which trades as Shell Oil Products US, is acquiring the remaining Tri Star stake from The Parman Corporation and Kimbro Oil Company, its partners since Shell first took a minority position in the business. The deal brings in 320 company-owned fuel and convenience sites across Tennessee and neighbouring states, plus supply agreements covering another 552 dealer-owned locations. Once it closes — expected by the end of 2026, pending regulatory sign-off — Shell's US network will run to roughly 550 company-owned sites and 650 dealer-owned ones, operated under Texas Petroleum Group, a Shell Mobility & Convenience subsidiary.

Shell frames this as capital discipline rather than a fresh bet on fuel demand: the company says it is directing 80% of its Mobility & Convenience growth spending to ten priority markets, the US chief among them, and that the acquisition clears its internal hurdle rate for returns. That is a narrower claim than "doubling down on petrol" — it is closer to consolidating a stake it already held in a business it says is already profitable, rather than expanding into new fuel-retail territory. The distinction matters for how the deal should be read: Shell is not detecting fresh appetite for gasoline in Tennessee, it is buying out partners in a forecourt network it has run alongside them for years.

Selling the chemicals Shell doesn't want

The more telling capital-allocation signal sits on the other side of the ledger. Shell is weighing offers of up to $8bn for four US chemicals plants in Louisiana, Texas and Pennsylvania — including a Monaca, Pennsylvania site with 1.6 million tonnes of annual polymer capacity — with ExxonMobil, LyondellBasell, Apollo Global Management and Kuwait Petroleum's chemicals arm all reported as potential bidders. The unit lost $1.125bn on an adjusted basis in 2025, and while US chemicals utilisation rates have recovered to around 85% in the first half of 2026, Shell appears unpersuaded that a cyclical rebound changes the underlying case for owning the assets.

Taken together, the Tri Star and chemicals moves describe the same instinct from two directions: keep the businesses that generate steady cash close to the pump, sell the ones that require betting on a commodity-chemicals cycle Shell doesn't control. Chief executive Wael Sawan has been explicit that he expects oil prices to stay elevated over the next five to ten years — "all the easy oil and gas has been found," he told a conference in June — and Shell shares have tracked crude's swings this year, rallying as Brent rebounded from a July low near $71 back above $90 after the Strait of Hormuz ceasefire broke down. A management team confident in that thesis has more reason to own forecourts, which monetise fuel demand directly, than crackers and polymer plants, which monetise a feedstock cycle several steps removed from it.

What it signals, and what to watch

Neither transaction is large enough to reshape Shell's balance sheet on its own. But the pattern — buy control of a proven US retail network, shop a loss-making chemicals unit to rivals and private equity — reads as sharpening rather than retreat: Shell concentrating around fuel distribution and upstream production, and treating chemicals as a business better owned by someone with a different view of the cycle, or more patience for it. The test will be whether the chemicals sale actually completes near the $8bn mark being discussed, and whether Shell brings similar bolt-on retail deals to other priority markets on its list of ten. The company has set an end-2026 close for both the Tri Star deal and, tentatively, the chemicals sale — a tight enough window that the next set of headlines should confirm or complicate the story within a few months.
