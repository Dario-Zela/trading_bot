Employers urged to give interview feedback, as applicants say they want it

In one sentence
A push for employers to give rejected applicants interview feedback comes as the UK job market stays tough, making each unexplained rejection costlier.

Few experiences in working life are quite as quiet as the rejected interview. A candidate prepares for days, travels or logs on, answers the questions, and then hears either a form email or nothing at all. A push for employers to change that, by giving unsuccessful applicants real feedback, is gathering attention, as reported by BBC Business. The timing is pointed: the UK job market is widely described as tough, and applicants have more reason than usual to want to know what went wrong.

The ask

The proposal is simple. Employers who interview candidates and then turn them down should tell them something useful about why. That means more than a template line about "a high standard of applicants". It means a sentence or two on what fell short, or what might have tipped the decision.

The case for it rests on an imbalance. An employer sees dozens of applicants and learns from every round. The applicant sees one process and, without feedback, learns nothing from it. Candidates who are told where an answer was thin, or where experience was lacking, can fix it before the next interview. Those left guessing tend to repeat the same mistakes.

The reporting available for this piece does not give the names of those pressing for the change, any survey figures on how many applicants say they want feedback, or any response from employers' groups or regulators. Those details sit in the original BBC report and are not reproduced here.

Why employers hold back

The objections are familiar, and they are not frivolous. Detailed feedback takes time that hiring managers often do not have, particularly when they are handling many applications. There is also a legal worry: candid comments about why someone was not chosen can be picked over in a discrimination claim, which is why many firms default to the blandest wording available.

There is a middle path. Feedback does not have to be an essay. Two or three specific, job-related points, delivered by a short call or email, carry most of the value and little of the risk. Firms that already do this tend to reserve it for candidates who reached the final stages, which is where the effort of the applicant, and the sense of being owed an explanation, is greatest.

A tough market sharpens the point

The backdrop matters, though it should not be overstated. This is a story about hiring manners, not a shift in the labour market. But when vacancies are scarce and competition for each role is stiff, each rejection costs an applicant more, and the information in it is worth more. In a buoyant market a candidate can shrug off silence and apply elsewhere. In a slack one, the same silence leaves people repeating an approach that is not working.

There is a reputational angle for employers too. Rejected candidates are often also customers, clients or future applicants, and a courteous, informative rejection costs little compared with the goodwill it protects.

What to watch

The test of any such push is whether it moves from encouragement to practice. The things to look for are whether employers' bodies or large recruiters sign up to a feedback standard, and whether any survey data emerges to show how often applicants currently receive it. Until then, the campaign remains a request, and one that costs employers very little to grant.
