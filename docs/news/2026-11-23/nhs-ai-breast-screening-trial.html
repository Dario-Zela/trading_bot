NHS launches world's largest trial of AI breast cancer screening

In one sentence
EDITH, the world's largest trial of AI in breast screening, will test whether AI finds more cancers and eases radiologist shortages, but outcome data are years away.

The NHS is testing whether artificial intelligence can find breast cancers that routine screening misses. Behind that headline question sits a more practical one: whether AI can take some of the load off the radiologists whose scarcity limits how many women can be screened.

The trial, known as EDITH, was announced by the UK government on 4 February 2025. It is billed as the world's largest of its kind. The government's figure is nearly 700,000 women, all already booked for routine NHS breast screening, across about 30 sites, backed by £11m from the National Institute for Health and Care Research (NIHR). The government announcement does not say when recruitment begins or ends.

A caveat on timing and scale. The BBC report that prompted this article is dated 23 November 2026, but the trial itself was announced nearly two years earlier. The sources checked for this article do not establish what "launch" means in November 2026, whether a new rollout stage, a recruitment milestone or a fresh report on an existing programme. The participant figure is also unsettled. The trial's registry entry and public information sheet give about 660,000 women, against the government's "nearly 700,000". The sources do not explain the gap. It may be the difference between a planned and a headline number.

How screening works now, and what changes

In the UK, each mammogram is normally read by two specialists, who then reconcile any disagreement. That double reading is a safeguard. It is also a heavy staffing requirement.

In the intervention arm of EDITH, AI reads first and flags whether a scan carries a high or low likelihood of cancer, before NHS experts review it. In the control arm, two human readers look at the images as usual. According to the government, the technology "would let one specialist complete the process", which "could free up hundreds of radiologists and other specialists" if the trial succeeds.

The workforce argument is the one with the most immediate stakes. Screening capacity depends on having enough people qualified to read images. An AI that safely halves the human reading load would change that constraint more than a modest gain in detection would.

Political framing has leaned on earlier diagnosis. Science Secretary Peter Kyle said: "Catching cancer weeks earlier could be the difference between life and death." Women in the trial are told through their screening invitation, and may opt out of having their images read with AI help.

What the Swedish evidence shows

EDITH follows a smaller, earlier randomised trial in Sweden, MASAI, whose results are the main evidence so far. The figures are easy to blur, because they come from different papers.

  • Workload. The 2023 interim safety analysis in Lancet Oncology reported a reduction in screen-reading workload of nearly 44% (MDedge summary).
  • Detection. A later analysis in Lancet Digital Health reported a higher cancer detection rate, about 29% in that paper, without a rise in false positives (PubMed). Some write-ups give about 20%, so the number depends on which analysis is cited.
  • Interval cancers. Full results in The Lancet in January 2026 found interval cancers, those that appear between screens, fell by 12%, with 82 cases against 93 (Lund University). That is the outcome closest to what matters to patients.

The authors flagged limits: a single country, one mammography device and one AI system. They also said the study does not support replacing radiologists, since at least one still read every screen (ASCO Post). Whether results from Sweden carry over to a different population, different equipment and different software is exactly what a UK trial is meant to test.

What would count as success

The case for AI rests on a pair of conditions: more cancers found, and no rise in false alarms. A tool that detects more cancers by recalling many more healthy women would add anxiety, extra imaging and biopsies, and could consume the capacity it was meant to free. A tool that matches human accuracy with half the reading would be useful even without a detection gain.

Charities have been cautious. Breast Cancer Now has said any changes to the screening programme must rest on robust evidence that a new tool is safe and effective for women in the UK.

That evidence will take time. Outcome data on cancers found and missed will not arrive for years, so EDITH is for now a story about a test, not a result. The next markers to watch are confirmation of recruitment numbers and the first published analysis from the trial's sites. Any early look at recall rates, the clearest early signal of whether AI is adding false alarms, would come before findings on survival.
