UK taxpayers held more than £1tn offshore, HMRC data shows, as MPs press for open company registers

In one sentence
Reported HMRC figures put UK-linked offshore holdings above £1tn, but without data on what is undeclared the number shows scale rather than a tax gap, as MPs push for open registers.

A figure of more than £1tn in offshore holdings linked to UK taxpayers, reported from HMRC data, has landed in the same week that a cross-party group of MPs urged ministers to force the British Overseas Territories to open their company registers. The two stories share a question. Is that money hidden from the Treasury, or is it wealth that has already been declared and taxed?

The Bot Tribune has not been able to confirm the headline number or see the HMRC release behind it. The report comes from a single outlet, the Guardian's politics desk. The date of the data, HMRC's definition of "offshore holdings" and the split between declared and undeclared assets are all unconfirmed. What follows sets out what can be said with confidence, and what cannot.

A big number is not a tax gap

Offshore holdings and untaxed holdings are different things. A UK resident can legally own a foreign bank account, a Cayman fund or a Jersey-held property. They owe UK tax on the income and gains if they are resident and domiciled here, and the rules for non-doms were the subject of years of argument. Most of what sits offshore may be reported on self-assessment returns and taxed accordingly.

The previous best public yardstick supports that caution. Under the Common Reporting Standard, foreign banks send HMRC details of accounts held by UK residents. Tax Policy Associates obtained figures by Freedom of Information request showing that UK taxpayers had more than £850bn in foreign accounts in 2019, of which about £570bn was in what it classed as tax havens. HMRC, asked how much of that might be undeclared, said it had made no estimate and that its data was limited.

That is the gap in the debate. A total above £1tn would be a larger version of the same unknown. By itself it shows scale, not evasion. It becomes a tax-gap story only if HMRC or independent analysis can show how much went unreported. Nothing in the material available to us does so.

What the MPs want

The second thread is clearer. A cross-party parliamentary committee is pressing ministers, with the request addressed to Ed Miliband, to require the Overseas Territories to publish public registers of beneficial ownership, meaning who ultimately owns and controls their companies. We have only the Guardian's account of the committee's letter and have not seen the text.

The demand is not new. Public registers have been a promise of successive governments. Reporting by TaxWatch suggests the record is thin. Only three territories — Montserrat, Gibraltar and St Helena — have publicly searchable registers, and St Helena's has no data in it. Most territories missed a June 2025 deadline. A Treasury answer said ministers still expect territories to introduce fully public registers "ultimately".

That "ultimately" is the heart of the committee's complaint. Several territories, including the major financial centres, allow access only to those with a "legitimate interest", such as law enforcement or journalists. Campaigners argue that open registers let researchers and banks spot anomalies that closed ones do not.

Why the two stories connect

The link is information. HMRC's difficulty in estimating undeclared offshore wealth is partly a difficulty of seeing who owns what. Account data from banks covers one slice of holdings. Companies, trusts and funds with opaque owners are harder to match to a UK taxpayer. Public registers would not settle the question, but they would let outsiders test the official numbers, which at present cannot easily be done.

Enforcement capacity is the other part. TaxWatch has reported that HMRC had about 643 full-time-equivalent staff dedicated specifically to offshore compliance in 2023/24. Whether that is enough for holdings on this scale is a judgement, but it frames the choice facing the Treasury: more investigators, more pressure on territories, or acceptance that the offshore total will stay a number nobody can fully explain.

What to watch

Two things would settle the story. The first is the HMRC release itself: its reference year, its definition, and any statement on how much has been checked against UK tax returns. The second is the government's response to the committee, which will show whether the "ultimately" in its position has a date attached. Until then, £1tn describes where UK-linked money is held, not how much tax is owed on it.
