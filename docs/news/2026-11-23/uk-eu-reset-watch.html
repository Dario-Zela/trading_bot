UK-EU reset: Burnham defends the deal in the Commons as critics circle

In one sentence
Burnham has defended the UK-EU summit outcome to MPs as 'landmark' while the Conservatives vow to scrap it and critics question the lack of a Commons vote.

Andy Burnham has taken the argument over the UK-EU summit to the House of Commons. The Prime Minister defended its outcome to MPs, and Downing Street describes the result as "landmark". Burnham said it could make the next decade "much better". The sharper question in the chamber was one of process: the agreements are being defended, not put to a vote.

The reporting available so far is thin on the detail of what was agreed. The texts of the agreements, and the exact wording of the Prime Minister's remarks, should be read against the official record rather than against headlines.

A deal defended, not voted on

The procedural point is the one that will outlast the news cycle. A broad reset of Britain's relationship with its largest trading partner is being presented to the Commons for scrutiny and defence. According to the reporting, there is no vote on the agreements themselves. MPs have raised that gap, and it gives opponents a line that does not depend on the merits of the deal.

The merits are also hard to judge from the available material. Nothing in it quantifies the effect on trade, sterling or business planning. Companies that trade across the Channel will want the detail before they adjust anything, and the early coverage supplies none.

Squeezed from both sides

Downing Street has also rejected claims that Burnham is "backtracking" on closer EU links. That denial is a response to pressure from two directions. Critics on one side want the reset to go further, and critics on the other think it already goes too far. Government has to hold a position between the two, and each statement defending it invites the charge that it is either timid or reckless.

The Conservatives have chosen their side. Their deputy leader says the party would scrap the new arrangements if elected, according to BBC Politics. That report is the only source for the pledge, and the date of the statement and the terms it targets have yet to be checked. Opposition parties often promise to reverse a government's signature policy. The promise matters more if the deal proves substantial, because it would turn Brexit back into a live dividing line ahead of an election. It would also give businesses a reason to treat the arrangements as provisional.

Two former prime ministers weigh in

The wider argument is also being conducted by past leaders. Theresa May reportedly said she was "aghast" at Burnham's EU plans and told him "Brexit is done", according to BBC Politics and Guardian Politics. Tony Blair reportedly argued that rejoining the EU would only make sense if the EU innovates on technology. Neither remark comes with a date or a policy document, and the coverage does not set out which of Burnham's proposals provoked them. Together they suggest the debate is moving from whether Brexit was a mistake to what any closer relationship would have to deliver.

What to watch

Three things will show how firmly the deal holds. The first is whether the government concedes any form of parliamentary vote or fuller scrutiny, since the process complaint is the easiest one for MPs of all parties to press. The second is whether the published texts allow economists and trade bodies to put numbers on the effect. The third is whether the Conservative pledge to scrap the arrangements is repeated with specifics. A repeat would harden it into a commitment, and only then would it start to affect business planning.
