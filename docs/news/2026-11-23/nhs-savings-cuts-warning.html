NHS savings drive leaves English hospitals facing 'unpalatable' cuts

In one sentence
Thirty-one English NHS trusts have flagged in their risk registers that savings demands threaten normal functioning, with named plans to cut posts, wards and services.

Thirty-one NHS trusts in England have told their own boards that this year's savings demands threaten their ability to function normally, according to a Guardian review of published risk registers. The warnings turn a budgeting exercise into a question about what patients will lose: a ward, a GP out-of-hours service, a hydrotherapy pool, or the staff who run them.

The reporting is based on documents trusts must maintain and publish, not on a settled national decision. The figures below are as reported by the Guardian; the underlying board papers have not been independently reviewed here.

What the risk registers show

All 31 trusts have listed the financial squeeze as a threat. For 27 of them, lack of money is the single biggest risk. Six scored it 25, the maximum on a standard risk matrix. They include King's College Hospital in London, which has an £85m savings target this year; the St George's, Epsom and St Helier group, with £91m; and acute trusts in North Midlands, Gloucestershire, Doncaster and Bassetlaw, plus Birmingham and Solihull mental health trust.

The word "unpalatable" appears in a Torbay and South Devon board paper, which discusses "unpalatable" options as the trust pursues a £54.4m cost improvement target against an underlying deficit of about £100m. It is a trust's own language about its choices, not a verdict from a national body. Torbay is cutting 199 non-clinical posts and raising staff parking and food charges.

The Guardian's account does not give a national percentage efficiency target, so how the demands compare with earlier years cannot be established from this reporting. What it does show is the scale at individual trusts.

Where the cuts fall

The plans vary in size and in how directly they touch patients:

  • Nottingham University Hospitals plans to cut its workforce by 1,279 to meet a £106m target for 2026-27. A third of the savings it has identified are rated high risk, meaning they may not materialise.
  • Barts Health plans to cut 1,100 posts.
  • East Kent cut pay rates for extra "bank" shifts, which the report links to understaffing and waits of up to a month for some Kent cancer patients before chemotherapy. The causal link is the Guardian's reporting and is worth treating cautiously.
  • York and Scarborough is closing a 28-bed step-down ward at Bridlington to save £1m, and is weighing cuts to Whitby services and urgent treatment centre hours, each worth roughly £200,000.
  • Harrogate and District is ending out-of-hours GP care in Hambleton and Richmond after 15 years; it is unclear who will provide it next.
  • Sherwood Forest plans to close the King's Mill hospital hydrotherapy pool, saving £150,000 to £200,000 and affecting about 1,500 patients a year. Sara Hazzard of the Chartered Society of Physiotherapy called aquatic physiotherapy "a clinical lifeline" for people with chronic conditions.

Trusts attribute their difficulties to rising drug prices, strikes, the cost of pay rises, summer heatwave demand and the rollout of electronic health records. Privately, the report says, some argue NHS England set unreasonable targets. One unnamed chief executive said finances are "as tough as they have ever been" and that "very few trusts will break even".

One-off or pattern?

The evidence for a pattern is the breadth: 31 trusts, across acute, mental health and community settings, all recording the same risk. The evidence against reading it as a settled national trend is that the cuts are plans, and several are described only as options. Risk registers are also, by design, cautious documents; a high score records exposure, not an outcome.

NHS England did not respond directly to the Guardian's request for comment. A spokesperson said the NHS had brought its finances within budget last year for the first time in more than a decade, and that boards are being transparent about risks. That framing cuts both ways: a balanced budget last year is consistent with the argument that the savings were found, and with the argument that the easy ones are now spent.

Campaigners are blunter. Dr Tony O'Sullivan of Keep Our NHS Public said: "AI can't staff a ward, and another reorganisation won't reopen a single bed," and urged the health secretary to seek emergency funding from the chancellor. Emma Rowland of NHS England told MPs the NHS is too short of cash to fund the beds it needs this winter, a statement as reported by the Guardian.

What to watch

The next evidence will come from trust board meetings, where the options in papers like Torbay's become decisions, and from year-end financial returns showing how many trusts break even. Whether the high-risk savings at trusts such as Nottingham are delivered, and whether service closures survive local opposition, will show if this is a cluster of hard cases or the shape of the year. Winter bed pressure, which Ms Rowland has already flagged, will test the thinnest margins first.
