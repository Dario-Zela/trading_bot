Centrica boss 'hugely concerned' as household energy bills look set to rise

In one sentence
Centrica's Chris O'Shea is 'hugely concerned' as a forecast 26% price-cap rise to £2,166 looms, with unpaid bills already adding about £1.50 a week to each electricity customer.

The chief executive of Centrica, the owner of British Gas, says he is "hugely concerned" about what a sharp rise in household energy bills will do to customers' ability to pay. Chris O'Shea made the remark on the BBC's Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg. It links a cost-of-living problem to a FTSE 100 utility, because the same households that struggle to pay are the ones whose unpaid bills end up on everyone else's account.

The rise

Ofgem is due to announce the next price cap on Wednesday. The consultancy Cornwall Insight forecasts a 26% increase, to £2,166 a year for a typical household, from 1 January. That is about £443 more than the current level. The cap covers roughly 20 million households in England, Scotland and Wales.

The forecast is Cornwall Insight's, not Ofgem's, and the regulator's figure may differ. Mr O'Shea appears to accept the direction of travel, though. The cause is wholesale energy prices, which suppliers pay and which have climbed this year because of the conflict in the Middle East.

The timing is also awkward. The first quarter is when households use the most energy, so a higher unit price arrives when consumption peaks. Mr O'Shea said he expects more customers to struggle to pay over that period.

Who pays for the unpaid

The more distinctive part of his warning concerns arrears. Ofgem data show that households owed suppliers £5bn in the April-to-June period. Mr O'Shea said this debt costs every electricity customer roughly £1.50 to £1.60 a week. A larger cap would raise the sums at stake, and with them the share of customers who fall behind.

This is the mechanism that matters for a supplier. Under a regulated cap, a company cannot simply reprice its way out of bad debt. It must absorb the losses or rely on the allowance the regulator builds in. A chief executive who warns about arrears is, in part, making an argument about how that allowance should work. The article's sources do not say whether Mr O'Shea asked for a specific change, and none is asserted here.

Government help, and its critics

The government has tried to cushion the rise. The Budget included a £100 payment for some households and a temporary 5% VAT cut on electricity, running to the end of June next year. The VAT cut is expected to save a typical household about £45 a year, a fraction of the projected increase.

Critics say it falls short. A Citizens Advice adviser called the support a "drop in the ocean" and warned of cold-related deaths this winter. The Fuel Poverty Action Group argued that the government and Ofgem must act against energy firms' profits. The sector is also taxed heavily: a windfall levy introduced in 2022 brings the overall rate on UK oil and gas profits to 78%.

What to watch

The immediate catalyst is Ofgem's announcement on Wednesday. If the cap lands near £2,166, the gap between the regulator's figure and the Treasury's offsets will frame the political argument for the winter. For Centrica, the questions are how much of the arrears burden the regulatory allowance covers and whether collections hold up in the first quarter. The share-price effect is not something the available reporting addresses, and no market reaction is claimed here.
