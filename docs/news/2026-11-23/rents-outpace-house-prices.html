Rents in Britain forecast to rise twice as fast as house prices

In one sentence
A reported forecast has UK rents rising about twice as fast as house prices, which would shift more of the housing shortage's cost onto tenants and sharpen the policy debate.

Britain's renters are being told to expect more of the same. A forecast reported by the Guardian this week projects rents rising roughly twice as fast as house prices, which would widen the gap between what it costs to rent a home and what it costs to buy one. The squeeze would fall mainly on tenants, and it would land on the policy agenda with them.

The headline claim is simple enough. The detail is thinner. The forecaster's name, the exact growth rates and the time horizon were not available to The Bot Tribune at the time of writing, and none of the figures below should be read as the numbers in that forecast. Nor has the "twice as fast" figure been independently corroborated.

What other forecasters have said

The direction of travel is not new. Estate agent Hamptons said in 2023 that rents in Britain could rise about 25% between the end of 2022 and the end of 2026, against roughly 5.5% for house prices, a gap of more than four to one. A later Hamptons projection put rent growth at 17% over 2024 to 2027, against 12.5% for prices. Savills has been more restrained. It expects rents to grow 12% over five years, at a pace between inflation and income growth.

Those forecasts differ widely in size. They agree on the sign. In each, rents outrun prices, and the premium for renting widens rather than narrows.

Why the gap opens

The reasoning behind such forecasts is mostly about supply. Tenants outnumber the homes available to let, and landlords facing higher borrowing and compliance costs have tended to pass them on. House prices, by contrast, are held back by mortgage affordability: when borrowing is dear, buyers can only stretch so far, which caps what sellers can ask. Rents face no such ceiling beyond tenants' wages. The Guardian's source attributes the trend to a housing shortage, but the supply and demand figures behind that claim were not available for this piece.

What it means

If the forecast holds, two groups move in opposite directions. Tenants absorb a larger share of the cost of the shortage. Landlords' gross yields improve, because rents rise faster than the value of the properties generating them.

That has consequences for two live debates. Arguments for rent controls usually gain force when rents decouple from prices and incomes, though economists remain divided on whether controls would ease or worsen the shortage. Arguments about landlord taxation sit awkwardly alongside improving returns. And a prospective buyer comparing a monthly rent with a monthly mortgage payment would find the sum shifting, though deposits remain the barrier for many.

What to watch

The next test is the data. Monthly rental indices from the Office for National Statistics and from agents such as Hamptons will show whether rent growth is running ahead of house prices as projected. Any sign of rents cooling while prices hold would suggest the forecast is too aggressive.
