Longest-ever UN climate talks end with no deal, leaving COP31 divisions exposed

In one sentence
COP31 in Antalya closed without a cover decision after the EU and vulnerable states rejected a text silent on fossil fuels and short on adaptation finance, deferring the fight to 2027.

The United Nations climate conference in Antalya, Türkiye, closed on 22 November without agreeing a political cover decision, the first time in years that a COP has ended without one. Delegates from nearly 200 countries had spent two extra days beyond the scheduled 20 November finish. They left with a set of technical decisions and no shared statement on how fast to cut emissions or who should pay for adaptation. The Telegraph's syndicated report called it the longest climate summit ever. That is the least certain claim in the coverage, and this article has not been able to confirm it against the UNFCCC's own record. Other accounts describe only a two-day overrun, and recent summits at Baku and Belém also ran well past their deadlines.

The failure was one of process as much as substance. UN climate decisions need consensus, so a handful of objecting parties can stop a text that a large majority would accept. That is what happened.

The text that failed

The draft, called the Antalya Pact, reaffirmed the 1.5°C goal and called for deep, rapid and sustained emissions cuts. It did not mention coal, oil or gas by name, and it did not call for a transition away from fossil fuels. An early version had included language on "the immediate transition away from fossil fuels in energy systems". The Guardian reported that this was removed from the second draft after opposition from several oil-producing states. The text instead leaned on the 2023 COP28 consensus on fossil fuels, without restating it.

What the draft did contain was an energy-system target. It aimed to raise electricity's share of global final energy use to 35% by 2035, from about 23% now, and to scale carbon capture, utilisation and storage to gigatonne levels by the same year. Reporting from Down to Earth says the electrification goal was no longer explicitly linked to the earlier aim of tripling renewable capacity by 2030 and doubling the pace of efficiency gains, as a 19 November draft had been. At least 75 countries backed a voluntary "35-by-35" electrification pledge alongside the talks.

The objectors were a mixed group. The European Union, the United Kingdom, Norway, Panama, Vanuatu, Belize and the Marshall Islands all opposed the text. Ireland's climate minister, Darragh O'Brien, speaking for the EU, called it "unconscionable" because it made "no reference to fossil fuel". The EU also issued a joint declaration of "profound disappointment" and criticised the reliance on carbon capture. Vanuatu's climate minister, Ralph Regenvanu, said carbon capture had been given attention it did not deserve. Saudi Arabia defended its inclusion, arguing that climate change requires "all solutions".

China supported the text, and Ghana, despite reservations, said it would too. That matters for interpretation. The blockage did not run along a simple rich-versus-poor line. Climate-vulnerable island states and the EU argued that the text was too weak. Oil producers pressed for less fossil-fuel language, and several large emitters were prepared to accept what was on the table.

The money question

The second dividing line was adaptation finance. Developing countries estimate their climate finance needs at $5.1 trillion to $6.8 trillion through 2030, or $455 billion to $584 billion a year. They put adaptation needs alone at about $305 billion a year through 2035. An earlier draft had included a $120 billion goal and an aim to at least triple adaptation finance by 2035. Neither survived. The final text only agreed to "discuss" pathways to tripling, with technical work on baselines to follow.

The text did launch an Antalya–Addis Ababa Road Map on Adaptation and Resilience Finance, to be advanced by the COP31 and COP32 presidencies. But it set no new quantified target. Loss and damage received no distinct section and no fresh pledges, and civil society groups say it was folded into adaptation. Disputes over what developed countries owe under Article 9.1 of the Paris Agreement remain open. Panama's delegate said that "those who have most contributed to this crisis must shoulder a proportionate responsibility", a line that sums up why the finance track keeps stalling.

The practical consequence is that there is no agreed new baseline for either emissions ambition or money. Governments drafting their next national climate plans, and investors who price projects against them, are working from the existing commitments and nothing newer.

Reaction: failure of politics, not of Paris

UN Secretary-General António Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed" and that "there is no ambiguity in the science". COP31 President Murat Kurum, Türkiye's environment minister, said "We did our best" and acknowledged there was no agreement on the text. According to reports, no new draft will follow.

Assessments split on how serious the damage is. Ani Dasgupta, chief executive of the World Resources Institute, called it a failure of political leadership rather than of the Paris Agreement. Nakul Sharma of the think tank E3G described a split outcome rather than a diplomatic collapse, and noted that a weak cover decision could itself have dented Paris's credibility. Li Shuo of the Asia Society Policy Institute was more severe, calling it a major setback comparable to Copenhagen in 2009. Harjeet Singh of the Fill the Fund campaign took a view that cuts against the usual instinct to rescue a text: "No deal is better than a deal that locks in failure."

The parallel with Copenhagen has limits. Delegates did adopt a number of the roughly 160 agenda items. These included a Just Transition Work Programme, decisions on the Adaptation Fund, carbon markets and transparency, a reiterated goal of universal early-warning coverage by 2027, and a two-year work programme on mountains. The machinery of the process is working. The political layer on top of it is not.

What happens now

With no cover decision, the route forward runs through meetings that already exist rather than any new mandate. The next formal negotiating session is the Bonn climate conference in June 2027. COP32 follows in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from 8 to 19 November 2027, where the unresolved questions are due back: the fossil-fuel transition, adaptation finance, loss and damage, equity and implementation. The adaptation finance road map, the structure of the Belém–Antalya just transition mechanism and a high-level mountains dialogue are also slated for that meeting.

The text also calls for dialogues in November 2027 and November 2028 on making the consensus process more inclusive and predictable, with the UN climate secretariat to prepare options. That is a quiet admission that the rules themselves are part of the problem.

The test over the next twelve months is whether the finance and fossil-fuel disputes can be settled in Bonn and in the national plans governments publish in between, or whether they simply return to Addis Ababa in the same form. If the Antalya impasse is a guide, the countries that wanted stronger language and those that wanted weaker language will arrive with the same positions. The presidencies will then have one summit in which to build a text that none of them has yet found.
