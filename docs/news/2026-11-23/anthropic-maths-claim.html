Anthropic researcher says he may have cracked a major maths problem

In one sentence
Anthropic's Levent Alpöge says he and Claude have proved the Riemann Hypothesis, but the claim is unconfirmed and the problem is still officially open.

An Anthropic mathematician says he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics. No expert has yet confirmed the result, and until one does, the problem officially remains open.

Levent Alpöge, a member of Anthropic's technical staff, published a four-page statement on his website. "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis," he wrote. He also says he has settled a broader version of the problem. He credits the AI model with much of the work, saying it "far outpaced me near the finish line" and at times went beyond his own understanding. Anadolu Agency reported the announcement on 21 November. One report says he reached the result on a Sunday and waited to announce it after speaking with Anthropic's chief executive, Dario Amodei, and other mathematicians.

What the problem is

The hypothesis dates from 1859. It concerns the "zeros" of a function that encodes how prime numbers are spread along the number line. Bernhard Riemann conjectured that all the interesting zeros sit on a single line. Billions have been checked by computer, and none has been found off it. A proof has still eluded everyone. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists it as one of seven Millennium Prize Problems, each with a $1m prize.

What has been checked, and what hasn't

Alpöge released his argument in Lean, a language in which a computer verifies each logical step. That matters, because it makes the claim easier to test than a long paper that only a handful of people can read. Andrew Sutherland of MIT reports that the submitted code has passed several independent computer checks, with more still under way.

Those checks are preliminary, and they do not settle the question. A Lean file proves only what it says it proves. Experts will want to confirm that the statement formalised in it is the Riemann Hypothesis itself, not a subtly weaker one. Accounts say the claim has not yet gained broad acceptance among mathematicians.

The timing has also drawn comment. One outlet noted that the announcement came shortly after Anthropic's IPO, which has raised questions about when it was published. That is an observation about timing, not evidence against the proof.

Why the checking matters

AI labs have had public embarrassments with maths claims before. An OpenAI researcher was reported earlier to have deleted a post claiming a major breakthrough after it was challenged. Anthropic's own earlier work, reported from August, was more modest: it produced a partial result about zeros on the critical line, not a proof of the full hypothesis.

This claim differs in one way. The machine-checkable proof gives sceptics something concrete to examine, and it should shorten the wait for an answer. If it holds, it would be a landmark for mathematics and for the use of AI in research. If it fails, it will be another case of an AI-lab announcement outrunning its verification.

The next thing to watch is the outcome of the remaining Lean checks. After that comes whether specialists in analytic number theory confirm that the formal statement matches the real conjecture. Only then does the claim become a result.
