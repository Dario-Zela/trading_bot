Can AI spot Black Friday deals? The BBC tests it

In one sentence
BBC Technology has tested whether AI shopping tools can find genuine Black Friday bargains, a question that turns on price history, live data and who profits from recommendations.

The pitch is simple: ask a chatbot to find the cheapest price, and let it wade through the discount noise of Black Friday for you. BBC Technology has put that pitch to the test, asking whether AI shopping tools can pick out genuine bargains from the marketing, just ahead of the UK's biggest retail weekend. Black Friday falls on 27 November, with Cyber Monday following on 30 November.

This piece rests on the BBC's framing alone. The test results, the products tried and any figures are not reproduced here, and the BBC's own article is the place to look for them.

The problem the tools are meant to solve

Black Friday has long had a credibility problem. Consumer group Which? has in past years compared sale prices with prices at other times of year and found that many headline "deals" were no cheaper than usual. A "was £200, now £120" tag tells a shopper little if the product spent most of the autumn at £130.

That is the gap AI tools claim to fill. A human can check a price history on a tracking site, but not for fifty items across a dozen retailers in an evening. A tool that can read listings, compare sellers and flag a fake discount would be useful. Whether it can do that reliably is a separate question from whether it can sound confident while trying.

Why the test is harder than it looks

Three weaknesses are worth keeping in mind when judging any such test.

  • Freshness. Prices change by the hour in a sale. A language model working from a snapshot, or from a retailer page cached earlier, can quote a price that no longer exists.
  • Verification. A real bargain needs a baseline: what the item cost last month, and what the same model costs elsewhere. Without access to price-history data, an assistant is repeating the retailer's own claim.
  • Incentives. Shopping features sit inside products run by companies with commercial relationships, including affiliate arrangements and sponsored listings. Whether a recommendation is ranked on price or on commission is rarely obvious to the user.

None of this means the tools are useless. Summarising reviews, matching specifications and narrowing a long list are tasks they can do well. The harder claim is that they can tell a good price from a merely advertised one.

What to watch

The verdict will come from how the tools fare against checkable prices once the weekend's offers are live. The sharper test is whether anyone compares what the assistants recommended with what the same items cost in the weeks before and after. Post-sale price checks from consumer groups, typically published in the days after Cyber Monday, will show how many of this year's "deals" held up, and whether an AI-assisted shopper did any better than one scrolling by hand.
