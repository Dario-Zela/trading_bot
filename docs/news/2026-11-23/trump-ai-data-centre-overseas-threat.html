Trump threatens to bar US firms from building AI data centres overseas

In one sentence
Trump has reportedly threatened to bar US firms from building AI data centres abroad, but no wording or legal instrument is confirmed, so it is a threat, not policy.

President Donald Trump has threatened to bar American companies from building AI data centres overseas, according to a report from BBC Technology. The report is the basis for this article. The exact wording of the threat has not been confirmed, and no executive order, bill or effective date has been identified. Searches by this desk found no formal instrument. For now it is a threat, not a measure.

If it became policy, it would change what a data centre is in trade terms. At the moment, where a company puts its computing capacity is mostly a question of power prices, land, planning rules and latency. A ban would make the location a matter of US trade and security policy.

What is known, and what is not

The confirmed part is thin. The BBC reports a threat from the president. Beyond that, the specifics are missing: whether it would cover all foreign sites or only some countries, whether it would apply to new builds or to expansions, and whether it would work through law, licensing or pressure on individual firms.

The threat does sit alongside other recent statements. Trump has said communities that reject data centres will end up poorer and that the US risks losing the AI race to China. In a related warning, reported by SCMP, he said that if voters turned down data centres, companies would be "forced to go overseas". That is the opposite of a ban. It treats offshore building as a fallback that domestic opposition is pushing firms towards. A threat to forbid it would be a sharp turn, or a lever aimed at making domestic projects easier to approve.

Washington already has tools in this area. The Commerce Department's chip export rules require licences for advanced processors going to overseas data centres that serve significant capacity to Chinese firms. Those rules govern where chips can go. A ban on US firms building abroad would instead govern where the firms themselves can invest.

Who would be exposed

The largest US cloud providers are the obvious targets. They have spent heavily on capacity outside the US, including in Europe and the Gulf, and are expanding in Asia and Latin America. Their reasons are practical. Customers in regulated sectors want data held in-country. Grid connections in some US regions are slow to obtain. Local politics around data centres is hardening in several American states, with New York among those that have moved to restrict large projects.

Host countries would be affected too. The UK and the EU have courted US-built capacity as a route to AI growth and to the compute their own firms need. The UK government has promoted AI growth zones. A US restriction would force a choice. Hosts could treat American firms as partners and accept that their capacity depends on Washington's goodwill. They could instead hedge, backing domestic or non-US operators, and then face the cost of building without US capital and chips.

What to watch

The first test is whether the White House puts the threat into a document: an executive order, a Commerce rule or a bill. Without one, the threat's practical effect may be to unsettle investment plans without changing them. The second is the reaction of the big cloud providers, whose capital spending guidance will show whether anyone is rerouting projects. The third is the response in London and Brussels, where officials who have been signing hosting deals will want to know how seriously to take it.

This piece will be updated when the wording is confirmed. Until then, the sensible reading is that it is a signal about priorities rather than a rule.
