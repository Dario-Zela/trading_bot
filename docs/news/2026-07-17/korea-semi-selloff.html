Korean chip stocks slide even as Taiwan Semi posts solid earnings

A TSMC fabrication plant in Hsinchu, Taiwan — the company posted a beat-and-raise quarter even as regional chip stocks sold off. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
TSMC beat estimates on every metric, but a Bank of Korea rate hike and a Kospi circuit-breaker day dragged Samsung and SK Hynix down anyway.

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co posted the kind of quarter chipmakers dream about Wednesday — earnings and revenue both above Wall Street's estimates, and a forecast for the current quarter that beat consensus too. The stock fell anyway. So did nearly every other chip name in Asia. The reason has less to do with Taiwan than with Seoul.

South Korea's central bank raised its benchmark interest rate a quarter point to 2.75% on Thursday, its first hike in three and a half years, after inflation ran at 3.2% in June — the hottest reading since 2023. The Bank of Korea didn't stop at one move: it signaled more increases are coming as it tries to rein in prices and prop up a weak won. Investors read that as the end of easy money for a market that had been riding a semiconductor boom to record highs, and they sold. The Kospi dropped 6.37% to 6,820.60, breaking below the 7,000 level and tripping a circuit breaker. Samsung Electronics fell 8.77%. SK Hynix, the world's dominant supplier of the high-bandwidth memory chips that feed AI data centers, dropped 11.53%. SK Square lost 12.3%; Samsung Electro-Mechanics fell 9.62%.

The numbers TSMC actually posted

Strip away the sentiment and TSMC's quarter was unambiguous. The company earned $4.31 per ADR versus a $3.81 consensus, on revenue of $40.2 billion against $39.83 billion expected — a 33.7% jump from a year earlier. Gross margin hit 67.7%, and high-performance computing revenue, the bucket that includes AI accelerator chips, grew 20% from the prior quarter alone. Management didn't hedge on the outlook either: third-quarter revenue guidance of $44.6 billion to $45.8 billion cleared the $43.67 billion analysts were modeling, and TSMC lifted its full-year growth forecast from "more than 30%" to "slightly above 40%," citing AI accelerator demand and the chip-hungry compute needs of agentic AI workloads.

None of that stopped TSM shares from sliding toward technical support in early trading. When a sector-wide repricing is underway, even the company doing everything right gets pulled down with it.

A crowded trade meets a rate shock

Part of the selloff is simple positioning math: semiconductors now account for roughly a fifth of the S&P 500's market cap, a concentration that makes any wobble in the group ripple far beyond it. Adding a Korean rate hike into that mix — raising the cost of the leverage that's financed some of the sector's run — gave momentum traders a reason to take profits, particularly in Samsung and SK Hynix after their own AI-driven rallies. The demand story underneath the two, notably for HBM memory bound for cloud AI infrastructure, hasn't broken; supply for those chips still trails demand, which is exactly why the selloff reads as a repricing of risk rather than a verdict on fundamentals.

A separate thread complicated the picture further this week. Apple won Chinese regulatory approval on July 15 to bring Apple Intelligence to iPhones sold in China, using Alibaba's Qwen model and Baidu's AI technology rather than its own. Shares of both Chinese firms jumped on the news. For chip investors it's a reminder that AI capital isn't only chasing Nvidia's and TSMC's customers — it's also flowing toward the software and model layer in China, a market that had been mostly shut out of the current AI trade.

The next test lands with Samsung's and SK Hynix's own guidance updates, and with whatever the Bank of Korea signals at its next meeting about the pace of further hikes. Until then, the gap between what TSMC's earnings say about chip demand and what Korean equities say about chip risk is likely to stay wide.
