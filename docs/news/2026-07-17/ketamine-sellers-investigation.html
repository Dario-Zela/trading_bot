Federal officials probe online ketamine sellers over at-home use

Ketamine, a Schedule III controlled substance, in vials seized by the Drug Enforcement Administration. — US Drug Enforcement Administration / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Federal officials are probing online ketamine sellers over at-home use, days after the FDA sent 14 warning letters to unapproved retailers.

Federal regulators have opened an investigation into online retailers selling ketamine directly to consumers for use at home, according to a Wall Street Journal exclusive, part of a widening effort to rein in a drug-delivery model that grew up largely outside the traditional clinic setting.

Ketamine has lived two lives for decades: a workhorse anesthetic stocked in hospital operating rooms, and, under the street name "Special K," a party drug with a long record of abuse. What's new is the middle ground it has occupied since the pandemic — a fast-growing telehealth industry that ships vials, lozenges and nasal sprays to people's homes as an unsupervised treatment for depression and anxiety, no clinic visit required.

That business exists because of a COVID-era waiver. In 2021 the Drug Enforcement Administration relaxed its usual requirement that prescribers examine patients in person before dispensing controlled substances, a flexibility regulators have repeatedly extended and never fully unwound. It let a wave of startups build subscription-style ketamine programs on the promise of at-home relief for treatment-resistant depression — a market the Food and Drug Administration has watched swell with unapproved products it says it never signed off on.

A pattern of warning letters

The federal probe follows a more concrete enforcement action: on July 7, the FDA's Center for Drug Evaluation and Research sent 14 warning letters to online sellers of ketamine products, ordering nearly all of them — a dozen based in the US, one in the Netherlands — to stop marketing unapproved and misbranded drugs to American consumers. Recipients included firms trading under names like LyfeUnit, Ketamine Store and All Ketamine HCL, several pitching injectable, pharmaceutical-grade product for shipment straight to a buyer's door.[^1]

The agency's stated concern is less about ketamine's psychiatric risks than its physical ones: unregulated, potentially contaminated injectable doses, the FDA warned, can cause septicemia or sepsis in patients dosing themselves without clinical supervision.[^2] Only one ketamine-based product carries full FDA approval for psychiatric use — Johnson & Johnson's Spravato, an esketamine nasal spray cleared for treatment-resistant depression that is administered under observation, not shipped home.[^3]

Why this is a policy story, not a market one

The companies under scrutiny are mostly private, venture-backed telehealth startups rather than publicly traded pharmaceutical firms, and the enforcement pattern so far — warning letters, not injunctions or criminal charges — has not touched Spravato's maker or any listed drugmaker's earnings. For healthcare-policy watchers, the episode is a signal that the DEA's pandemic-era telemedicine leniency is drawing renewed federal attention six years after it was first granted. For markets, it registers as background noise: a single-digit-billion-dollar niche industry, not a sector with listed exposure large enough to move index-level healthcare names.

What happens next depends on whether the DEA follows the FDA's lead. The agency has a pending rule that would tighten telemedicine prescribing of controlled substances, a proposal still unfinished as of this month; its release would be the next concrete marker of how far this scrutiny extends beyond warning letters.
