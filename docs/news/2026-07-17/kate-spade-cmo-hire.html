Tapestry hires L'Oréal veteran as Kate Spade's new CMO

A Kate Spade New York handbag — the brand is trying to shed its reputation for deep discounting. — Make Lemons from Cheverly / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Tapestry hires L'Oréal marketing veteran Allison Badea as Kate Spade CMO, betting a brand-first turnaround can end 14 quarters of sales declines.

Fourteen straight quarters of sales declines is the kind of streak that ends careers, and at Kate Spade New York it has. Tapestry, the handbag conglomerate that also owns Coach, has hired Allison Badea — most recently global senior vice president of brand experience at L'Oréal's Maybelline New York — as the struggling brand's new chief marketing officer. She replaces Kaisy Mae O'Reilly, who had held the job on an interim basis since early 2025.

On its own, a CMO appointment is a personnel note. This one reads as a strategy memo. Badea spent her career building prestige and mass-beauty brands inside L'Oréal's portfolio — stints at Kiehl's, Lancôme and Biotherm, plus a run as general manager of Giorgio Armani Beauty U.S. — rather than climbing up through fashion retail's discount-and-clear machinery. Tapestry did not hire a merchandising operator to plug a sales hole. It hired a brand builder.

That distinction matters because of how Kate Spade got into this hole in the first place. Quarterly sales fell roughly 10% to $219.6 million in Tapestry's most recent reporting period, down from $244.9 million a year earlier, even as the brand added 400,000 new customers — a sign that people are walking in, buying on markdown, and leaving without the brand registering. Unaided brand awareness, the metric that shows whether a name has cultural weight rather than just a coupon code, has not moved. Last August, Tapestry took an $855 million write-down tied to Kate Spade, an accounting acknowledgment that the brand was worth less than the company had been carrying it at.

Borrowing Coach's playbook

The fix Tapestry has settled on is essentially the one that already worked next door. Coach, once similarly diluted by mall discounting, spent years pulling back wholesale distribution, cutting online flash sales and pushing prices up rather than down — a bet that scarcity sells better than clearance racks. It paid off: Coach's own quarterly sales jumped 31% to $1.7 billion, with 2.4 million new customers and, notably, real traction with Gen Z shoppers who are supposed to be allergic to legacy handbag brands.

Retail analyst Neil Saunders of GlobalData put the underlying diagnosis bluntly: Kate Spade had become "too value-oriented and overly reliant on excessive, and margin-depleting, promotions." Tapestry's plan borrows Coach's medicine — fewer flash sales, less wholesale ubiquity, higher price points — paired with a creative reset under designer Nicola Glass and a marketing push aimed squarely at younger shoppers, including more shoppable social content. Tapestry's own language for it, in a statement announcing Badea's hire, was tidier: "We're reigniting the magic of Kate Spade New York."

CEO Joanne Crevoiserat has been careful not to promise a quick turn. "What gives us confidence is that where we focus and invest, we see signs of progress," she said, adding that turnarounds take time and rarely move in a straight line. Badea's early test will be simple to state and hard to execute: can a brand known for accessible, promotion-driven fashion convince shoppers to pay more for less of it, without losing the customers who came for the discount in the first place. The next data point is Tapestry's fiscal fourth-quarter and full-year results, due in August, when analysts will be watching Kate Spade's revenue and margin lines for the first signs of whether the Coach treatment is taking.
