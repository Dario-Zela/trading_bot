US Navy disables non-compliant tanker as Iran strikes enter fifth night

A U.S. Navy visit, board, search and seizure team — the kind of unit now enforcing Washington's blockade of Iranian ports. — Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class Scott Pittman / U.S. Navy (public domain)
In one sentence
US forces boarded a tanker and disabled another as Iran strikes hit bridges near Bandar Abbas for a fifth straight night, with Brent near one-month highs.

Just after 2 p.m. Eastern on Thursday, American warplanes began a fifth consecutive night of strikes on Iran. By the time the last aircraft turned for home near dawn Friday, CENTCOM said it had hit targets spread from the southwest coast to the Pakistani border. A few hours earlier and a few hundred miles to the south, Marines from the 11th Marine Expeditionary Unit had fast-roped onto the deck of a crude tanker in the Gulf of Oman — the clearest signal yet that the United States is no longer content to police the Strait of Hormuz from a distance.

The two threads are the same story. Washington's air campaign against Iran, now well into its fifth month since strikes began on February 28, has entered a phase focused less on symbolic deterrence and more on physically controlling the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil passes.

Bridges down at Bandar Khamir

The night's strikes reached further inland than usual. Iran's semi-official Fars news agency reported that a bridge in Bandar Khamir, a coastal district of Hormozgan province, was hit; other Iranian outlets, including Tasnim, said as many as three bridges were struck in the same area, among them the main highway link between the port city of Bandar Abbas and Lar in neighbouring Fars province. Iranian media said the strikes landed while civilian traffic was crossing, with vehicles thrown from the roadway; Fars put the toll at seven dead and nine wounded, figures that have not been independently verified. Power was knocked out across parts of the surrounding Kahurestan area and mobile networks went down.

The targeting pattern is not incidental. Bandar Abbas is Iran's principal port and the closest major population and logistics center to the strait itself. Cutting the bridges, together with reported strikes on an airport, a railway line and a communications tower in the same stretch of coast, reads as an effort to sever the port from Iran's interior — road and rail arteries rather than military hardware. CENTCOM has not detailed individual targets beyond saying the goal is to "further degrade Iranian military capabilities," and has offered no damage assessment of its own.

The blockade tightens

The more novel development sits offshore. Trump reinstated the naval blockade of Iranian ports on July 14 after a mid-summer lull, and CENTCOM has been posting a running tally of enforcement since. As of Thursday, the command said it had redirected three commercial vessels attempting to reach Iranian ports, disabled one that refused to comply, and boarded a fifth to confirm it was operating within the rules. The disabled vessel was the Belma, an empty Curaçao-flagged tanker that U.S. forces struck with Hellfire missiles on its smokestack after it pressed on toward Kharg Island — Iran's main crude export terminal — despite warnings.

The boarding was more theatrical: CENTCOM released footage of armed Marines from the Camp Pendleton-based 11th MEU, deployed aboard the amphibious assault ship USS Boxer, fast-roping from helicopters onto the deck of the M/T Wen Yao, a previously sanctioned crude carrier, to conduct what the command called a "verification boarding." In its own words, CENTCOM described the strait as "free and open," with the caveat that applies to every vessel except those trying to breach what it called America's "steel wall" around Iranian ports. That framing — open to the world, closed to Iran — is a narrower blockade than the version Trump floated a week earlier, when he briefly threatened a 20% fee on all cargo transiting the strait before walking it back under pressure from shippers and Gulf allies.

What it's doing to the water

The numbers underneath the enforcement statistics are the real story for anyone hedging energy exposure. Traffic through the strait has thinned sharply: roughly 18 to 22 vessels a day before the current round of fighting resumed, versus as few as six in a twelve-hour window earlier this month. Shipping brokers estimate close to 230 loaded tankers are effectively stranded inside the Gulf with no port willing to receive them. War-risk insurance premiums, which cover vessels operating in the zone, have climbed to four or five times pre-war levels since fighting first broke out in February — a cost that compounds every time a tanker owner has to reroute or wait out a boarding.

Brent crude has responded accordingly, trading near $85 a barrel this week, close to a one-month high and reversing roughly a third of the price decline that followed the interim peace deal reached after the February war's opening phase, when an Israeli strike killed Iran's supreme leader and touched off the broader conflict. Analysts tracking the campaign have floated $100 a barrel as the next threshold if the current intensity — nightly strikes plus active interdiction — holds for several more weeks. None of that requires the strait to actually close; it only requires insurers, shipowners and traders to keep pricing in the chance that it might.

What comes next

The blockade's own history is a caution against assuming this is the peak. During its first run this spring, before a short-lived truce, CENTCOM said it redirected more than 140 vessels and disabled nine over roughly two months — figures far above where the current, five-day-old restart stands. If enforcement scales the way it did then, boardings like the Wen Yao's are likely to become routine rather than newsworthy. The nearer-term variables to watch are whether Thursday's bridge strikes near Bandar Abbas mark a shift toward hitting logistics infrastructure rather than military sites, and whether Tehran responds to the port city's isolation with retaliation at sea, where its Revolutionary Guard has previously laid mines and harassed shipping. The next CENTCOM enforcement update and the next OPEC-adjacent commentary on Brent's trajectory are the two data points most likely to move first.
