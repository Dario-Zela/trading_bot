Boeing nears FAA approval that could unlock delayed 737 Max deliveries

A Southwest Airlines 737 MAX; Southwest holds about 90% of all MAX 7 orders and has waited years for deliveries to begin. — Tomás Del Coro / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
FAA certification of Boeing's 737 MAX 7 looks days to weeks away, positioning roughly 30 finished jets for delivery after years of delay.

Boeing is within weeks — possibly days — of clearing the last regulatory hurdle standing between it and a fleet of finished, unsold jets parked on the tarmac in Moses Lake, Washington.

The Federal Aviation Administration is nearing certification of the 737 MAX 7, the smallest and longest-delayed variant of Boeing's best-selling family, according to reporting from the Wall Street Journal and multiple aviation trade outlets this week. Boeing's own program manager for 737 derivatives has said the company is "95% complete with all our certification deliverables" on the MAX 7. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford has said he has identified no issues that would block sign-off before the end of the year, and industry trackers now put MAX 7 approval as likely by the end of July — thirteen years after Southwest Airlines launched the program.

In one sentence: The FAA is close to certifying Boeing's 737 MAX 7, which could free roughly 30 finished jets sitting idle and unlock deliveries stalled since 2021.

What's actually being signed off

The holdup was never about volume production — it was a single component. In 2021, Boeing engineers found that the engine anti-ice system on the MAX 7 and MAX 10, if left running too long by pilots after icing conditions passed, could overheat the composite nacelle inlet enough to crack it and shed debris in flight. Regulators let the already-certified MAX 8, 8-200 and 9 keep flying, but held the line on the two newer variants until Boeing redesigned the part. That redesign work — which Boeing says also cuts noise and vibration — has now moved through flight testing: the MAX 10 has completed roughly 98% of its certification test program and entered the FAA's final inspection phase.

Separately, the FAA is weighing whether to restore Boeing's authority to issue its own airworthiness certificates on new 737s — delegated sign-off power it stripped after the two fatal MAX crashes in 2018 and 2019. Getting that authority back would speed up every delivery going forward, not just the MAX 7 and MAX 10.

The backlog waiting to move

About 30 finished MAX 7 airframes are already sitting at Moses Lake, built and waiting for a signature. Southwest holds roughly 90% of all MAX 7 orders — 289 aircraft — and has used the wait to keep older 737-700s in service longer than planned. Around nine completed MAX 10s are similarly parked; that variant alone makes up more than a quarter of Boeing's remaining 737 MAX backlog, with United (277 jets), Alaska Airlines (105) and others on the order book. Combined MAX 7 and MAX 10 orders top 1,700 aircraft.

Even with certification, deliveries won't be instant. Southwest's own 2025 annual report projected zero MAX 7 deliveries this year, pushing first handoffs into early 2027 as the aircraft moves through production ramp-up and customer acceptance. The MAX 10 is expected to trail further behind, with Bedford pointing to certification before year-end rather than a specific delivery date.

A muted market response

Boeing shares have not moved much on the certification news itself — up roughly half a percent after hours when the story first broke, then essentially flat to lower the following session, and down over 3% on July 13 amid separate reports of supply-chain strain on the MAX production ramp. The tepid reaction reflects a stock that has spent years pricing in certification as eventual rather than urgent; the harder question for investors is less "will the FAA sign off" and more whether Boeing's supply chain can turn a certification into a delivery cadence.

The next concrete marker is the MAX 7 certification decision itself, expected by month's end. After that, attention shifts to the MAX 10, which Bedford has pegged for approval before the close of 2026, and to Boeing's next earnings call for an update on how quickly the Moses Lake backlog actually starts moving to customers.
