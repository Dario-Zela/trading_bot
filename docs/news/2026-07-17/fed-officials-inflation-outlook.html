Fed officials split on inflation risk as AI capex and tariffs cloud outlook

In one sentence
Dallas Fed's Logan called for a rate hike while saying wages aren't yet inflationary; Kansas City's Schmid says don't assume any of this is temporary.

Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan spent Thursday doing something unusual for a central banker: making the dovish case and the hawkish case in the same breath. Wages, she told an audience in Houston, are not currently feeding inflation. But she is worried they could start. And by the end of her remarks, she had become the first Fed official to publicly call for another interest-rate increase since Kevin Warsh took over as chair in May.

That combination — currently-benign, structurally-worried — captures where the Fed finds itself two weeks before its July 28-29 meeting. Logan's Kansas City counterpart, Jeff Schmid, spent the same day arguing the opposite framing is dangerous: don't assume anything about this inflation is temporary. Between them, traders got a clearer picture of the Fed's internal debate and no clearer picture of what it will do next.

Wages now, AI later

Logan's headline line was reassurance: current wage growth, in her assessment, is not providing inflationary pressure relative to productivity. But she qualified it immediately, noting that some Texas businesses have told her they are raising pay faster than expected, and calling a wage-price spiral an "early days" risk rather than a live one.

Where she was more definitive was on artificial intelligence. Investment demand tied to AI, she said, is "big, real" and carries near-term inflationary effects — even as she remains "very optimistic" about the technology's long-run productivity payoff. She extended the point to the physical infrastructure behind it: electricity prices driven by data-center demand will likely be, in her words, "another source of modest inflationary pressure," a conclusion she said holds even under the most conservative estimates. US data-center electricity consumption is projected to more than double by 2030, and Logan's remarks put a Fed governor's name behind treating that buildout as a genuine price-pressure channel, not a rounding error.

On energy more broadly, Logan pointed to a supply-side wrinkle that complicates the usual story about high prices calling forth new output: gas takeaway capacity and other midstream infrastructure constraints are limiting how much producers can actually ramp up even with oil prices elevated, she said, and recent gains in US oil exports have come mostly from drawing down inventories rather than new production. With fighting in the Middle East now in its fifth night and the US Navy moving to disable a non-compliant tanker near the Strait of Hormuz, that supply bottleneck is not an abstraction — it's a mechanism by which a geopolitical shock could show up in pump prices faster than domestic producers can respond.

The rate call

All of that fed into the sharper news of the day: Logan, a voting FOMC member this year, said "modestly" higher interest rates would better balance the outlook and risks, arguing inflation "has been too high, for too long" and that labor, spending and financial data don't show current policy meaningfully restraining the economy. Her framing was explicitly asymmetric — "better modest restriction now than severe restriction later" — the same logic she has used since calling for a rate increase back in early June. Consumer prices were running 3.5% above a year earlier as of the latest reading, with wholesale costs up 5.5%; Logan has said her best guess is that inflation is heading toward the "mid-2s," not all the way back to the Fed's 2% target on its own.

Schmid, speaking at a Kansas City Fed forum in Nebraska, arrived at a similar policy conclusion from a different angle. He called the labor market "roughly stable" — in balance, with growth showing resilience — but said inflation is "proving persistent across a broad selection of goods and services" and is "concerning." More pointedly, he rejected the idea that the Fed should "look through" particular categories of price increases, the framing that treated 2021's inflation as transitory: "I am uncomfortable ever assuming that a burst of inflation is likely to be temporary," he said, adding that inflation shocks are "not intrinsically transitory." He also tied the Fed's credibility to process, arguing that accountability requires the institution to explain how it makes decisions and to tolerate open criticism of them.

Balance sheet, and a nod to continuity

Logan used part of her remarks to reframe a longer-running debate over the Fed's balance sheet, arguing the goal should be an efficient and effective balance sheet rather than a target for its size — and floating, without specifics, that a more optimal regulatory liquidity regime for banks could eventually allow the balance sheet to shrink further. Neither point came with a timeline or proposal attached; both read as an invitation to a future debate rather than a policy shift underway now.

She closed on institutional footing, saying Fed meetings and the data-driven structure of policymaking "have not changed a bit" under Warsh, and that the new chair — who took the gavel from Jerome Powell in May — shares "tremendous dedication to the institution" that Powell showed. It was a notable thing to say out loud, at a moment when Warsh has already announced task forces to overhaul major Fed operations: Logan was vouching for continuity precisely because continuity is the question markets are asking about a new chair. She also flagged a more local concern — that immigration restrictions are squeezing the Texas labor force, even as the resulting slower population growth has made otherwise-modest payroll gains look consistent with a balanced labor market rather than a weakening one.

What it adds up to

Strip away the specific topics and the throughline is this: two regional Fed presidents, one dovish-sounding on current conditions and one hawkish-sounding on principle, reached the same place — modestly tighter policy is warranted, and none of the AI investment boom, data-center power demand or oil-supply constraints currently in view point toward inflation cooling on its own. That both arrived there from different starting premises is precisely what leaves traders without a clean signal: it looks less like a Fed converging on a model of the economy and more like several officials independently concluding that the risks skew the same direction for different reasons. Logan is a voter this year; Schmid is not. The next hard data point is the July 28-29 FOMC meeting, where the question is no longer whether officials are worried about inflation stickiness, but whether Logan's rate-hike call — the first under Warsh — finds company at the table.
