Japan's JERA studies US listing as it expands overseas

JERA's Hekinan thermal power station in Aichi, Japan — the country's largest coal-fired plant and part of the fleet inherited from TEPCO and Chubu Electric. — Jihara19 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
JERA, Japan's largest power utility, is studying a US stock listing as it pours billions into American shale gas, LNG and data-center power deals.

JERA, the Japanese utility that generates roughly three in ten kilowatt-hours consumed in the country, has begun studying a listing on a US stock exchange, according to people familiar with the matter cited in a Reuters exclusive. No decision has been made on timing, venue or size, the sources said — but the study itself marks a shift for a company that has spent a decade absorbing the fallout of Fukushima rather than courting Wall Street.

JERA was created in 2015 as a 50-50 merger of the fuel and thermal-power units of Tokyo Electric Power Company and Chubu Electric Power, a consolidation forced partly by the need to rebuild TEPCO's balance sheet after the 2011 nuclear disaster. The resulting company inherited nearly 40% of Japan's thermal generation capacity and became one of the world's largest buyers and traders of liquefied natural gas, moving around 35 million tonnes a year.

A widening American footprint

A US listing would formalize a pivot that has been building in plain sight. JERA agreed in September to pay roughly $1.7 billion for Haynesville shale gas assets from a Blackstone-and-Williams-backed venture — its first move into US gas production — and separately signed a letter of intent for supply from Alaska's $44 billion LNG export project. In June, Nikkei reported the company would spend about ¥500 billion ($3 billion) building a gas-fired power plant to feed a US data center directly, sidestepping grid bottlenecks tied to America's AI-driven power crunch. Offshore, JERA folded its wind business into JERA Nex bp, a London-headquartered joint venture with BP formed last August with a 13-gigawatt project pipeline spanning the US, Europe and Asia-Pacific.

The capital question

The listing study also answers a question JERA has been dodging since April 2024, when it first told Bloomberg it might tap outside investors — "trillions of yen" would be needed, it said then — to fund a 2035 strategy built around ¥5 trillion in investment, a sixfold expansion of renewables capacity to 20 gigawatts, and targets of ¥350 billion in net profit and ¥700 billion in EBITDA. A US listing would let JERA raise dollar-denominated capital closer to where much of that spending — shale gas, LNG terminals, gas-fired plants for data centers — is now happening, rather than relying solely on Tokyo markets or its two utility parents.

Whether JERA follows through will say something about how far Japan's post-Fukushima utility consolidation can travel. A company assembled to stabilize two regional monopolies is now weighing whether its next chapter gets financed in New York. Watch for confirmation of an underwriter mandate or bourse choice — the next concrete marker in a process JERA's sources say remains at an early, exploratory stage.
