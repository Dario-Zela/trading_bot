US tightens export rules on data centres serving Chinese firms, and Trump threatens curbs on US builders abroad

In one sentence
A new Commerce rule requires licences for advanced chips going to overseas data centres serving Chinese customers, while a BBC-reported Trump threat targets US firms' own overseas AI builds.

Washington has moved to shut a route that let Chinese companies use America's most advanced AI chips without buying them. The route was renting: a Chinese firm that cannot import top-end Nvidia hardware can instead rent time on servers in a data centre abroad. A new Commerce Department rule is meant to end that, and a separate presidential threat suggests the controls could eventually reach American companies' own overseas building plans.

The loophole

US export controls have long focused on the product. The most advanced AI chips cannot be sold to China, so the box itself is restricted. But the compute those chips produce is not a box. A data centre in Southeast Asia can legally hold the hardware and sell access to anyone, including customers in China. Critics have said for some time that this lets China use processing power built on US technology while the ban stays formally intact.

The Bureau of Industry and Security, part of Commerce, issued an interim final rule titled "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" on 16 November, according to law-firm summaries. The Boston Globe reported it as taking effect immediately. Latham & Watkins and Greenberg Traurig put the effective date at 19 November, so the exact start date is worth checking against the Federal Register text.

The stated aim, per the Globe, is to stop existing restrictions being "circumvented through remote computing arrangements." The rule targets where the compute sits and who it serves, not only who buys the chips. Law-firm summaries say a licence is now required to send advanced chips to data centres, anywhere in the world, that supply large amounts of compute to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other countries in the same restricted group. Pillsbury puts the threshold at more than 12 million aggregate "total processing performance", the metric the controls use to measure chip capability. Existing arrangements reportedly get 90 days to apply for licences, and applications will be judged case by case.

The rule also adds geography. Chip shipments to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now need licences too, because Commerce says these countries "present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion." Overseas data centres serving customers in China, Russia, Iran and Cuba must identify their end users. Shipments to US-headquartered companies are excluded, subject to reporting requirements. Law-firm summaries also say the rule rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule, the previous attempt at a global framework for AI chip exports.

A diversion case in the background

The Globe ties the timing to a specific episode. It reports that Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based firm, is suspected of diverting Nvidia chips to China, and of providing computing power to Chinese firms from Southeast Asian data centres, including in Malaysia. Singapore has confirmed it investigated Megaspeed over possible false declarations about servers exported through Singapore to Malaysia. Malaysia says it "will not allow itself to be used to get around export controls."

The Globe also reports friction inside the administration. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Commerce was late in restricting Nvidia chip exports to Megaspeed, which put pressure on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Trump suggested Lutnick may have delayed restrictions to avoid a "blow up" in US-China relations, adding: "We're having a very good relationship with China right now." The Globe says Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang resigned as CEO last week amid scrutiny of his ties to a Chinese executive who formerly led Megaspeed. Sources differ on how directly that is connected to the rule, and this piece does not rely on the link.

The rule landed as Trump left for Asia, where he was expected to meet Xi Jinping for the third time this year under a fragile trade truce. That makes the move awkward to read. It tightens controls on China's access to compute, but it does so through a licensing rule aimed at third-country data centres rather than at Beijing by name.

The wider threat

The BBC's business desk reports a further step: Trump has threatened to bar US firms from building AI data centres overseas. That is a threat, not a rule. I found no published text, agency, or timetable for it, and the Commerce rule does not contain it. It should be read as a signal rather than policy.

It is still a notable one. The licensing rule restricts what goes into foreign data centres. A ban on building them would restrict what American cloud and chip companies do with their own capital and know-how abroad. Those companies have been expanding capacity in the Gulf, Southeast Asia and Europe, partly to serve local customers and partly because power and land are scarce at home. A build-out limit would hit that strategy directly and would fall on allies who host the sites as well as on China.

Who is exposed

The near-term burden falls on operators of data centres that sell compute to Chinese customers, especially in the five named countries, which now face both licensing and end-user checks. Chinese AI developers that rented capacity abroad face the loss or rationing of that supply. US firms whose overseas capacity is owned and used by US-headquartered customers get a carve-out, though with reporting duties.

One unverified thread in the triage material concerns ASML, the Dutch maker of chip-manufacturing equipment, and whether tighter US rules could cut its revenue. The only source offered was an aggregator, and it named no rule, date or company guidance. Nothing in the Commerce rule as summarised touches chipmaking equipment, so no ASML effect can be shown here.

Congress is also moving. Rep. Michael Lawler's bill to extend export controls to remote access has passed the House, according to the Globe, and Senate proposals would let Congress review licences for advanced chip sales to China and push allies such as the Netherlands and Japan to match US restrictions.

What to watch

Three things will show how far this goes. The first is the 90-day licensing window, and whether Commerce approves or refuses applications from existing Southeast Asian operators. The second is whether the overseas build-out threat produces any text, from the White House or Commerce, that says what would be restricted and where. The third is the outcome of Trump's meeting with Xi, which will show whether Washington treats the new rule as leverage in the truce or as a floor it will not trade away.
