Anthropic's claimed maths breakthrough awaits independent checking

In one sentence
An Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude may have proved the Riemann hypothesis in a 250,000-line Lean file, but independent mathematicians have yet to check it.

An AI company says its model may have solved the most famous open problem in mathematics. Nobody outside the company has yet confirmed it. The claim is the Riemann hypothesis, which has stood unsolved for 167 years. Until independent mathematicians have examined the work, it is a company claim and nothing more.

The central claim comes from Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic. Reports say he announced on Friday that he and the company's Claude model had found a proof of the hypothesis. According to those reports, the proof also extends to the broader Generalised Riemann Hypothesis. The BBC reported the story under the headline that Anthropic's models "may have solved" the problem. The article's full text could not be retrieved, so this account relies on secondary reports of it. The details below should be read with that in mind.

What is being claimed

The Riemann hypothesis concerns where the zeros of a function, the Riemann zeta function, fall. The zeros are the inputs at which the function returns zero. The hypothesis says they all lie on a single line. The question matters because it is tied to how prime numbers are spread along the number line. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's seven Millennium Prize problems, and the prize is $1m.

As reported, the work was not released as a conventional paper. It was published in Lean, a proof assistant: software that checks every step of a mathematical argument against fixed logical rules. The proof is reported to run to more than 250,000 lines.

Why checking is the story

Lean raises the odds that a proof is sound, but it does not close the question. A Lean check confirms the argument follows from the stated assumptions. It does not confirm that the problem has been stated correctly. A subtle error in how the Riemann hypothesis was translated into Lean could produce a verified proof of the wrong thing. Humans have to read the definitions, and with a file this long that is slow work.

The record so far is mixed. In August, Anthropic said an unreleased model had made progress on a related question about the zeros, finding a better lower bound. It did not claim a proof. Scientific American ran a piece headlined that no, AI had not just solved the thorniest problem in maths, and made the point that neither humans nor AI had advanced the hypothesis itself. Separately, a counterexample to the Jacobian conjecture was reported with the help of Anthropic's model. Some mathematicians were unimpressed by that result. A different claim, involving the Navier-Stokes equations, has been attributed to OpenAI, and the credit for it is disputed. These sources are not consistent on dates and model names, so they are context rather than evidence.

The company's own position is also unclear. The Verge is reported to have said Anthropic has not independently endorsed Alpöge's announcement. If that holds, the claim is closer to one researcher's statement than a company finding. It would still carry a lot of weight, given where he works.

What to watch

If the proof is right, it would be the largest result yet from an AI system and would settle a problem that has absorbed number theorists since 1859. If it is wrong, the likeliest failure is a flaw in the formal statement. Either outcome should show up quickly. The Lean files are public, so specialists can check the definitions of the zeta function and the hypothesis first. The first sign will be whether established number theorists, rather than Anthropic staff, say the formal statement matches the real one. A Clay Institute ruling would take far longer, since its rules require publication and a waiting period.

The market is already treating Anthropic as a company whose claims move prices. See the Anthropic IPO and the Nasdaq sell-off. A claim this large, and unchecked, is exactly the kind of thing that can swing sentiment either way.
