Poll analysis: rejoining the EU has majority support in about four in five UK seats

In one sentence
A modelled analysis reported by the Guardian suggests rejoining the EU has majority support in about four in five UK seats, but its methodology has yet to be published in full.

A constituency-level analysis reported by the Guardian suggests that voters in about four in five parliamentary seats would now back rejoining the European Union. If the picture holds, it turns a national argument over Brexit into a map of local electoral risk for the two right-of-centre parties, the Conservatives and Reform UK. Both have sought to treat the question as settled.

The headline number is a modelled one. The underlying report was not available for this article, and the secondary coverage reviewed does not name the producer of the model, its sample size or its margins of error. Those gaps limit how much weight the result can bear.

What the model says

According to a summary of the analysis carried by secondary outlets, a model covering 632 Great Britain constituencies puts 512 seats in favour of rejoining, 73 in favour of staying out and 47 too close to call. That is roughly 81% of seats favouring rejoin.

The same summary says that of the 394 seats that voted Leave in 2016, voters in 274 would now prefer to rejoin. It puts support at 58.3% in England, 68.9% in Scotland and 57.9% in Wales. These figures come from that secondary summary and have not been checked against the original.

Constituency-level figures of this kind are almost never direct polls of each seat. They are usually produced by multilevel regression and post-stratification (MRP), which combines large national samples with local demographic data to estimate opinion area by area. The method is well established, but the estimates for individual seats carry wide uncertainty, and a "majority" in a seat can mean a lead of a few points that sits inside the margin of error. The 47 too-close-to-call seats suggest the modellers recognise this.

How it compares

Earlier analyses have found strong but lower seat counts. A Focaldata model for Hope not Hate and Best for Britain put 341 seats at a Remain majority, up from 229 at the 2016 referendum. More in Common's MRP, published around the tenth anniversary of the vote, found only 11 of 632 seats would vote to stay out of the EU in a referendum held today. Estimates therefore range widely depending on the question asked, whether don't-knows are excluded and how the model is built. "Rejoin" is also a different question from "remain": it asks voters to endorse a return on terms that would include the euro-free, rebate-free membership the UK is unlikely to be offered.

A separate Messina Group survey of 11,461 people, fielded from 12 to 24 September, found 49% backing rejoining and 28.5% opposed. That is a plurality rather than a landslide, which fits a picture of broad but shallow support.

Why the right is exposed

The political significance lies in distribution. A national lead for rejoin of 15 to 20 points is a diffuse fact. A lead in four in five seats, including most that voted Leave in 2016, bears directly on individual MPs and candidates, particularly Conservatives defending marginals and Reform hoping to win in former Labour areas.

The analysis lands as Labour moves towards closer ties. The government has just sealed UK-EU trade and youth-experience deals, with food checks, energy and Channel migration still open. Both opposition parties have ruled out reopening the question; the model, if sound, suggests that position is more costly in the seats they most need than in the national polls.

There are limits. Support for rejoining in a poll is not the same as the issue deciding a vote; many Leave-to-rejoin switchers rank the economy, immigration and the cost of living higher. Which party positions the analysis measures against is also unclear from the secondary reporting.

What to watch

The first test is publication of the full methodology: sample size, fieldwork dates and seat-level intervals. After that, the next MRP from a different pollster will show whether 80% is a consistent finding or an outlier at the top of the range.
