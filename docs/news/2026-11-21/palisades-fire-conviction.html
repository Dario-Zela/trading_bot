Man convicted of starting the Palisades fire

In one sentence
A federal jury convicted Jonathan Rinderknecht of starting the Palisades fire in a retrial; sentencing is set for March 2027 and the finding may shape civil and insurance claims.

A federal jury in Los Angeles has convicted Jonathan Rinderknecht, a former rideshare driver, of starting the fire that became the Palisades fire, one of the most destructive urban wildfires in US history. The verdict came on Friday, 20 November, on the third day of deliberations in a retrial. The first trial had ended without a result.

The conviction names a defendant and puts a finding on the record about where the fire began. The finding has consequences beyond the criminal case, because several civil disputes over who is liable for the losses turn on the same question.

The verdict

Rinderknecht was found guilty on two federal counts: destruction of property by means of fire, and arson affecting property used in interstate commerce. Jury selection began on 2 November. In the first trial the jury deadlocked on three arson charges, and the judge declared a mistrial. Reports from the courtroom said the first jury had leaned towards acquittal, 10-2.

For the retrial, prosecutors narrowed the case. The property-destruction count was confined to the earlier fire, known as the Lachman fire, and a third charge involving timber set afire was dropped. The two counts carry a mandatory minimum of five years in federal prison and a maximum of up to 40. Sentencing is set for 17 March 2027.

Bill Essayli, the First Assistant US Attorney, said after the verdict: "The question at the time was who is responsible. And now we have the answer to that question." Rinderknecht's father, Joel, said he disagreed with the jury and called much of the prosecution's case a "false narrative". Defence counsel Steve Haney represented Rinderknecht at trial.

The prosecution's case

The government's theory is that the Palisades fire did not start on 7 January 2025, the day it exploded across the hills above Pacific Palisades. Prosecutors say Rinderknecht lit a fire just after midnight on 1 January near the Skull Rock Trailhead in Topanga State Park. It was apparently put out, but it smouldered underground and reignited about six days later in high winds. That is the link between a small New Year's Day fire and one that burned more than 23,000 acres over more than three weeks and killed 12 people.

Prosecutors described a motive of anger and isolation. They said Rinderknecht was alone and upset on New Year's Eve 2024, partly over failing to find companionship and partly over a former co-worker he had briefly dated. Several passengers from his Uber shifts that night recalled him being angry. After dropping off a fare in Pacific Palisades, prosecutors said, he drove to the trailhead and "maliciously" started the fire.

The evidence they cited included:

  • multiple 911 calls and videos he filmed near the ignition site;
  • a screen recording of himself trying to call 911;
  • phone geolocation data that contradicted his account to investigators and placed him in a clearing about 30 feet from the fire as it grew.

The defence case was not detailed in the coverage reviewed for this article. The first jury's split suggests the case was closer than the verdict implies, and the change from deadlock to conviction came after the prosecution narrowed its charges.

Why the finding matters beyond the courtroom

Prosecutors have put the damage at about $25bn, which would place the fire among the costliest in US history. Thousands of structures were destroyed in Pacific Palisades and surrounding areas. The same night, the Eaton fire in the Altadena area burned more than 14,000 acres and killed 19 people. Officials attributed that fire to electrical arcing on an out-of-service Southern California Edison tower. The Palisades fire has no comparable utility explanation. The criminal verdict supports a human cause.

It is also not the end of the legal argument. The coverage reviewed did not discuss civil litigation or insurance, and the effects below are inferences from how such cases usually work. A criminal conviction does not automatically bind parties who were not defendants. Public agencies and utilities facing damage claims will still contest their own responsibility, including over whether the January 1 fire was properly extinguished and monitored. A jury finding that a deliberately set fire reignited could nonetheless anchor insurers' subrogation claims and shape how damages are argued. Insurers who paid out on losses typically seek to recover from whoever is found liable, and an individual defendant is unlikely to be able to cover a loss of this scale.

The case also tests how arson is prosecuted when the consequences are far larger than the act appears to be. Prosecutors had to show a chain from a small, apparently contained blaze to a catastrophe six days later. The first jury did not accept that chain. The second did.

What comes next

The next date is the sentencing hearing on 17 March 2027, where the judge will decide where in the five-to-40-year range Rinderknecht falls. An appeal is possible, and the defence has already signalled its objection to the prosecution's account. The civil claims over the fire, and any bearing the verdict has on them, will be watched more closely than the sentence itself.
