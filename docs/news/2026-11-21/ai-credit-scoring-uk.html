Should AI be judging our creditworthiness?

In one sentence
UK lenders are using AI to decide who gets credit, and the FCA is policing it with the Consumer Duty and existing law, not AI-specific rules.

A UK borrower turned down for a credit card or loan is increasingly likely to have been judged by a statistical model, not a person. The law that governs those decisions was largely written with human underwriters in mind. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) has so far chosen to police the gap with existing rules, not new ones aimed at AI.

No AI statute, by design

The UK has no AI-specific law for credit decisions. The government's 2023 AI white paper gave oversight to existing sector regulators instead, and for lending that means the FCA. The regulator's main lever is the Consumer Duty, which took effect for open products on 31 July 2023. It requires firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers and to test whether they are doing so. A model that systematically produces worse outcomes for a group of borrowers is, on this reading, a Duty problem whether or not anyone intended it.

Law-firm commentary suggests the FCA will treat the Duty as a primary lens for customer-facing AI. PwC's reading is that the Duty reaches model design, data choices and outputs, not just the customer-facing screen.

The proxy problem

The concern is less that a lender codes in a prohibited characteristic than that a model finds a stand-in for one. Postcode, employment pattern or device type can correlate with age, ethnicity or disability. The Equality Act 2010 covers indirect discrimination, so a neutral-looking variable that disadvantages a protected group can create liability even without intent.

The FCA's February 2025 research note on AI in credit decisions pointed to historical decision data and past exclusion as the main sources of bias. The same note tested whether consumers could spot errors in an algorithmic decision. Participants struggled, and how well they did depended on how the explanation was presented. A right to contest a decision is of limited use if the person cannot tell what went wrong.

Looser rules on automation

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, which received Royal Assent in June 2025, loosened the UK GDPR limits on solely automated decisions. It keeps safeguards, including the right to human review and to contest a decision. The Act also uses a narrower definition of "solely automated": a decision with no meaningful human involvement. Critics say this matters. An Oxford law faculty blog argues the change removes a critical protection for algorithmic credit decisions and suggests the UK consider a technology-neutral "right to reasons" for adverse credit outcomes. The Information Commissioner's Office is developing a statutory code of practice on AI and automated decision-making.

Who answers for a decline

In practice, accountability sits with the lender, which must be able to show that its model produces fair outcomes and that a human can meaningfully review it. The regulator has said little about how it will test that. A lender with a poorly documented model faces the Duty, the Equality Act and data protection law at once, enforced by different bodies.

The next data points are the ICO's code of practice and any FCA guidance on how the Duty applies to credit models. I did not find an up-to-date FCA position for 2026, so the regulator's current stance should be checked directly. Until a decline can be traced to a specific variable and tested for proxy effects, the question of who is responsible will be settled case by case.
