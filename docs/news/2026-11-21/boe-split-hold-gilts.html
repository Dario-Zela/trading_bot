Three MPC members voted for a hike in a 6-3 hold, and gilts are pricing tightening

In one sentence
The 6-3 July hold with three hike votes was a prelude: the Bank raised Bank Rate to 4% by 8-1 on 5 November, and gilts are pricing more, with sector effects still unquantified.

The brief behind this piece describes a Bank of England that held Bank Rate in a 6-3 vote, with three policymakers wanting a rise, and asks whether gilts are pricing a tightening. That framing is out of date. The 6-3 hold was real, but the Bank has since done what the three dissenters wanted. On 5 November the Monetary Policy Committee raised Bank Rate by 25 basis points to 4.00%, its first increase in more than three years, by 8-1. The question for markets is now how many more rises follow, not whether the Bank will tighten.

The split that came first

On 30 July the MPC voted 6-3 to keep Bank Rate at 3.75%, according to AJ Bell's account of the decision. Governor Andrew Bailey led the majority, with Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Clare Lombardelli, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor. Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann voted for a quarter-point rise. Pill and Greene had already argued for a rise in June, and Mann joined them in July.

The Bank's guidance at that meeting leaned the same way as the dissents. It judged risks to the inflation outlook to be "tilted to the upside", and warned that the risk of second-round effects in prices and wages grows the longer energy prices stay high, though it saw "little evidence so far" of them. A 6-3 hold with that wording is not a neutral position. One report of the September meeting also describes a 6-3 hold with the same three dissenters, but that source mixes preview and outcome, so the September split should be treated as unconfirmed.

The vote alone would not have supported a "tightening expected" claim. Market pricing and forecasters did.

Gilts did the pricing

The AJ Bell late market roundup of Friday 18 September, the day after the September meeting, put the 10-year gilt yield at 5.31%, up from 5.21% the day before. Two forecaster moves in it are the kind of evidence the thesis needed:

  • Panmure Liberum's Simon French shifted from expecting no change in Bank Rate over twelve months to 25bp rises at both the November and February meetings.
  • RBC Capital Markets expected a 25bp hike in November.

Brent crude was $104.37 a barrel that day, which is the underlying pressure. Higher energy prices were feeding inflation, and Morgan Stanley said higher expected oil prices would slow disinflation and weigh on growth in 2027.

That is roughly what happened. Inflation reached 3.5% in September, up from 3.1% in August and the highest since October 2025, with energy contributing about one percentage point. The Bank now expects inflation of around 4.5% in the first quarter of 2027 and around 5% in the second, about 1.25 points above its July projection.

November: a hike, and a dissent from the other side

In November the three-member hawkish bloc dissolved into the majority. Pill and Greene voted with Bailey for 25bp. Mann, who had wanted only 25bp in July, pushed for 50bp and a 4.25% rate, arguing the economy had entered a high-inflation, tight-labour-market regime. The votes, as recorded in MPA Magazine's live blog of the decision, ran 8-1.

The post-meeting commentary was not that of a Bank that considers the job done:

  • Bailey saw an argument for a further increase unless inflation risks soften, followed by a more conditional approach.
  • Pill said a single hike "is unlikely to be sufficient".
  • Greene expected additional hikes to be needed.
  • Lombardelli saw a case for a further increase "in the near term".
  • Taylor called it a "'one-for-now' insurance hike" and said the next move "could be up, down, or sideways".

Gilts sold off on the day and then partly recovered, with sterling firmer. Since then the 10-year yield has climbed above 5.35%, and the two-year yield rose above 5% on 16 November, a three-year high, according to Trading Economics reports. Investinglive reported that markets were pricing three more Bank of England hikes, while analysts saw a shallower path. Those two accounts differ, and the gap between them is where the next few months will be decided.

What it means for banks and housebuilders

The original angle was the effect on UK bank margins and housebuilder financing. The evidence there is thinner than for rates themselves, and it is worth saying so. No post-hike data on bank or housebuilder share prices turned up in the reporting checked for this piece, so any sector call is inference rather than observation.

The mechanics are conventional. Higher short rates tend to widen the gap between what banks earn on loans and pay on deposits, but the benefit erodes if deposit costs catch up, and a rate path that hurts loan demand and raises defaults works against it. Trackers reprice almost immediately. Fixed mortgage rates follow swap rates, not Bank Rate; one broker said the two are "not joined at the hip". Several lenders had in fact trimmed some fixed rates in the days before the decision as swaps eased on lower oil prices.

For housebuilders the channel is the cost of mortgages to buyers and the cost of development finance, and both follow the longer end of the curve. A 10-year gilt above 5.3% and a two-year above 5% are the numbers that matter there, more than the 25bp itself. One critic, Duncan Kreeger of broker TAB, argued that the rise answered fiscal indiscipline rather than a necessary policy step. The gilt market's reaction suggests that the fiscal premium and the policy rate are not easily separated.

What to watch

The next decision is on 17 December. The test is whether the committee's split is now over the pace of tightening instead of whether to start. Mann wanted 50bp; Taylor calls November insurance. If the 8-1 becomes a spread of views between a second hike and a pause, the market's three-hike pricing will be tested against the analysts' shallower path. The inflation data between now and then, with energy the swing factor, will do most of that work.
