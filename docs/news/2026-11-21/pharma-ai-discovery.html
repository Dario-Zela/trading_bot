Big Pharma says AI is shortening drug discovery and courts frontier labs

In one sentence
Drugmakers say AI is shortening discovery and are courting labs like Anthropic, but the public record offers few quantified timelines and little clinical proof so far.

Drugmakers are telling investors and the public that artificial intelligence is shortening the road from idea to drug candidate, and they are courting the frontier AI labs, Anthropic among them, as partners. The harder question is whether the claim comes with numbers. In the material reviewed for this piece, it mostly does not.

The pitch

Discovery is the early stretch of drug development: finding a biological target, designing molecules that act on it, and winnowing thousands of candidates to a handful worth testing in people. It is slow and expensive, and most candidates fail. A tool that trims even a few months from it, or raises the hit rate, would matter in a sector where a single approval can cost billions of dollars to reach.

That is the case drugmakers are making. The underlying Reuters report is the sole source cited for this brief and could not be retrieved in full, so the specific timeline claims attributed to individual companies are not reproduced here. Those claims are best read as company statements, not measured results.

Frontier labs move in

The other side of the courtship is visible in Anthropic's own recent moves. Reuters reported in September that the company had quietly set up a biology laboratory for physical experiments. An Anthropic spokesperson later said the lab was not specifically for drug discovery. The company has since launched Claude Science, a workbench aimed at research teams, and has named partners including Novo Nordisk, which is testing it on R&D workflows, as well as Bristol Myers Squibb, Generate Biomedicines, Enveda and BigHat Biosciences. Anthropic has also signalled interest in neglected diseases that large drugmakers regard as unprofitable.

The terms of these arrangements, including how long they run and who owns what is discovered, have not been made public in the reporting reviewed here.

Where the evidence stands

Independent assessments are more guarded than the corporate messaging. Analysts have noted that billions of dollars have flowed into AI drug discovery while clinical impact remains limited. A review in Nature Reviews Drug Discovery, as summarised by eMarketer, attributes the thin record to missing evidence, not proof that the approach cannot work. Drugs take years to move through trials, so a faster discovery stage will not show up as approvals for some time.

There is also a structural point. Big pharma holds decades of proprietary experimental data and already runs its own AI tools. That makes partnerships with model builders more likely than wholesale outsourcing, and it leaves open who captures the value: the drugmaker with the data, or the lab with the models.

What to watch

The test is straightforward. A claim of shorter discovery should be stated as months saved per programme, against a named baseline, and followed by candidates entering and surviving trials. Until companies publish that, or partnership terms emerge, the pitch is a statement of intent. The first real data points will be early-phase trial readouts from AI-assisted candidates, and any disclosure of financial terms from the Anthropic tie-ups as the company's public-market story develops.
