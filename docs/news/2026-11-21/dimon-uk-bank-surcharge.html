Dimon says JPMorgan will find ways to offset 'unfair' UK bank tax rise

In one sentence
Jamie Dimon says JPMorgan will find ways to offset the UK bank surcharge doubled to 6% for 15 months, a move he calls unfair, while the £3bn Canary Wharf tower remains uncommitted.

Jamie Dimon, chief executive of JPMorgan Chase, says the bank will look for ways to offset the rise in the UK bank surcharge announced in last month's Budget. He called the increase "unfair". The remark matters beyond the sums involved because JPMorgan has a planned £3bn headquarters tower at Canary Wharf, and Dimon has spent months suggesting that the tax climate could affect it.

What Dimon said

Speaking at a London event on Thursday, in comments first reported by the Financial Times, Dimon said he intended to set "an example so that [governments] understand the trade-offs of these unfair penalties", according to City AM's account. He added that any offsetting would be done "more in sorrow" than in anger. He did not say which parts of the business would absorb the cost, whether in headcount, lines of business or investment, and JPMorgan declined to comment further.

The remarks follow a consistent line. In October he argued in London that a windfall tax aimed only at domestic banks would not be fair. Earlier in the year he told an interviewer that he had "always thought it was wrong" to single out a company "that had nothing to do with the crisis".

The tax change

Chancellor John Healey's first Budget, on 28 October, doubled the banking surcharge, which sits on top of corporation tax, from 3% to 6%. Banks therefore face a combined rate of 31% rather than 28%. The increase runs for 15 months and is meant to help pay for a £100 household energy payment to about 20 million households in council tax bands A to D.

The Office for Budget Responsibility expects the change to raise about £1.5bn and puts total surcharge receipts at £2.4bn in 2027. It also assumes some banks will shift profits out of the UK in response, costing around £190m in receipts, a figure it calls uncertain. That assumption is the Treasury's own acknowledgement of the behaviour Dimon is now describing. Reports citing the FT put the extra cost to JPMorgan at roughly £100m–£150m a year. That is real money but small beside the investment at stake.

Bank shares rallied on the day, because the rise was smaller than the return to the 2023 rate of 8% that had been mooted. A JPMorgan spokesperson said the tax "lacks principle" and overlooks global competition, while insisting the bank remains committed to the UK. Healey said he was grateful to the banks but asked them to help fund relief for families. David Postings of UK Finance welcomed the time limit but said "a tax rise is still a tax rise". Will Howlett of Quilter Cheviot urged scepticism about the time limit, saying the rate would probably stay high unless the Middle East conflict eased.

The tower

JPMorgan unveiled plans last year for a tower of about 3m sq ft at Canary Wharf to serve as its new London headquarters. City AM puts the expected benefit to the local economy at up to £10bn over six years, with some 7,800 jobs in the industry. The bank employs about 23,000 people in the UK, with large hubs in London and Bournemouth.

Dimon has never committed to the project in the face of higher taxes. In May he told an audience that the bank would reconsider the headquarters if the government became hostile to banks. Asked whether it was a "binary decision", he said he did not know what he would do. People close to him have said that "threatened" is too strong a word. The latest comments are about offsetting the cost, not cancelling the build, and nothing reported so far says the tower is off.

The arithmetic favours caution on that point. A cost of £100m–£150m for 15 months is modest against a £3bn commitment, and a headquarters decision rests on far more than one surcharge: planning, lease terms, talent, and the competitiveness of London against New York, Paris and Frankfurt. The more plausible reading is that Dimon is signalling a willingness to move marginal activity, such as hiring and expansion, rather than reversing a flagship project. That is an interpretation, not something he has said.

What to watch

The surcharge returns to 3% only if the 15-month limit holds, and Howlett's doubt is one the Treasury will have to answer when the OBR next updates its forecast. The first test of whether Dimon's offsetting has any bite will be whether JPMorgan names the activities it is trimming, and whether the Canary Wharf plans proceed on schedule. The surcharge is due to rise in January, which gives the bank, and the Treasury's profit-shifting assumption, an early test.
