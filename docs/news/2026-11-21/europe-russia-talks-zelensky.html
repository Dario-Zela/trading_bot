Europe says it is ready to talk to Russia as Zelensky calls Putin a 'killer'

In one sentence
European leaders say they are ready for direct talks with Moscow, as Zelensky calls Putin 'a killer' after Trump called him a friend and Washington's tone diverges from its allies'.

European leaders say they are ready to open direct talks with Moscow on ending the war in Ukraine. They said so in the same week that Donald Trump called Vladimir Putin his "friend" and Volodymyr Zelensky answered that, for Ukraine, the Russian president is "an enemy" and "a killer". The two episodes are connected. Washington's tone toward the Kremlin has softened while Moscow's missiles keep falling on Ukrainian cities, and Europe is deciding how far to go without American cover.

A friend in Shenzhen

On Thursday 19 November, Trump met Putin and China's Xi Jinping in Shenzhen for a trilateral meeting that commentators quickly labelled "Yalta 2.0". The joint statement, titled "Declaration Toward Peace", did not mention Ukraine. Trump greeted Putin as a friend. At a White House press conference on Friday he went on praising the relationship: "I have fantastic relationships with Putin. That's a very good thing." He said Zelensky and Putin both want to end the war but "don't know how". Asked whether Putin was a friend of the United States, he replied: "I can't speak to America."

Zelensky was asked about it by CNN. How Trump describes Putin, he said, is up to Trump. "For us, he is an enemy. And of course, for us, he is a killer." He argued that warm language eases the pressure on the Kremlin and lets Putin believe he can carry on.

The timing gave the remark its edge. Overnight from Thursday into Friday, Zelensky said, Russia fired more than 400 attack drones and over 110 missiles, one of the largest strikes of the war. He said the targets were "ordinary civilian infrastructure", including energy facilities, logistics sites and bridges over the Dnipro. At least seven people were killed in Kyiv. The strikes followed Ukrainian attacks on the Crimea bridge, which destroyed a large part of the structure. Putin responded that there would be "no negotiations" on a partial ceasefire.

Berlin's answer to "Yalta"

Europe's response had come a day earlier, at a Coalition of the Willing meeting in Berlin hosted by Chancellor Friedrich Merz. The name "Yalta" carries weight in Central and Eastern Europe, where it stands for great powers settling the fate of smaller states in a room those states were not in. Merz said: "A Yalta format will not bring peace." Emmanuel Macron said the Shenzhen statement did not amount to a reorganisation of the world, and that negotiators needed Ukraine, Europe, the United States and Russia at the same table: "Europe is not a subject on a menu of discussions." Euronews also quoted the UK prime minister, named in its report as Andy Burnham, saying there is no decision about Ukraine without Ukraine in the room.

The leaders also agreed that Europe should begin face-to-face negotiations with Russia, according to Euronews, a step not yet taken at that level. Europe has assembled a joint team of seven representatives, from France, the UK, Germany, Poland, Italy, the EU institutions and NATO, for the next round of talks.

This is not wholly new. In June, the UK, France and Germany backed direct talks with Russia, and German, French and British envoys met Russia's deputy foreign minister, Mikhail Galuzin, in Moscow. Macron had argued for opening a channel since autumn 2025, while Merz and Starmer, then prime minister, were more cautious. What has changed is the setting. The earlier moves looked like Europe supporting an American process. The Berlin meeting looks more like Europe preparing for the chance that no such process exists.

What Moscow wants

The Kremlin is not making this easy. Putin's line, reported this week, is that Europeans should come to Moscow to define their positions, after which the Kremlin would decide whether to continue dialogue with Ukraine. That treats Europe as a petitioner, not a party. Moscow has not committed to a multilateral format, and earlier Kremlin comments have shown a preference for dealing with the Trump administration alone.

There is a counterweight inside the American camp. Euronews reported that Trump envoy Jared Kushner said having Europe at the table is essential to ending the war and that Russia should accept European participation. Steve Witkoff and Kushner may travel to Moscow after meeting Ukrainian and European officials. The gap between Washington and its allies is therefore real but not total, and it runs through the president's own tone more than through his negotiators' stated positions.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is positioning or policy. The first is whether the seven-person European team actually gets a meeting in Moscow, and on what terms. The second is whether Witkoff and Kushner's Moscow trip happens and whether Europeans are in the room or briefed afterwards. The third is how Russia answers the Ukrainian strikes on the Crimea bridge and the Dnipro crossings, since Putin has ruled out a partial ceasefire. If Moscow keeps insisting that Europe come to it on its terms, the readiness to talk that Berlin announced will meet its first test quickly.

Note: some details here rest on single-outlet reporting by Euronews and CNN as relayed by others. The BBC original could not be accessed directly.
