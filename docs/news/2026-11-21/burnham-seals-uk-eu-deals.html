Burnham seals UK-EU trade and youth-experience deals, with food checks, energy and Channel migration still open

In one sentence
Britain and the EU agreed food, carbon-market and youth deals in Brussels, but EU states reportedly hold implementation until a tuition-fees deal, and migration remains unresolved.

Britain and the European Union agreed deals on food trade, linked carbon markets and youth mobility at a summit in Brussels on Friday 20 November. It was the first big test of Andy Burnham's promise to move the UK closer to its largest trading partner. The headline version, that the deals are sealed, needs a qualification. The EU's member states have reportedly said they will hold off on implementing the youth scheme until there is agreement on university tuition fees. The detail below comes from wire and news-agency reporting of the summit. The full legal texts were not available.

What was agreed

Burnham went into the summit having said the existing post-Brexit settlement was not good enough. Pre-summit coverage, including ITV's report that talks were "going down to the wire", described a package covering food and agriculture, energy and emissions trading, and a youth experience scheme. Channel migration was a separate ask. The prime minister's camp had talked it up as a "Channel action" deal.

Reporting from the summit says the two sides did clinch the food and youth strands. The food element is a veterinary and plant-health arrangement that would remove many of the post-Brexit checks on agricultural and food exports. It builds on the framework agreed by Keir Starmer in May 2025, when a youth scheme was committed to only in principle, "on terms to be mutually agreed". Linking the two emissions markets was also agreed.

At the closing press conference the European Council president, António Costa, said the EU remained open to "the closest possible relationship between the European Union and the United Kingdom". That answers the question the desk had about an unsourced quote. It was said on the record, by Costa. Ursula von der Leyen reportedly called the agreement a "turning point".

What is still open

The sticking point is tuition fees. EU students at English universities currently pay international rates. Brussels has wanted that cut as part of any youth-mobility arrangement. Burnham insisted that the "deal is done", even as talks on fees continue over roughly the next six months. A statement from EU member states, seen by AFP, said they would delay implementation until a fees agreement is reached.

That gap explains the dispute over how to describe the outcome. Energy Secretary Ed Miliband said on Saturday, according to the BBC, that the wider deal does not depend on a separate fees agreement. The two positions can both be true. London may regard the text as signed and in force, while the EU regards the youth strand as signed but not yet switchable. Which reading governs is a matter of the legal drafting, and the legal texts were not available for this piece.

The pre-summit reporting also suggested the UK had given ground on the size of the youth scheme. One account put an EU demand at 100,000 visas a year for people aged 31 and under, with UK negotiators initially pushing for a lower cap. That figure was pre-summit and unconfirmed, and it should not be read as the agreed number.

Other strands remain unresolved or unreported. Alignment with EU regulation is one. Channel migration is another: no post-summit source consulted for this piece confirms a returns or crossings agreement.

Prices and the longer horizon

The government has sold the package partly on cost. Downing Street has said the food deal would make food cheaper and add about £9bn to the economy, a claim that predates this summit and comes from No 10 itself. The BBC's Faisal Islam has argued that cost-cutting measures of this kind do not by themselves lower prices in shops. Retailers and importers decide whether savings on paperwork reach shelves. The saving on a pallet of cheese is not the same thing as a lower price on a supermarket shelf.

Burnham has also said the UK will "explore" even closer ties with the EU after the Brussels package. No scope, timetable or negotiating mandate has been given. Reports say the two sides will "brainstorm" a longer-term relationship. Whether that is a genuine opening or reassuring language ahead of a hard domestic debate is not yet clear. Burnham has floated rejoining the bloc, and a separate poll analysis in this edition suggests rejoining has majority support in about four in five UK seats. Government rhetoric still sits well short of that, and the summit concentrated on smaller steps.

What to watch

Three things will settle how large this is. The first is the published text of the youth-scheme and food agreements, and whether implementation is explicitly conditional on fees. The second is the six-month fees negotiation, which Burnham has already conceded is continuing. The third is whether any migration arrangement emerges to match the "Channel action" billing. Until then, the summit has produced agreements, and one of them cannot yet take effect.
