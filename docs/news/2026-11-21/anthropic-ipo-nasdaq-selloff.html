ANTHROPIC IPO · By M. Halloway · 2026-11-21 · 5 min read

Anthropic's pop fades as tech sells off and the Nasdaq gives up its 2026 gains

In one sentence
Anthropic's record $81bn IPO closed up 17% on day one, but a tech sell-off that began before the listing wiped out the Nasdaq's 2026 gains within two sessions.

Anthropic's market debut was supposed to be a test of whether public investors would pay up for a frontier AI lab. By the end of its second session the question had changed to whether the market could absorb a listing of that size without damage to everything around it. The record $81bn offering, priced at $83 a share under the ticker ANT, closed its first day 17% higher. Technology stocks then sold off for a second day, and the Nasdaq Composite gave up its 2026 gains, according to the discovery brief behind this report. That year-to-date figure has not been independently confirmed against the Nasdaq's own closing data.

The debut

Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares to raise about $81.3bn, the largest IPO on record. It is bigger than the $75bn SpaceX originally sought in June, though short of SpaceX's expanded $86.25bn final offering, according to Decrypt's account of the deal.

The opening was strong. Shares began trading at $111.50, a 34% premium to the offer price, and briefly pushed the fully diluted valuation past $2tn. They then slid toward $93 before settling at $97.17, still 17% above the offer price.

The pop was real but it was smaller than the opening print. A stock that opens 34% up and closes 17% up has given back half its first-day premium within hours. That is a conventional pattern for a heavily subscribed deal, and it does not by itself signal weakness. It does show that demand at the open was running ahead of demand at the close.

The sell-off

The broader market did not wait for Anthropic's price to settle. On debut day the Nasdaq Composite was down about 2% by the afternoon, with the S&P 500 off 1.9% and the Dow down 2.2%, per the reports gathered here. Breadth was poor: one commentary counted just 18% of stocks rising, with 725 new lows against 38 new highs.

The chip names took the worst of it. Nvidia fell 3.4%, Micron 2.2% and Broadcom 2.1%. AMD dropped 7.7%, partly because the investigative outlet Hunterbrook disclosed that an affiliated fund held a short position in it. Microsoft lost 2%, Oracle 3%, and the AI-infrastructure plays CoreWeave and Nebius fell 5% and 7.8%. Cybersecurity was a partial exception, with Palo Alto Networks up 6%.

"Anthropic's record-breaking IPO has failed to rekindle enthusiasm for tech stocks," Joe Maher, a market economist at Capital Economics, told Reuters.

Friday brought a further leg down. Bloomberg's market wrap said an early rally fizzled as Anthropic's solid debut failed to sustain enthusiasm for the AI trade. The S&P 500's losing streak had run to its longest since April 2024, and the VIX volatility gauge had climbed to its highest since March, according to the weekend wrap-ups.

Cause or coincidence?

The brief asks whether the debut drove the sell-off or merely coincided with it. The evidence points to the second.

First, timing. The market was already under pressure before ANT printed. One report described an early rally on debut day that evaporated while investors waited for the stock to start trading. A listing cannot cause a decline that began before it traded.

Second, the pattern of losses. If Anthropic's offering were simply absorbing capital from other tech holdings, the damage would be concentrated in large AI-exposed names that investors might sell to fund an allocation. Instead, the losses ran across software, semiconductors, AI infrastructure and, as the next section shows, non-US markets. That looks more like a broad de-risking than a funding squeeze.

Third, the deal's own structure. The IPO sat alongside a conditional $26bn private placement from AI-sector companies, with Nvidia and Google each committing about $10bn and AMD $2.5bn. Broadcom, Micron and Microsoft pledged $1bn each and TSMC $500m. The disclosures also showed roughly $900.9bn in infrastructure and computing commitments through 2045. Investors reading those documents saw a business whose growth depends on the same hardware suppliers whose shares were falling, which ties the two stories together even if it does not make one the cause of the other.

The fair reading is that Anthropic's debut became the occasion for the market to reassess AI spending rather than its trigger. The listing did not give the trade a lift, and a lift was what bulls had hoped for.

Spillover and what is left to test

The stress was not confined to Wall Street. European semiconductor names, including ASML, fell in the spillover. European equities traded as US ADRs fell again on Friday and were down more than 7% on the week, according to MT Newswires. That item named no index, tickers or trigger, so the figure should be treated as indicative of risk-off conditions and not as a measured benchmark move.

For index-heavy portfolios the arithmetic is blunt. The Nasdaq's year-to-date gain was built over ten and a half months and, on the brief's account, was surrendered within two sessions. Funds with heavy exposure to the large AI names took the same hit, and a fresh listing of $2tn scale adds to the weight of AI paper that institutions must now decide how to hold.

The question for the next wave of AI listings is pricing. Anthropic's $83 offer price held up, as ANT stayed above it through the sell-off. That is a point in the deal's favour. But a first-day gain that halves within hours, and a market that fell on the day, give later issuers little reason to expect the same reception.

The next data points are concrete. One is whether ANT holds above $83 as the first-week trading settles. Another is how Nvidia and AMD trade once the Hunterbrook short disclosure has been digested. The third is the lock-up and analyst-initiation calendar, since research coverage from the underwriting banks typically begins after a quiet period and gives the market its first structured valuation of the company.
