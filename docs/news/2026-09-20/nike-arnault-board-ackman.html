Nike adds LVMH's Alexandre Arnault to board as Ackman doubles down on turnaround bet

In one sentence
Nike added LVMH's Alexandre Arnault to its board as it exited the S&P 100, and Bill Ackman praised the move — though Pershing Square exited its own Nike stake at a $600m loss months ago.

Nike's board now has twelve seats, and one of the new ones belongs to a 34-year-old LVMH executive whose surname is doing most of the talking. Alexandre Arnault, deputy chief executive of Moët Hennessy and son of LVMH chairman Bernard Arnault, joined Nike's board on September 15, the company confirmed, in a move that lands squarely in the middle of the sportswear giant's worst stretch in decades.

The timing is not subtle. Nike shares closed around $35.50 this week, down from a November 2021 peak above $179 — a decline of roughly 78% that has erased more than $200 billion in market value. On September 21, after eighteen years, Nike drops out of the S&P 100 index entirely, replaced by Palo Alto Networks. It stays in the broader S&P 500, the Dow and the NYSE, but the demotion is a blunt marker of how far the company has fallen relative to its own history.

A luxury résumé for a volume problem

Arnault's background is almost entirely in premium goods: chief executive of Rimowa, the German luggage maker, from 2016 to 2020; head of product and communications at Tiffany & Co. from 2021 until early 2025; board seats at Carrefour, Birkenstock and Moncler along the way. None of it overlaps with what Nike actually needs to fix, which is a mass-market wholesale and inventory problem, not a premium-positioning one.

Mark Parker, Nike's executive chairman, framed the appointment in those terms anyway, saying Arnault "has earned a reputation for helping iconic global brands evolve, innovate and grow in a changing, complex marketplace." That is board-speak for signaling intent rather than describing a fix. Nike's actual turnaround, branded internally as Win Now, is built around five more pedestrian pillars — culture, product, marketing, marketplace and in-person retail — and centers on CEO Elliott Hill's effort to rebuild the wholesale relationships his predecessor spent years unwinding. Wholesale revenue rose 8% to $7.5 billion in the most recent quarter even as Nike Direct sales fell 8% to $4.6 billion, evidence the repair is underway but incomplete. Full-year revenue still slipped 2% to $46.4 billion, with Greater China sales down 17% in the fourth quarter alone as domestic rivals Anta and Li Ning keep taking share.

A luxury board seat does not touch any of that directly. What it may do is reposition how Nike is perceived by investors and partners — less a commodity sneaker maker fighting margin erosion, more a brand capable of premium repositioning, the kind of story that got Birkenstock and Moncler re-rated. Whether the market buys that framing is a separate question from whether Hill's operational plan is working.

Ackman's applause, and the money that already left

The loudest voice cheering the appointment was Bill Ackman, who posted on X: "This is a great development for @Nike. I am an @alexarnault fan, and Alex can help. And Nike needs help." It is a notable statement given the arc of Ackman's own Nike trade, which has been more turbulent than the framing of a supportive tweet suggests.

Pershing Square first bought into Nike in the spring of 2024, built the stake to 18.8 million shares by early 2025, then converted the equity position into deep in-the-money call options — a maneuver that kept the exposure alive while making it disappear from the fund's 13F filings, since options don't have to be reported the same way. That structure let Ackman claim he was "doubling down" even as headlines read that Nike had vanished from his portfolio. It did not work out: Pershing sold the remaining options in November 2025, booking a cumulative loss of roughly 30% — north of $600 million — on the position. By Pershing's most recent 13F, filed in August, Nike is not among its holdings at all; the fund's money has since rotated into Uber, Visa, Mastercard, S&P Global and Netflix.

So the endorsement this week comes from a former shareholder, not a current one. Ackman's public enthusiasm for the Arnault pick is real, but it is commentary from the sidelines rather than fresh capital backing the thesis — a distinction that matters for anyone reading the tweet as a signal that smart money is re-underwriting the turnaround.

What the appointment actually signals

Taken together, the two threads point to the same tension. Nike's board is adding a credential that speaks to brand elevation and premium strategy at the exact moment its index status and market capitalization reflect a volume business in retreat. Ackman's applause adds visibility to the move without adding conviction in the form of a position. Neither, on its own, changes what shows up in Nike's next earnings report: wholesale recovery, inventory discipline, and whether China stabilizes.

The next real test arrives with Nike's fiscal first-quarter results, expected in late September, which will show whether Win Now's wholesale gains are outpacing the continued slide in China and direct sales. A board seat can change how a company is discussed. It does not, by itself, change what the numbers say.
