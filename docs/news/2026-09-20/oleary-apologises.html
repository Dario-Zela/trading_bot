Ryanair's O'Leary apologises 'unreservedly' for 'high-fare rapists' remark

Michael O'Leary, Ryanair's chief executive, whose off-the-cuff remarks are a recurring feature of the airline's public profile. — World Travel & Tourism Council / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Ryanair's Michael O'Leary apologised 'unreservedly' to survivors of sexual violence after a week defending his 'high-fare rapists' jibe at rivals — reputational theatre with no market consequence.

Michael O'Leary built a career on saying the quiet part loud — charging for toilets was never real, but the jokes about it did no harm to Ryanair's share price. This time the quip went further than the brand could carry, and after a week of trying to ride it out, Europe's most talkative airline boss folded.

Speaking to reporters in Dublin on 10 September, ahead of Ryanair's annual general meeting, O'Leary predicted a wave of passengers switching to Ryanair because they "can't afford to fly with the high-fare rapists around Europe" — his description of choice for Aer Lingus, Lufthansa and British Airways. Asked whether the word was appropriate, he said "Absolutely." Pressed again on whether it trivialised sexual violence, he told reporters he would "happily offend" the airlines named.

A week of defiance

That defiance held for several days. The Dublin Rape Crisis Centre, led by chief executive Rachel Morrogh, called publicly for an apology. Taoiseach Micheál Martin criticised the remark, and in London, UK transport secretary Heidi Alexander said the comment would cost Ryanair "the confidence of women who have been subject to sexual assault." O'Leary did not budge — he maintained rape was a serious crime while insisting his phrasing was fair game against commercial rivals.

The reversal

On Friday 18 September, in a video statement, O'Leary changed course. He said conversations with "family and friends" had brought home how much "upset and offence" the word had caused, "especially to victims and survivors," and apologised "sincerely and unreservedly to those people." "I am truly sorry for the offence and upset I have caused you by my careless use of those words," he said, calling it a careless choice rather than a considered one.

The Dublin Rape Crisis Centre welcomed the statement and said it hoped the episode would "promote a greater public understanding of the impact language can have," adding it had offered O'Leary training on the effects of sexual violence.

Theatre, not a market event

For a company whose chief executive has spent three decades trading in provocation — Muslim-passenger profiling jokes, toilet-charge threats, standing-only seating proposals that never happened — this fits a well-worn script: outrageous line, sustained pressure, scripted climbdown, life goes on. There is no sign here of the kind of consequence that would interest a market desk. No shareholder resolution, no analyst note, no dent in booking data has been reported, and Ryanair's AGM business — largely a routine reappointment of directors — proceeded without disruption.

What changed is reputational, not financial. O'Leary's climbdowns rarely cost him with investors; his fare-cutting, engine-scale purchasing and hedging record have historically discounted the personal outbursts as noise. The apology reads as management of that noise rather than any concession the board was forced to extract.

The next test of Ryanair's standing will be its half-year results, expected in early November, where traffic growth and fare trends — not the CEO's choice of words — will decide how the stock trades.
