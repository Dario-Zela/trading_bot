FDA grants accelerated approval to Bayer's Hyrnuo for HER2-mutant lung cancer

Bayer's headquarters tower in Leverkusen, Germany — Wikimedia Commons / obi (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Bayer's Hyrnuo can now treat HER2-mutant lung cancer from diagnosis, but the accelerated approval rests on a 69-patient trial, not proof the drug extends lives.

Bayer's lung cancer drug Hyrnuo can now be offered to patients the moment they're diagnosed, rather than only after a first round of treatment has failed. The Food and Drug Administration granted the expanded accelerated approval on September 9, clearing sevabertinib — Hyrnuo's chemical name — as a first-line option for adults with locally advanced or metastatic non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer whose tumors carry an activating mutation in the HER2 kinase domain.

In one sentence: Bayer's Hyrnuo can now treat HER2-mutant lung cancer from diagnosis, but the accelerated approval rests on a 69-patient trial, not proof the drug extends lives.

The approval, reviewed under the FDA's Project Orbis alongside UK regulators, follows a phase 1/2 trial called SOHO-01. In the 69 treatment-naïve patients evaluated, the drug produced an objective response rate of 75% — six percent complete responses, seventy percent partial — and 73% of responders were still responding at six months. Those are real numbers, and doctors treating a genetic subtype of cancer with few options will welcome them. But they are also surrogate endpoints: shrinking tumors, not confirmed survival gains. Hyrnuo's continued marketing rests on Bayer completing a confirmatory trial, SOHO-02, pitting the drug against standard chemotherapy in previously untreated patients.

A narrow lane, tightly contested

HER2-activating mutations show up in roughly 2% to 4% of NSCLC cases — a sliver of the roughly 234,000 US lung cancer diagnoses expected this year, by National Cancer Institute estimates. Bayer is not even alone in that sliver. Boehringer Ingelheim's zongertinib, sold as Hernexeos, won its own first-line accelerated approval in February, several months ahead of Hyrnuo. The two drugs differ mainly on label wording — Hyrnuo covers non-squamous NSCLC, Hernexeos unresectable or metastatic disease broadly — and on dosing, with Boehringer's pill taken once daily against Hyrnuo's twice-daily 20mg regimen. Christine Roth, a Bayer oncology executive, framed the approval as proof that "precision medicine" can match the right patients to the right therapy — true enough clinically, but it also describes a business model in which two companies are now racing for a slice of a slice of the lung cancer market.

The accelerated-approval question mark

The FDA's accelerated pathway was built to get promising cancer drugs to patients faster, using tumor shrinkage as a stand-in for survival data that takes years to collect. Its record on delivering afterward is uneven. Of 167 oncology indications granted accelerated approval by mid-2024, 61% eventually converted to full approval, but 19% were withdrawn — including, this year alone, Xpovio's lymphoma indication in April and Tazverik's sarcoma and lymphoma indications in June, the latter pulled after a safety signal turned up in its own confirmatory trial. A 2023 law change now lets the FDA demand confirmatory trials be underway before or shortly after approval, and move faster to pull drugs when sponsors don't deliver — a sign regulators themselves have grown warier of the pathway's early, generous years.

Hyrnuo's adverse-event list — diarrhea, liver toxicity, interstitial lung disease, heart and eye effects, pancreatic enzyme elevation — is typical of kinase inhibitors in this class, and not itself unusual. The open question is whether SOHO-02 confirms a survival or progression-free benefit against chemotherapy, or whether Hyrnuo joins the list of drugs whose early response rates didn't hold up.

What this means for Bayer's bet

Bayer has been explicit that its pharmaceutical growth strategy through 2027 leans on precision oncology and cardiovascular drugs to offset patent cliffs elsewhere in the portfolio. An accelerated approval generates a press release and a narrow revenue stream, not proof that the strategy scales — a distinction that matters for a company betting its next growth phase on a string of biomarker-defined drugs rather than one broad blockbuster. Hyrnuo's addressable population, split further with Hernexeos, is a rounding error next to what a broad-label lung cancer therapy would command.

The next real signal is SOHO-02 data, which will determine whether Hyrnuo converts to full approval or joins the growing list of accelerated approvals that didn't survive contact with a confirmatory trial. Until then, the approval is a clinical milestone for a small group of patients — and a modest, unproven data point for Bayer's oncology thesis.
