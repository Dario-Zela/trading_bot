Minister floats benefit cuts or prison for parents over children's crimes

Jake Richards, the UK's minister for sentencing and youth justice, who floated tougher penalties for parents of young offenders. — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons (Official portrait, 2024)
In one sentence
A UK minister floated jailing or cutting benefits for parents of young offenders, drawing quick pushback from academics and youth-justice charities over evidence the approach doesn't work.

A government minister has floated one of the more provocative ideas in Labour's youth justice agenda: parents whose children commit crimes could lose welfare benefits, face fines, or in the most severe cases go to prison, if they are judged to have failed to keep their children in line.

Jake Richards, the minister for sentencing and youth justice, told reporters that the system "should hold parents to account in a proportionate way for ensuring that they play their role in keeping communities safe." He described the approach as "carrot-and-stick" — support for struggling families paired with sharper consequences when parents disengage from it.

An old tool, sharpened

The proposal builds on plans set out in May to expand Parenting Orders, a mechanism that already lets courts compel parents to attend counselling or parenting classes, or face fines of up to £1,000, when their child offends. What is new is the scale of the escalation being discussed: benefit reductions as a lever, and prison — Richards said — reserved for "the most extreme cases," with sentencing left entirely to judges' discretion. He has said he expects the jail option to be used "very, very rarely."

The timing is notable. Parenting Orders have all but fallen out of use — from more than 1,000 issued in 2009/10 to just 33 in 2022/23 — a decline the government reads as evidence the tool needs teeth, and critics read as evidence it never worked to begin with.

Pushback, fast

That second reading arrived within hours. Nikki Rutter, an assistant professor at Durham University who studies child behaviour, told the BBC that "we have a huge amount of evidence that parenting orders aren't effective. It's why we're seeing the decrease in them. The police know these are not effective measures." She warned the financial angle risks compounding the problem it aims to solve: "Poverty is inextricably linked to a lot of criminal activity. The more you're fining parents, the less money they've got. The less money the family have, we're seeing a further dive into poverty."

Jess Mullen, chief executive of the Alliance for Youth Justice, made a related point about the custodial option specifically, questioning what stability or support a jailed parent could offer a child already in trouble.

Why it's only a trial balloon, for now

Richards was careful to frame all of this as options "on the table" rather than settled policy — no bill, no green paper, no date. That caution matters. Welfare conditionality is politically combustible in Britain even in its established forms; tying benefit eligibility to a third party's criminal conduct — a child's, not the claimant's own — would mark a meaningful widening of what the state considers fair grounds for withdrawing support. It is the kind of move a government tests in interview transcripts before it tests in a Commons vote, precisely to see how much resistance it draws before committing political capital.

On that count, the early signal is not encouraging for the more aggressive version of the plan: academic and charity voices moved quickly to say the underlying tool has a poor track record, not just a fairness problem. Whether the idea advances will likely turn on whether the Ministry of Justice ties it to hard evidence beyond deterrence rhetoric — and on how survivable it looks once opposition MPs and welfare groups get a formal proposal to attack rather than a minister's aside.

The next marker to watch is whether the expanded Parenting Orders plan reappears in a formal consultation or legislative vehicle; until then, this remains an idea being pressure-tested in public rather than a policy in motion.
