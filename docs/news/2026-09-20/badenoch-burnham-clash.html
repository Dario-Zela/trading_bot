Badenoch accuses Burnham of 'fiddling while Europe risks burning'

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government has stuck to a 2.7%-of-GDP defence spending path that the Conservatives say is too slow. — UK Government / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Badenoch accused PM Burnham of 'fiddling' on defence spending as Tories push a faster 3%-by-2030 target funded by housing benefit cuts he has rejected.

Two months into Andy Burnham's premiership, the Conservative opposition has found its opening line of attack, and it is not the economy in the narrow sense — it is the war next door.

Kemi Badenoch used a Commons clash this week to accuse the prime minister of "fiddling while Europe is at risk of burning," reaching for a line that fuses domestic austerity politics with the sharper anxieties now circulating in European capitals over Russian intent. "In France and Poland," she told MPs, "they are having serious conversations about what a war on this continent would actually mean. In Britain, we are still arguing about whether we can afford to be serious."

The dispute, in numbers

Stripped of the rhetoric, this is a fight about a fairly narrow gap. NATO allies have committed to reaching 3.5% of national income on defence by 2035. Burnham's government has stuck with the previous administration's trajectory of 2.7% of GDP by 2027-28. Badenoch wants to go further and faster — 3% by 2030 — and has proposed paying for it chiefly by trimming housing benefit, a move her party says would raise roughly £4 billion.

Downing Street's answer has been unambiguous. Burnham has said national security "cannot come at the expense of social security," and his aides have briefed that the housing benefit plan would tip an unquantified but significant number of households into homelessness. Conservative frontbenchers have countered, not entirely helpfully for their own case, that displaced tenants would simply "move somewhere cheaper."

It is a live enough worry inside the military establishment that retired officers have weighed in unprompted. Admiral Lord West and General Lord Dannatt, both former chiefs of the general staff or their naval equivalent, have warned publicly that Britain faces intensifying Russian cyber and hybrid threats and that the case for reaching 3.5% sooner rather than later is not merely political theatre. Dannatt went further, warning that Burnham and Defence Secretary John Healey were "playing fast and loose with the nation's security" — the kind of line opposition researchers dream of borrowing, and Badenoch duly has.

Why this is the fight Badenoch wants

For an opposition leader still finding her footing against a Labour government barely settled into office, defence spending is useful terrain: it lets Badenoch sound serious about a topic that polls well with voters worried about Ukraine fatigue and Russian sabre-rattling, while pinning Burnham — a politician whose reputation rests on Greater Manchester devolution and social policy rather than foreign affairs — on ground he has visited less often. It also lets her avoid relitigating the Conservatives' own fourteen years in office, when defence spending as a share of GDP fell for long stretches.

Burnham's camp, for its part, is betting that voters will not thank a government for cutting housing support to hit an accounting target five years out, particularly with gilt yields already elevated and the Treasury under pressure on multiple fronts. The instinct to frame the choice as "security versus security" — military against social — is a deliberate attempt to make Badenoch's arithmetic look as much like a threat to voters' own household budgets as a boost to the armed forces.

What to watch

None of this moves markets on its own — no budget line has actually changed, and the 2.7%-by-2027-28 trajectory remains government policy for now. But the argument previews where the pressure will land whenever the Treasury does next revisit medium-term spending plans, likely around the Autumn Statement. If Burnham comes under enough pressure to accelerate the defence timetable without new revenue-raisers, that is a gilt-market story, not just a Westminster one — worth filing away rather than trading today.
