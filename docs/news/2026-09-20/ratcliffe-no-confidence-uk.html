Ratcliffe says he has 'no confidence in the UK', warns of winter gas shortfall

Sir Jim Ratcliffe, Ineos founder and Manchester United co-owner, has criticised UK tax, immigration and energy policy for years. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ratcliffe told the BBC Britain risks a winter gas shortfall and is 'on the slide' — familiar grievances, no new numbers.

Sir Jim Ratcliffe has said this before, in one form or another, for years. This week he said it again, on the BBC, with a little more edge: he has "no confidence in the UK," the country is "on the slide," and — the one line with a shelf life — Britain risks running short of gas this winter if the weather turns hard.

"The UK is on the slide," the Ineos founder and Manchester United co-owner told the broadcaster's Big Boss interview series. "It's quite difficult to see how we arrest it." Asked about the coming months, he went further: "The risk you've got this winter is that we run out of gas... if we have a really sharp cold spell, then there is conceivably the possibility that we'll run out of gas and have to switch the industry off."

Familiar grievances, new microphone

Ratcliffe's complaints are not new — he has been making versions of this case since well before he moved his own tax residence to Monaco in 2020. The list is consistent: taxes he considers punitive, immigration policy he thinks politicians won't confront, and what he calls Britain's "green eye towards wealth," which he blames for pushing affluent residents abroad. "If you tax everybody to death, they're all going to leave," he said. "And that's what's happening."

On energy, the complaint has a sharper edge because it touches Ineos's own business. Ratcliffe described the failure to press ahead with further North Sea licensing — including the Rosebank and Jackdaw fields — as "insanity," arguing that a country sitting on domestic gas reserves has no business importing so much of what it burns.

What's missing

What the interview does not supply is anything a trader could act on. There is no barrel or therm count, no probability attached to the "really sharp cold spell," no citation of National Grid or Ofgem storage data, and no timeline beyond "this winter." Ofgem itself has separately flagged elevated risk to UK gas security this season, but that is a regulator's assessment with its own methodology — Ratcliffe's comment is a businessman's hunch, offered in the conditional tense. A government spokesperson pushed back along similar lines, saying Britain maintains a diversified energy mix and pointing to a rise in business investment over the past two years.

The capital-flight argument is the more interesting thread, if only because it recurs across enough departures — bankers, founders, now industrialists — that it has become a genuine talking point in the UK's tax debate. But Ratcliffe himself is a poor test case for it: he left six years ago, and his remarks describe a trend he watches from outside rather than a decision he is currently making.

What's next

Ofgem's winter outlook and National Grid's early cold-snap forecasts will be the actual data points worth watching over the coming weeks — not a billionaire's television interview. Closer to home, Ratcliffe has other fires: Manchester United's Muslim Supporters' Club has separately called for him to step back over unrelated "colonised" remarks, a row that has nothing to do with gas markets but will keep his name in the papers regardless.
