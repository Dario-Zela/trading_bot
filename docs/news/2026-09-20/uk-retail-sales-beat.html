UK retail sales beat forecasts with 0.5% August rise, but physical stores lag

Shoppers on Oxford Street, London — high street footfall fell even as overall retail sales beat forecasts — Matti Mattila / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
UK retail volumes beat forecasts with a 0.5% August rise, but the gain came from online and food sales while high street footfall fell 3.1% and fuel volumes dropped on higher prices.

UK retail sales rose 0.5% in August, the Office for National Statistics said on Friday, confounding a consensus among economists polled by Bloomberg for a 0.2% decline. It is the kind of number that reads as unambiguous good news until you look at where it came from.

The rebound followed a 0.5% fall in July and left volumes 2.4% higher than a year earlier — the strongest annual pace since the reading before last. Non-store retailers, mostly meaning online sellers, bounced back hardest after a weak July that ONS attributed to promotions being pulled forward into June. Food stores also had a good run, with supermarkets benefiting from a late-summer heatwave that pushed up sales of beer, wine and picnic food. Alcohol and beverage retailers had their best three months since spring, helped along by warm weather and the men's football World Cup.

Fuel sales pulled the other way. Automotive fuel volumes fell as pump prices climbed, with retailers reporting that drivers were making fewer journeys and filling tanks only partially rather than cutting back on spending outright — a sign the fuel drag is a price story, not solely a demand one.

Online up, high street down

Strip out the headline number and the composition looks less like a consumer revival and more like a continuation of a familiar split. Online sales accounted for 28.8% of total retail spending in August, up from 28.4% in July, according to PwC UK's Jacqueline Windsor, who noted that "the continuing decline in high street footfall meant that online retailers benefited the most." Separate figures from the British Retail Consortium bear this out: total UK footfall was down 1.7% year-on-year in August, an improvement on July's 2.1% drop, but high street footfall specifically was down 3.1%, versus a 1.0% rise for out-of-town retail parks.

Helen Dickinson, the BRC's chief executive, used the data to press the government rather than celebrate it. "Retailers don't need warm words, they need lower costs," she said, calling on Chancellor Healey to use his first Budget, weeks away, to act on business rates and energy costs. Deloitte's Oliver Vernon-Harcourt struck a more upbeat note, saying sales "performed better than expected in August as improving consumer confidence and the last of the summer heatwaves encouraged an uptick in spending across almost all categories." PwC's Windsor put the ex-fuel growth figure at 0.6% for the month — even stronger than the headline — while cautioning that the underlying footfall trend had not changed direction.

A complication for the Bank

The timing matters. The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee held its base rate at 3.75% this month on a 6–3 split, with Governor Andrew Bailey warning inflation could top 4% as energy prices — driven higher by the renewed conflict involving Iran — feed through the system. A retail sales beat, arriving days later, cuts against the case for an imminent cut. Barclays has pencilled in a 25 basis point hike for November rather than easing, and JPMorgan has pushed its own hike call into November and February, reversing what had been an expectation of loosening.

What the MPC will have to weigh is whether 0.5% volume growth represents households spending more, or the same households paying more for less — and spending it increasingly online rather than in shops that employ people on the high street. The value data offers a clue: online spending values rose 2.5% on the month and 10.1% year-on-year, comfortably outstripping the volume growth, which points to inflation and shifting channel mix doing more work than a genuine surge in real consumption.

The next test comes with September's retail figures in mid-October, alongside the BRC's monthly footfall tracker, and then the Budget itself, where the retail sector's push on business rates and energy costs will meet a chancellor weighing the exchequer's own inflation-driven arithmetic.
