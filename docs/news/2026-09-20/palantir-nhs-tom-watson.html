Tom Watson joins Palantir as ministers weigh break clause on £330m NHS contract

Tom Watson, Baron Watson of Wyre Forest, the former Labour deputy leader now serving as Palantir's UK senior vice president. — Roger Harris / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Palantir hired former Labour deputy leader Tom Watson as UK senior VP just as ministers weigh scrapping its £330m NHS data contract.

Tom Watson spent the best part of two decades in the House of Commons chasing corporate power on behalf of ordinary voters — most famously helping bring down the Murdoch press over phone hacking. This week he joined one. Palantir Technologies, the American data-analytics firm co-founded by Peter Thiel, announced on September 18 that the former Labour deputy leader is its new UK senior vice president, working alongside Louis Mosley, who runs the company's UK and European business.

The timing is awkward, and Palantir knows it. Watson takes up the post just as the government decides whether to pull a break clause on Palantir's £330 million contract to run the NHS Federated Data Platform — the software meant to let hospitals and GPs share patient data across England. The health secretary is reviewing the deal "line by line," according to technology minister Liz Kendall, with the earliest exit point falling in the first quarter of 2027.

From Labour whip to Palantir payroll

Watson's relationship with Palantir did not start this month. He has advised the company since 2024 and sits on its advisory board; he also chairs the advisory council of Lodestone Communications, the lobbying firm Palantir retained that same year. What changes now is the paycheck's size and formality: he becomes a full-time, salaried Palantir executive rather than an outside adviser, with a brief centred on aligning the company's public-sector contracts with the government's British-employment priorities.

To take the job, Watson — who became a life peer as Lord Watson of Wyre Forest in December 2022 after sixteen years as MP for West Bromwich East and four as Labour's deputy leader — went on leave of absence from the House of Lords in March 2026 and has handed back his parliamentary pass. That matters procedurally: the Lords Code of Conduct bars members from taking payment for parliamentary advice or services, and requires them to register any outside interest a reasonable person might think could sway their conduct. Watson can draw a Palantir salary; what he cannot do, on paper, is trade directly on his seat while doing so. The Register of Lords' Interests is where the first hard evidence of how cleanly that line is drawn will eventually surface.

A contract already under fire before Watson arrived

The Federated Data Platform predates Watson's hire by three years — Palantir won it in November 2023 under the previous Conservative government — but it has been a target for critics since long before he signed on. Parliament's Science, Innovation and Technology Committee published a report calling Palantir's expanding footprint in the NHS "an unacceptable point of weakness," pointing to data-security worries and to the company's separate work for US defence and immigration-enforcement agencies. The contract's 3+2+1+1 structure gives the government its first chance to walk away when the initial three-year term lapses in early 2027, and the committee urged ministers to take it.

London's mayor, Sadiq Khan, has already shown what that scepticism looks like in practice: he blocked a separate £50 million Palantir contract with the Metropolitan Police. Kendall's public comments — "the current health secretary is reviewing every single aspect of that contract to make sure we get the right deal for Britain" — suggest the FDP review is a genuine live process, not a formality on the way to renewal.

Revolving door, or a company hiring its own defender?

The criticism arrived almost as fast as the announcement. Green Party MP Sian Berry called the appointment a "corrosive revolving door" between politics and corporate lobbying, and accused Palantir of hiring Watson "to lobby his friends in the Labour government." She added it was a "disgrace" that he still holds the Labour whip in the Lords. An investigation by the outlet the Nerve in April 2026 had already flagged that four members of the House of Lords, Watson among them, had advised Palantir in some capacity — evidence, to Berry and others, of a company methodically building relationships inside the institutions that award its contracts.

Watson has not pretended the criticism is baseless, exactly, but he has drawn a distinction. Palantir, he said, is "the most interesting company on the planet, doing work of real consequence," while conceding that "some criticism is propaganda. Some raises legitimate questions." It's a carefully hedged line, and it points to the actual gap in this story: there is, so far, no public evidence that Watson has lobbied ministers on the NHS contract specifically, or that his hire was designed to influence that review rather than Palantir's broader UK employment commitments, which is the remit the company has publicly assigned him. The optics of a former deputy Labour leader joining a company mid-review of its largest UK contract are bad regardless of what he actually does in the job — but bad optics and demonstrated influence are not the same thing, and nobody has yet shown the latter.

What to watch

Two things will tell the real story here. The first is procedural: whether Watson's entry in the Register of Lords' Interests, once filed, discloses anything beyond the advisory relationship already known. The second is substantive: what the health secretary decides on the Federated Data Platform break clause when the initial term lapses in early 2027. If ministers walk away from Palantir despite Watson's presence on its payroll, the revolving-door charge loses most of its force. If they don't, the coincidence of timing will keep inviting the question of whether it ever was one.
