NATO backs US-Denmark Greenland security deal as Trump claims 'permanent control'

Greenland's Premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen and Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen brief media at the Ministry of State in Copenhagen — Christian Ursilva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The US, Denmark and Greenland plan to sign a security pact at UNGA giving Washington basing and veto rights in Greenland, but Trump's 'permanent control' claim awaits ratification votes in Copenhagen and a fractious Nuuk parliament.

The United States, Denmark and Greenland are set to sign a security pact on the sidelines of the United Nations General Assembly in New York this week — the culmination of eight months in which President Donald Trump moved from threatening to seize the Arctic island by force to negotiating access rights to it instead. Whether the agreement amounts to the sweeping victory Trump has claimed, or something considerably narrower, depends on two votes still to come, in Copenhagen and in Nuuk.

"I am pleased to announce that the United States of America has entered into an Agreement with the Kingdom of Denmark, and Greenland, that gives the United States permanent control over security, and all other needs, in Greenland, completely addressing ALL of our many U.S. concerns," Trump wrote when he announced the deal on 18 September. Secretary of State Marco Rubio went further, calling it a "huge win" that would ensure Greenland "will forever be part of the strategic defense area of North America" and exclude "any adversary" from the surrounding region.

The Danish and Greenlandic governments describe the same document rather differently.

What the pact actually does

According to officials briefed on the text, the agreement grants the US permanent basing, access and overflight rights in Greenland, allowing it to expand its existing military footprint around Pituffik Space Base — the former Thule Air Base in the island's northwest — and potentially build new installations. It bans any non-NATO country from stationing troops or bases there, and creates a screening mechanism intended to block Russian and Chinese investment in sensitive Greenlandic assets, from mining to ports.

What it does not do, both governments insist, is transfer sovereignty. "The agreement recognises Greenland's interests," said Greenland's premier, Jens-Frederik Nielsen, while Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said it "strengthens our common security" while preserving the kingdom's "sovereignty and territorial integrity." Greenland's foreign minister, Múte B. Egede, added that the pact recognises "Greenland's right to self-determination" and treats Greenlanders' status "in accordance with international law, including international human rights law."

NATO, whose members spent much of the year uneasy about a founding ally publicly threatening to annex another member's territory, welcomed the outcome. "The Arctic and the North Atlantic are essential for our collective security, and this agreement will help advance security, stability and cooperation in this vital region," alliance spokesperson Allison Hart said, adding that NATO "will continue to do its part to strengthen security in the Arctic."

How permanent is 'permanent'

The gap between Trump's framing and the deal's substance has drawn scrutiny. Fact-checkers note that under NATO's Article 5 and a 1951 US-Denmark defense agreement, Washington already had a legal obligation to defend Greenland and a standing military presence there — meaning the new pact may formalise and expand access rather than create it from nothing. Mikkel Olesen, a researcher at the Danish Institute for International Studies, pointed out that barring Russian or Chinese bases costs Denmark little, since neither country was seriously courting a foothold there anyway.

Richard Weitz of the NATO Defense College offered a blunter summary: Trump "backed down and settled for less" than outright ownership, but still "got pretty much everything he wanted, except formal annexation." That reading fits a pattern that has held since a January poll found 85% of Greenland's roughly 57,000 residents opposed joining the United States — a number that made annexation talk politically untenable for Washington's interlocutors even as it kept the underlying US interest, unfettered military access to a rapidly thawing Arctic, very much alive.

Two parliaments, two very different politics

The signing in New York is a beginning, not an ending. The text still needs ratification by Denmark's Folketing and by Greenland's Inatsisartut, the 31-seat assembly in Nuuk — and the arithmetic in the latter is less comfortable than the diplomatic language suggests. Nielsen's Demokraatit holds 10 seats after last year's election, but Naleraq, an unambiguously pro-independence party that has accused successive governments of moving too slowly toward statehood, holds 8; Inuit Ataqatigiit has 7 and Siumut 4. Any perception that the agreement locks Greenland into permanent American tutelage in exchange for modest guarantees could hand Naleraq and like-minded independence advocates a rallying point, even if the government whips the vote through.

In Copenhagen, the arithmetic is more favourable to Frederiksen, but the politics carry their own risk: a Danish government that appears to have traded away Arctic leverage to end a months-long crisis with its most important ally will face questions about what, precisely, it got in return beyond the removal of an immediate threat.

The episode has also left a mark on how European capitals think about Washington. A NATO ally spent the better part of a year publicly rebuffing threats of annexation from another member state before the dispute cooled into a negotiated text — a sequence with few, if any, postwar precedents inside the alliance, and one Nordic and Baltic states are unlikely to forget the next time Arctic security is on the agenda.

What comes next

The signing ceremony is expected on the margins of UNGA's High-Level Week, which runs through 28 September, but the more consequential dates come after: the Folketing and Inatsisartut votes that will determine whether the pact takes legal effect. Watch for how quickly Nuuk schedules its debate, and whether Naleraq and Inuit Ataqatigiit attempt to attach conditions — on independence timelines, on economic compensation, or on further limits on US basing — that Washington has not yet priced in. Until both parliaments act, "permanent control" remains, for now, a description Trump has given to an agreement neither Copenhagen nor Nuuk has signed off on.
