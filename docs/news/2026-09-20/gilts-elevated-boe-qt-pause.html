UK gilt yields hold near multi-year highs as BoE pauses long-dated QT sales

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which this month paused sales of long-dated gilts under its QT programme. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields hold near 5.29% and 30-year near 5.75% even after the BoE paused long-dated gilt sales to ease pressure on borrowing costs.

UK government borrowing costs are stuck near their highest levels since the financial crisis, and a dovish pivot from the Bank of England has only partly loosened their grip. The 10-year gilt yield sat near 5.29% on Friday and the 30-year near 5.75% — both a shade below the 19-year highs hit earlier in the month, but still elevated enough to keep the Treasury's debt-servicing bill climbing.

The proximate cause was a lacklustre auction. On September 10, the Debt Management Office sold five-year gilts at a yield of 4.786%, up sharply from 4.613% at the prior sale — a jump that traders read as a sign institutional buyers, particularly pension funds and insurers, are demanding more compensation to hold UK paper. The 10-year yield touched 5.295% that same day, the highest since August 2007. Thirty-year yields ran as high as 5.86%.

The BoE tries to calm things down

A week later, the Bank of England moved to take some of the sting out. Alongside holding Bank Rate at 3.75% on September 17 — a decision that split the Monetary Policy Committee 6-3 — the Bank rewrote its quantitative tightening programme in a way clearly designed to relieve pressure on the long end of the curve, where the market damage had been worst.

Under the new framework, the BoE will pause all gilt sales for six months while it consults with the Treasury on selling bonds directly to the DMO at market prices rather than through its own auctions. More strikingly, it effectively abandoned sales of long-dated debt altogether: £120 billion of gilts maturing in 2049 and beyond will be permanently retained to back banknotes in circulation, and £222 billion maturing by 2034 will simply be held to maturity rather than sold down. Only £146 billion maturing between 2035 and 2049 will be actively sold, at a pace of roughly £20 billion a year — a fraction of the near-£70 billion annual unwind of the past twelve months. "Today we provided clarity over the future of our quantitative tightening policy," Governor Andrew Bailey said.

The market reaction was immediate and, for one session, emphatic: 30-year yields posted their largest one-day drop since April, falling from 5.86% to around 5.75%, and 10-year yields eased roughly 9 basis points. Matthew Amis at Aberdeen Investments called the shift "gilt positive, in particular for long-end maturities."

The relief has proven partial

What's notable is how little of that relief has stuck. Four days on, 10-year yields have crept back to around 5.29% and 30-year yields remain camped at 5.75% — essentially back where they were before the announcement, just below the month's peak rather than meaningfully off it. A central bank move explicitly engineered to support gilt prices bought, at best, a pause in the climb rather than a reversal.

That matters because the BoE's options for propping up the long end were never really about growth or inflation management — QT policy is a balance-sheet tool, not an interest-rate one — and investors appear to be treating this as exactly that: a technical adjustment to bond supply, not a signal that underlying doubts about the UK's fiscal trajectory have eased. Bailey himself warned the same week that inflation could still top 4%, hardly a backdrop that argues for lower yields on its own. With the MPC split three ways on the rate decision, the market is left pricing both persistent rate pressure and continued fiscal risk premium into gilts — a combination the Bank's supply-side fix was never going to fully offset.

What's next

The DMO runs a pilot switch auction on September 24, exchanging long-dated bonds for shorter maturities — the first real test of whether the new framework changes auction dynamics rather than just central bank optics. Autumn Budget signals from the Treasury, expected in the coming weeks, will matter more: if fiscal plans fail to reassure gilt buyers on borrowing levels, the BoE's QT rewrite will have bought time, not resolution.
