Badenoch takes government to court over Wandsworth's 94% council tax hike

Kemi Badenoch, who has instructed lawyers to challenge the government's council funding formula over Wandsworth's proposed tax rise. — Roger Harris / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Badenoch is instructing lawyers to fight a Whitehall funding formula she says forced Tory flagship Wandsworth into a 94% council tax rise.

Kemi Badenoch has instructed lawyers to challenge the government's local authority funding formula, opening a legal front in a fight over why one of the Conservative Party's most prized local strongholds is being asked to raise council tax by 94 percent.

Wandsworth, the low-tax south-west London borough Conservatives have run — with a brief interruption — for nearly half a century, says a Whitehall overhaul of how funding is allocated between councils will strip it of £84 million a year by 2029-30, a cut of roughly 40 percent to its settlement. To close the resulting gap, projected to reach £137 million by 2028-29, the council has set out a Band D council tax rise from just over £1,020 to close to £1,978 — an increase of £958 on a typical bill.

"I think it is outrageous that Wandsworth Council is having to put up council tax," Badenoch said on BBC Newsnight, adding that the borough was "not doing it because they want to." She accused ministers of using the new formula to move resources from "areas that don't vote Labour" to places that do — a charge the government rejects. A spokesperson for the Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government said the changes were "fixing an outdated funding system so funding goes where need is greatest," pointing to more than £78 billion made available for council finances and transitional protection for authorities most affected.

A fight over Whitehall's discretion

The legal question is less about whether ministers can redesign the funding formula — they plainly can — than about whether the process by which they did so is challengeable in court. Wandsworth has already lodged a formal appeal under the Sustainable Communities Act, which obliges ministers to respond to a council's objection that a government policy damages residents' well-being, though it stops short of giving courts power to overrule the decision. Badenoch's move to bring in lawyers as party leader, rather than leaving the fight to the council alone, signals an attempt to escalate that into a fuller judicial review of the methodology behind the cuts. Wandsworth argues that for every £1 raised from its taxpayers, only 4p currently comes back in government funding, and that boroughs which kept council tax low through years of restraint are effectively being penalised for it. The government's counter is that six authorities, Wandsworth among them, have had exceptionally low bills for so long that convergence was overdue; ministers have also waived the usual referendum threshold for Wandsworth and five other councils, including Westminster and Kensington and Chelsea, meaning the rise would not need voter approval.

A final decision on the funding formula, and any ruling on a legal challenge, is expected early next year, around the time councils must set their 2027-28 budgets. Wandsworth's Labour opposition leader, Simon Hogg, has called the proposed rise "outrageous" in its own right, an unusual point of cross-party agreement that has done nothing to narrow the gap between the two sides on who is to blame.
