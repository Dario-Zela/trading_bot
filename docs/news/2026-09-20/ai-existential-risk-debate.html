King Charles warns of AI 'existential danger' as AI workers split on the risk

King Charles III, who told AI executives at a Scotland summit that the technology's dangers must be brought under control 'before it is all too late.' — The White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
King Charles told AI executives at a Scotland summit the technology poses an 'existential danger' in the wrong hands, days after the BBC reported many AI workers privately doubt that framing.

King Charles gave the tech industry twenty minutes of his time on Thursday, and used most of it to ask whether anyone in the room could actually stop artificial intelligence going wrong.

The King hosted senior figures from Nvidia, Google DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic at Dumfries House in Ayrshire for a summit billed as a discussion of AI's benefits to society and the natural world. What he delivered was closer to a warning. "There seems urgency in adequately considering the existential dangers of such technologies falling into the wrong hands, and being used in potentially catastrophic ways," he told the gathering, adding: "Surely, then, we need sufficient means of control before it is all too late?" The development of AI, he said, was "intriguing and deeply concerning in equal measure."

It was a short appearance — the King stayed roughly twenty minutes before leaving the room to Jensen Huang of Nvidia, Demis Hassabis of DeepMind, OpenAI finance chief Sarah Friar, Anthropic's Tino Cuéllar, UK AI minister Kanishka Narayan and a Vatican adviser, Paolo Benanti, to hash out whatever shared principles they could agree on. But a monarch's words carry further than a minister's. Royal interventions in policy are rare and deliberately so; when they happen, they tend to be read as a signal that an issue has moved from niche to national concern. For a UK government still working out how hard to regulate frontier AI models, that is not a neutral fact.

The industry's own doubts

The timing is awkward for AI labs for a different reason. Days before the summit, the BBC reported on private text exchanges and conversations showing that engineers, researchers and product managers inside the world's leading AI companies are far less convinced than their chief executives that the technology poses a civilisation-ending threat. One former OpenAI employee told the broadcaster that existential-risk claims are "always vague," resting on large logical leaps or far-fetched hypotheticals rather than anything demonstrable in current systems. Colin Fraser, a data scientist at Meta, put it more bluntly on social media, writing that large language models simply "don't have that dog in them" — they lack any mechanism, in his view, for the kind of autonomous, world-ending behaviour their own bosses sometimes describe.

That gap matters because the public case for self-regulation — trust the labs, they understand the risk best — depends on the labs speaking with one voice. Sam Altman and Dario Amodei have both warned publicly that AI could end humanity if mismanaged; large numbers of the people building their products evidently don't buy it, or at least think the framing is overblown next to nearer-term problems like model jailbreaking or military automation. Regulators in London and Brussels, weighing whether to rely on industry-led safety commitments or write firmer rules, now have a much harder time treating the industry as a single credible witness.

Where the two threads meet

Put the King's remarks next to the BBC's reporting and the shape of the story becomes clearer: a head of state has just lent institutional weight to exactly the kind of catastrophic-risk framing that a chunk of the AI workforce privately doubts. That doesn't resolve anything — it sharpens the disagreement. If regulators decide, as some in Whitehall and Brussels are inclined to, that they cannot wait for the industry to reach internal consensus before acting, this week gave them one more prominent voice — inconveniently, an apolitical one — to point to.

The near-term catalyst is the follow-through from the Dumfries House meeting: whether Nvidia, DeepMind, OpenAI and Anthropic can actually agree the "shared principles" the summit was meant to produce, and whether UK ministers treat that agreement as sufficient or press ahead with statutory obligations regardless. Given the EU is already running its first high-risk compliance audits under the AI Act, the UK's choice on that question will say a lot about whether it intends to lead on AI safety policy or follow Brussels' lead.
