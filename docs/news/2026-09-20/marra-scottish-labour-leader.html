Michael Marra elected Scottish Labour leader, backs Rosebank and Jackdaw oil fields

Michael Marra, elected leader of Scottish Labour on 19 September 2026 after the party's worst-ever Holyrood result. — Scottish Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
New Scottish Labour leader Michael Marra has already broken ranks to back the stalled Rosebank and Jackdaw oil and gas fields, pressuring Westminster's energy decision.

Scottish Labour has a new leader, and he introduced himself to the country by breaking with a decade of his party's caution on North Sea oil.

Michael Marra, the 47-year-old MSP for North East Scotland, was elected leader on Saturday, beating fellow MSP Joe Fagan by 4,266 votes to 2,485 in a ballot of party members. He takes over from Anas Sarwar, who quit in July to become a trade minister in Andy Burnham's government, and inherits a party at its lowest ebb since devolution — Scottish Labour won just 17 seats at May's Holyrood election, its worst result ever, leaving it level with Reform UK and only narrowly ahead of the Scottish Greens and Conservatives.

Marra used his first remarks as leader to stake out a position his predecessors mostly hedged on: he wants the Rosebank and Jackdaw oil and gas fields approved. "I want Rosebank and Jackdaw to be approved," he said, describing the decision as "the biggest issue in my region in terms of people's voting intention" and calling for "a more rational policy position that delivers for the Scottish economy and Scottish jobs."

Why the fields are still in limbo

Rosebank, west of Shetland and majority-owned by Norway's Equinor alongside Shell, and Jackdaw, a Shell gas field east of Aberdeen, were both approved under the last Conservative government and are substantially built. But the Court of Session ruled in January 2025 that the original consents were unlawful because regulators failed to account for the emissions produced when the oil and gas is eventually burned, not just extracted. Fresh environmental assessments went out for public consultation in August, and the offshore regulator OPRED — acting with the energy secretary's sign-off — is now expected to rule on both fields within months.

That decision sits with the UK government, not Holyrood, which is precisely why Marra's intervention carries some weight beyond party positioning. Scottish Labour holds 37 Westminster seats and has spent months lobbying London for a more permissive line on North Sea licensing; a new leader endorsing the fields on his first weekend removes any ambiguity about where the party's Scottish wing stands, even as UK ministers weigh the politics of approving fossil fuel projects against their own climate targets.

The more immediate test for Marra is arithmetic, not energy policy. A 17-seat group tied with Reform UK for second place at Holyrood has little means of forcing anything through the Scottish Parliament, and the SNP remains in government. What Marra has bought himself, for now, is a clear dividing line — rational energy pragmatism versus what he calls a party that voters "know what we are against" without knowing what it is "for." Whether that line survives contact with a UK Labour government still not committed on Rosebank is the more relevant question for markets watching North Sea output.
