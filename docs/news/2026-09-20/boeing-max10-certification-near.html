Boeing says 737 MAX 10 certification is close as 777X delays persist

A Boeing 737 MAX 10 at its 2019 rollout event; the type is now in the final documentation phase of FAA certification. — lamblukas / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Ortberg says MAX 10 certification is imminent, but 777X slips further and China's touted 200-jet order still isn't a signed deal.

Boeing chief executive Kelly Ortberg told investors this week that the FAA's amended type certificate for the 737 MAX 10 — the largest and last-to-arrive member of the MAX family — is coming "very soon." Flight testing wrapped in July. What remains, Ortberg said, is paperwork. "All the real work is done now," he told the Morgan Stanley Laguna Conference on September 16.

That single sentence carries real weight for Boeing's order book. The MAX 10 now accounts for roughly 30% of the company's 737 backlog — more than 1,400 aircraft — and airlines from Lufthansa Group, which ordered 20 in a deal disclosed this year, to United have been waiting years for the type to clear regulators. Certification would let Boeing start converting that backlog into deliveries and cash, beginning with production-line qualification aircraft built at Everett, with customer deliveries targeted for 2027.

The other widebody in the room

Ortberg's optimism on the narrowbody sat next to a more familiar refrain on the wide-body side. The 777X — already seven years behind its original 2020 service date — has slipped again. FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford has indicated the agency won't get to 777X certification until after it clears the 737-7 and MAX 10, and Boeing now says ETOPS testing, which governs how far twin-engine jets can fly from a diversion airport, has run into an engine mid-seal issue on the GE9X. Ortberg said GE is working the problem and some testing could stretch into 2027, though Boeing still targets first deliveries that year — a promise launch customer Lufthansa has already had to recalibrate around once.

Boeing has been careful to frame the 777X slip as procedural rather than technical, and there's no evidence yet to contradict that. But procedural delays still cost money: airlines including Emirates have pushed back their own widebody fleet planning to match, and every quarter of delay pushes out revenue recognition on a jet that was supposed to be flying commercially six years ago.

China's order flow still runs thin

The more structural signal came in the same investor session, when Ortberg walked back expectations he had helped set months earlier. In May, President Trump announced China would order 200 Boeing jets following his summit with Xi Jinping — the country's first major Boeing purchase in nearly a decade, after Beijing spent years favoring Airbus and, during the tariff standoff in April 2025, paused Boeing deliveries outright. Ortberg had called the commitment a breakthrough at the time.

This month he tempered it. "We didn't get an order for 200," he told the Laguna conference, clarifying that Chinese officials had signaled intent to proceed with purchases rather than signed a firm order, with individual airlines expected to announce their own deals incrementally. Reports of a further tranche of 300 to 500 aircraft — potentially timed to Trump's planned state visit to Beijing — remain speculative. Meanwhile Airbus has kept winning business on the ground: Chinese carriers ordered 148 A320-family jets earlier this year alone, extending an edge built up over the years Boeing spent locked out of the market.

What it adds up to

Certification is a near-term catalyst that traders can price on a calendar; China demand is a structural question that a single FAA approval does nothing to answer. A MAX 10 certificate would unclog a backlog and start cash flowing on jets airlines have waited years for. It would not change the fact that Boeing's largest growth market has yet to convert political goodwill into firm paper, or that its flagship widebody remains a program defined, for the seventh straight year, by dates that move. The next marker is the FAA's actual sign-off on the MAX 10, expected within weeks; the one that matters more is whether China's "200" becomes a number with a contract attached to it.
