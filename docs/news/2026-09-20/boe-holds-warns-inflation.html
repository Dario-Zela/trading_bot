BoE holds at 3.75% but Bailey warns inflation could top 4% as MPC splits 6-3

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The BoE held rates at 3.75% on a 6-3 split, with Bailey warning Middle East-driven energy prices could push inflation above 4% and force a hike.

The Bank of England held its benchmark interest rate at 3.75% on Wednesday, but the vote that got it there — a 6-3 split, the widest dissent on the Monetary Policy Committee since the current tightening debate began — told a more unsettled story than the headline number suggested. Three of the Bank's nine rate-setters wanted to raise borrowing costs immediately, to 4%. The other six held the line, for now.

Governor Andrew Bailey used the press conference that followed to warn that inflation, already running at 3.1% in August, could climb above 4% in the first quarter of 2027 — roughly double the Bank's target — if the conflict in the Middle East keeps pushing energy prices higher. It was a notably blunt caveat to attach to a decision to sit still, and it left little doubt that the MPC's next move, whenever it comes, is more likely to be up than down.

A split that keeps widening

The dissenters — Catherine Mann, Megan Greene and Huw Pill — argued that with inflation already more than a point and a half above target and energy costs showing no sign of easing, waiting longer risked letting price expectations drift. It is a bloc that has grown steadily more crowded over the year: the same three-way hawkish dissent first appeared in July, up from two dissenters in June and just one in April. Each meeting has shaved a little more consensus off the majority's case for patience.

That majority — Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Clare Lombardelli, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor — held that financial conditions were already doing enough of the tightening work, and that moving now, on the back of an external oil shock rather than domestic demand, could prove premature. The minutes framed it as a judgment call about how much of the current inflation is temporary pass-through from energy markets versus a more durable shift in the outlook. Six votes to three suggests that judgment call is no longer comfortable.

"If the conflict in the Middle East persists for an extended period, as appears to be the case, and the risk of second-round effects emerging increases, it is likely that policy may have to tighten," Bailey said.

Energy is doing the damage

The Bank's own numbers make the source of the pressure explicit. Brent crude has traded near $106 a barrel and UK wholesale gas around 207p a therm since the conflict escalated, both well above pre-war levels and, in the MPC's words, "more volatile and higher than pre-conflict." The committee's minutes call the Middle East situation "the dominant source of uncertainty for the inflation outlook" — an unusually direct acknowledgment that a geopolitical event now sits at the centre of UK monetary policy, rather than at its margins.

Bailey was careful to note that the pass-through so far has been contained. "So far that feed-through has been quite subdued," he told reporters after the decision. "But it is early days." That qualifier is doing a lot of work: it is the difference between an energy spike that fades from the inflation figures within a couple of quarters and one that seeps into wages, services prices and inflation expectations — the "second-round effects" his written statement flagged as the trigger for tightening.

What the hold buys, and what it costs

Alongside the rate decision, the Bank paused its programme of active long-dated gilt sales for six months, a quieter but consequential move that sent 30-year yields — sitting near multi-decade highs around 5.90% — lower, and 10-year yields to roughly 5.34%. It is the clearest sign yet that the Bank is trying to manage two problems at once: keeping a lid on inflation expectations with rate policy while easing pressure on a gilt market that has been trading close to levels not seen in a generation.

Markets did not treat the hold as dovish. Sterling weakened against both the dollar and the euro in the hours after the announcement — an odd reaction to a hawkish-sounding statement, but one that reflects how thoroughly a near-term cut had been priced in before Wednesday. Traders had been positioning for the Bank to start easing by early 2027; Bailey's inflation warning and the three-way dissent make that timeline look considerably less certain than it did a month ago.

The next test comes with the October CPI print and the MPC's November meeting, where markets will be watching not just the vote count but whether any of the six-strong majority starts to shift. A 6-3 split that becomes 5-4, or worse, would tell its own story — one in which the Bank of England's "wait and see" on energy-driven inflation runs out of road before the Middle East does.
