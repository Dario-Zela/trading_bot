Bank of Japan hikes to 1.25% in fastest tightening since 1990, yen slides on split vote

The Bank of Japan's headquarters in Nihonbashi, Tokyo, where the board voted 7-2 to raise rates to 1.25%. — Fg2 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The BoJ hiked to 1.25% — its fastest pace since 1990 — but two dissents and no forward guidance sent the yen lower, not higher.

Japan's central bank raised its benchmark interest rate to 1.25% on Friday, the fastest pace of policy tightening it has delivered since 1990 — and the currency it was defending fell anyway.

The Bank of Japan's board voted 7-2 to lift the short-term rate by 25 basis points, pushing borrowing costs to their highest level since April 1995. It was the third hike since the bank began normalizing policy in March 2024, but the gap since the last one — three months, from June to September — was the shortest interval between increases in more than three decades. Markets had priced the move as close to certain. What they had not priced was what came with it: two dissenting votes and a governor who, once again, declined to commit to anything beyond the next data print.

In one sentence: The BoJ hiked to 1.25% at its fastest pace since 1990, but two dissents and a falling yen suggest traders don't believe the tightening cycle will hold.

A hike that satisfied no one

Governor Kazuo Ueda framed the decision as a response to inflation that has run persistently above target even as headline momentum cools. Core CPI, which excludes fresh food, came in at 1.7% in August, down from 1.8% in July — a figure that sits below the BoJ's 2% goal, not above it, and gave the two dissenting board members their opening.

Toichiro Asada and Ayano Sato voted against the increase, with Asada arguing that the pace of core inflation remained insufficient to justify tightening and that the board should hold. Both were appointed to the board by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, a longtime advocate of reflationary policy who took office last October. Their dissent matters less for the vote itself — 7-2 is still a comfortable majority — than for what it signals about the board's composition heading into 2027, when two of the more hawkish members are due to rotate off. Markets read the math quickly: replace hawks with Takaichi appointees, and the case for a fourth hike gets harder to build.

Asked at the post-meeting press conference about the timing of the next move, Ueda offered no anchor. "That depends on how price conditions develop," he said. "There could be various possibilities. We shouldn't rule anything out." It was the kind of answer designed to preserve optionality — and the kind that gives currency traders nothing to trade on.

The yen moves the wrong way

In textbook terms, a rate hike should support a currency: higher yields draw capital, tightening signals confidence, the exchange rate firms. The yen did the opposite. It slid past 157 to the dollar in the hours after the announcement, roughly a yen weaker than its level heading into the decision — the kind of inversion that gets circled in every trading desk's morning note.

The explanation lies less in the hike itself than in everything around it. Two dissents plus a governor unwilling to sketch a path for future moves told the market that this increase was closer to a ceiling than a floor. Japan's 10-year government bond yield, which had climbed toward 3% — its highest since 1996 — in the run-up to the meeting on hawkish positioning, held roughly steady rather than extending the move, a sign that duration traders, too, read the outcome as underwhelming rather than decisive. The Nikkei, notably, rose about 1.5% on the day, a reaction more consistent with relief that policy would stay accommodative than with alarm at tightening credit conditions.

The yen's weakness carries a political edge as well. US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has pressed Tokyo for months to move more aggressively against what Washington views as an undervalued currency, reportedly raising the issue directly with Ueda at the G20 earlier this month. A hike that fails to hold the currency line undercuts that pressure campaign and leaves Bessent's argument — that the BoJ has the tools to arrest yen weakness if it chooses to use them — looking harder to sustain.

A tightening cycle traders don't fully believe

What makes this hike distinctive isn't the size — 25 basis points is the standard increment the BoJ has used at every step since 2024 — but the frequency. Moving three months after the last increase, rather than the roughly six-month cadence the bank had kept since starting normalization, was meant to project momentum. Instead, the reaction in currency markets reads as skepticism that the momentum will continue, given the composition of the board and the governor's refusal to pre-commit.

That skepticism has a direct read-through for yen carry trades, which depend on the gap between Japanese and foreign borrowing costs staying wide enough to fund positions elsewhere. A BoJ perceived as approaching the end of its tightening cycle — rather than mid-cycle — makes unwinding those trades less urgent, which in turn removes one of the forces that would otherwise support the yen. Options markets and cross-currency desks will be parsing every subsequent BoJ speech for signs of which reading is correct.

What comes next

The BoJ's next scheduled policy meeting falls in late October, but the more immediate catalyst is Japan's September CPI release, due in the coming weeks, which will show whether core inflation is reaccelerating toward 2% or drifting further below it — the exact data point Asada cited in his dissent. A print that firms up the inflation case would strengthen Ueda's hand against the board's dovish wing; a soft one would validate the dissenters and make a near-term fourth hike far less likely. Until then, the yen's move below 157 stands as the market's verdict: a rate hike delivered, but a tightening cycle not yet trusted.
