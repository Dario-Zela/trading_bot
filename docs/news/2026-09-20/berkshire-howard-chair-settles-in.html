Berkshire's Howard Buffett settles into the chair as Abel runs the business

Warren Buffett, who led Berkshire Hathaway since 1965, becomes chairman emeritus as his son Howard takes the chairman's seat. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Warren Buffett, 96, ended 61 years as Berkshire's chairman, becoming chairman emeritus as son Howard Buffett takes the board while Greg Abel runs the company as CEO.

Warren Buffett has spent 61 years being the answer to the question of who runs Berkshire Hathaway. As of Friday, he no longer is. The 96-year-old investor stepped down as chairman, taking the title of chairman emeritus, and handed the board seat to his eldest son, Howard Buffett. Greg Abel, who took over as chief executive in January, keeps running the businesses. Nothing about the arrangement was sudden — Berkshire has been walking toward this moment since Abel was named CEO-in-waiting back in 2021 — but the formal exit of the man who built the company still marks the end of something.

"Father Time always wins," Buffett wrote in a letter to shareholders announcing the move, a line notable mostly for how little drama it carries. He is not leaving the board, and he says he intends to keep offering opinions when asked. But the chairmanship, the seat with the actual power to hire, fire and set direction, now belongs to Howard, a 71-year-old Illinois farmer and philanthropist who has sat on Berkshire's board since 1993 and has never worked as an investor.

A split, not a handover

Buffett has been explicit that this is a division of labor rather than a promotion. "Greg runs the company; Howard will guard its culture and values — both worth more than anything on our balance sheet," he wrote. It is an unusual structure for a company this size: an operating CEO with full authority over capital allocation and acquisitions, and a non-executive chairman whose stated job is closer to institutional guardianship than management. The model exists chiefly to solve one problem — that Berkshire's decentralized structure, in which dozens of subsidiary managers operate with minimal oversight from Omaha, has always depended on trust built over decades rather than formal controls. Howard's role is to be the person who can intervene if that trust breaks down, most notably by removing a chief executive who strays from the company's investing discipline, without being the person making daily capital decisions himself.

Howard's background is the point. He is not a natural stock-picker; his career has been in farming and in the Howard G. Buffett Foundation's agricultural and conflict-mitigation work. Analysts who cover Berkshire read that as intentional: a chairman without investment ambitions of his own is less likely to second-guess Abel's calls, and more likely to focus narrowly on the culture-and-succession mandate his father assigned him.

Abel's first nine months

The transition invites an obvious question — is it working? — and the early evidence is mixed. Berkshire's stock has been roughly flat since Abel took the CEO chair on January 1, trailing the S&P 500 by a wide margin this year, according to Motley Fool and CNBC data. Operating earnings, by contrast, grew 17.7% in the first quarter versus a year earlier, and Abel has started moving on the company's famous cash hoard, which peaked near $397 billion in the spring. He ended a 14-quarter streak of net stock selling, built a roughly $10 billion stake in Alphabet, resumed share buybacks worth about $4.5 billion, and closed a $6.8 billion cash acquisition of homebuilder Taylor Morrison in July.

That is a more active posture than Buffett's late-tenure caution, when the cash pile grew for years largely unspent. Investors have adopted what several analysts call a "show me" stance: welcoming the capital deployment in principle while withholding judgment on whether Abel's specific bets — a tech stake, a homebuilder, buybacks at current prices — will match the 19.7% annualized return Buffett generated since 1965, nearly double the S&P 500 over the same stretch.

What the split is meant to protect

The governance structure answers a narrower question than performance: not whether Abel makes good investments, but whether Berkshire's culture — manager autonomy, an aversion to leverage, a reluctance to overpay — survives a CEO who did not spend fifty years building it personally. Howard's mandate, and the reason he holds the gavel rather than an outside director or an investment professional, is to be a check specifically on cultural drift, with the power under Berkshire's succession plan to act if a future CEO's conduct or judgment falls short.

The next real test arrives with Berkshire's annual meeting next spring, and before that, its fourth-quarter results in early 2027, which will show whether Abel has kept deploying the cash pile or reverted to the accumulation Buffett favored in his final years. Until then, Berkshire shareholders are watching a company running an experiment it has never run before: separating the person who invests the money from the person charged with making sure it still gets invested the Berkshire way.
