China's CPI and PPI miss even as hard-tech and export equities surge

Unitree's G1 humanoid robot — the company's Shanghai listing drew a 700%+ revenue surge, part of the hard-tech rally decoupling from weak domestic demand — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
China's August CPI and PPI improved on paper, but imports missed forecasts and consumer stocks slid even as chip and robotics equities hit multi-year highs.

China's statisticians handed markets a tidy set of numbers on September 9th: consumer prices up 0.8 per cent year-on-year in August, producer prices up 3.8 per cent, both roughly where economists had pencilled them in. On the surface, a country that has spent three years fighting deflation looked to be climbing out of the hole. Underneath, the story was thinner. Import growth — the most direct gauge of how much Chinese households and factories are actually buying — came in at 28.2 per cent, missing the Reuters consensus of 30 per cent by a wide margin. Consumer-goods inflation, stripped of food and energy, kept sliding. The rebound in the headline prints was a commodities-and-chips story, not a households-are-spending-again story, and the equity market has drawn exactly that distinction with unusual clarity.

The data that didn't quite add up

Ding Meng, an economist at China CITIC Bank International, put the caveat plainly after the release: "A sustained rise in inflation will still depend on a continued recovery in domestic demand," he said, adding that weak consumer spending and a struggling property market remain the key risks. Danske Bank's Allan von Mehren went further, trimming his 2026 China growth forecast to 4.6 per cent from 4.8 per cent on the back of a run of disappointing consumption data, and noting that until the housing market finds a floor, household confidence — and the private spending that follows it — is likely to stay depressed.

The National Bureau of Statistics itself attributed the CPI pickup mostly to volatile global commodity prices and seasonal food costs rather than stronger underlying appetite, conceding that consumer-goods inflation continued to decline even as the headline figure improved. Producer prices told a similar tale from the other direction: the jump to 3.8 per cent, the strongest reading in three months, tracked a global run-up in commodity costs and — pointedly — surging demand for chips and other high-tech components, rather than a broad-based industrial reflation.

That distinction matters because it lines up almost exactly with what happened in trade data released a day earlier. China's August exports rose 25 per cent year-on-year to $401.4 billion, in line with forecasts and the fastest pace since the spring, pushing the trade surplus to a 2026 high of $119.1 billion. Semiconductor exports alone were up 129.8 per cent; car shipments rose 43 per cent; the surplus with the United States widened past $29 billion, the largest since President Trump returned to office. Imports, again, undershot. "China continues to rely on exporters to support the economy," said Zhiwei Zhang, president and chief economist at Pinpoint Asset Management, as domestic demand remains subdued and governments abroad grow more vocal about the resulting imbalances.

Where the money is actually going

Equity investors have been pricing this divergence for months, and by September it had become one of the starkest splits in the Chinese market's recent history. The STAR 50, Shanghai's chip-heavy tech board, is up roughly 50 per cent since the start of the year and has repeatedly set fresh highs, with more than half of its biggest gainers drawn from the semiconductor supply chain — specialty gases, sputtering targets, memory. Cambricon Technologies and Moore Threads, both AI chipmakers, have led the charge; the Hang Seng Tech Index has climbed alongside them. Meanwhile the CSI 300's consumer sub-index is down roughly 20 per cent this year, and Kweichow Moutai — the baijiu maker that has long served as a rough proxy for Chinese discretionary spending — has fallen more than 11 per cent.

The listings calendar has amplified the effect. CXMT, the country's largest memory chipmaker, raised more than $8.6 billion in a Shanghai IPO in July and jumped 466 per cent on its trading debut. Unitree, the humanoid-robot maker, listed in Shanghai in August after reporting first-quarter revenue up more than 700 per cent year-on-year on a spike in chip-driven demand. Combined IPO and secondary-listing proceeds across Hong Kong and Shanghai have already topped $54 billion this year, ahead of all of 2025. "China's domestic consumption is weak, but technology remains a structural bright spot," Orient Securities analyst Zheng Yueling said of the split — a line that could serve as a caption for the entire market.

What the split is pricing

For trading desks, the read-through is less about any single data point than about what kind of economy is being priced into which assets. Hard-tech and export names are trading on a global AI capex cycle that has little to do with the health of the Chinese consumer — they are, in effect, a China-domiciled bet on Nvidia-adjacent demand, semiconductor localisation, and robotics adoption abroad. Consumer and property-linked equities are trading on a domestic demand picture that the August data, for all its improvement, did not actually validate. That is a coherent allocation decision, not obviously a mispricing — but it does mean the rally in STAR 50 and Hang Seng Tech is more insulated from a domestic slowdown than headline index levels might suggest, and more exposed than usual to global AI-spending sentiment and export-control headlines out of Washington.

It also raises the stakes on Beijing's next move. A trade surplus approaching $1.2 trillion for the year is drawing exactly the kind of foreign pushback Zhang flagged, and a persistently weak consumer is the more direct argument for the fiscal stimulus policymakers have so far metered out cautiously. September's official manufacturing and property data, due in the coming two weeks, will show whether the anti-involution campaign against industrial overcapacity is gaining traction beyond chips — and October's CPI and PPI prints, covering September, will be the next test of whether the domestic side of the ledger can catch up to the export side, or whether the two continue to move in opposite directions.
