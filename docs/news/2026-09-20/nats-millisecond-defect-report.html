A millisecond software fault triggered the UK air traffic meltdown, report finds

NATS' London Area Control Centre at Swanwick, Hampshire, which lost part of its automation during the September 8 failure — Peter Facey / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A NATS report found a software defect that corrupted flight data for under a millisecond triggered six hours of UK airspace restrictions, grounding 2,000+ flights on September 8.

It took a millisecond for a piece of code to go wrong, and six hours for the United Kingdom's airspace to recover from it. In between, more than 2,000 flights were cancelled, delayed or diverted, an estimated 330,000 passengers had their journeys wrecked, and the country got a blunt lesson in how little margin sits between "critical national infrastructure" and "a bug in a subroutine nobody had looked at in years."

On September 18, NATS — the company that runs UK air traffic control — published a preliminary report into what happened on September 8. The finding is almost comically small next to the damage it caused: a defect in a rarely-used corner of the National Airspace System, the software that processes flight data, produced corrupted output during a window of roughly one millisecond. That was enough to poison the data feed into London Area Control Centre, the facility that handles everything flying above 24,500 feet over England and Wales. The centre's systems noticed the corruption and did what they were built to do — disconnect to protect themselves — which promptly took away part of the automation controllers rely on to keep aircraft safely separated.

A millisecond, traced

According to the report, the fault sat in the part of the system that allocates identification codes to individual aircraft — normally an automatic process, but here triggered by a manual request. While that request was being handled, the system received a higher-priority message and paused what it was doing. When it resumed, the code meant to pick back up where it left off failed to execute correctly, and for less time than it takes to blink corrupted data rippled into subsequent flight updates. NATS chief executive Martin Rolfe put it plainly: "This was a software issue in a specific part of our flight data system, that we have traced to a small subsection of coding." Engineers had a fix in place within about three hours, but restoring confidence in the data meant a full system restart and reload — and, because the fault's blast radius wasn't immediately clear, NATS imposed flow restrictions across the entire UK rather than just the London centre. The queue took more than two days to clear at some of the 15 affected airports.

What makes the finding land harder is the timing. On June 19, the Civil Aviation Authority formally closed the last of 34 recommendations issued after NATS' previous meltdown — the August 2023 bank holiday failure, when a flight plan containing two identically-named waypoints 4,000 nautical miles apart broke the flight-planning system and disrupted more than 700,000 passengers. Eighty-one days after regulators declared that chapter closed, a different subsystem failed in a different way. The specifics changed; the pattern — a single malformed input cascading through automation nobody had stress-tested for that exact edge case — did not.

Who actually eats the cost

The airlines are furious, and the ownership structure explains why. NATS' en-route business is a public-private partnership: the Airline Group, representing British Airways, easyJet, Ryanair, Virgin Atlantic and others, holds 42%; NATS staff hold 5%; Heathrow's operator holds 4%; and the government holds 49% via a golden share that lets it override the rest. In other words, the airlines footing the bill for the disruption are also part-owners of the monopoly provider that caused it — and under UK261 passenger-rights rules, the CAA has indicated the incident likely counts as "extraordinary circumstances," meaning airlines owe passengers meals, hotels and rebooking but not the fixed cash compensation a straightforward cancellation would trigger. Airlines UK, the industry body, estimated the financial fallout would run into tens of millions of pounds, falling entirely on carriers rather than on NATS. Ryanair alone put its immediate hit at £2.5–3 million and its chief operating officer renewed a call, first made after the 2023 failure, for Rolfe to resign. Shares in Ryanair, easyJet and IAG all dipped in the days after the outage, though the moves were modest — a reminder that markets, so far, are treating this as an operational headline rather than a structural repricing of aviation risk.

That gap is the more interesting story than the outage itself. NATS is not a listed company, so there's no share price directly punishing it for the failure; its cost of capital is set through regulatory price controls, not equity markets. The airlines that absorb the disruption cost are hedged mainly through insurance and their own balance sheets, not through pricing NATS' reliability into anything. And the passengers who bear the actual inconvenience have the thinnest protection of all, since "extraordinary circumstances" is precisely the clause that keeps compensation off the table. The risk is real and recurring, but it's diffused across parties with limited ability or incentive to price it properly — which is a fairly good description of how infrastructure risk tends to hide until it doesn't.

What regulators do next

NATS says a permanent fix is now in safety testing, and a fuller report — likely with more detail on why redundancy designed to catch exactly this kind of fault didn't stop it from cascading — is due to follow the preliminary version. Separately, NATS has been pressing the CAA to approve close to £1 billion in investment through 2033, covering system modernisation that predates this incident but will now be read through it. The test for regulators is whether they treat this as a one-off defect to be patched, as they largely did in 2023, or as evidence that a 25-year-old public-private structure needs harder technical mandates — independent code audits, mandatory chaos-testing of edge cases, tighter reporting timelines — rather than a list of recommendations to close and move on from. The next marker is the full report; the one after that is whether a third failure, on some other Tuesday, arrives before it's published.
