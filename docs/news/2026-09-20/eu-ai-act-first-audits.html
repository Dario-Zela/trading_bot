Brussels opens first high-risk compliance audits under the EU AI Act

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission and the AI Office overseeing the audits — Sarra Benyaich / Euro Pictures, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
The EU AI Office and 24 national regulators began document audits of hiring, lending and healthcare AI this month — using live transparency powers ahead of the delayed 2027 high-risk deadline.

Inspectors from the European Union's AI Office and 24 national market watchdogs have started knocking on doors this month, asking companies to open up the algorithms that screen resumes, price loans and sort hospital patients by urgency. It is the first time Brussels has tried to enforce, rather than merely write, its Artificial Intelligence Act — and the answer to whether European AI regulation has teeth is starting to arrive in the form of document requests.

The inspections, which began in the first days of September, are being led on the ground by France's CNIL, Germany's BfDI and Spain's AESIA, working alongside the Commission's AI Office in Brussels. Auditors are asking employers, banks and healthcare providers using automated résumé-screening, credit-assessment and triage systems to hand over architecture diagrams, data-governance logs, human-oversight schematics and risk-management records — the paper trail the law has required companies to keep since 2024 but that regulators are only now checking.

What's actually being enforced

Here the picture is more tangled than "high-risk audits" suggests. The AI Act sorts systems into risk tiers, and hiring tools, credit scorers and medical triage software all sit in the highest "Annex III" category, which is supposed to carry the toughest obligations — conformity assessments, CE marking, registration in an EU database. But in June, the Council and Parliament signed off on a "Digital Omnibus" simplification package that pushed the deadline for those Annex III obligations back from August 2026 to December 2, 2027, after industry lobbying over readiness.

What did not move is Article 50, the transparency chapter, which took effect on schedule on August 2. It requires that people be told when they're dealing with an AI system, and it gives the AI Office and national regulators standing to request information and inspect technical files under the Act's general market-surveillance powers, independent of the Annex III timeline. That is the legal hook for September's audits: regulators are using live powers to check whether companies are on track for a deadline that is still fifteen months away, rather than punishing anyone for missing it yet.

EU tech chief Henna Virkkunen described the broader shift in blunter terms when the AI Office sent its first information requests to general-purpose model providers in late August. "As a first step in enforcing the AI Act, our AI Office has formally sent requests for information to a number of providers of general-purpose AI models based in different regions of the world," she said, adding that the requests concern "model security, independent external evaluations, and the monitoring of models once they are available on the market." Recipients of that separate wave of requests reportedly included OpenAI, Anthropic and Google.

Who is exposed, and to what

The AI Office has been built up for this moment: the Commission added roughly 38 staff to the unit this summer, tasked with monitoring companies from startups to the largest model developers, and it has stood up a whistleblower channel for confidential tips about non-compliance. On the penalty side, the law's three-tier fine structure under Article 99 caps the worst violations — banned practices like social scoring or real-time biometric surveillance — at €35 million or 7% of global turnover, whichever is higher. Most high-risk and transparency failures, the category that would cover a resume-screening or credit-scoring tool caught out in these audits, sit in the middle tier: up to €15 million or 3% of turnover. Supplying misleading information to auditors is its own offence, capped at €7.5 million or 1%.

No fines have been issued yet, and the Commission has been careful to frame September's activity as document review rather than enforcement action. But the sectors chosen are not accidental. HR platforms, consumer lenders and private healthcare providers are exactly the businesses that spent the past two years hoping the Digital Omnibus delay would also buy them a pass on scrutiny. It hasn't: regulators can still audit governance and documentation under general market-surveillance powers even while the substantive Annex III duties are paused, and a company found unprepared now has little excuse by the time the real deadline lands.

What it means for the AI trade

For investors, the immediate financial exposure is limited — no penalties have landed, and the companies most likely to be named are private HR-tech and lending-software vendors rather than listed giants, aside from the GPAI providers now fielding separate information requests. The more durable signal is about compliance cost. If Brussels can make an untested law bite through information requests alone, before the hard deadlines even arrive, that raises the baseline cost of selling AI hiring, lending and clinical tools into Europe — and gives regulators elsewhere, from the UK to individual US states, a template to point to. The next marker to watch is whether any of September's document requests escalate into a formal Article 99 investigation before the December 2027 Annex III deadline arrives; a first fine, even a modest one, would tell the market more about the Act's real force than the audits themselves.
