BEYOND THE TAPE · REPARATIONS · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-20 · 4 min read

Caribbean nations draft escalation plan pressing UK for slavery reparations

Barbados prime minister Mia Mottley has become the region's most prominent voice on reparations, arguing the Caribbean has "no desire to bankrupt anybody" but wants justice. — UNCTAD / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Caribbean governments have drawn up an escalation plan — legal threats included — to force reparations talks with the UK ahead of November's Commonwealth summit, but London still refuses to pay.

There is no invoice attached to this one, and that is rather the point. When Caribbean governments meet to talk reparations, the conversation tends to snag on numbers — economists at the University of the West Indies have floated sums running into the trillions of dollars for Britain alone — and then go nowhere. This week's gathering in Barbados tried a different tack: forget the ledger for now, and build the machinery to make London negotiate at all.

Officials from across the region drew up what they are calling an action plan — not a bill, but a sequenced set of pressure points — timed squarely ahead of the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting in St John's, Antigua and Barbuda, from 1 to 4 November, which King Charles is expected to attend.

From petition to negotiation

The plan came out of a reparations conference convened by Barbados, home to the Caricom Reparations Commission, whose chairman, Professor Sir Hilary Beckles, framed it as a shift in phase rather than tone. "We are now entering the second stage of this movement, the stage of negotiation," he has said of the broader campaign. At the Barbados gathering he returned to the human cost he says still shapes the region: Jamaica, he noted, became independent in 1962 with roughly 80% of its Black population unable to read or write. "After 300 years of British colonisation, they moved into independence with 80% illiteracy," he said. "How do you build a modern nation, not even a competitive economy, with 80% illiteracy?"

Barbados prime minister Mia Mottley, whose government has become the region's most prominent reparations advocate, struck a deliberately conciliatory note on the money question. "We have no desire to bankrupt anybody," she said, "but we have a desire for justice. The evidence is clear. What is absent is the will."

That combination — moral clarity, financial vagueness — is the plan's central design choice. It keeps a coalition of 15 Caricom states, with different economies, colonial histories and appetites for confrontation, pointed in the same direction without forcing them to agree on a price.

The legal fallback

What the plan does specify is what happens if London keeps declining to talk. Ralph Gonsalves, the former St Vincent and the Grenadines prime minister who has become the campaign's legal voice, argues that Britain's own signatures on modern human-rights conventions create a live obligation regardless of what the law said in the 18th century, pointing to the UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination as the relevant forum. Officials including David Comissiong, deputy chair of Barbados's national reparations task force, say the region is weighing "several legal strategies," among them a request for an advisory opinion from the International Court of Justice — the same route Pacific island states used to push climate liability onto the world stage. National commissions are also preparing to approach British institutions with direct historical ties to the slave trade, among them Lloyd's of London and the Church of England, with demands for payment, alongside formal letters to the royal family seeking an apology.

The backdrop gives the campaign some real, if thin, legal wind. In March, 123 countries backed a UN resolution branding transatlantic slavery among the gravest crimes against humanity; Britain abstained. In August, a UN committee found states can be legally obliged to consider reparations for historical injustice. Neither creates an enforceable claim, but both narrow the room for London to dismiss the issue as settled history.

Where London stands

The UK government's position has not moved. A spokesperson for Keir Starmer's administration, addressing the campaign this year, reiterated that Britain "does not and will not pay reparations," while acknowledging the slave trade was abhorrent and pointing instead to cooperation with Caribbean states on present-day challenges — climate finance, debt relief, migration. That formula has held since Starmer visited the region and faced the question directly, and there is little sign a change of government mood, let alone law, is imminent before November.

Which leaves the escalation plan doing a narrower job than its billing suggests. It cannot compel a negotiation; the ICJ route produces an advisory opinion, not a binding award, and getting a case heard at all would likely take years. What it can do is guarantee the topic is impossible to keep off the Commonwealth agenda, and turn a diffuse moral argument into a series of concrete asks — at Lloyd's, at Lambeth Palace, at Buckingham Palace — that individual British institutions will have to answer for themselves, whatever the government in Westminster says.

The next marker is CHOGM itself. Antigua and Barbuda's Gaston Browne chairs the summit, and Caribbean delegates have said they intend to ensure reparations is on the formal agenda rather than confined to the margins. Whether London engages with that agenda item, or simply repeats its abstention-by-other-means, will say more about the state of the campaign than any figure produced in Barbados this week.
