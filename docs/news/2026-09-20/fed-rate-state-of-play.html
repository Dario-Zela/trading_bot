Fed's Warsh-era hike settles in at 3.75%-4% as oil keeps inflation elevated

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, led his first FOMC decision on September 16. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Fed's new chair Kevin Warsh won a unanimous 12-0 vote to hike rates to 3.75%-4%, citing oil shocks from the Iran war rather than domestic demand.

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates for the first time in more than three years on Wednesday, lifting its benchmark rate a quarter point to a range of 3.75%-4%. It was the first policy decision of Kevin Warsh's chairmanship, and it passed the Federal Open Market Committee by a clean 12-0 vote — notable given how contentious his path to the job had been.

The move ends a hold that began in July 2023 and reverses, in effect, the cutting cycle the Fed had pursued through 2024 and early 2025. But the more striking detail sits in the Fed's own explanation: officials pointed not to an overheating economy but to oil.

A unanimous vote, an independent chair

Warsh told reporters after the meeting that inflation has been "too high ... for too long," adding that the committee "must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed." He characterized the hike as withdrawing "a dose of accommodation" — phrasing precise enough to unsettle traders, who read it as leaving the door open to further tightening this year.

The unanimity mattered because of what preceded it. President Trump, who nominated Warsh in January and saw him confirmed by the narrowest Senate margin for the post in history — 54-45 in May — said publicly that he had told his pick to "vote with the board" since "it's not going to matter" anyway, then separately accused the rest of the FOMC of being "very hostile" and "very political." Asked directly whether Warsh had voted the way Trump wanted him to, the president said he didn't think so. A chair installed amid open questions about Fed independence just delivered a hike his patron didn't want and got every other member to sign on.

The oil channel, not the demand channel

What's driving the inflation numbers is Middle East supply, not domestic spending. Brent crude has been on a volatile run since the Iran war began in February — spiking toward $118 in late March, sliding to around $70 by July, then climbing back above $100 in August and briefly touching $109 in early September after renewed strikes on shipping and energy infrastructure near the Strait of Hormuz. U.S. headline inflation had already jumped to 3.3% in March, its highest since 2024, largely on the back of energy costs. West Texas Intermediate slipped 3.1% to roughly $102.55 a barrel in the hours around Wednesday's decision, still far above where it sat before the conflict began.

That distinction — supply shock versus demand overheating — is the real story in this decision. A demand-driven inflation problem responds cleanly to higher rates; a supply-driven one, tied to tanker routes and missile strikes rather than consumer spending, does not. Warsh's committee raised rates anyway, betting that tighter policy can at least keep the energy shock from bleeding into broader price expectations.

What's next

Updated projections released alongside the decision showed 16 of the FOMC's 18 participants expecting at least one more hike before year-end, with none penciled in for 2027. The next scheduled decision lands October 28, ahead of a December meeting that will carry a fresh round of economic projections. Whether that next move happens depends less on the Fed's read of the U.S. consumer than on what holds at Hormuz.
