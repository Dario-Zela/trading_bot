Dimon says JPMorgan will offset the 'unfair' UK bank tax rise as gilts and sterling digest the EU deal

In one sentence
Jamie Dimon says JPMorgan will offset an 'unfair' UK bank-tax rise, testing whether the City's lenders absorb the cost or pass it on; exact figures remain unconfirmed.

JPMorgan's chief executive, Jamie Dimon, has said the bank will find ways to offset what he calls an "unfair" increase in the UK bank tax. That puts a question to the City: when the Treasury raises a levy that banks regard as unjustified, do the lenders absorb the cost, or do they move it onto customers, staff and investment plans? This article could not independently confirm the exact wording of Dimon's latest remarks or the market moves that accompanied them. Where figures are uncertain, it says so.

The row so far

Dimon's objection is not new. Reporting from August and since describes a consistent line: that JPMorgan did not cause the financial crisis and should not face a punitive regime more than a decade on. On a podcast he put the cost to shareholders in round numbers: "It may sound great, 'tax the banks', but it's $5 billion that my shareholders paid on that extra tax. I just think things like that have adverse consequences."

He has also tied the tax to JPMorgan's investment in London. The bank's planned Canary Wharf headquarters, which press reports put at around $4bn, has been described as dependent on "a continuing positive business environment in the UK". Reports also say Dimon warned Chancellor John Healey by phone against a windfall levy on bank profits ahead of the Budget.

A JPMorgan spokesperson responded to the surcharge rise with a statement that singling out the industry again, "one of the UK's biggest generators of growth and jobs, lacks principle".

The size of the increase is the least certain fact here. One report puts the surcharge at 3% on top of the 25% corporation tax rate. A later report says it was doubled to 6% to fund an energy support package. The later account is probably the current position, but the exact rate and start date should be checked against the Treasury's own documents before anyone relies on them.

Absorb or pass on

The phrase "find ways to offset" is the interesting part, because a bank has few ways to do it. It can cut costs, which in practice means headcount and property. It can reprice lending and fees. It can shift activity, and the capital behind it, to other jurisdictions. Or it can accept lower returns in the UK. Each has a different political cost, and none is free.

There is a limit on the threat, too. A surcharge of this kind applies to profits made in the UK, so moving the booking location only helps for activity that is genuinely mobile. Dimon's Canary Wharf warning is therefore mainly about where future investment goes, not about avoiding the tax on existing business. Whether it is a negotiating position or a plan will show in the bank's capital-allocation decisions, not in its statements.

Markets and the wider backdrop

The row lands in the same week as the UK-EU reset signed in Brussels. The question for gilts and sterling is whether investors read a bank-tax increase as a sign of fiscal strain, or as a one-off revenue measure that sits apart from the trade deal. The triage material for this piece carried no confirmed closing levels for gilts, sterling or the FTSE, and none are quoted here. A link between the bank-tax dispute and any move in the gilt market is, on present evidence, an untested hypothesis.

For UK-listed banks the direct effect would be on earnings: a higher surcharge subtracts from net profit in proportion to UK taxable profit, and analysts typically adjust forecasts within days of a change. JPMorgan itself is a modest taxpayer here compared with the domestic lenders, so Dimon's comments carry more political than financial weight. The banks that bear most of the bill are mostly not the ones making the loudest threats.

What to watch

Three things will settle the matter. The first is the rate and effective date in the Treasury's published Budget documents. The second is whether JPMorgan confirms or revises the Canary Wharf timetable. The third is how the domestic lenders describe the cost in their next results, and whether any of them mention repricing. The Commons vote on the UK-EU reset and the next gilt auction on the Debt Management Office calendar are the nearest dates for the market side; neither was confirmed in the material available.
