Trump threatens to ban US firms from building AI data centres overseas

In one sentence
Trump has reportedly threatened to bar US firms from building AI data centres abroad; the effect on hyperscalers and UK/EU plans depends on whether it becomes a binding rule.

US President Donald Trump has threatened to stop American companies from building AI data centres outside the United States, according to a BBC Business report. Whether that threat becomes an actual rule is the question that matters. The details available so far are thin: the exact wording, the legal mechanism and any timeline could not be verified for this article, and the account below rests on the BBC's headline and summary rather than the full text.

What has been said

The BBC reports that Trump threatened a ban on US firms building AI data centres overseas. The summary describes a curb on AI infrastructure built offshore by American companies, with consequences for the large cloud providers, known as hyperscalers, and for data-centre plans in the UK and the EU.

What is not yet known is considerable. It is unclear whether the threat is a formal proposal, an off-the-cuff remark or a negotiating position. Nor is it clear whether it would be an executive order, a licensing condition tied to chip exports, or something that needs Congress. A ban on building would also differ sharply from a ban on operating, or from a requirement that some share of capacity stay onshore. Each would land differently on companies.

Why a threat is still a signal

A threat is not a policy, and presidential threats often fade or are traded away. But this one fits a pattern of treating AI computing capacity as a strategic asset, closer to energy or defence supply than to ordinary corporate investment. Washington already uses export controls on advanced chips to decide who can build large AI clusters and where. Restricting where US firms can build would extend that logic from what leaves the country to where the companies themselves may put their money.

The stated or implied aim would presumably be to keep jobs, power demand and compute capacity at home. That is an inference from the shape of the proposal, not something confirmed in the reporting.

Who would feel it

Hyperscalers have spent heavily on capacity abroad, and the UK and EU have courted that investment, offering sites, grid connections and in some cases dedicated AI growth zones. A binding curb would put those plans at risk, or force the companies to rely on local partners and non-US builders. Governments that have been counting on American capital for their own AI ambitions would need to rethink.

There is also a counter-pressure. Companies serving European customers often face data-residency and sovereignty requirements that push compute onto European soil. A US rule pulling the other way would leave them caught between two regimes. European officials already argue for homegrown capacity and strategic autonomy, a theme that runs through the current debate on telecoms and the UK-EU reset; a US restriction would strengthen that case.

What to watch

The test is whether text appears: an executive order, a Commerce Department rule or a bill. Until then, the signals to follow are company statements on overseas capex, any carve-outs for allies such as the UK, and the reaction in London and Brussels. If a draft emerges, its definition of "building" and its treatment of existing projects will decide how much changes in practice.
