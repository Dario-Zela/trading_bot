Anthropic researcher says he may have cracked a major unsolved maths problem

In one sentence
An Anthropic researcher says he may have solved a major open maths problem, but the claim is unverified and awaits checking by expert mathematicians.

An Anthropic researcher says he may have solved a major unsolved problem in mathematics. The claim, reported by BBC Technology, has not been checked by outside experts. Until it has, it is a claim and not a result.

The wording matters. "May have" is the researcher's own hedge, and it is the right one. Mathematics has an unusually hard test for success: a proof is accepted only when specialists have read it, followed every step and found no gap. Announcements, preprints and social-media posts don't count, however confident their authors are.

The name of the problem, the researcher and the technical details of the argument could not be independently confirmed in the reporting available for this piece. They are left out here rather than guessed at.

How a claim like this is judged

There is a familiar path for a purported proof. The author posts a write-up, usually as a preprint. Specialists in the relevant subfield read it, and the first questions are usually about whether a key step quietly assumes what it is trying to prove. Informal discussion among experts can settle small claims within days. Long or novel arguments can take months, and some of the best-known proofs in modern mathematics were found to contain errors that were later repaired, or not repaired at all.

There is also a second route that did not exist a few years ago. Proofs can be written in formal languages such as Lean, where a computer checks every logical step. If an argument has been formalised, the question of whether it is correct largely goes away. Whether this claim comes with such a formalisation is not clear from what has been reported.

A year of machine mathematics

The claim lands in a year in which AI and mathematics have drawn steadily closer. In July 2025, systems from Google DeepMind and OpenAI reached gold-medal standard at the International Mathematical Olympiad, a contest for school-age students. Olympiad problems are hard, but they are designed to be solvable in a few hours. Open research problems are a different category: nobody knows whether an answer is within reach, and there is no answer key.

That gap is why this kind of claim draws scrutiny. If a frontier lab's researcher, with or without help from an AI model, has produced a correct proof of a famous open problem, it would be a notable moment for the field. It would also raise practical questions about credit, since mathematicians have not settled how to attribute work done jointly with a model.

The opposite outcome is just as plausible. Many claimed solutions to famous problems have collapsed on inspection, and an employer's interest in showcasing its technology is a reason for readers to wait for others to check the work.

What to watch

The test is simple. Look for a public write-up detailed enough to be checked, comments from named mathematicians who work on the problem, and any formal verification. A confirmation from specialists would turn this from a headline into a result. Silence, or a pointed objection to a single step, would say nearly as much.
