Denmark accuses Russia of an assassination plot as a Russia-bound ship is attacked in the Gulf of Finland

In one sentence
Denmark says Russian intelligence was behind a plot to kill a Ukrainian-American rapper and has expelled Moscow's defence attaché, but the evidence is not public and the Gulf of Finland report is unconfirmed.

Denmark has accused Russia of orchestrating a plot to kill a Ukrainian-American rapper on Danish soil, and has ordered Moscow's defence attaché out of the country. It is an unusual step: a NATO government publicly attributing a targeted-killing attempt to a foreign state, and answering it with an expulsion. Reporting on the Gulf of Finland, where a Russia-bound ship is said to have been attacked, is thinner. The two stories are separate and the evidence for them is uneven. They sit within the same pattern of Baltic and Nordic friction with Russia.

What Copenhagen says

According to reports published on 20–21 November, Denmark's intelligence service, PET, concluded that Moscow was behind a plan to kill the rapper Kyivstoner, whose real name is Albert Vasilyev and who holds both Ukrainian and US citizenship. The plot is said to have targeted a concert near Copenhagen. Danish broadcaster DR named him. Reports say armed suspects were stopped shortly before the event and that PET believes a local gang was used to carry it out. Two gang-affiliated men who were charged have reportedly pleaded guilty. Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen announced that Russia's defence attaché must leave within seven days.

The attribution rests on PET's assessment. The detailed evidence linking the gang to Russian officials has not, so far as the published accounts show, been made public. The guilty pleas establish that a criminal plot existed. They do not by themselves establish who directed it. That gap matters, and the account here relies on press reports of the Danish statements rather than on the underlying documents.

PET's chief reportedly said an attempted Russian assassination was not something the service had seen in Denmark before. The service had already warned in September that Russia was actively preparing sabotage, particularly against defence firms supplying Ukraine.

Moscow's reply and the earlier Polish case

The Russian embassy in Copenhagen called the expulsion "vengeful" and said Denmark had presented no evidence of Russian involvement in what it termed a "mysterious crime". That is a denial of evidence rather than of the plot's existence, and it is the standard Russian response to such attributions.

The target is not new to this story. In August, Poland said it had foiled a Russian-ordered plot against the same rapper and arrested a Russian citizen. Prime Minister Donald Tusk described him as "inconvenient to the Putin regime". Russia's FSB has accused Vasilyev of helping organise a July drone attack on a Moscow-region defence plant, which he denies. Two European states, then, have now reported plots against one man within about three months. That raises the question of whether the Danish and Polish cases are linked, which neither government's public account has yet answered.

The Gulf of Finland

The second thread is less settled. A report of an attack on a Russia-bound ship in the Gulf of Finland, the sea approach to St Petersburg and Ust-Luga, has circulated, but no date, vessel, attacker or casualty figure could be confirmed here. Nearby incidents give context without identifying the event. In February a Liberian-flagged cargo ship bound for Russia suffered an explosion in the gulf, with no cause established in the reports found. In December 2025 Finland detained the Fitburg, sailing from Russia, over suspected undersea-cable damage, and later released it. On 11 November Ukraine's SBU said it had struck the Russian missile ship Naro-Fominsk near Gogland Island, and Finland said it had detected a strike on a Russian warship. Damage and casualties in that case are unknown.

The unconfirmed ship report should not be folded into the Danish attribution. What the incidents have in common is geography: the Baltic has become a place where attacks on Russia-linked vessels and accusations of Russian sabotage ashore both occur.

What to watch

The attaché's seven-day deadline falls in the last days of November, and the first test is whether Moscow retaliates by expelling a Danish diplomat, as it usually does. The second is whether Danish prosecutors or PET release more detail, in court or otherwise, on the chain from gang to Russian officials. The charged men's sentencing could surface it. Until then Denmark's claim stands as a serious official attribution that is not yet publicly substantiated. The Gulf of Finland report needs a named vessel and an official statement from Finland, Estonia or Russia before it can be treated as an established event.
