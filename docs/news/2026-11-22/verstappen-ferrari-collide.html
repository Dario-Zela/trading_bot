Verstappen wins an extraordinary race as the two Ferraris collide

In one sentence
Verstappen won a wild Las Vegas Grand Prix, but Ferrari's Hamilton–Leclerc collision and Leclerc's 10-second penalty were the story of the race.

Ferrari had two cars running together in the points and a rivalry that has been audible on the radio all season. With 12 laps left in the Las Vegas Grand Prix, the two met in the way the team's critics had been predicting. Charles Leclerc tangled with Lewis Hamilton while they fought over sixth place, Hamilton was spun round, and the stewards handed Leclerc a 10-second penalty. It was one more entry in a long list of Ferrari mistakes in a race that Max Verstappen went on to win.

The win itself was extraordinary. Over 50 laps there were 35 lead changes and 352 overtakes, and Verstappen finished a second clear of the McLarens. The Ferrari clash decided nothing at the front. It did decide what the weekend means for a team that keeps losing points to itself.

A clash that had been coming

The two Ferrari drivers did not arrive at the collision as strangers. Earlier in the race they had bickered through the first stint, and Leclerc said on the radio that Hamilton was "squeezing" him and that it would "end badly". Hamilton's complaint ran the other way. He wants Ferrari to set racing rules between the pair, a request he made after their clash at Monza and again after Mexico. In Las Vegas, reports say, he also had an expletive-laden exchange over Leclerc needing to work with him to overtake.

Hamilton's afternoon was already compromised. He had picked up a 10-second penalty for going off track while passing Lando Norris, and he served it under the safety car. He then fought back to get ahead of Leclerc, only for Leclerc to spin him out. Reports differ on which corner it was. The stewards found that Leclerc "initiated the overtaking manoeuvre from a considerable distance behind", which is the language of a lunge that was never going to work.

Leclerc dropped from sixth on the road to 10th once the penalty was applied. Hamilton recovered to seventh, the best Ferrari finish of the day. The pair split a handful of points between them in a race where the car was plainly quick enough to score properly.

What Ferrari's wall did, and did not do

The question for the team is less who was at fault on the track than why the pair were still left to settle it themselves. Team principal Fred Vasseur asked Leclerc to see him after the race, which is a polite way of saying the conversation will not be about tyre strategy.

This is the third time in a few races that the two have collided or come close. Hamilton had asked for rules of engagement after both Monza and Mexico, and no visible framework appears to have followed. A team can reasonably let its drivers race. It is harder to defend letting them race when a penalty and a spin are the likely result, in the closing stages, with places at stake that the team is paid for in the constructors' standings.

There is also the matter of what a clash costs beyond the points. Hamilton was already carrying a penalty. A sensible instruction to hold position, issued before Leclerc committed, would have cost Ferrari nothing. Instead it lost a place, and arguably a net swing of several points.

The race Ferrari were not in

Up front, the day belonged to Verstappen and George Russell, who traded the lead repeatedly. Verstappen repassed Russell every time he lost the position, with two outside moves at Turn 2 and one dive at Turn 5. Russell then locked up with three laps to go while trying to get past team-mate Kimi Antonelli, hit the barrier and eventually finished ninth.

Antonelli, newly crowned world champion according to Sky Sports, then lifted at the start of the last lap and was passed by both McLarens. Norris took second and Oscar Piastri third. Verstappen called the race "crazy. Really, really surreal and something I've never seen before in F1." It was his fourth win in six races, and it puts him 37 points clear of Hamilton, third in the drivers' standings, with Qatar and Abu Dhabi to come.

That gap is the context the Ferrari story lacks. The championship is settled, and Ferrari are not in contention for second either. What remains at stake is smaller and more internal: whether Vasseur can get his two drivers to stop costing the team points before the season ends, and what a winter of this does to the pairing. The next test comes at Qatar, where Ferrari will need to show that the post-race conversation produced an actual rule.
