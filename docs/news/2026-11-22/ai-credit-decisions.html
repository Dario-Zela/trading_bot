TECH · AI IN LENDING · By R. Okonkwo · 2026-11-22 · 3 min read

Should AI be judging our creditworthiness?

In one sentence
Lenders are handing credit decisions to models, and EU, UK and US rules demand explanations and human review, but evidence of real-world enforcement remains thin.

A loan application is refused. The applicant asks why. In a growing number of cases, the honest answer is that a statistical model ranked them below a line, and nobody at the lender can say precisely which inputs put them there. Whether that is acceptable, and who has to explain it, is now a live question for regulators in Brussels, London and Washington.

What the rules say

The EU's AI Act names the use case outright. Systems used to assess the creditworthiness of natural persons, or to set their credit score, are listed as high-risk in Annex III. That brings obligations on data quality, documentation, human oversight and logging. Fraud-detection systems are carved out.

The timetable is less settled than the text. The high-risk obligations were due to apply from August 2026, but the Commission has proposed pushing them back, potentially to late 2027, as part of its "digital omnibus" simplification package. Until that is resolved, lenders are planning against a moving date.

The older tool is the GDPR. Article 22 gives people a right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that significantly affects them, and a refusal of credit is the textbook example. In December 2023 the EU's top court, in the SCHUFA case (C-634/21), held that a credit score can itself count as such a decision where a lender draws strongly on it. That matters because many lenders argued a human nominally signs off. A 2025 ruling in Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22) added that people are entitled to a meaningful explanation of the logic behind such a decision, not just a description of the model.

The UK has moved the other way. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 replaces the blanket Article 22 restriction with a more permissive regime. Solely automated decisions are generally allowed, subject to safeguards: telling people about the decision, letting them make representations, obtaining human intervention and contesting the outcome. Tighter limits remain for sensitive categories of data. The Information Commissioner's Office still publishes guidance on what meaningful human review looks like.

In the US there is no AI-specific credit statute, but the Equal Credit Opportunity Act and Regulation B require creditors to give specific reasons for an adverse decision. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau said in a 2022 circular that a complex algorithm is no defence: a lender that cannot explain its model cannot lawfully use it to refuse credit.

Enforcement is the weak point

The rules on paper are fairly consistent: say why, allow a challenge, keep a human in the loop. Evidence that they bite in practice is thinner. Most challenges never reach a regulator. A borrower refused a credit card has little appetite for a data-protection complaint, and the "reasons" lenders supply are often generic codes such as "insufficient credit history", which satisfy the letter of the rule without telling anyone what to change.

That gap is where model risk accumulates. A bank that cannot reconstruct why a model refused a given applicant also cannot easily show it is not discriminating by proxy, for example through postcode or employment type. Fintechs using alternative data face the sharper version of the problem, because their inputs are harder to defend.

What to watch

Two things will settle it. The first is whether the EU confirms or delays the AI Act's high-risk date, which decides when documentation and oversight duties become auditable. The second is whether national data-protection authorities, and the ICO under its new powers, bring any case that tests what counts as a meaningful explanation of a refusal. Until one does, the right to contest a "no" exists mainly as text.
