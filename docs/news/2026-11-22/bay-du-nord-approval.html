Bay du Nord approval moves Shell closer to a final investment decision

In one sentence
Shell did take a 30% stake in Equinor's Bay du Nord in October, but no new Canadian approval is evident and the final investment decision is still targeted for early 2027.

Shell is a partner in Equinor's Bay du Nord oil project off Newfoundland, and a final investment decision is still pending. The framing that a fresh Canadian approval has moved Shell closer to that decision is only partly supported. The Shell role is real. A new approval is not evident in the public record.

The source for the claim is a Simply Wall St stock note. That is an aggregator, not a regulator or a company release. The record below is drawn from Equinor, the Newfoundland and Labrador government and trade press.

What is confirmed

On 9 October 2026, Equinor said Shell would acquire a 30% interest in Bay du Nord. Equinor keeps 70% and remains operator. The price was not disclosed, and the release names no closing conditions.

The deal follows a change in the ownership table. In early July, BP agreed to sell its roughly 37% non-operated stake to Equinor, which briefly put the operator on course for full ownership. Equinor said then that it would look for partners as the project developed. Shell is the first to be named.

Philippe Mathieu, Equinor's executive vice president for exploration and production international, said: "Shell's entry strengthens the project as we continue to mature it towards a final investment decision."

The approval question

The federal environmental approval dates from 2022. Ottawa cleared what was then described as a C$12bn project after an environmental assessment found it would not cause significant adverse effects. That is the approval most widely reported.

In March 2026, the provincial government signed a benefits agreement with Equinor and BP covering life-of-field benefits, royalties and an option for up to 10% provincial equity. It was reported as clearing a key hurdle.

Neither of these is a new decision made this month. A regulator, a date and a set of conditions for any additional approval were not found. Equinor's own release says only that the final decision depends on "market conditions, regulatory approvals and each company's internal decision processes", and it does not name the approvals still outstanding. Until a specific decision is identified, the stock note's "approval" most plausibly refers to the older permissions or the benefits agreement.

The project in numbers

  • Cost: about C$14bn, up from the C$12bn quoted in 2022. The sources do not say whether the difference reflects inflation, scope or both.
  • Resources: more than 400 million barrels of recoverable oil in the initial phase, in the Flemish Pass about 500 km offshore, in 600–1,170 metres of water.
  • Concept: a phased subsea development tied back to a floating production, storage and offloading vessel (FPSO). Cappahayden, Harpoon and Baccalieu are named as possible future tiebacks.
  • Timing: front-end engineering and design is being finalised. Equinor targets a final investment decision in early 2027 and first oil in 2031.

What to watch

The next real data point is the investment decision itself, targeted for early 2027. Before then, the markers are completion of the FEED work, the closing of the BP and Shell transactions, and whether the province exercises its equity option. A cost figure that moves again from C$14bn would also bear on the decision. Shell's entry brings in a second large balance sheet to share a bill of that size, but it does not commit anyone to build.
