Mexican drug lord on FBI's Ten Most Wanted list arrested in shoot-out

FBI Ten Most Wanted poster for Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as "Chapo Isidro". — FBI / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Mexican forces arrested FBI Ten Most Wanted fugitive Fausto Isidro Meza Flores in Sinaloa after a deadly gun battle, with US intelligence reportedly aiding the raid and extradition now sought.

Mexican special forces have arrested Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, a cartel leader on the FBI's Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list, after a gun battle in the north-western state of Sinaloa that left at least 11 people dead. Reports put the arrest on Friday, 20 November, in Guasave. Mexico's defence ministry said he was taken with his alleged second-in-command and both were flown to Mexico City to face prosecution.

Meza Flores, 44, is known as "Chapo Isidro". He leads Los Mazatlecos, a group that operates around Guasave, Los Mochis, Mazatlán and Nayarit and is allied with the Beltrán Leyva organisation, a long-running rival of the Sinaloa Cartel. The FBI added him to its Ten Most Wanted list on 4 February 2025. The US State Department had offered up to $5m (£3.8m) for information leading to his capture.

The raid

Officials say the troops came under gunfire and explosives before they entered the property. Accounts of the toll differ. Most reports say four soldiers and seven suspected cartel members were killed. The shooting also forced the suspension of a baseball game in Guasave, and spectators fled the stadium. Seven other people were reportedly detained. Cartel members then blocked roads and set fire to vehicles and businesses, a familiar pattern after high-profile arrests in Sinaloa. The casualty figures come from Mexican authorities and wire reports, and have not been independently confirmed.

Who he is

Meza Flores began in the 1990s with the Juárez Cartel under Amado Carrillo Fuentes. After Carrillo Fuentes died in 1997, he moved to the Beltrán Leyva faction and stayed loyal to the brothers after Arturo Beltrán Leyva was killed in 2009. He has been linked to a 2010 shoot-out in Tubutama, Sonora, in which about 30 people died. The US Treasury froze his assets and those of seven relatives under the Kingpin Act in 2013.

US charges centre on conspiracy to manufacture and distribute methamphetamine, cocaine, heroin and marijuana for import into the United States, plus firearms offences. The FBI has also accused him of kidnapping, extortion and torture.

What it signals

The detail that sets this arrest apart from other cartel captures is US involvement. Reports say the operation drew on US intelligence, with support from the Joint Interagency Task Force-Counter Cartel. That points to continuing operational cooperation between Washington and Mexico City on cartel targets, though the public record does not yet show how much the US contributed on the ground.

The arrest does not by itself reshape that cooperation. Los Mazatlecos have operated in a crowded and violent region, and past captures of senior figures have often brought fighting over succession rather than a lasting drop in violence.

The next question is extradition. The US is seeking him, and Mexican prosecutors in the organised-crime unit SEIDO now hold him. Whether he is sent north or tried in Mexico first will show how far the cooperation extends.
