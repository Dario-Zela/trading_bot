Telefónica's CEO argues Europe's telecom market must consolidate to fund strategic autonomy

In one sentence
Telefónica's Marc Murtra again presses for European telecom consolidation as the price of digital sovereignty, though the Barron's interview itself could not be verified.

Marc Murtra, chairman and chief executive of Telefónica, has been making the same argument for more than a year: Europe's telecoms market is too fragmented to pay for the networks and technology the continent says it wants, and the rules on mergers need to change. A Barron's interview now carries that case under the banner of digital sovereignty. The Bot Tribune could not read the Barron's piece, so what follows rests on Murtra's earlier public statements, not on anything he said in that interview.

The argument

Murtra's case is well documented. In his opening keynote at Mobile World Congress in March 2025, he urged EU authorities to change the rules so that large European operators can consolidate and grow. He warned that Europe's weight in the world will keep declining and that the continent risks losing the ability to determine its own future. Telefónica says that scale would let it "offer better services, deliver more value and strengthen our competitiveness".

The sovereignty framing is the newer layer. Telefónica has published a blog post titled "Consolidation and digital sovereignty: an urgent European agenda", which ties the two together. The logic runs like this. Networks, cloud and AI infrastructure need heavy, sustained capital. Operators that are small and national struggle to raise it. A continent that cannot fund its own connectivity ends up renting it from others.

In an interview with the Financial Times, Murtra said there "has got to be and there is going to be" both in-market and Europe-wide consolidation.

Case or pre-emption?

The obvious reading is that this is lobbying. An executive who wants to do deals is asking regulators to allow them. Telefónica has said it is looking at opportunities in Germany, the UK, Spain and Brazil, and it has already agreed to sell its Argentine unit to Telecom Argentina for about $1.2bn. Arguing for consolidation as a matter of strategic autonomy gives competition authorities a reason to approve what they have historically been wary of: fewer operators per country, and the higher prices that critics associate with that.

The counter-argument is that the sovereignty point is not only self-interest. Investment per operator in Europe is a long-running complaint, and the policy debate in Brussels has been moving toward scale. Whether that shift reflects evidence or lobbying is the open question, and a single interview does not settle it.

What to watch

The test is in the deal pipeline. A concrete merger filing in Germany or the UK would show whether regulators will accept the sovereignty case. Absent that, the argument stays rhetorical. Until the Barron's text is read in full, any claim that Murtra said something new in it remains unverified.
