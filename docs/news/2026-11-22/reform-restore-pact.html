Reform UK weighs a pact with Restore Britain as polls stall and Farage rules out a successor

In one sentence
Reform UK is reportedly weighing a pact with Restore Britain as its polling stalls, while Farage says no one else is fit to lead, and Labour MPs sign up for a course on countering populism.

Reform UK, the party that spent much of the past two years at or near the top of British opinion polls, is reported to be weighing an electoral pact with a smaller rival on the populist right, Restore Britain. The reported reason is that its poll numbers have stopped climbing. In the same coverage, Nigel Farage is said to have told colleagues that no one else is fit to lead the party. Taken together, the two reports describe a movement looking for ways to widen its reach while keeping its leadership exactly where it is.

The details come from Guardian Politics headlines. The full reporting could not be independently checked, so what follows separates what is attributed from what is inference.

A plateau and a possible partner

The reported logic of a pact is simple. Reform's rise rested on drawing together voters disillusioned with both the Conservatives and Labour. A party that stops growing has to find votes somewhere else. A smaller right-populist party that competes for the same voters, even at a low level, can split the vote in marginal seats. First-past-the-post punishes that kind of division more severely than almost any other feature of British politics.

Restore Britain is the vehicle associated with Rupert Lowe, the former Reform MP who fell out with the party leadership in 2025 and went on to build a separate platform. Any deal would therefore be more than a tactical electoral arrangement. It would mean a reconciliation, or at least a truce, between two camps that parted on bad terms. That makes it harder to arrange than a standard non-aggression pact, and it is a reason to treat the reports as an option under discussion, not a decision.

The headline does not say what form a pact might take. The usual options are a formal electoral pact, in which each party stands aside in agreed seats, or a looser understanding, or a merger. Each carries a cost. A pact gives up candidates and local organisation. A merger gives up identity. Neither was detailed in the material available.

Farage and the succession question

The second thread is the leadership. Farage is reported to have said no one else in Reform is fit to lead it. As a statement of confidence in himself, that is unsurprising, since Reform is more closely identified with its founder than almost any other British party is with its leader. As a statement about the party's future, it is more revealing.

A party that depends on one figure has no obvious answer to the question of what happens when that figure is unavailable, unpopular or simply older. Ruling out a successor closes off the debate, but it does not settle it. It also sits uneasily with a pact. A deal with Restore would bring in a party with its own leading personality and its own grievances about how Reform is run. The more Farage insists the party is his to lead, the harder he may find it to offer a partner a share of the leadership.

This is interpretation, and the available reporting does not say that the two issues were linked in Reform's own deliberations. But the polling plateau, the search for allies and the refusal to name an heir all point to the same underlying problem: a party built around one person is finding it hard to grow beyond what that person can win alone.

Labour's unease

The other side of the contest is also in motion. Separately, Guardian Politics reported that a group of Labour MPs has signed up for a leadership course framed as a response to rightwing populism. No figures on how many MPs enrolled, or on Labour's current polling, were available for this article.

The symbolism is still worth noting. Backbenchers who sign up for training in how to counter populism are signalling that they do not feel the party's current approach is enough, and that they would prefer to build their own answer instead of waiting for one from the front bench. Reform is the populist force they have in mind. If Reform's polling is stalling, as reported, the course may be a case of Labour MPs preparing for a fight that is still shifting beneath them.

The government's attention is elsewhere this week, on the UK-EU reset signed in Brussels and the Commons vote that follows. That deal is the kind of European settlement Reform has built much of its identity against, which gives the populist right a fresh target even as its own arithmetic gets harder.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is a serious move or a passing story. The first is whether either party confirms talks and says what a pact would involve, in particular which seats each would contest. The second is whether Reform's polling resumes its climb or settles at its current level, because a party that is rising has less need of allies. The third is whether Farage's position on succession survives contact with a negotiating partner who wants influence in return for stepping aside.

For now, a pact would consolidate the right-populist vote only if the two sides can agree on who leads it. Reform's position, as reported, is that it is Farage.
