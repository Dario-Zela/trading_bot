Can AI really spot Black Friday deals? The BBC tests it

In one sentence
Independent testing by Which? found ChatGPT gave the right lowest price and retailer for only 8 of 25 products, so AI deal-spotting looks shaky ahead of Black Friday on 27 November.

Black Friday falls on 27 November, five days from now, and a growing number of shoppers will ask a chatbot whether a "deal" is any good. The BBC has run a test of whether AI tools can tell a real discount from an inflated one. Its methodology and results could not be checked for this piece, so they are not reported here. What can be said is what other recent testing shows, and it suggests caution.

What the Which? test found

The closest published evidence is a Which? test from 18 October. It put 25 popular products to ChatGPT, among them laptops, TVs, washing machines and vacuum cleaners, and asked it to find the lowest price and the retailer selling at that price. Which? chose ChatGPT because it was the chatbot most used by respondents to its own survey.

The chatbot got both the lowest price and the retailer right for only eight of the 25 products. In 68% of cases there was at least one problem: a wrong price, a missed lower price, or a price for a different product altogether.

The examples are specific. For a Lenovo Chromebook, Which? found a lowest reputable price of £599. ChatGPT said £399, which turned out to be the lowest price in the past six months rather than a current one, and the result pointed to the wrong laptop. For a Microsoft Surface Pro, it quoted £919.08 at the Microsoft Store as the cheapest, but the store's actual price was £999 and Amazon had it at £899. In a few cases the tool seemed to rely on price-comparison pages that were out of date.

OpenAI told Which? that ChatGPT links to retailers so users can check prices, that early listings may not show the lowest price, and that it is working to refresh pricing data faster.

Why the task is hard

Spotting a fake deal needs two things: the live price, and the price history. Chatbots are weak at both. Live prices change by the hour and sit behind retailer pages, so a model can only report what it happened to retrieve. History matters because a "was £X" reference price is exactly what an inflated deal manipulates.

The scale of the problem is well documented. Which? has previously found that 83% of offers on sale during the last Black Friday period were the same price or cheaper at other times of the year. TechRadar's own analysis found the Black Friday price was the lowest of the preceding week in only 27% of cases.

A TechRadar writer who tried ChatGPT's Shopping Research tool found it handled a simple AirPods query well but would not recommend it yet over a human doing the legwork.

The market angle is thin

The Which? survey found 32% of respondents had used AI chatbots for shopping or product research, so the audience is real. But nothing in the evidence reviewed here shows AI price tools measurably shifting consumer demand. For now this is a service story about retail, not a market one.

What to watch

The BBC's own findings will show whether its results echo Which?'s error rate. The next real data point is the trading week itself: retailers' Black Friday and Cyber Monday (30 November) sales figures will show how much shoppers acted on any of this advice. Until then, the standard advice from Which? stands: treat a chatbot's price as a starting point and check it on the retailer's own page.
