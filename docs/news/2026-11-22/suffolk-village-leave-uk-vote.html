Suffolk village votes to 'leave the UK' over asylum housing plans

In one sentence
Barnham, Suffolk, voted 257-16 in a symbolic poll to 'leave the UK' over asylum housing at RAF Barnham, a protest that has no legal force and has yet to move ministers.

A Suffolk village has voted, overwhelmingly and without any legal effect, to leave the United Kingdom. Barnham, a community of roughly 600 people near Thetford, backed secession by 257 votes to 16, with one spoiled ballot, in a protest at plans to house asylum seekers at the former RAF Barnham base on its doorstep.

The poll is symbolic. It cannot alter the village's place in the UK, and it cannot stop the Home Office using the site. Its purpose is political: to show ministers the strength of local feeling and to test whether that feeling can shift a policy of moving asylum seekers out of hotels and into large military sites.

A second village follows the first

Barnham is the second English village to hold such a vote. The first was Piddington in Oxfordshire, which in September voted by 285 to 26 to leave, on a reported turnout of 92%, over a plan to use a former Ministry of Defence site. Piddington went on to style itself the "Principality of Piddington". Barnham will rebrand as the "Bailiwick of Barnham".

The Suffolk site is a decommissioned base that once stored munitions and, according to the East Anglian Daily Times, nuclear weapons. Reports put the plan at up to 931 people, including arrivals by small boat, though earlier coverage cited a figure of 1,256 and the paper's own lede says "nearly 1,000". The Home Office has not yet housed anyone there. West Suffolk councillors agreed at an urgent meeting on 1 July that the base was the wrong place for the purpose.

What the vote does and does not show

Turnout is the awkward number for organisers. Reports say about half of the village's residents voted, and one account put it nearer 70% of those eligible, estimated at 400 to 500 people. Either way it fell well short of Piddington's. Helen Beck, who led the Barnham Residents Association, offered a frank reading of that gap: "I think that some people probably stayed away because they thought, look what happened in Piddington, what's the point?"

Backing for secession was lopsided among those who did vote, but the organisers' claim that the result "sent a powerful message" to government is an assertion, not an outcome. Beck herself was cautious: "I don't think it'll change the government's attitude, but we hope that we have made our voice heard."

That caution fits the record so far. After Piddington, ministers did not retreat. Reports say Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy vowed that the base there would go ahead, and the government's line has been that military sites are preferable to hotels. The EADT's account contained no direct ministerial response to the Barnham result.

The politics around it

The vote has been picked up by the government's opponents. Reform UK's Zia Yusuf said Prime Minister Andy Burnham was on "borrowed time" and that "people's lives are being turned upside down". Burnham's remark that regions should take their "fair share" of migrants has drawn criticism. Reform is, separately, weighing its own electoral options, a matter covered elsewhere in this edition.

The pattern matters more than any single result. Two villages, in different counties, have borrowed the vocabulary of 2016 to attach a constitutional flourish to a planning dispute. The device is cheap, photogenic and hard for ministers to ignore entirely, which is its appeal. Its limit is that it persuades no one who is not already persuaded, and the legal route to blocking a site runs through planning and the courts, not a village hall.

What to watch

A planning application for RAF Barnham is expected in the coming weeks, with a decision to follow. That will show whether local opposition, backed by the district council's stated position, has any purchase on the outcome. Whether a third village stages a vote will indicate if this is a one-off protest or a repeatable tactic.
