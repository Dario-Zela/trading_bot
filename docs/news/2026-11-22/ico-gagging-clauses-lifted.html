Gagging clauses lifted for ex-staff ahead of ICO culture inquiry

In one sentence
The ICO has reportedly freed former staff from gagging clauses so they can speak to an inquiry into its workplace culture, though details remain unconfirmed.

The UK's data protection regulator has told former staff they are free to speak to an inquiry into its own workplace culture, setting aside confidentiality clauses that might otherwise have kept them quiet. The report comes from BBC Politics and could not be independently confirmed. The date, the scope of the waiver and the clauses covered have not been published in anything found.

The move matters because of what the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) is. It enforces data protection law and polices how other organisations handle personal information and complaints. Whether it will let people describe its own conduct is therefore a fair test of how open it is about itself.

How the ICO got here

The inquiry follows the resignation of John Edwards as Information Commissioner on 19 June, after an independent workplace investigation. Computer Weekly reported that the investigation found evidence of "vulgar and highly sexualised language" directed at female staff. Technology Secretary Liz Kendall later told MPs she was "appalled" to learn Edwards was understood to be preparing legal action against one of the women who had raised concerns.

On 8 July Kendall announced an independent review of the ICO's culture, accountability and governance. As City AM reported, it covers what senior leaders knew about concerns raised during Edwards' tenure. It is framed as a lessons-learned exercise, and the ICO said it would commission it jointly with the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology (DSIT). Staff were told of DSIT's role only after Kendall's announcement, and former officials said confidence would depend on the review being seen as independent of the current executive. Paul Arnold, the interim chief executive, is overseeing the regulator during the transition. A new board, a majority of whom Kendall said would be women, is due to be announced.

Why the clauses matter

Confidentiality and settlement terms often decide whether a workplace inquiry hears from the people best placed to describe what happened. Former employees are the obvious witnesses on how complaints were handled over the years. Current staff may fear for their jobs. Former staff may be restrained by the terms of their departure. Lifting those restraints removes one practical obstacle to the inquiry hearing from them.

The pressure on the ICO to do so was visible. Nicola Cain, chief executive of the consultancy Handley Gill, told City AM that "the biggest risk for the organisation appears to be retaining, or regaining, the trust and confidence of its own staff." Computer Weekly has reported that staff felt unable to challenge senior leadership, and that the PCS union said concerns about the culture were widely known among staff. Former officials quoted by City AM described growing caution in enforcement and declining morale before Edwards left. Those accounts suggest the inquiry's questions reach beyond one person.

Transparency about the review has been uneven. MLex reported that the regulator had declined to disclose details of it. A waiver for former staff would be a step in the other direction, but only if it is broad and clearly communicated.

What remains unclear

The BBC's account does not, in the material available, say how many people are affected, which clauses are covered, or whether the waiver also covers settlement agreements. It does not say whether the waiver applies to the joint ICO-DSIT review or to a separate process. Nor does it say whether anything said can be used in published findings. The ICO's own statement on the change was not found.

The next markers are the review's terms of reference, its leadership and timetable, and the announcement of the new board. Those will show whether the waiver gives former staff a real route to be heard.
