FCA to review its handling of Lutnick-linked whistleblower as MPs demand inquiry

In one sentence
The FCA will review how it handled Simon Andriesz, a BGC whistleblower who raised concerns tied to Howard Lutnick and later died, as 15 MPs demand a formal inquiry.

The Financial Conduct Authority is to examine its own conduct in the case of Simon Andriesz, a former senior employee of the brokerage BGC Partners who raised concerns about the firm and later died in Thailand. BGC was founded and run by Howard Lutnick, now the US Commerce Secretary. The review follows a demand from a group of British MPs for a formal inquiry. As reported, it is a review of the regulator by the regulator, and it was prompted by a death.

The case

Andriesz spent years raising concerns about BGC. He began internally in 2016 with accounting irregularities. In 2020–21 he reportedly told the FBI that Lutnick had undeclared business ties with Jeffrey Epstein. He died last month, according to the reports, and the death has been described as a suicide. Lutnick has been drawn into Congressional scrutiny of the Epstein affair, and the Commerce Secretary has agreed to testify to the House Oversight Committee.

This article rests on press reports. Several of the claims about Andriesz's allegations have not been tested in any proceeding that the sources describe, and none of them is an FCA finding.

What the regulator is accused of

The complaints concern process rather than the underlying allegations. Andriesz had been told by the FCA that there was "insufficient evidence" to conclude that he had suffered retaliation. He believed that conclusion made it harder to win a claim against BGC at tribunal. He also accused the regulator of failing to hold his former employer to account, and of wrongly telling him he did not qualify for whistleblower protection.

Those are three separate points, and the review will have to address each of them:

  • Evidence threshold. Whether the retaliation assessment was reasonable on the material available.
  • Downstream effect. Whether the FCA's stated view affected his tribunal prospects.
  • Eligibility. Whether he was correctly told he fell outside whistleblower protection. This is the most checkable of the three, because eligibility turns on defined legal categories rather than judgment.

The political pressure

A coalition of 15 MPs, drawn from several parties, says the death raises "deeply troubling questions" about how whistleblowers are treated when they approach the FCA with concerns about wrongdoing and subsequent retaliation. They want a formal inquiry covering both the death and the regulator's handling of the case.

An internal review answers to the FCA's own board. An external inquiry, which is what the MPs are asking for, would answer to Parliament. The gap between the two is the point to watch. Details of the review's scope, its leadership and whether it will be published have not been established in the reporting reviewed here.

What to watch

Three things will show how far this goes: whether the FCA names an independent reviewer, whether the findings are published, and whether the relevant parliamentary committee takes up the MPs' request. The regulator's whistleblowing regime depends on people believing a report will not cost them their careers. How the FCA handles this review will bear on that belief across the City, whatever it concludes about this one case.
