UK threatens to expel Israeli diplomats over East Jerusalem consulate closure plan

In one sentence
Britain has reportedly warned it will expel 27 Israeli diplomats if Israel closes the UK consulate in East Jerusalem around 8 October, as last-minute talks failed.

Britain has told Israel it will expel Israeli diplomats from London if Jerusalem goes ahead with closing the British consulate in East Jerusalem. According to Israeli and Arab outlets citing officials, the number is 27, one for each British diplomat affected. The closure is due to take effect around 8 October. The two governments have until then to find a way out, and a last-minute mission by a senior British official has so far failed to produce one.

How it got here

The immediate trigger was a British decision last month to announce economic sanctions and import restrictions aimed at Israeli settlements in the West Bank. Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar responded by ordering the consulate shut and revoking the diplomatic credentials of its staff. Reports say the 27 British diplomats were given 30 days to leave.

The consulate is not an ordinary outpost. Britain, like most Western states, runs a separate mission in Jerusalem that deals with Palestinians and is distinct from its embassy in Tel Aviv. It has done so for generations, on the basis that the status of East Jerusalem, which Israel captured in 1967 and later annexed, is unresolved. Israel does not accept that framing. Closing the consulate would be a statement that it will no longer tolerate a foreign mission whose existence implies a divided city.

The British response

The warning was reportedly delivered by the British ambassador to Israel, Simon Walters. It offers a simple trade: each British diplomat forced out would be matched by an Israeli one. Such tit-for-tat expulsions are the standard currency of diplomatic disputes. They are usually kept in reserve, because once they begin, each side finds it hard to stop.

London first tried persuasion. National Security Adviser Jonathan Powell travelled to Israel for undisclosed talks aimed at preventing the closure. They ended without agreement, according to Israeli reports. Sending a national security adviser, rather than relying on ambassadors, suggests the government regarded the matter as serious before it moved to threats.

The Foreign Office has also reportedly taken legal advice from Dominic Grieve, a former Attorney General, that Israel would be acting unlawfully if it closed the consulate on the grounds that it sits in East Jerusalem. The argument is that a country cannot use its claim over a disputed territory to override the Vienna Convention protections that consulates enjoy. This legal position matters for Britain's case. It is also the point on which Israel is least likely to concede, since conceding it would amount to accepting that its sovereignty over East Jerusalem is contested.

These details come mainly from press reports, and neither government has published a full account of the exchange. The 27 figure and the legal advice should be read with that in mind.

What is at stake

The practical economic consequences are limited. Trade between the two countries is modest by the standards of either, and the sanctions that started the dispute target settlement goods, not Israel as a whole. A mutual expulsion would hurt the day-to-day running of both missions more than it would hurt commerce.

The larger effect is political. Britain is among several Western governments that have moved from criticising settlements to attaching costs to them, and Israel's reply, which is to punish the diplomatic presence of the country imposing the costs, shows how far apart the positions have become. If London follows through, it will have shown that its pressure on settlements is backed by more than statements. If it backs down, other capitals considering similar measures will take note.

The next marker is the closure date. Whether Israel delays it, whether the first British departures are enforced, and whether London announces named Israeli diplomats to be expelled will show whether this is a negotiating position or the start of a lasting rupture.
