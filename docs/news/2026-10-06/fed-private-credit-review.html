Fed examines private-credit risks over opacity and bank exposure

In one sentence
New York Fed examiners are reviewing big banks' loans to private-credit firms, a sign regulators cannot yet fully measure how far bank risk runs through the sector.

The Federal Reserve is looking into how much risk the nation's biggest banks have taken on through lending to private-credit firms. The concern is that nobody, regulators included, can say with confidence where that risk ends up. According to an exclusive report from Semafor on 5 October, examiners from the Federal Reserve Bank of New York have been visiting large lenders to review their loans to private-credit managers.

What examiners are asking

The visits have reportedly taken in JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Barclays and Morgan Stanley since the spring. The questions are basic ones: how large the exposure is, how it is managed, and how good the collateral behind it is. A review framed around those questions suggests the Fed does not yet have a reliable picture of its own.

Private credit is lending by non-bank funds directly to companies, outside public bond and syndicated-loan markets. It has grown largely in the space left by post-2008 bank regulation, often at the expense of traditional bank lending. Banks have not left the field, though. Many now lend to the funds themselves, through credit lines and other financing, instead of lending to the end borrower.

That shift is why the opacity matters. Bank lending to non-bank financial institutions has grown from roughly $300bn in 2016 to more than $1.5tn, about 11% of all bank loans, according to figures cited in coverage of the review. A loan to a fund appears on a bank's books as exposure to a financial firm. It does not show what sits beneath the fund. As one official has put it, the opacities between bank involvement and where the funding ends up are such that it is hard to know the exposure.

A wider regulatory unease

The New York Fed's work follows other warnings. The Financial Stability Board published a report on private-credit vulnerabilities in May. It pointed to valuation opacity and reliance on private credit ratings, which can amplify strains in a downturn. Private-credit borrowers also tend to have lower credit quality and higher leverage than comparable borrowers in public markets, the available evidence shows. Reports of markdowns on loans at JPMorgan have added to the attention.

The Fed has not published a formal timeline or deliverable for the review, and the figures above come from secondary reporting and the FSB, not from the central bank. The headline size of the private-credit market, commonly put at around $2tn, is an estimate. The lack of an authoritative figure is part of the problem.

What follows

The practical stakes lie in what the Fed does with the findings. If examiners find exposures larger or less well collateralised than banks have reported, the likely responses are higher capital requirements on lending to non-banks or limits on how far banks can finance the sector. Either would shrink the credit available to mid-sized companies that now rely on private lenders. The next markers are any supervisory guidance that emerges from the review and banks' third-quarter disclosures, due in the coming weeks, which may show how they describe these exposures.
