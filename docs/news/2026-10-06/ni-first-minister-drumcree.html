First minister rejects calls to resign over Drumcree row

In one sentence
Michelle O'Neill has dismissed DUP calls to resign over her role in protests that blocked an Orange march at Drumcree, a row likely to stay within Northern Ireland politics.

Northern Ireland's first minister, Michelle O'Neill, has refused to step down after the Democratic Unionist Party called for her resignation over her part in protests that stopped an Orange Order parade from passing along the Garvaghy Road in Portadown, County Armagh.

O'Neill, of Sinn Féin, had described the planned Orange march on 27 September as "unwanted, sectarian and triumphalist". Large numbers of residents remained on the mainly nationalist road. Police are investigating the protests as unlawful.

Asked about the calls to go, she answered: "I'm going to continue to turn up for the people of the Garvaghy Road. I'm proud to stand with them and I will continue to stand with them."

A dispute with a long memory

Drumcree is one of the oldest flashpoints of the Troubles and the peace that followed. For years, Orange lodges walked from the Drumcree parish church back towards Portadown town centre by way of the Garvaghy Road, which residents regarded as an unwelcome show of dominance. The standoffs in the 1990s brought some of the worst street violence of the period. Since then the parade has been restricted by the Parades Commission, which rules on contested marches.

That history explains why a single march can draw such a charged response. The question of who may walk where is, in Northern Ireland, rarely only about a road.

The politics of the row

The pressure on O'Neill comes mainly from unionists. Timothy Gaston of the Traditional Unionist Voice accused her of being the "first minister of lawlessness" for her role in the street protests. She replied: "I am the first minister of what is right."

The exchange shows the awkwardness of her position. She leads the devolved government for the whole of Northern Ireland, yet she has stood with one community in a dispute with another, and her opponents say that office requires more neutrality. Her supporters say she is standing with residents against a march that she says is sectarian.

The resignation call is unlikely to succeed. The first minister is appointed under the power-sharing rules, and the DUP, which holds the deputy first minister's post, cannot remove her by statement alone. The call is better read as a signal to unionist voters than as a serious attempt to topple her.

What to watch

The next developments are likely to be the outcome of the police investigation into the protests, and whether any further parade applications at Drumcree come before the Parades Commission. Neither looks likely to shake the Stormont institutions. Any sign of the DUP linking the row to its participation in the executive would change that assessment. For now, the dispute looks contained within Northern Ireland's own politics, and there is little sign it will affect UK-wide governance.
