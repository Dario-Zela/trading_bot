Houthis appear to strike Saudi Aramco's Rabigh refinery as Yemeni forces claim control of the Red Sea corridor

In one sentence
Satellite data show a large fire at Aramco's Rabigh refinery after a Houthi strike claim, but damage and market impact are unquantified as Yemeni forces claim gains near Bab el-Mandeb.

Yemen's Houthi movement says it struck an Aramco refinery on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast, and satellite data show a large fire at the site. The claim came on the same day Saudi-backed Yemeni government forces said they had pushed the Houthis out of several areas around the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. Neither the damage to the refinery nor any effect on oil flows has been quantified. Both claims come from parties to the war.

What the imagery shows

The site is the Rabigh refinery, north of Jeddah. A Houthi military spokesperson described the operation against it as "precise and direct" and said it started a fire. The Houthis also said they targeted King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh in the same series of missile and drone attacks. There is a separate report of an attack in Jeddah. Together these suggest a multi-target operation, but the Jeddah report is not independently confirmed.

The independent evidence is thermal and visual. Oilprice.com reported that satellite detection showed a fire radiating about 410 MW, and imagery circulating online showed thick black smoke. The imagery is consistent with a fire at or near storage tanks. A storage-tank fire is a different thing from damage to the processing units that determine how much crude a refinery can run. Tank fires can be dramatic and still leave throughput largely intact. Tank damage can also cost a refinery days or weeks of operations through lost feedstock and product handling. The available evidence does not say which case this is.

I found no Aramco statement on the damage and no Saudi estimate of lost throughput. Aramco has put out refinery fires before without disclosing the effect on output, and the absence of a statement here should not be read as either good or bad news. In August, CNBC reported that Aramco extinguished a refinery fire after a Houthi claim of attack, so this is not the first such episode this year.

A corridor claimed, a coast still exposed

The second thread is the ground war. Yemeni officials said Saudi-backed forces took several areas around Bab el-Mandeb, with Saudi-led coalition air cover, and had "secured" the approaches to the strait. The strait is the southern gateway to the Red Sea, and roughly 12–15% of global maritime trade is commonly said to pass through it, depending on the measure. Earlier Houthi control of the Yemeni side of the passage gave the group leverage over that traffic.

The refinery strike undercuts the control narrative, though it does not refute it. Holding ground near a strait is a different matter from stopping missiles and drones launched from farther inland. The Houthis' long-range reach against Saudi territory does not depend on the coastline around the strait. The reported timing is also telling. The Houthis framed their strikes as a response to the offensive, which suggests the two events are linked. A government push on the ground may be met with escalation against Saudi infrastructure rather than a retreat.

What it means for oil

Houthi attacks on Saudi energy sites go back years, and the market has learned to discount most of them. Rabigh sits on the Red Sea, so it is more exposed to Yemen than Gulf-coast facilities. It is also a refinery, not an export terminal, so a long outage would mainly affect product supply and Saudi domestic balances rather than crude exports.

The case for concern is cumulative. The same week brings an announced offensive near a chokepoint and a strike on a Red Sea refinery. It also comes alongside unresolved questions over Hormuz (see Iran and Hormuz), which leaves less spare routing capacity if two corridors are threatened at once. I found no reliable report of a price move linked to the Rabigh fire, so any market effect is unquantified.

Three things will settle it. The first is whether Aramco or Saudi officials give a damage figure or a restart timeline for Rabigh. The second is whether Houthi launches continue after the claimed expulsion, particularly against Jeddah or other Red Sea sites. The third is whether tanker insurers and owners change how they treat Red Sea transits. Absent those, the story stays a fire of unknown size and a map that both sides describe differently.
