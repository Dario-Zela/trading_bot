Average five-year fixed mortgage rate returns to 6% for the first time in three years

In one sentence
The average UK five-year fixed mortgage rate hit 6.00% on 5 October, a three-year high, as rising gilt and swap yields pushed lenders to pull sub-5% deals.

The average rate on a five-year fixed mortgage in the UK reached 6.00% on 5 October, according to Moneyfacts. That is the first time it has stood at that level since late September 2023, when it was 6.03%. The average two-year fix is close behind at 5.98%, its highest since December 2023. For borrowers who spent 2024 and 2025 assuming each remortgage would be cheaper than the last, the direction has reversed.

The move

The rise was quick. At the start of September, Moneyfacts counted 1,494 fixed-rate deals priced below 5%, excluding Northern Ireland-only products. Nine remain, a fall of 99%. Counting Northern Ireland products, the total went from 1,691 to 107.

Lenders have been repricing in volume. In September, Barclays raised rates four times, and HSBC, Lloyds, Nationwide, NatWest, Santander and TSB each did so three times. Variable-rate deals have held up much better: those below 5% fell only from 411 to 389. The pressure is concentrated in fixed pricing, which is set off swap rates, and those track gilt yields rather than the Bank of England's policy rate.

That is the mechanism. Moneyfacts described how, over recent weeks, "pricing margins among major lenders" came under immediate pressure from renewed swap-rate volatility. A lender that sells a five-year fix must hedge it at the five-year swap rate. When gilt yields rise, swaps follow within hours, and the product reprices within days. Property industry commentary this week attributes the swap move to turmoil in the gilt market and to expectations that the Bank of England may raise rates. The specific yield levels and the data behind that repricing are not set out in the sources reviewed here, so the size of the gilt move is not quantified in this article.

What it costs

The arithmetic is simple. Interest on a £250,000 balance at 6% comes to about £15,000 in the first year, before any capital repayment. At the 4–5% rates available much of last year, it was roughly £10,000 to £12,500. For a household remortgaging off a deal struck in the low-rate period, the difference lands directly in the monthly budget. Property News puts the average rate across the whole outstanding mortgage stock at 4.28%, so a large cohort of borrowers still has a repricing ahead of it.

The disappearance of sub-5% products matters beyond the headline average. The average describes the middle of the market. The cheapest deals, the ones that went to borrowers with large deposits, are now almost gone, so the options that remained for people with strong credit have narrowed.

The housing data are mixed. Property News cites an average UK house price of £298,000, up 3.6% on the year, with London and Surrey seen as the most exposed to higher borrowing costs and cities such as Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Liverpool and Newcastle more resilient. Higher rates typically work through to prices with a lag, through weaker approvals first.

What it signals

For rates traders, the mortgage market is a delayed readout of the gilt curve. A 6% five-year fix says the swap market has priced out the easing that borrowers had been counting on, and it adds to the evidence that UK term funding has become more expensive. The same shift tightens credit conditions more generally, since lenders pass on hedging costs and stricter affordability tests follow.

It also bears on the wider credit picture, which central banks and regulators are already watching. The Federal Reserve's review of private credit, covered elsewhere in this edition, is a reminder that funding stress shows up in several places.

The near-term catalysts are plain. The first is the Bank of England's next rate decision, which will show whether the Monetary Policy Committee treats the gilt move as noise or as a reason to tighten. The second is the next Moneyfacts reading, due in the coming days, which will show whether lenders have finished repricing or whether the average moves further above 6%. If swap rates settle, lenders have room to trim margins and the sub-5% products could return in small numbers. If they keep rising, the 2023 peak is the next reference point.
