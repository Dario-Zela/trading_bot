Pentagon drops Anthropic tools as Trump puts a spy chief in charge of AI taskforce

In one sentence
The Pentagon has stopped using Anthropic's AI tools after labelling it a supply-chain risk, as Trump puts spy chief Jay Clayton in charge of a new AI taskforce.

The US Department of Defense has stopped using Anthropic's AI tools, months after Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth formally branded the company a supply-chain risk. The same week, President Trump named his Director of National Intelligence, Jay Clayton, to run a new White House AI taskforce. The two moves are separate decisions, but they point the same way. Washington is treating AI vendors as a security question, not just a software purchase.

How the blacklist came about

The dispute began as a contract fight over guardrails. Anthropic was, by its own account and press reports, the only AI company deployed on the Pentagon's classified networks. It wanted written limits that would bar the military from using its Claude models for mass surveillance of Americans or to power fully autonomous weapons. The administration declined to accept those terms.

In late February, after Trump and Hegseth accused the company of endangering national security, the Pentagon told Anthropic it and its products were deemed a supply-chain risk, "effective immediately." The label is more usually associated with foreign suppliers seen as compromised, and it could force other government contractors to drop Claude too. Anthropic called the move "unprecedented and unlawful" and sued.

People familiar with the matter told the BBC that Claude was still in use at the department as late as the week of the designation, for research, analysis, intelligence gathering and in military operations against Iran. The designation was a blacklist applied to a tool that was already embedded in operations. Ceasing to use it has taken months.

The courts have not been kind to the government's case. A judge temporarily blocked the ban in March. On 28 August, US District Judge Rita Lin ruled in a 59-page order that the designation was illegal and baseless, finding the Pentagon had punished Anthropic for publicly criticising its stance on battlefield AI. The government was expected to fight that ruling. The exact legal footing for the department's current stop in use, and whether it complies with the court's order, was not clear from the reporting reviewed here.

A spy chief takes the wheel

On Sunday 4 October, Trump announced what he calls the "Super Intelligence Force", his preferred rebrand of AI policy. Clayton, a former chair of the Securities and Exchange Commission and former US Attorney for the Southern District of New York, will lead it. The other named members are Andrew Ferguson, chair of the Federal Trade Commission, and Emil Michael, the Pentagon's undersecretary for research and engineering. The group reports to Trump and chief of staff Susie Wiles.

Its brief, in Trump's words, is to "coordinate the Federal Government's engagement with Consumers, Public Interest Groups, Religious Organizations, Critical Infrastructure Providers, and Super Intelligence Companies." Clayton said it would have 120 days to produce a report on AI's risks and opportunities and on what role the federal government should play. He replaces venture capitalist David Sacks as the White House's AI lead, whose term as special adviser expired.

The announcement followed a Tuesday meeting between Trump and tech leaders including Elon Musk, Mark Zuckerberg and Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei. Amodei's presence matters. The White House is in the odd position of convening the head of a company its own Pentagon has blacklisted. In April Trump said Anthropic was "shaping up" and that a deal for defence use was possible.

The lineup is telling. A securities lawyer who now oversees the intelligence agencies replaces a venture investor. A competition and consumer-protection regulator and a Pentagon technology official sit alongside him. That is a security-and-enforcement bench, not an industry-promotion one. No public detail yet exists on whether the taskforce will touch procurement rules.

What it means for AI vendors

The evidence supports a cautious reading. Two data points do not make a doctrine. The Pentagon episode grew from a specific clash over usage restrictions, and a federal judge has found the government's handling unlawful. The taskforce has a 120-day clock and no published powers.

Still, the pattern is plain enough. Contract terms that a vendor writes into its own models, such as limits on surveillance and weapons use, can now provoke a national-security response. And the person steering policy comes from the intelligence world.

For investors, the practical question is how much revenue is exposed. Defence and intelligence work is a small part of the business for most AI labs today, and Anthropic's commercial customers have not been barred from using it. The larger risk is precedent. If a supply-chain designation can be used against a domestic supplier over a policy disagreement, any AI firm that depends on government contracts, or sells to those who do, has to price in political risk alongside technical performance.

What to watch

Three things will show where this goes. The first is the government's appeal of Judge Lin's ruling, which will decide whether the designation survives. The second is whether Clayton's taskforce says anything about procurement or vendor terms before its 120-day report, due around early February. The third is whether the Pentagon's other AI suppliers accept the terms Anthropic refused.
