Conference Board employment trends index slips to 107.56

In one sentence
The Conference Board's Employment Trends Index slipped to 107.56 in September from 108.08, a small but notable softening in a gauge meant to anticipate US payroll changes.

The Conference Board's Employment Trends Index slipped to 107.56 in September from 108.08 in August, a fall of 0.52 points. It is a small move. It is also the sort of one that labour-market watchers look at closely when the headline jobs numbers still look steady.

The index is built to lead, not describe. It combines several labour-market indicators into a single gauge intended to anticipate changes in nonfarm payrolls roughly six months ahead. Payroll reports tell investors what hiring did last month. The ETI is an attempt to say what it will do next.

What the number says

A drop from 108.08 to 107.56 is about 0.5% in a month (0.52 divided by 108.08 is 0.48%). On its own that is within the range of ordinary monthly noise for a composite index, and a single reading says little about direction.

The case for paying attention rests on context. The decline sits alongside a broader softening in leading economic indicators, according to the triage summary of Benzinga's report. The reading is therefore less a verdict than an addition to a pattern. A lone dip can be reversed or revised away. A run of them tends to show up later in payroll data.

The index also remains well above where it would sit in a contraction. At 107.56, the labour market it describes is cooling at the margin, not deteriorating. The distinction matters, because the policy implications of the two are very different.

Why traders care

The labour market is central to two things markets price constantly: the path of Federal Reserve policy and the strength of consumer spending. If hiring holds up, the case for the Fed to stay on hold is easier to make, and household income supports demand. If hiring slows, the argument for easing strengthens, but so does the risk to consumption.

That is why a modest signal from a leading indicator can matter more than its size suggests. Recent payroll figures have looked stable. An index that anticipates payrolls turning softer implies that stability may not last through the fourth quarter. Traders holding a view that the Fed stays put, and inflation forecasts that assume steady wage growth, would have reason to test both assumptions against it.

The caveat is that the ETI is one input among many. It has missed turns before, and a decline of this size does not by itself move the odds on a rate decision. It is better read as a prompt to look harder at the data that follows.

What to watch

The next test is the October ETI, which will show whether September was a one-off or the start of a sequence. Before that, the next monthly payrolls release will show whether actual hiring is beginning to reflect what the index has been signalling. Two further declines in the ETI alongside flat or falling payroll growth would make the cooling story harder to dismiss. A rebound would suggest September was noise.
