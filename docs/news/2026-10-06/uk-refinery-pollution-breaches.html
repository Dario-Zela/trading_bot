UK oil refinery broke toxic pollution limits dozens of times, documents show

In one sentence
Documents show Stanlow refinery in Cheshire committed 58 permit breaches in 2023–24, including cyanide, benzene and sulphur dioxide, while enforcement remains slow and partial.

One of the UK's largest oil refineries breached its environmental permit 58 times across 2023 and 2024, according to documents shared with the BBC. The breaches included releases of cyanide, benzene, oil and sulphur dioxide. The case is being treated as a test of how firmly regulators enforce the rules on a sector that is central to the country's fuel supply.

The site is Stanlow, in Ellesmere Port, Cheshire. It is the second-largest refinery in the UK, processes around 10 million tonnes of crude a year and supplies roughly 18% of the country's road transport fuel. Its operator, EET Fuels, formerly Essar, bought it from Shell in 2011 for £842m.

What the documents show

The 58 recorded violations involved discharges to local watercourses (Thornton Brook, the River Gowy and the Mersey Estuary) as well as emissions to air. The Mersey Estuary is a Site of Special Scientific Interest.

The reported exceedances include:

  • Cyanide released to waterways at more than eight times the permitted level.
  • Sulphur dioxide emitted to air at seven times the permitted level.
  • Oil released at more than 13 times the permitted level on at least one occasion.
  • Benzene and heavy oils, also recorded among the breaches.

Two incidents stand out. In July 2023 an oil leak contaminated groundwater. The Environment Agency classed it as "significant". EET Fuels told the BBC the loss was on a "low scale" and "strictly localised to two boreholes, all well within the Stanlow site itself".

In September 2024, more than a tonne of oil escaped over about 36 hours. The documents indicate the company was late giving the quantity estimate, which is required within 24 hours. Drone footage showed oil spread over more than a mile, with brown discolouration around the reedbed. A closure notice on cockle beds ran for five weeks while testing was carried out. The estuary supports wading birds such as dunlin, along with European eels.

Enforcement

The Environment Agency says it is investigating. A criminal investigation into the 2024 incident is under way, and a decision on the 2023 offences is pending. Natural England dropped its own inquiry. It described the incident as "regrettable" but cited ambiguity in the permit over oil-release limits.

That last point matters. If a permit's wording is vague enough to stop a conservation regulator pursuing a case, the weakness lies in how permits are written as well as in how they are policed.

The operator has history. EET Fuels pleaded guilty this year to 12 charges of breaching environmental law between 2018 and 2020, with sentencing due next month. Together with the 58 later permit violations, that is a long run of problems, and the outcome of the older cases will show what sanction courts attach to them.

EET Fuels says it "takes environmental responsibilities extremely seriously". It points to £24m of infrastructure investment and an improved Environment Agency compliance rating, from F in 2023 to E in 2024. On that scale, E is still near the bottom.

What it means

The evidence supports a narrow conclusion: the breaches were frequent, and formal consequences have so far been slow or limited. It does not show that regulation is failing across the sector. That would take comparable data from other sites.

The broader stake is credibility. UK air-quality and emissions targets depend on large industrial operators complying with their permits, and compliance depends on a believable prospect of penalty.

The next markers are the sentencing for the 2018–2020 offences next month and the Environment Agency's decisions on the 2023 and 2024 incidents. Those outcomes will show whether enforcement at Stanlow has teeth.
