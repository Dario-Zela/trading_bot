India's stocks slide despite solid GDP growth

In one sentence
Indian equities fell for a second month in September despite 7.8% GDP growth, as foreign selling, slowing profit growth and 5.3% US yields outweighed the domestic data.

Indian shares have fallen for two straight months even though the economy is growing faster than almost any other large one. The gap between a strong GDP print and a weak equity market has become the main puzzle for emerging-market desks. The usual explanations are valuation, foreign selling and slowing earnings. A fourth, US bond yields, may matter more than any of them.

The numbers

The Nifty 50 lost 6.1% in September, its worst month since March. The Sensex fell 5.8%, a second consecutive monthly decline. On 1 October the Sensex opened at 72,192.89, down 287 points, and the Nifty at 22,543.70, down about 77.

The economy has not slowed in step. GDP grew 7.8% in the April–June quarter. Growth at that pace would normally support equities, which is what makes the fall notable.

Who is selling

Foreign investors are the clearest source of pressure. They sold about $2.7bn of Indian shares in September, and net foreign selling for the year has reached roughly $26.8bn. Local reports put foreign institutional selling on one session just before 1 October at ₹9,980 crore, a large single-day figure.

The flow data fits a rebalancing story. India has long traded at a premium to other emerging markets, and a market priced for steady growth leaves little margin when the earnings numbers soften. Some market estimates put profit growth at 13–14%, down from 18–19% in the first quarter. That is still healthy, but the slowdown is the kind of change that valuation-sensitive funds act on. This piece could not verify current forward P/E figures against five- and ten-year averages or against regional peers, so no valuation multiple is cited.

The rate and oil backdrop

The US 10-year Treasury yield has stayed near 5.3%. A yield that high gives global allocators a competitive alternative to emerging-market equity risk, and India is one of the largest and most liquid places to sell when they trim exposure. A softer rupee and elevated oil prices add to the pressure. India is a large net importer of crude, and the Hormuz and Red Sea disruptions that have kept energy markets on edge weigh on its terms of trade.

The evidence does not settle whether this is mainly contagion or a local valuation reset. Two things favour contagion: the selling has coincided with higher US yields, and the domestic growth data have not deteriorated. One thing favours a local reckoning: earnings growth has slowed while foreign selling has continued all year, not only in the last few weeks. Both can be true at once.

What to watch

For positioning, the questions are whether foreign outflows slow from September's pace and whether US 10-year yields move back below 5%. The next test is the July–September earnings season, where further cuts to profit-growth estimates would support the valuation reading over the contagion one. Until then, India's strong GDP leaves the market's decline unexplained by the headline numbers alone.
