CFTC launches first formal rulemaking on crypto markets

In one sentence
The CFTC has opened its first formal crypto rulemaking, proposing rules for leveraged trading and the exchanges that offer it, while leaving unlevered spot markets outside its reach.

For most of the past decade the Commodity Futures Trading Commission has regulated crypto the way a lifeguard watches a very large beach: by running toward whoever is visibly in trouble. On Monday it began, formally, to put up fences. Chairman Michael Selig launched an advance notice of proposed rulemaking (ANPRM) on crypto asset transactions and markets, the agency's first rulemaking exercise aimed squarely at the sector.

An ANPRM is the earliest rung of the federal rulemaking ladder. It binds no one. It asks the public questions, and the answers shape the proposal that follows. Stakeholders have 60 days to comment once the notice appears in the Federal Register. Final rules on this kind of path typically take 12 to 18 months to arrive, so nothing changes in an exchange's compliance manual this quarter.

Two frameworks, one boundary

The notice outlines two complementary frameworks. Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions, or CTX, would set conduct rules for leveraged, margined or financed crypto transactions, the territory covered by Section 2(c)(2)(D) of the Commodity Exchange Act. Regulation Crypto Asset Markets, or CAM, would create a new registration category for the platforms that offer them. The agency says the aim is "fit-for-purpose" rules that prevent abusive practices and impose a uniform national regime.

The dividing line is leverage. Crypto activity involving borrowed funds, margin or financing falls inside the CFTC's perimeter. Plain, unlevered buying of bitcoin or ether does not. The agency can police fraud and manipulation in those spot markets, but it lacks authority to write market-structure rules for them, and state money-transmission law still governs them. CoinDesk notes that this leaves out the largest slice of crypto activity by volume.

That is why the notice is better read as a boundary-drawing exercise than a full rulebook. The ambiguity over where CFTC jurisdiction starts has been a feature of the market, and it has created room for arbitrage between venues and jurisdictions. A defined category for leveraged trading narrows that room.

Why now

Selig has framed the move as a response to Congress. Lawmakers have not delivered a crypto market structure bill to President Trump's desk, and the agency is acting within the statute it already has. He has said the rules should "codify a pathway for crypto asset exchanges to operate under uniform national oversight." The CFTC's move also follows the SEC, which has been proposing crypto rules of its own.

The posture shift is real. Enforcement establishes what is forbidden after the fact; rulemaking has to say in advance what is required: capital, margin, surveillance, disclosure, how orders are handled. Some of the largest US venues, including Coinbase and Crypto.com, already hold designated contract market status and could plausibly seek the narrower CAM registration.

What it means for traders and venues

For firms running algorithmic strategies in crypto derivatives, the practical questions sit in the details the ANPRM invites comment on. These include whether risk controls, such as pre-trade limits and kill switches, become mandatory; how order routing is supervised across venues; and what retail leverage caps, if any, apply. Exchanges that route orders or margin positions in ways that have been lightly supervised should expect to restructure both, if the rules land as the equities and futures playbook suggests they might.

The 60-day comment period doubles as a poll. It will show whether the industry prefers federally supervised platforms for complex products, which could narrow the spot gap in practice even if the statute does not move.

The next data points are concrete: the Federal Register publication date, which starts the comment clock; the comment letters from exchanges and trading firms; and whether Congress revives market structure legislation that would make parts of this exercise redundant, or widen the CFTC's reach to spot markets. Until then, the rulebook is a set of questions.
