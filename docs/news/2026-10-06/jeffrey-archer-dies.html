Jeffrey Archer, novelist and former Tory peer, dies aged 86

In one sentence
Jeffrey Archer, the bestselling novelist and former Tory peer jailed for perjury in 2001, has died aged 86, a day before his final novel's publication.

Jeffrey Archer, the bestselling novelist who rose to the House of Lords and fell to a prison cell, has died at 86. His publisher, HarperCollins, announced his death on Monday. It said he died suddenly and peacefully at home, between writing sessions. No cause was given.

The timing has a novelist's neatness that Archer, a connoisseur of the twist ending, would probably have appreciated. His final novel, Adam & Eve, was due to be published on Tuesday, the day after he died. On Sunday he had signed books for hundreds of fans at a sold-out event with the broadcaster Gyles Brandreth at the Leicester Square Theatre in London. Brandreth wrote that Archer had been in "most wonderful, ebullient form".

The writer

Archer's books have sold more than 300 million copies over a career of five decades. He made his name with Not a Penny More, Not a Penny Less, a caper about swindled investors that drew on his own financial ruin after a failed investment. Kane and Abel, the 1979 saga of two men born on the same day on opposite sides of the Atlantic, became his signature work and an American television mini-series. Later came the Clifton Chronicles and a steady run of thrillers and short-story collections, each timed to the autumn bookshops.

Critics were seldom kind. His prose was plain and his plots ran on coincidence and momentum. Readers did not mind. He wrote the sort of book people pack for an airport and finish before landing.

The politician

Archer entered the Commons as a Conservative MP in 1969 and left in 1974, again over money. He returned to public life under Margaret Thatcher, becoming deputy chairman of the party in 1985. He resigned the next year after a newspaper reported that he had paid a sex worker, Monica Coghlan, to leave the country. He sued the Daily Star and won substantial libel damages in 1987. He was made a life peer in 1992.

In 1999 he was the Conservative choice for the first London mayoral election. He withdrew when a former friend's evidence showed that he had lied about the 1987 case, in which he had presented a false alibi.

The fall

In 2001 Archer was convicted of perjury and perverting the course of justice and sentenced to four years in prison. He served two. The conviction ended any prospect of high office and cost him his place in the Lords. He wrote about the experience in his Prison Diaries, which sold well. The pattern held to the end: whatever happened to him became material.

He remained a figure Britain found hard to file. He was a fabulist in public life and a reliable storyteller on the page. The sales figures say the public forgave him, or at least kept buying. Obituaries will argue over which part of the career lasted longer. The books will stay in print for the foreseeable future, and Adam & Eve arrives in shops on Tuesday as planned.
