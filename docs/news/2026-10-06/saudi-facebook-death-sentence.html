Rights groups urge Saudi Arabia to spare man sentenced to death over Facebook post

In one sentence
A 24-year-old Sri Lankan migrant worker faces execution in Saudi Arabia over a deleted Facebook comment, as Colombo and rights groups press Riyadh for clemency.

A 24-year-old Sri Lankan migrant worker in Saudi Arabia has been sentenced to death over a comment he posted on Facebook, and rights groups and his own government are pressing Riyadh to spare him. Anojan Sivarasa was convicted of blasphemy after an appeals court upgraded what had been a prison term. Saudi authorities have published no execution date, and it is unclear when, or whether, the sentence will be carried out.

How a comment became a capital case

The case began in Sri Lanka. In late June, footage circulated widely online of a young Muslim man kicking a dog as it followed Hindu pilgrims. The video set off religious arguments on social media. During the exchange, one user posted derogatory remarks about a Hindu deity. Anojan, who was working in Saudi Arabia, replied in Tamil with a post insulting the Prophet Muhammad.

By the account of reports on the case, he deleted the comment quickly and posted a video apologising. It had already been screenshotted and passed to his employer and then to Saudi officials. He was arrested in July.

The first-instance court sentenced him to five years in prison and a fine of 3 million Saudi riyals, roughly $800,000. Then prosecutors appealed, and on 17 September the appellate court granted their request to have the case dealt with under Sharia law. The result was a death sentence.

Pleas from Colombo and from home

The case has caused alarm in Sri Lanka. President Anura Kumara Dissanayake met the family on 21 September and promised diplomatic efforts. Government bodies and several political parties are coordinating appeals for clemency. Buddhist and Hindu religious leaders have submitted a joint memorandum. A four-member legal delegation is reported to be travelling to Saudi Arabia.

The family's last contact with him was on 28 September. His brother, Hamsidan, said: "My brother hasn't called for two days." His mother has appealed directly for mercy: "I plead for mercy for the wrongs committed by my son. Please forgive him and send him back to us."

A familiar gap between image and practice

Rights groups say executions specifically for blasphemy are comparatively rare in the kingdom. Drug and murder cases account for most of its death sentences. Online speech has nonetheless produced severe punishments before. In 2023, Amnesty International and UN experts condemned the death sentence imposed on Mohammad bin Nasser al-Ghamdi over posts on social media. That sentence was later quashed on appeal, and Amnesty called in August 2024 for his immediate release.

The al-Ghamdi outcome shows that appeals can work, and that international attention may help. It also shows how arbitrary the process is. The same legal system can treat an online remark as a crime for which the state may take a life, then reverse course. Anojan's case adds a further vulnerability. As a migrant worker he has little standing, and he may have limited access to counsel or consular support.

The case sits awkwardly beside Riyadh's reform narrative. The kingdom has spent years courting investors and tourists with promises of modernisation. The reforms have been mostly economic and social, and they have not extended to speech, religion or the penal code. A capital sentence for an apologised-for and deleted comment suggests the limits are firm. The evidence for that reading is the sentence itself, upgraded on a prosecutor's appeal after a milder ruling.

What to watch

The immediate question is whether the Sri Lankan legal delegation is given access to the court record and to Anojan himself. Further review in the Saudi system is possible, and so is a royal intervention. Any ruling from the Saudi side would most likely come without public notice, as the sentence did, so the next sign may be a statement from Colombo or from the family.
