Ministers preparing to cut heat pump subsidies to fund defence spending

In one sentence
Ministers are reportedly preparing to cut hundreds of millions from the £2.7bn Boiler Upgrade Scheme to help fund defence, but the figures are unconfirmed ahead of the Budget.

Ministers are preparing to cut the money available for heat pump subsidies to help pay for higher defence spending, according to a Guardian report on 12 October that rests on anonymous sources. No cut has been announced, and the government has not confirmed the figures. If the reporting is right, it would put net-zero support and rearmament in direct competition for the same pot of public money before the Budget.

What has been reported

As summarised in secondary accounts of the Guardian's story, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero has been told to find about £2bn of savings from its budget. Some of that is expected to come from the Boiler Upgrade Scheme, the main grant for heat pumps in England and Wales. The scheme would not be scrapped. Instead, hundreds of millions of pounds could be taken from its roughly £2.7bn allocation.

The scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump. A temporary uplift to £9,000 is available for eligible off-gas-grid homes that heat with oil or LPG, running from 21 July 2026 to 31 March 2027. Air-to-air heat pumps get £2,500 and biomass boilers £5,000. The scheme has been extended to 2028.

A department spokesperson was quoted as saying that savings for the defence plan are a small part of its overall budget, which received a significant uplift at the spending review, and that details of national security funding are due in the autumn.

The gaps in the reporting matter. The size of the cut is given only as a range. The Budget date and the total defence sum have not been confirmed. Nor is it clear whether the cut would reduce future funding or affect grants for households already in the pipeline. Those two outcomes would have very different effects.

Why the design of the cut matters

The grant is paid to the installer as a discount at the point of sale, so it is built into the price that households see. Installers quote against it, and manufacturers set production and training plans on the expectation that it will continue. A cut that only trims future budgets would leave existing quotes intact but could shorten the scheme's life. A lower per-unit grant, or a pause in voucher approvals, would act on demand immediately.

That is why industry groups usually stress certainty over generosity. Trade press has carried reactions to the reports, though the coverage reviewed here goes beyond what the Guardian's sources support in places. One outlet claims about 250,000 homes would lose funding, a number that could not be traced to a primary source and is not used here.

Germany offers a comparison. Berlin's draft 2027 budget has been reported to cut heat pump subsidies by about €2.1bn through 2030, while raising defence and security spending towards more than €200bn by 2030. The parallel is not exact, since the two countries' schemes differ, but both governments appear to be treating climate subsidies as a source of savings when defence claims rise.

The fiscal backdrop

The pressure is not coming from defence alone. The Institute for Fiscal Studies has warned that the Budget is a "work of fiction" without a plan for about £35bn of pressures, with gilt yields still high. Markets also price a high probability of a Bank of England rate rise on 5 November. Higher borrowing costs narrow the Chancellor's room to fund new commitments without trimming old ones. Against a total in the tens of billions, a few hundred million from a heat pump grant is small. It is also easy to find, because the money is discretionary and the scheme has a defined budget.

The trade-off may be easier to justify in the short term than over the longer term. Heating is a major source of household emissions, and the government's carbon budgets assume a rapid shift away from gas boilers. Cutting the grant that makes that shift affordable would tend to push the cost into later years, when more installations would be needed in less time.

What to watch

Two events will settle the question. The first is the autumn announcement on national security funding, which the department has said will set out the detail. The second is the Budget, where the scheme's allocation will appear in the department's settlement. Until then, installers and manufacturers have only a leak to plan around, and the question that matters most to them is whether the Boiler Upgrade Scheme's current rules are protected for households already in the queue.
