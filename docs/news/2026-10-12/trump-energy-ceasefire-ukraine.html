UKRAINE · ENERGY TRUCE · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-12 · 4 min read

Trump announces Russia-Ukraine 'energy ceasefire' as Zelensky says it was news to him and Russia hits power plants

In one sentence
Trump's announced Russia-Ukraine 'energy ceasefire' was disputed within hours, with Zelensky caught off guard, Moscow silent and Russia reportedly striking Ukrainian power plants.

President Donald Trump announced at the weekend an "energy ceasefire" between Russia and Ukraine. Within hours, the announcement was disputed on three fronts. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said it was "news for me". Moscow had not publicly responded at the time of reporting. And Russia was reported to have struck Ukrainian power plants soon afterwards. The White House has described the deal as covering oil refineries first, with power grids possibly to follow. That is a much narrower pause than the phrase suggests.

This account draws on reporting by CNN, Axios and NBC. Several details, including the exact wording of the announcement and the sequence of strikes, rest on those outlets' accounts and could not be independently confirmed.

A truce with no agreed text

An energy truce is not a new idea in this war. Both sides have hit each other's fuel and power infrastructure for years. Russia has targeted Ukrainian generation and transmission, and Ukraine has targeted Russian refineries and oil depots. An earlier version of the idea was floated in previous rounds of US-led diplomacy. Kyiv has said in the past that it would accept a pause on energy strikes if Moscow did the same, and that Washington would carry the proposal to the Russians.

What is different this time is the order of events. A ceasefire normally follows an agreement between the parties. Here, the announcement came first, and by Zelensky's account Kyiv had not been told. A truce whose basic terms are contested before it takes effect has little to stand on. Neither side has said publicly what is covered, when it starts, or how a violation would be judged.

The White House framing, refineries first and grids perhaps later, deepens the problem. The two sides do not have symmetrical interests in the two kinds of target:

  • Refineries are mainly a Russian vulnerability. Ukrainian drones have repeatedly hit them, and a pause on those strikes would chiefly benefit Moscow.
  • Power plants and the grid are mainly a Ukrainian vulnerability. They are what Russian missiles and drones have targeted, and the cold season is when losing them hurts most.

If the truce covers the first category and not the second, Ukraine would be giving up its most effective long-range pressure while its own grid stays exposed. That is the central concern in Kyiv, and the reported strikes on power plants within hours of the announcement did nothing to ease it.

Moscow's silence

The Kremlin's silence is itself informative. Russia has often used ambiguity in US-brokered proposals to accept the parts that suit it and stall on the rest. A public rejection would be costly with Washington. A public acceptance would constrain Russian operations at the start of winter, when strikes on the grid carry the most weight. Saying nothing, while the strikes continue, leaves both options open.

The strikes may not prove a deliberate breach. If Russia never agreed to anything, there was nothing to breach, and if the White House view of a refinery-only first phase is right, power plants were outside the pause. That ambiguity is the weakness. A truce that cannot say whether an attack on a power station violates it cannot be monitored, and so cannot easily be enforced.

A widening rift with Washington

The episode comes amid visible strain between Washington and Kyiv. According to the NBC and Axios reports behind this story, Trump has suggested that Kyiv needs a new leader. Zelensky has called a diesel deal a "birthday gift" to Vladimir Putin. The wording and context of both remarks should be read in the original reports; they were not independently verified for this article. The diesel reference appears to connect to the US move to ease sanctions on Russian diesel, covered separately in this edition, which Washington says is meant to cool fuel prices and which senators and London have criticised.

Taken together, the threads form one picture. Washington is pursuing short, headline-friendly pauses and sanctions relief tied to energy prices. Kyiv is arguing that these steps relieve pressure on Russia without protecting Ukrainian civilians. European allies have to decide whether to follow the American line or hold a separate one, a question that grows more pressing as London and senators push back on the diesel decision.

Why markets are watching

The energy angle matters outside the war. Brent crude is near $100 a barrel, supported by Houthi attacks on Saudi targets, and a lasting halt to strikes on Russian refineries would ease some pressure on product markets. A truce that does not hold would not. Fuel prices are already central to the inflation debate in the US, UK and euro area.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is a real pause or a headline. The first is whether the Kremlin confirms the arrangement, and in what terms. The second is whether the White House publishes a written scope that says whether grids are in or out, and on what timetable. The third is whether strikes on either side's energy sites stop or continue in the coming days. If Russian attacks on power plants go on without a US response, that will answer the question of whether Washington-brokered pauses can bind either party. In that case, Ukraine would face the winter with its grid still a target.
