Roofing scammers con homeowners out of thousands of pounds

In one sentence
Citizens Advice logged 2,205 suspected roofing scams in the year to August, up from 2,098, with three quarters of fraudsters using doorstep pressure tactics.

A roof is the one part of a house its owner cannot easily inspect. That is the business model of the roofing scammer, and it is why the trade keeps topping the charts of consumer complaints. Citizens Advice says roofing fraud accounted for about one in ten of all the scams reported to it over the past year, making it the most common type.

The figures, reported by the BBC, come from the charity's Consumer Service. In the year to August it received about 9,000 complaints about roofing work, covering construction, repairs and weatherproofing. Of those, 2,205 — roughly one in four — were suspected scams. A year earlier the number was 2,098. That is a rise of about 5%, so the picture is of a stubborn problem getting somewhat worse, not a sudden surge.

How the pitch works

Three quarters of the fraudsters used "doorstep" sales tactics, and victims described aggressive or intimidating behaviour. The pattern is familiar: a small job, a larger problem discovered once someone is up a ladder, and a price that climbs while the homeowner stands underneath.

Charlotte, 65, who lives alone in north-east England, found traders online who quoted £250 to fix a leaking gutter. They then said a further £2,500 of work was needed, and she paid £3,500. The next day she could see no sign that anything had been done. She said she felt afraid because the men knew she was on her own.

Crystal, 68, in the Midlands, felt pressured into having her roof redone and paid £5,500. The contractor sent his son, who left the eaves unfinished. Heavy rain then flooded a wall. A neighbour's house was damaged too, after lead was stripped from the chimney and let bricks fall into the loft and rain in. "I can't sleep. I don't eat properly," she said.

Charlotte's case is a reminder that the cold caller is not the only route in. Finding a trader online can end the same way, and the tactics that follow — inflated scope, pressure, payment up front — are the same.

What is being done

Dame Clare Moriarty, the charity's chief executive, said: "Scammers exploit the fact that roofs are out of sight and reach." Her advice is practical: ask for references and a written agreement, be wary of unsolicited callers, and keep photos and receipts. Traders must give written notice of the right to cancel. Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, a homeowner can ask for work to be put right, or for a refund, if it was not done with reasonable care and skill.

On enforcement, Lord Bichard, chair of National Trading Standards, called rogue traders "criminals deliberately preying on people in their own homes" and roofing fraud "particularly cruel". He said Trading Standards would pursue offenders through the courts. The BBC report cites no new prosecutions or powers, and nothing in it suggests a regulatory change.

Court outcomes do exist. In Nottinghamshire, eight members of an organised crime group were sentenced to a combined 13 years and six months for defrauding East Midlands residents. Cases like that are local and slow, and the Citizens Advice numbers suggest they have not changed the overall picture.

What to watch

The next marker is Citizens Advice's figures for the year to August 2027. If the 2,205 suspected scams rise again, the argument for tougher rules on unsolicited roofing sales will be harder to ignore. For now the response rests on warnings, and on homeowners declining to deal with anyone who turns up unasked.
