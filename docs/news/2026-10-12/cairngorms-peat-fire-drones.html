Drones hunt peat fires hidden beneath the Cairngorms

In one sentence
Thermal drones can locate smouldering peat that hides beneath burnt ground, and Scottish agencies already use them, though the BBC's Cairngorms specifics are unverified.

Fires in peat do not always go out when the flames do. The ground can go on burning slowly, out of sight, and reignite days or weeks later. Thermal-imaging drones are increasingly used to find those hidden hot spots, and a BBC Technology report has put the technique in the Cairngorms. The details of that report could not be checked for this article, so the account below rests on published evidence of how the method works in Scotland and elsewhere.

Why peat fires are hard to finish

Peat is partly decomposed plant matter, packed with carbon and often dry enough in summer to smoulder. A fire can burn down into the layer rather than across it. The surface looks black and quiet, while heat travels below.

Crews have traditionally dealt with this by walking the ground, soaking it and returning to check. That is slow and labour-intensive, and it puts people on unstable, hot terrain. A single missed pocket can restart a blaze.

The stakes are climatic as well as practical. Forestry and Land Scotland (FLS) says the Flow Country fire of 2019 burned for six days and is estimated to have released 96,000 tonnes of carbon dioxide, doubling Scotland's peatland emissions that year. By FLS's estimate, its restored bogs, some 12,500 hectares, would need a full year to recapture that carbon.

What the drones do

A thermal camera on a drone picks up heat rising through the surface above a buried fire. A 2019 pilot study in the journal Drones, by researchers including Liverpool John Moores University's Serge Wich and Steve Longmore, tested the idea on underground peat fires in Indonesia. It found that such fires can be detected this way and that a simple temperature-threshold algorithm can flag them automatically. The authors also examined how camera type and flight strategy affect reliability. They concluded the approach was promising but needed more testing in real firefighting conditions.

Scottish agencies have already used it. After a fire on Dava Moor, in the Highlands, FLS flew a thermal drone normally used for counting deer. Doug Harvey, FLS East Region manager, said in a July 2025 statement that the organisation's "main contribution was piloting the thermal imaging drone to help locate the danger areas once the flames were out." That let Scottish Fire and Rescue Service crews target their dousing and then leave the site.

The contrast with earlier practice is notable. FLS says it monitored the site of the 2023 Cannich fire for about three months.

What remains unconfirmed

The BBC report's specifics could not be verified here: how many drones are involved, how deep a thermal camera can sense a fire, which organisations are taking part, and how large the burned areas are. Those figures determine whether this is a pilot or a routine capability.

There are also physical limits. A thermal camera reads surface temperature, so deep or well-insulated burning may show only faintly, and sun-warmed ground can create false readings. The Indonesian study's call for further real-world testing applies equally to Scottish moorland.

What to watch

The practical test is whether agencies adopt thermal surveys as a standard step before crews stand down from a peat fire, and whether post-fire monitoring times fall as a result. Any published detection results from the Cairngorms work would also show how well the method performs in Scotland's cooler, wetter peat than in the tropical sites where it was first studied.
