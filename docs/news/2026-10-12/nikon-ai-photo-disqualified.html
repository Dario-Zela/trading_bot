Nikon rules prize-winning photograph was AI-generated after backlash

In one sentence
Nikon disqualified its Small World in Motion microscopy winner for breaking generative-AI rules after experts challenged it in public, exposing weak pre-award verification.

A microscopy video that won first prize in Nikon's Small World in Motion competition has been disqualified. Nikon ruled that it broke the contest's rules on generative AI. The decision came after scientists picked the clip apart in public. The disqualification is the notable part. The entry had already been announced, praised and shared before anyone with the right expertise looked closely.

The headline calls the work a photograph, but it was a video. Small World in Motion is Nikon's microscopy competition for time-lapse and moving images, run for 15 years, according to Amateur Photographer, which reports no earlier incident of this kind.

The entry and the objection

The winning work was by Ning Xu, an optical engineering researcher at Tsinghua University at the time of filming. It showed cilia, the tiny hair-like structures that sweep the airways, in a child with primary ciliary dyskinesia, a rare genetic disease. According to the ABC, it was magnified 100 times and used light-wave techniques to colour different parts of the sample. The prize was US$3,000 (about A$4,300).

The challenge came from specialists, not from the judges. Days after the announcement, the ABC reports, microscopy experts questioned the clip on social media. Commenters on a Nikon LinkedIn post, since deleted, said the nuclei did not look like nuclei and that the cilia seemed to appear from nowhere at an implausible size. Robert Hirst, a specialist in diagnostics for the disease, said he "knew immediately that the video was fabricated".

The microscopy community then published an open letter asking for external review of the winning work and urging Nikon to enforce its rules. It warned that a "potentially misleading portrayal of PCD is shaping public understanding of an already poorly understood rare disease." Edward Phelps, an associate professor at the University of Florida, said it is "unacceptable to artificially render any part of a scientific image."

What the verification did and did not do

Xu did not deny using AI. He said it was used to distinguish and visualise features in a reconstructed greyscale image, "mainly to improve the visual presentation, rather than provide an anatomical reconstruction", and that he believed he stayed within the guidelines. He cooperated with Nikon's review.

This is where the case is more instructive than a simple story of a fooled judge. The contest page says entries are judged on "originality, informational content, technical proficiency and visual impact." The reporting reviewed here does not say what the rules required of entrants on AI. Nikon's ruling is that the video "did not comply with the competition rules regarding generative AI". The gap between "enhancement" and "generation" was left to be settled after the prize was announced.

In this case the working check was the audience. Nikon's own process, whatever it involved before the award, did not catch the problem. Post-award scrutiny did, and it came from people with specialist knowledge of the subject, not from a provenance trail or metadata check. The BBC's account, which prompted this report, is the closest to the triage framing; some aggregator pages describe forensic pixel analysis, but outlets with direct reporting do not, so that detail is not relied on here.

Nikon moved reasonably quickly, within days according to the reports, and said its decision concerned the video's eligibility, not Xu's professional reputation or scientific contributions. It named Nguyen Nam Nhat of Vietnam as the new winner, for a video of a microscopic roundworm encountering a single-celled organism, Dileptus. That video had originally placed second, and the other winners each moved up one place. The ABC reported that it had contacted Xu for comment and did not report a response.

A pattern, not a one-off

Photography has been here before. Amateur Photographer notes the 2023 Sony World Photography Awards, where a Creative Open category winner turned out to be an AI construct, and a Hasselblad Masters 2026 finalist whose entry was removed for AI use. Microscopy adds a wrinkle. In scientific imaging the image is meant to be evidence, so a rendered region is a factual error as well as a rules breach. Here the misrepresentation touched a rare disease that few people understand.

Nikon has said it will review the contest's rules and procedures. The open letter's call for external examination of the winning work remains outstanding. What to watch is whether the review produces explicit wording on AI-assisted enhancement versus generation, and whether it adds a pre-award check, such as requesting raw acquisition files from finalists, instead of relying on audiences to do the checking afterwards.
