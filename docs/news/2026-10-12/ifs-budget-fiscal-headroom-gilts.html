IFS warns Budget is a 'work of fiction' without a plan for £35bn of pressures as gilt yields stay high

In one sentence
The IFS says inflation could force Chancellor John Healey to find up to £35bn by decade-end through tax rises or benefit cuts, against about £20bn of headroom in 2029-30.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies has told the Chancellor that a Budget which ignores the effect of inflation on departmental spending would not be believable. John Healey delivers his first Budget on 28 October. The IFS says inflation could leave him needing up to £35bn by the end of the decade, raised through tax rises or benefit cuts, with only about £20bn of headroom against the fiscal rules in 2029-30.

The warning lands while the gilt market is already unsettled. Ten-year yields have recently touched their highest since 2008, in a global bond sell-off tied to the Iran war and oil prices. The Budget therefore faces a bond market that is watching it closely.

The arithmetic of a squeeze

The IFS's central argument is about inflation. It says inflation is a bigger problem for the Chancellor than the narrowing of headroom. Departmental budgets are set in cash terms, so higher prices cut what they buy. The Chancellor can accept real-terms cuts to public services or commit more money. According to City AM's account of the report, topping up budgets could mean up to £35bn by the end of the decade, and the IFS reckons covering it would take tax rises or benefit cuts of about that size.

The headroom figures run as follows:

  • The IFS puts headroom in 2029-30 at about £20bn. The March forecast was £24bn.
  • Higher interest rates on government debt could add about £10bn to borrowing in 2029-30. Much of that is offset by extra tax revenue that inflation brings in.
  • The gap between a £20bn cushion and a possible £35bn bill is the problem. Without new measures, the numbers do not add up.

Helen Miller, the IFS director, put the risk bluntly. "Simply pencilling in implicit cuts that he has no intention of delivering would make the Budget a work of fiction," she said. She argued the Chancellor should set spending limits he would genuinely live within. If he avoids the choice, the IFS says, his plan for meeting the fiscal rules "will not be credible".

Gilts set the price of credibility

This is the channel through which the IFS warning reaches markets. Other analysts put headroom lower still: Pantheon Macroeconomics has estimated about £13bn, against the OBR's £23.6bn in March. Estimates of the damage done by higher borrowing costs vary, so the starting point for the Budget is itself uncertain.

Gilt yields were reported at an 18-year high of about 5.27% in September, and other reports put the peak anywhere between roughly 5.15% and 5.38%. A Budget that leaves a spending gap unfunded invites investors to demand more for holding gilts. That in turn shrinks headroom further, which is the loop the Treasury wants to avoid. In an inflationary setting, "implicit cuts" that nobody expects to be delivered are the kind of assumption the market is likely to discount.

The domestic picture also bears on equities. Rate-sensitive UK stocks are exposed to gilt moves, while oil majors such as Shell and BP, heavyweights in the FTSE 100, are driven by crude. Those threads were not checked against a dated closing report and are not a basis for any claim about this week's index moves.

The Chancellor's position

Healey has said the Budget will bring "fiscal discipline" to support "economic resilience", offering "breathing space for today, and hope for the future". Allies have said he will stay well within his borrowing rules. He is also due at an IMF summit in Bangkok, where he wants to show international audiences that borrowing is under control. At home, the cost of the Iran war is driving calls for more cost-of-living support, which pulls the other way.

Business data adds to the inflation concern. BDO's inflation index rose to 103.65 in September, its highest since June 2023, while its output index slipped from 100.30 to 99.19. That is a combination of rising prices and weakening activity.

The Bank of England is also part of the same equation. Markets price roughly a 90% chance of a hike to 4.0% on 5 November, a week after the Budget. A hike would raise debt interest costs further.

What to watch

The test comes on 28 October, when the OBR publishes its forecast alongside the Budget. The question is whether Healey sets departmental limits that rise with inflation and funds them, or leaves real-terms cuts in the plans. The first option means announcing tax rises or benefit cuts; the second risks the IFS verdict. Gilt yields on the day will show which the market thinks he has chosen.

The figures here come from City AM's reporting of the IFS analysis. The IFS report itself was not checked.
