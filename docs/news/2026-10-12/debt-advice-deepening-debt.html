Debt advice that may leave people owing even more money

In one sentence
A BBC report suggests some debt advice may leave people owing more, raising the question of whether FCA consumer-protection rules reach every firm giving it; the report's detail is unconfirmed.

Most people who seek help with unmanageable debt are looking for relief. A report from BBC Business suggests that some of the advice on offer may leave them owing more than when they started. The specific findings of that report could not be independently confirmed for this piece, so what follows describes the regulatory ground it sits on and not the detail of the BBC's reporting.

A familiar problem

The worry is not new. Debt advice in Britain comes in two broad kinds. One is free: charities such as Citizens Advice and StepChange, and the state-backed Money and Pensions Service. The other is paid, and it is run by commercial firms that charge fees for setting up and managing repayment plans.

The fee-charging end of the market has drawn criticism before. Citizens Advice has described a client who paid a debt management firm £38 a month. Only £1 a month reached each of her eight creditors, and the rest went in fees. The charity's chief executive said some paid-for firms cash in on people's money troubles and only prolong their debt problems. The Financial Conduct Authority later required such firms to send more of each payment to creditors and to tell customers that free help exists.

The arithmetic explains the harm. A plan that takes fees from the first pounds a person pays can cut repayments to creditors. Interest and charges can then keep running on the balances, so the total owed can rise even while the person pays every month.

The regulator's question

Firms that give debt advice by way of business generally need FCA authorisation, which brings conduct rules and the Consumer Duty. That duty requires firms to deliver good outcomes for retail customers. On paper, the protection is already there.

The question raised by reports of advice that deepens debt is whether the rules are reaching everyone who gives it. Several things could stop them:

  • firms operating outside the regulatory perimeter;
  • lead generators and referrers who pass customers on without giving the advice themselves;
  • authorised firms whose products are technically compliant but poor value in practice.

Which of these applies to the firms in the BBC's story is not established here. Nor is it clear whether the FCA has opened a review. Those points need confirming against the original report and any regulator response.

What to watch

The test is whether the FCA responds publicly to the BBC's findings. It could announce a review of the debt-advice market or take action against named firms. A statement that existing rules already cover the practice would be a response too. Until the BBC's detail is checked, the safest reading is that this is a long-running consumer-harm issue with a visible regulatory gap, and not yet a new scandal.
