UK pairs cyber-defence push with pressure on AI firms as King warns online threats are evolving

In one sentence
The AI minister is pressing frontier AI firms on safety as the King warns online threats are evolving, but no deadline, funding or penalty has been reported.

The UK government is treating the safety of frontier AI as a security question, not only a regulatory one. In the same few days, the King has warned that hostile online actors are getting better at their work, and the AI minister is preparing to press the companies building the most advanced models to do more to keep them safe. So far the evidence is of intent. The reporting describes remarks the minister was expected to make, not a published package with figures or a deadline.

The minister's message

Kanishka Narayan, the AI minister, was due to use what is described as his first speech in the role to argue that Britain cannot rely on technology companies to police their own models, according to the Guardian's account of the plan. He is also expected to call for more AI infrastructure to be built in the UK. Ministers are reported to be weighing whether to back internationally coordinated safety standards. A call for common standards has been signed by leaders including those of France, Germany, Australia, Singapore and Ireland. The UK and the US are not among the signatories.

The detail is thin. The advance reporting names no deadline, no funding amount and no penalty for firms that do not comply. We could not read the Guardian original, and the secondary coverage paraphrases the speech rather than quoting it. Until the text is published, the call to firms is best read as a statement of direction, not a policy.

What the King said

On Saturday 10 October the National Cyber Security Centre published a letter from King Charles marking its tenth anniversary. The NCSC was set up in 2016 as part of GCHQ. The King warned of "hostile online activity" that threatens trust and security. He said "malicious actors" continue to "evolve their tactics by using automated technologies". He added that "strong cyber security is fundamental to making the online environment safer and more resilient", and he praised the centre for protecting democratic institutions and "the critical infrastructure upon which daily life depends".

The letter is ceremonial, not a policy document, and it does not say what the new tactics are. The King has also spoken on AI directly. At Dumfries House in September he told AI executives, among them Nvidia's Jensen Huang, with Sir Demis Hassabis also present, that the technology risks "developing darker capacities". He warned of "existential dangers" if it fell "into the wrong hands".

Why the two belong together

The government has been building this case for months. In April, security minister Dan Jarvis asked leading AI companies at the CYBERUK conference to help build AI-powered cyber defences. In May, ministers launched a Cyber Resilience Pledge for businesses, backed by £90m. The same month, technology minister Saqib Bhatti introduced codes of practice asking AI developers to build resilience against tampering, hacking and sabotage. The NCSC has said that the number of nationally significant incidents more than doubled in 2025.

The practical concern is that the same models that can help defenders can also help attackers, and that an unreleased model is itself a target for hostile states. That is why the minister's message sits under the security banner. The risk is no longer only that a chatbot says something harmful. It is that a model, or its weights, falls into the wrong hands.

A live example is close to home. This week the BBC reported that hackers used Asos's own app system to push a notification to potentially millions of customers. Asos said the incident went beyond the basic contact details it first disclosed, and it said it was investigating unauthorised activity involving third-party platforms. The NCSC offered help. The case is a retail breach rather than an AI one, but it shows the kind of exposure the King's letter describes.

What to watch

The test is whether the speech text carries specifics: a deadline for firms, a reporting duty, or a decision on joining the international standards call. If it does, AI-enabled cyber risk has moved into national security policy. If it does not, it is another statement of concern, joining the voluntary pledges and codes of practice already issued.
