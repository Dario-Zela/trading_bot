Reform UK would suspend the asylum system for a parliamentary term

In one sentence
Reform UK says it would void pending asylum claims and halt the system for five years, but the legal basis and the costs of doing so have not been set out.

Reform UK says that if it formed a government it would stop the UK's asylum system for a full parliamentary term, about five years. Nigel Farage and the party's home affairs spokesman, Zia Yusuf, were due to set out the plan at a press conference on Monday. It is an opposition pledge from the party that has led or challenged in the polls, not government policy. Its practical test is whether any UK government could suspend asylum processing while the country remains bound by the 1951 Refugee Convention and the European Convention on Human Rights.

What Reform is proposing

As reported by LBC, Brit Brief and others, the plan has several parts:

  • Asylum applications in progress would be declared void.
  • Asylum tribunals, legal refugee routes and resettlement schemes would be scrapped.
  • About 90,000 outstanding asylum and humanitarian protection appeals would not be heard. The claimants would be deported instead.
  • Resettlement schemes would close immediately. Reform says there is "no mandate" for them, and it would deport anyone who had already arrived under them.
  • The Home Office would review cases of people granted asylum over the past five years.
  • Tariffs and airline bans would be used against countries that refuse to take back their nationals.

Brit Brief reports that Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood recently said resettlement schemes would reopen. It says about a quarter of those expected to be admitted are Palestinians and women and girls from Afghanistan. Reform would shut those schemes at once.

Farage told The Telegraph: "The country is currently facing a national security emergency." He added: "We cannot accept more asylum seekers."

The coverage I could find does not give Reform's legal basis for the suspension, and it carries no cost estimate. I did not find the BBC's own report, so its exact wording and any stated mechanism are unchecked.

The legal wall

The UK is a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention. Its central rule, in Article 33, is that a state may not return a refugee to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened. Separately, the European Court of Human Rights has long read Article 3 of the ECHR, the ban on torture and inhuman or degrading treatment, as barring removal to a real risk of such treatment. That ban has no exceptions. The Human Rights Act 1998 makes the ECHR rights enforceable in UK courts.

These rules do not oblige Britain to run any particular asylum process. They do bar sending someone back to danger, and in practice that requires someone to decide whether the danger exists. Voiding pending claims and deporting 90,000 appellants unheard collides with that requirement. Courts would be asked to block individual removals one at a time. A blanket suspension would rest on legislation that overrides those duties in domestic law while leaving them in place internationally.

Parliament can legislate in defiance of treaties, since UK courts follow statute over unincorporated treaty obligations. Doing so would put Britain in breach in international law. That is why Reform's wider platform has pointed to leaving the ECHR and the Refugee Convention, as reported in 2025 coverage of Farage's earlier deportation pledges. The Conservatives' shadow home secretary, Chris Philp, said the plan "largely copies" Tory policy, and noted that his party's own approach involves "coming out of the ECHR."

Withdrawal is not quick or cheap in political terms. The ECHR is also written into parts of the Good Friday Agreement settlement in Northern Ireland, which makes exit constitutionally fraught. A suspension that depended on leaving the treaties would therefore be a package of measures, and the asylum pause could not be treated as a standalone one. None of this is spelled out in the reporting so far.

Reaction, and the fiscal question

Labour called the plans "a clear sign of desperation and weakness." Party chairwoman Bridget Phillipson said Farage was adopting "more and more extreme policies" and accused Reform of abandoning people fleeing war and persecution. The government has said it has carried out about 60,000 deportations in 18 months, a figure Labour used in response to an earlier Reform plan for a UK Deportation Command.

The costs are unstated and cut both ways. Ending processing would in time end some of the spending on tribunals and accommodation, but that saving depends on the claimants actually leaving. Removing 90,000 people, many to countries that would have to accept them, would need escorts, detention space, return agreements and litigation budgets. Tariff threats could also draw retaliation. The Institute for Fiscal Studies is already warning of £35bn of unfunded pressures ahead of the Budget, so any costed version of the plan would land on a tight fiscal position.

What to watch

The pledge becomes testable if Reform publishes draft legislation or a costing. Three things would show whether it is a legal proposal or a slogan: whether it commits to leaving the ECHR and the Refugee Convention, whether it says how removals would work for people who cannot lawfully be returned, and whether the Office for Budget Responsibility or the IFS is asked to price it. Until then, it is a statement of intent whose central mechanism is not stated.
