AI is less regulated than milk, says Microsoft president

In one sentence
Microsoft president Brad Smith says AI is less regulated than milk, adding an industry voice to the UK, EU and US debate without, so far, a new proposal attached.

Microsoft's president has reached for the dairy aisle to make a point about artificial intelligence. Brad Smith, who runs the company's policy and legal operations alongside his title, argues that AI is less regulated than milk, according to a BBC Business report. The comparison is meant to sting. A pint of milk passes through rules on safety, testing, labelling and inspection before it reaches a fridge. The software that millions of people now use to draft letters, write code and answer medical questions faces no such gauntlet in most places.

This article draws on the BBC's headline and summary of Smith's remarks. It does not reproduce his exact words or the specific measures he wants, which are not set out here.

A familiar position

Smith is not new to this argument. Microsoft has for several years said publicly that AI needs rules, and Smith has been the company's most visible voice on the subject. There is a commercial logic to that. Microsoft is one of the largest backers and sellers of AI products. A clear rulebook gives big customers, such as banks, hospitals and governments, the confidence to buy. It can also raise compliance costs that smaller rivals find harder to bear.

That is why a remark like this is better read as colour than as news. Industry leaders calling for regulation is a recurring feature of the debate. What would change the picture is a concrete proposal, a draft bill or a vote. Nothing in the material available points to one.

Three regulatory landscapes

The call lands in three quite different places.

  • European Union. The AI Act, which came into force in 2024, is the most developed framework anywhere. It sorts systems by risk and phases in obligations over several years. Much of the current argument in Brussels is about timing and whether the rules are too heavy.
  • United Kingdom. The government has so far preferred a lighter approach, leaning on existing regulators rather than a single AI statute. It has repeatedly signalled interest in legislation aimed at the most powerful models, and it is simultaneously pressing AI firms on safety, as covered in this edition's report on the UK cyber-defence push.
  • United States. There is no comprehensive federal AI law. Policy is a patchwork of executive action, agency guidance and state bills, and the politics lean towards encouraging development.

Smith's milk analogy fits that patchwork well. In each jurisdiction, the gap he describes is between how closely a product is checked and how widely it is used.

What to watch

The comparison will matter only if it is attached to something specific. The signals to look for are a UK AI bill finally reaching Parliament, any change to the EU's implementation timetable, and movement on federal legislation in Washington. Until one of those moves, Smith's line is a good slogan about a debate that is still running.
