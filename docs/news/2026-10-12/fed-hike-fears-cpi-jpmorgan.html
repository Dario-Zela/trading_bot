Fed hike fears linger as US CPI and JPMorgan earnings headline a holiday-thinned week

In one sentence
The Fed's hike debate now turns on an oil-driven supply shock, and this week's CPI print and JPMorgan's results will show whether it tightens into it or looks through.

The Federal Reserve's argument over whether to raise interest rates has changed character. Earlier this year the question was whether demand was running too hot. Now it is whether policymakers should respond to an energy-led jump in prices that higher rates cannot do much to fix. The September consumer price index, due this week, is the next test of which view prevails.

US bond markets are closed today for Columbus Day, while equities trade. That leaves a thin calendar before the data arrives.

A committee split on the direction of travel

The Fed held the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% on 29 July. The vote was 9–3. Cleveland's Beth Hammack, Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all dissented, each wanting a quarter-point increase. Reports on the meeting describe it as the first decision since 2016 with three or more dissents in the same direction. No governor dissented, and Chair Kevin Warsh was not among the three.

Warsh's stated case for holding was that higher Treasury yields since June had already tightened financial conditions and done some of the work of a rate rise. He also declined to endorse market pricing for a September move, saying it would inform the decision but not determine it.

The committee then moved. An MUFG recap of the 15–16 September meeting reports a 25-basis-point increase, taking the range to 3.75%–4.00%, the first hike since 2023. It quotes Warsh saying the committee's "predominant focus is on the price stability side of our mandate." The recap is the only account of the decision this desk could find, and it does not give the vote split. Even so, the July dissent count shows how narrow the margin for patience was. The September hike did not end the argument. It moved the debate to whether one more increase is needed.

What the CPI print has to show

The September CPI report is scheduled for 8:30am Eastern on Wednesday, 14 October. Previews point to a rise in the annual headline rate from about 3.4% to roughly 3.6%–3.7%. The Cleveland Fed's nowcast, as of 7 October, was 3.60% headline year on year. Analysts attribute most of the expected acceleration to gasoline, after record September pump prices.

The composition matters as much as the headline. Brent crude has hovered near $100 a barrel, helped by Houthi strikes on Riyadh airport. Washington has also eased sanctions on Russian diesel to try to cool fuel prices, a move that has drawn objections in the Senate. An energy-driven overshoot is the kind of inflation a central bank can in principle look through, because it reflects supply rather than overheated demand. But the Fed has now been above its 2% target for more than five years, and the July dissenters argued that persistence leaves little room for that tolerance. MUFG says the Fed's own projections edged higher for 2026 inflation on oil prices, and that Warsh dismissed a path back to 2% by 2029 as too slow.

That sets up an awkward reading. A hot headline figure with benign underlying categories would support the look-through camp. A broadening beyond fuel would strengthen the case for tightening further, at the risk of doing so into a slowing economy. Equity and bond markets are already pricing some chance of another move at the 27–28 October meeting, and the print will shift those odds in either direction. The meeting date comes from a single preview and should be checked against the Fed's calendar.

JPMorgan as a consumer-credit check

Bank earnings begin this week with JPMorgan, the first of the large lenders to report. Its report date and consensus earnings per share were not confirmed from a 2026 source, so neither is given here. A figure of about $5.90 circulated ahead of the results, but it could not be verified.

The results matter beyond the headline number. JPMorgan's card and consumer loan books are among the best real-time indicators of household stress. Management comments on delinquencies, reserve builds and net interest income would show whether households are absorbing fuel costs. Weak consumer credit alongside hot inflation would sharpen the policy dilemma. Resilient credit would give the hawks more room.

What to watch

The sequence is the CPI release on 14 October, then the bank results, then the FOMC meeting at the end of the month. Abroad, Eurozone inflation data and the Bank of England, where markets price roughly a 90% chance of a hike to 4.0% on 5 November, face the same energy shock. The question each central bank faces is whether to tighten into it or look through it. The July dissent count, three of twelve voters, suggests the Fed's answer is not settled.
