MPs seek to overturn ruling that stops police being sued for failings

In one sentence
Andrew Mitchell and Jess Phillips have tabled an amendment to reverse a 2025 Court of Appeal ruling shielding police from civil claims, with debate due Tuesday.

Two MPs from opposite sides of the Commons are trying to rewrite the law on when police can be sued. They want to undo a Court of Appeal judgment that, in their reading, gives forces broad immunity from civil claims over how they handle criminal investigations.

Andrew Mitchell, a Conservative, and Jess Phillips, a Labour minister-turned-backbencher-in-spirit on this issue, have tabled an amendment to a bill that Parliament is due to debate next week. The amendment is down for debate on Tuesday, according to the BBC, which reported the move on 10 October. The bill itself was not named in that report.

What the ruling did

The judgment at issue is a 2025 Court of Appeal decision covering England and Wales. It held that police officers are immune from civil claims in two situations: when they act as witnesses, and over their conduct and statements while carrying out criminal investigations. The BBC did not give the case a formal name.

The underlying facts are stark. A domestic abuse victim had moved to a secret refuge. Her new address went into a police file sent to the Crown Prosecution Service without a confidentiality flag, and a CPS lawyer then disclosed it in court, so her abuser learned where she was. Her claims against the police and the CPS were rejected, reinstated on appeal, then struck out again.

The immunity principle is not new in itself. Courts have long limited negligence claims against police over investigations, on the theory that exposure to suits would distort decisions. Victims have had a partial route through the Human Rights Act 1998, as in the Supreme Court's Worboys litigation, which held that the state's duty to investigate serious violent crime can be enforced that way. The 2025 judgment is read by its critics as widening the shield well beyond that older line.

The amendment

Mr Mitchell and Ms Phillips propose that, except when officers are acting as witnesses, police would not enjoy immunity for what they do in preparing criminal proceedings. They want it removed where police have fabricated evidence, been dishonest or negligent, or recklessly disclosed victims' confidential information.

Mr Mitchell called the "police misconduct immunity created by the Court of Appeal's ruling" extremely concerning, and warned that leaving the loophole open would have "devastating consequences for victims".

Kate Ellis of the Centre for Women's Justice said forces may argue they cannot be "held legally responsible at all for serious mistakes made in a criminal investigation", and warned of an "accountability gap".

The ruling has already surfaced in other litigation. Ali Kololo, a Kenyan woodcutter who spent 11 years in prison after a wrongful conviction in Kenya, is suing the Metropolitan Police. Represented by Reprieve, he faces a force that says it will rely on core immunity. The Met would not comment on the live claim but said its thoughts were with Mr Kololo, victims and their families.

What the government says

Ministers have not backed the amendment. A government spokesperson said the home secretary and the lord chancellor had commissioned a review of police accountability because the current system is "not fit for purpose", and that any change to how police are treated should be weighed alongside the review's wider findings. Sussex Police and the National Police Chiefs' Council declined to comment.

The BBC also reported that a Supreme Court appeal this month could overturn the Court of Appeal judgment, which would make legislation less necessary. No figures on possible costs to forces or the Home Office have been published, and the vote count and level of cross-party support beyond the two sponsors remain unclear.

What to watch

Tuesday's debate will show whether the government resists, accepts or defers to its review. The Supreme Court's decision on the appeal, expected this month, could settle the question first. If immunity is narrowed by either route, forces would face a wider range of civil claims, and the bill for them would fall on police budgets and, indirectly, the Home Office.
