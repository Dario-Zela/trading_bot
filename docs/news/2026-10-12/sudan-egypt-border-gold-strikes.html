Dozens of gold prospectors killed in strikes on Egypt-Sudan border, rights group says

In one sentence
Air strikes near the Egypt-Sudan border killed at least 72 gold prospectors, a rights group says; Egypt is blamed by survivors but nothing is confirmed.

Air strikes on artisanal gold-mining sites near the Egypt-Sudan border on Saturday killed at least 72 people, according to a Sudanese rights group. The strikes hit River Nile state, in Sudan's north-east. The identity of the attackers has not been confirmed, though several parties point at Egypt.

The group, Emergency Lawyers, describes its toll as provisional and says it could rise. It has also said the strikes may amount to war crimes. Other accounts run much higher. The rebel-led Tasis alliance and survivors, as reported by Sudan Tribune and the Rio Times, put the number of dead above 200. None of these figures has been independently verified, and no official toll has been published. Reports also differ on the exact location: the BBC and others place the sites in River Nile state, while one outlet describes them as North Wadi.

Who is blamed

Survivors, Tasis and Emergency Lawyers have blamed Egyptian military aircraft. Egyptian authorities have said that their security operations stay inside Egypt's borders. As of the latest reports, neither the Egyptian government nor the de facto Sudanese authorities had commented publicly on these specific strikes. The attribution therefore rests on witness and rebel-aligned accounts rather than on any independent confirmation.

A border already in dispute

The strikes follow a pattern of reported incidents earlier this year. In June, miners in River Nile state described drone and artillery strikes on 16 June. An adviser to the Sudanese army chief, Abdel Fattah al-Burhan, said Egyptian forces had targeted Sudanese miners near the border, causing deaths. Egypt categorically denied carrying out strikes inside Sudan or seeking to seize mines. Cairo has said Sudanese prospectors crossed the border to dig near a concession run by an Egyptian company.

The rights group Darfur Victims Advocacy later reported that Egyptian forces arrested 67 Sudanese miners and wounded three in a raid in the Wadi El Atri area on 21 June. Survivors told the group the earlier strike had killed more than 50 miners inside Sudan, a figure that other sources have not corroborated. Miners say rights to the border area have long been contested.

What it would mean

If Egyptian responsibility were established, it would be a significant extension of Sudan's war, which has so far been fought mainly between the army and the Rapid Support Forces, into direct involvement by a neighbouring state. It would also put informal gold routes at risk. Artisanal mining along the Nubian Shield is a major source of cash in a country whose formal economy has been badly damaged. Fighters on several sides have a stake in the gold trade. But the evidence available now supports only that a large number of prospectors died in air strikes; it does not settle who flew them or why.

What to watch: whether Egypt or the authorities in Port Sudan respond publicly, whether the Emergency Lawyers toll is revised, and whether the UN Security Council takes up the appeals made after the June incidents. Independent verification of the site, through satellite imagery or on-the-ground investigators, would be the first step to resolving the competing accounts.
