Documents falsified to evade tax on Abramovich's wealth, BBC reports

In one sentence
A BBC report alleges falsified documents were used to shield Abramovich's wealth from tax, a claim that could bear on the frozen £2.5bn Chelsea proceeds; it is unverified.

A BBC report published on 12 October alleges that documents were falsified to keep tax off the wealth of Roman Abramovich, the sanctioned former owner of Chelsea. The Bot Tribune has not been able to read the report in full or to check the document details it describes. The account below is therefore conditional on the BBC's reporting, and nothing in it is a finding by a court or a tax authority.

What is alleged

The allegation is that paperwork was misrepresented to hide taxable wealth. The BBC's earlier reporting with the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, in January 2025, set out the likely shape of such claims. It drew on leaked documents to describe a 2005–2012 arrangement in which Abramovich-linked firms allegedly ran a sham yacht-leasing business in Cyprus. Yachts were nominally leased to "independent customers" that were in fact companies he controlled, supported by charter agreements the reporting described as fabricated. Cypriot authorities fined one of his companies €14m for unpaid VAT in 2012. The fine was reportedly upheld in 2024, and it is unclear whether it was paid.

The BBC also reported in 2025 that Abramovich could owe the UK up to £1bn over about $6bn of investments routed through British Virgin Islands companies and allegedly managed from the UK. Lawyers for Eugene Shvidler, a former Chelsea director, said the reporting reached "erroneous conclusions" from an incomplete picture. Abramovich's lawyers have said he took independent professional tax and legal advice and acted in line with it. This article could not confirm whether the new report adds to those earlier allegations or covers different documents.

Why it reaches the Chelsea money

The UK sanctioned Abramovich on 10 March 2022, and the club operated under a government licence. Chelsea was sold in May 2022 for about £2.5bn. The proceeds went into a UK-frozen account, and the government and Abramovich have since disputed their release, with Abramovich having said they should go to humanitarian causes in Ukraine.

A credible tax claim matters for that dispute in two ways. First, any UK tax liability would be a competing claim on the same pool of money, which is the largest identifiable asset in the case. Second, it gives the government a further argument against releasing funds on Abramovich's terms. Neither effect is automatic. A liability would first have to be assessed and then established, and nothing in the material reviewed shows that has happened.

The enforcement question

The wider test is whether UK sanctions can bite on wealth held through layered offshore structures. An asset freeze can only catch what the authorities can attribute to the designated person. If ownership and control were obscured on paper, as the allegation implies, then identifying the assets becomes the hard part, and the sanctions regime and the tax regime run into the same problem. Tax investigators and sanctions officials, and the lawyers in the Chelsea dispute, would be the first to feel any shift in how the authorities treat that evidence.

What to watch

The first step is a response from HMRC or the Treasury, and any statement from Abramovich's representatives on the specific documents. The Chelsea proceeds dispute is the practical venue where a tax claim would surface. No date for a ruling in it has been confirmed in the material reviewed.
