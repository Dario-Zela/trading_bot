Met chief says DEI 'toxicity' will not stop him recruiting more black and Asian officers

In one sentence
The Met commissioner says political 'toxicity' around DEI will not stop him recruiting more Black and Asian officers, defending the force's diversity push under scrutiny.

The head of the Metropolitan Police has said that the political "toxicity" surrounding diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) programmes will not deter him from recruiting more Black and Asian officers. The remarks, reported by the Guardian, put the commissioner on the record against pressure to scale back such efforts at a time when the term DEI has become a political battleground on both sides of the Atlantic.

The reporting available so far consists of the headline and a short summary. It does not include full quotations, new recruitment figures or the announcement of any change in policy. This account is limited to what those establish.

A long-running problem

The Met has spent years trying to make its ranks look more like the city it polices. London is among the most ethnically diverse cities in Europe, and the force has repeatedly been criticised for failing to reflect it. The issue gained force after the 2023 review by Baroness Casey, commissioned in the wake of the murder of Sarah Everard by a serving officer. It found the force to be institutionally racist, misogynistic and homophobic, and it tied low public trust, particularly among Black Londoners, to those failings. The commissioner, Sir Mark Rowley, took the job in 2022 promising to rebuild that trust, and recruitment from under-represented communities has been part of that pledge.

The politics have shifted since. In the United States, DEI schemes have been rolled back by federal agencies and many companies. In Britain, ministers and commentators have questioned whether targeted outreach amounts to unfair preference, while supporters argue that widening the pool of applicants is different from lowering standards or reserving posts. Police recruitment is also governed by the Equality Act, which permits certain positive-action measures, such as encouraging applications from under-represented groups, but not appointing someone solely because of their race.

What the stance signals

The commissioner's position is notable less for novelty than for timing and tone. A police chief declining to retreat from the language of diversity, even as the word itself has become contentious, signals that the Met intends to continue its current approach rather than rebrand it quietly. Whether that means new targets, funding or outreach is not clear from the material reported.

Public bodies from the NHS to the civil service face the same question of whether to keep, rename or drop diversity programmes. A commitment from the head of Britain's largest police force will be read as a marker.

What to watch

The test will be in the numbers. The Met's published workforce data and recruitment statistics will show whether the proportion of Black and Asian officers is rising, and whether the force can keep applicants through training. Any response from the Home Office, which sets the national policing framework, would show how far ministers are willing to challenge or support the stance.
