Drivers forced to use parking apps can pay up to 40% more

In one sentence
A report attributed to the Guardian says drivers pushed onto parking apps can pay up to 40% more, but the sample, comparison and operators could not be verified.

A driver pulls into a council car park, finds the pay machine gone or dead, and is pointed to a QR code. Scan it, download an app, register a card and a number plate, and park. A report attributed to the Guardian says that route can cost more than other ways of paying, by as much as 40%. The headline figure is a ceiling, and the underlying details could not be checked for this piece.

What is claimed, and what is not

The claim is that UK drivers who are pushed onto parking apps can pay up to 40% more than they would through other payment routes. That wording leaves open most of the questions a reader would ask.

  • The baseline is unknown. The comparison could be with cash, card machines or pay-by-phone calls, and the material available does not say.
  • The sample is unknown. "Up to 40%" describes the worst case found. It says nothing about the typical premium or how many car parks were checked.
  • Who is charged is unknown. The premium might be a flat convenience fee added to a tariff, or a higher headline tariff for app users. It might fall on the driver or be built into the operator's rates. Each version is a different story.
  • No operator, council, date or regulator response was available to place the finding.

A search for the original report did not find it or the 40% figure. The closest published material is about how well apps work, not what they cost.

The wider picture

Surveys do show unease about app-only parking. A RAC poll of 1,709 drivers found that 73% of those who had used an app to pay for parking in the previous year had run into problems, and the motoring group urged operators to offer at least two ways to pay. Uswitch research from August 2025 found that 47% of UK drivers said they had been unfairly fined while using parking apps, and a further finding was that nearly a third of users only use them because they have to.

Those are complaints about reliability and fairness, not price. They show why a price premium would matter. A driver with no smartphone, no signal or no wish to hand over data has nowhere else to go. If the app is the only way to pay, a surcharge on it is in effect a surcharge on being unable or unwilling to use it.

What to watch

The claim stands or falls on the Guardian's underlying data. The things to look for are the named operators, the tariffs compared and the way the 40% was calculated. If a consistent premium for app-only payment is confirmed, it would invite scrutiny from consumer regulators and from local authorities that licence or contract with the operators. Until then it is best read as an unverified ceiling, not a finding.
