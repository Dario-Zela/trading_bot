DUP could remove O'Neill as first minister, says Lockhart

In one sentence
A DUP politician reportedly says the party could remove Michelle O'Neill as first minister, but no formal move is confirmed and the only real lever is a DUP resignation.

A DUP politician has said the party could remove Michelle O'Neill as first minister of Northern Ireland, according to a BBC Politics report. The remark is hard to assess without its wording, date and context, none of which could be checked. No formal move against O'Neill has been confirmed, and searches found no report of a tabled motion or an announced DUP plan to act.

What is claimed

The headline attribution is to Lockhart, presumably the DUP MP Carla Lockhart, though the BBC item itself could not be consulted to confirm that. The claim, as relayed, is that the DUP has the means to end O'Neill's tenure. The Sinn Féin vice-president has been first minister since 3 February 2024, the first Irish nationalist to hold the post. She shares it with the DUP's Emma Little-Pengelly, who is deputy first minister.

The quote has not been verified and the report's context is unknown. It could be a considered threat tied to a specific dispute, or the sort of pressure that regularly passes between Stormont's two largest parties. Nothing found so far points to the former.

How the office works

Northern Ireland has no mechanism for a party to simply vote a first minister out. Under the Northern Ireland Act 1998 the first and deputy first ministers hold what is in effect a single joint office. They are appointed together, and if one leaves, the other falls with them. Sinn Féin, as the largest party in the Assembly, nominates the first minister. The DUP, as the largest unionist party, nominates the deputy.

The practical route available to the DUP is therefore not a removal vote but a withdrawal. If its nominee resigns, O'Neill loses her post automatically, and the executive cannot function until a new pair is in place.

That is what happened in February 2022, when Paul Givan resigned as first minister in a protest over post-Brexit trading arrangements. O'Neill, then deputy first minister, lost her office with him. The institutions then stayed dormant for most of two years, until the DUP returned in early 2024.

So if Lockhart's claim means anything concrete, it most likely describes a threat to collapse the arrangements rather than to unseat one person. That is a different matter from a constitutional removal. It would also bring down a DUP office-holder alongside the Sinn Féin one, a cost the party has accepted before but which makes it a blunt instrument.

What to watch

The test is whether the remark is followed by action: a resignation by Little-Pengelly, a formal statement from the DUP leadership, or a specific dispute named as the trigger. Without one of those, this stays a rhetorical exchange. A resignation would start a statutory clock for re-nomination, and if it ran out, the route to a fresh Assembly election. Until a source confirms the quote and its setting, readers should treat the claim as unproven.
