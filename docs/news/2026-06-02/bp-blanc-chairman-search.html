BP backs Amanda Blanc to lead chairman search despite investor unease over board's judgement

Dame Amanda Blanc, CEO of Aviva and BP's senior independent director, has been backed by the board to lead its second chairman search in under a year. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP has tasked Dame Amanda Blanc with finding a new chairman — but she led the search that produced the chair the board just sacked.

BP has asked Dame Amanda Blanc, its senior independent director and chief executive of Aviva, to lead the search for a permanent replacement for Albert Manifold — the chairman the board ejected barely seven months after appointing him.

The decision, announced by interim chair Ian Tyler on 1 June, has drawn a cool reception in the City. Multiple people close to BP's major investors told the Financial Times that Blanc would struggle to secure shareholder backing for a second appointment, precisely because she oversaw the first one.

A board that hired, then fired

Manifold's removal on 26 May was abrupt even by BP's volatile boardroom standards. The board cited "serious concerns" over governance standards, oversight and conduct — language that left investors in the dark and sent BP's London shares down as much as 9% on the day. Manifold, the long-serving former chief executive of CRH, pushed back: he called the characterisation a fabrication and vowed to fight what he termed a "false narrative."

The prior warning signs had been audible. At the April annual meeting, proxy adviser Glass Lewis recommended shareholders vote against Manifold's re-election after he excluded a climate resolution filed by activist group Follow This; roughly 18% of investors did so — a meaningful protest by UK corporate standards. One City adviser told the Irish Times: "I've never known a company take such a risk."

Blanc was the architect of that appointment. As senior independent director, she led the search that produced Manifold's name in October 2025, and praised his "relentless focus on performance" on his arrival. The irony of asking her to lead a corrective exercise is not lost on institutional shareholders.

The credibility gap

BP's stated position is that a rigorous process, involving the full board, will produce a credible outcome. Tyler, a former chair of Balfour Beatty and now the man holding the interim brief, said the final decision "will reflect our collective view." That framing — the whole board, not Blanc alone — reads as deliberate distancing from any perception of a one-person show.

Blanc is not without standing. At Aviva, she hit her 2026 financial targets a full year early and has been widely credited with a thorough overhaul of one of the UK's largest insurers. The question is not her competence in running an insurance company but whether her credibility with BP shareholders survives two failed searches in under twelve months.

BP has now burned through three CEOs and three chairmen in under three years. The current chief executive, Meg O'Neill — appointed in December following the unexplained departure of Murray Auchincloss — is expected to steer BP back toward oil and gas and away from the renewables pivot that eroded confidence in prior management.

What to watch: Blanc will need to demonstrate early, substantive engagement with major shareholders — including Elliott Management, which has been pressing for operational discipline — if her eventual recommendation is to hold. Any impression that the search is designed to validate a pre-determined shortlist will harden opposition. BP's next shareholder event provides the natural deadline for that credibility test.
