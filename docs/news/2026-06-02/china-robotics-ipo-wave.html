Chinese robotics firms queue IPOs to test appetite for the next phase of AI

Unitree's G1 humanoid robot — the Hangzhou company is targeting a $6.2 billion valuation in a Shanghai listing reviewed by regulators on June 1. — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Unitree and AgiBot target $6bn+ IPO valuations as 46 robotics firms queue in Hong Kong, testing whether public markets share private backers' conviction.

Two of China's best-known humanoid robot makers are readying share sales that will test whether investor enthusiasm for the sector can survive contact with a prospectus.

Unitree Robotics, the Hangzhou company whose acrobatic machines have become a fixture of trade shows from Tokyo to Vancouver, had its application to list on Shanghai's tech-focused STAR Market reviewed by the exchange's committee on June 1 — just 73 days after formally filing in March, a pace that signals regulatory momentum behind the deal. The company is targeting a valuation of roughly 42 billion yuan ($6.2 billion) and plans to raise 4.2 billion yuan, with more than half of the proceeds earmarked for what it calls an "intelligent robot model development" project: training large AI models designed specifically for robotic control. Revenue for the nine months to September 2025 was 1.17 billion yuan, with humanoid robots now accounting for more than half of sales and a net profit of 105 million yuan — rare evidence of near-term commercial traction in a sector that burns cash as a matter of routine.

AgiBot, the Shanghai firm that gained national attention when President Xi Jinping inspected its robots during an April 2025 factory visit, is targeting a Hong Kong float in the third quarter. The company is seeking a valuation of HK$40-50 billion ($5.1-6.4 billion) and could raise more than $1 billion by selling between 15 and 25 per cent of its shares. Backed by Tencent, BYD, Hillhouse Investment and LG Electronics, with Morgan Stanley among its underwriters, AgiBot produced its 5,000th humanoid robot this year — output that gives it a 30 per cent share of global humanoid robot installations by volume. Unitree holds 26 per cent.

A queue, not just a rush

The pipeline runs well beyond these two names. Hong Kong's exchange has received applications from at least 46 robotics-related companies, more than 10 per cent of all IPO applicants on the exchange. Leju Robotics completed a pre-IPO fundraising round of close to 1.5 billion yuan last year and has registered with the Shenzhen regulator. Deep Robotics is preparing to file with the SSE STAR Market after its mandatory regulatory tutoring period ends this month.

The question is whether public investors will price these businesses the way private backers have. The broader robotics sector already trades at around 40 times forward earnings on Chinese exchanges, versus 14 times for the CSI 300 Index. That premium is difficult to sustain for companies many analysts expect to remain loss-making for years. Listed humanoid-robot stocks fell 13 per cent in the first months of 2026 after a 47 per cent gain in 2025. Shen Meng, a Beijing-based investment banker, summarised the mood tersely: "Sentiment is driven more by market dynamics than by conviction."

China accounts for more than 80 per cent of global humanoid robot installations, and the government's 2025 action plan targets 100,000 deployed units by 2027. The industrial logic behind the sector is substantive. Whether IPO valuations reflect that logic — or merely the excitement surrounding it — is the question Unitree's STAR Market debut will begin to answer.
