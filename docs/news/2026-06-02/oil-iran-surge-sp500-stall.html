Oil surges 8% on Iran ceasefire standoff, stalling the S&P 500's rally

Empty supertanker AbQaiq transiting open water — the class of Ultra Large Crude Carriers that carry Gulf oil to global markets, now operating under renewed threat as the Strait of Hormuz crisis deepens. — U.S. Navy / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Crude oil's 8% surge — best day since April 2 — stalled the S&P 500 flat while software stocks hit records, exposing a new fracture in how equity markets price geopolitical risk.

Crude oil had its best day in two months on Monday, and the rest of the market spent most of the session deciding what to do about it. West Texas Intermediate surged 7.5% to settle near $93.95 a barrel — its strongest single session since April 2 — after the United States launched fresh strikes on Iranian military sites and Tehran retaliated against an airbase. Brent crude climbed nearly as far, to roughly $97.00. The S&P 500, up a tenth of a percent at 7,580, barely registered a pulse. The Dow Jones Industrial Average slipped 0.3% to 50,899. The Russell 2000 fell 0.7%.

The Nasdaq 100 set a fresh record at 30,413. ServiceNow rose 9%. Adobe gained 6.6%.

The divergence was the real story — not the oil spike, which followed a script traders have run before, but what the spike conspicuously failed to do to software stocks.

The Strike and the Stall

The immediate trigger was diplomatic collapse. Iran's nuclear negotiating team suspended talks through intermediaries, citing Israel's continued operations in Lebanon — confirmed by reports from Iran's Tasnim news agency. Iranian state media reported that Tehran had severed all message exchanges with Washington. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps subsequently claimed to have destroyed a US-linked airbase; Kuwait reported incoming missiles and drones the same day. President Trump, seeking to contain the narrative, reiterated overnight that Tehran wants a deal. The market read the facts rather than the framing.

The Strait of Hormuz, largely blocked since late February when the US-Israel air campaign began, reasserted itself as the structurally critical choke-point. In 2024, roughly 20 million barrels of petroleum per day — approximately 20% of global liquids consumption — transited the strait. With Iranian negotiators out of the room, traders priced in a longer disruption timeline, not just a bad weekend.

Treasury markets moved in tandem. The 10-year yield added six basis points to 4.51%; the two-year rose eight to 4.09%; the 30-year approached 5.01%. The ISM Manufacturing PMI for May, reported the same morning at 54.0 — the strongest reading in four years, well above the 53.0 consensus — compounded the rate anxiety rather than providing relief. December Federal Reserve rate-hike odds crossed 60%.

Exxon Snaps Seven Days of Losses

For the energy sector, the crude spike translated mechanically into equity gains. ExxonMobil (XOM) jumped 2.9%, ending a seven-consecutive-session slide that had accumulated as investors trimmed positions on ceasefire optimism. The Energy Select Sector SPDR Fund (XLE) advanced 2.3% to $57.56. BP added 3.5% to $43.34 — its best single session since March 11.

The pattern here is familiar: geopolitical risk premium flows from the commodity to the producer in near-direct proportion to crude's move. Exxon shares have already climbed 24% in 2026, against a 10% gain for the S&P 500, reflecting a sustained elevation in energy prices since the Hormuz crisis began. Analysts at Mizuho recently lifted their price target to $175 from $159; Barclays' Betty Jiang carries an Overweight with a $182 target.

But the rally's foundation is worth examining. Monday's energy move originated not in new supply disruption — Iranian exports through the strait had already been severely impaired since February — but in the market's revised expectation of how long the standoff would persist. A real ceasefire, when it comes, would reverse a substantial portion of Monday's crude gains and drag energy equities back with them. Traders buying Exxon on Monday were, in a straightforward sense, making a bet on continued diplomatic failure.

Software as a Geopolitical Hedge

The more structurally interesting story was what software stocks did while energy rattled everything else. ServiceNow rose 9%. Salesforce added 5.8%. MongoDB climbed 17.4%. HubSpot gained 16.5%. Nvidia, which unveiled its RTX Spark superchip earlier in the session, advanced 4.4% to $220.32, driving the Nasdaq 100 to a fresh record even as small-cap indices sold off.

That these stocks moved decisively upward on a day when crude notched its best session in two months demands an explanation. The simple version: software companies carry negligible direct exposure to energy input costs. Their gross margins are not denominated in barrels per day. A $97 Brent print changes almost nothing about the unit economics of a ServiceNow enterprise contract.

The more interesting version is structural. The traditional equity-oil correlation model assumed a geopolitical crude spike would raise broad inflation expectations, compress multiples, and pull risk assets lower across the board. On Monday, that model produced an incomplete picture. The Russell 2000 — populated by smaller, more energy-cost-sensitive businesses — fell 0.7%. The Nasdaq 100, concentrated in high-margin technology franchises with global demand uncorrelated to Middle East supply chains, set a record. The S&P 500, somewhere in between, flatlined.

This is not a one-day anomaly. It reflects a compositional shift in what the major indices contain, and in what investors are treating as a defensible asset class during commodity dislocations. A decade ago, defensive positioning during an oil shock meant Treasuries and utilities. The current pattern suggests that AI-levered software companies are acquiring a similar function — not in principle, but in the actual Monday price action. For portfolio construction, the implication is that energy risk and technology beta now pull in opposite directions more reliably than they once did.

What Traders Are Watching Next

The next meaningful inflection point is whether Iran re-enters talks. Iranian state media suggested the suspension was conditional rather than permanent, tied to the pace of Israeli operations in Lebanon. Any credible signal of resumed diplomacy would likely reverse a substantial portion of Monday's crude gains and take energy equities with them — while potentially clearing the way for broader equity markets to advance on their own terms.

On the macro side, Thursday's non-farm payrolls report lands against a backdrop of a surging manufacturing PMI and energy-driven inflation pressure. If jobs numbers come in hot, December rate-hike odds — already past 60% — will rise further, narrowing the room for equity multiples to expand even if geopolitical tension eases. The two risks reinforce each other: a stronger economy means higher rates, and higher energy prices mean higher inflation, and neither is the kind of environment in which the S&P 500's recent record run extends easily.

The IMF's next oil-supply assessment is due at month-end. Until then, traders are pricing a Strait of Hormuz that reopens on a timeline set in Tehran, not Washington.
