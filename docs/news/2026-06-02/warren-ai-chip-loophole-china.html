Warren presses Commerce to close AI chip loophole for Chinese companies' overseas units

Senator Elizabeth Warren, ranking member of the Senate Banking Committee, is pressing for Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify on the chip export gap. — U.S. Senate / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Warren demands Commerce Secretary Lutnick testify after an 18-month regulatory gap let Chinese firms' overseas subsidiaries buy Nvidia Blackwell chips without a licence.

A gap in U.S. semiconductor export rules — one that sat open for roughly eighteen months — allowed Nvidia's Blackwell processors and other advanced AI chips to reach overseas subsidiaries of Chinese companies, the Commerce Department acknowledged on Sunday. Senators Elizabeth Warren (D-MA) and Andy Kim (D-NJ) wasted no time framing it as a preventable failure, and called on Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to explain himself to Congress.

The mechanism was straightforward: Chinese AI companies had set up subsidiaries in countries such as Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, and Singapore. Because the export restrictions targeted entities inside China, the overseas arms could buy advanced American chips without a licence. The gap emerged after the Trump administration decided in May 2025 not to enforce the Biden-era "AI Diffusion Rule," which had set licensing requirements for the global sale of frontier AI semiconductors. That decision effectively froze the regulatory framework in place, leaving the subsidiary pathway unaddressed.

One chip supply-chain source put the quantity of chips that moved through the gap in the hundreds of thousands, according to Reuters — a figure that, if accurate, represents a significant volume of the world's most capable AI hardware.

A Sunday fix and a Monday demand

On Sunday, Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security issued guidance clarifying that licence requirements apply to any entity whose headquarters or parent company is in China, regardless of where the subsidiary sits. In plain terms: operating through a Malaysian data centre is no longer a workaround.

Chris McGuire, a senior fellow for China at the Council on Foreign Relations, noted the clarification makes Blackwell shipments to China-headquartered companies outside China illegal again — but also observed that the guidance "does not mandate data centres to cease using chips" already in place. Existing installations are not retroactively banned.

For Warren and Kim, the clarification answered the what while leaving the why open. In a joint statement, the senators said: "On Sunday afternoon, the Trump Administration revealed that its failure to update export control regulations over the last year and a half may have inadvertently allowed America's most advanced AI chips to flow to companies headquartered in China, potentially fuelling China's military capabilities."

Warren — ranking member on the Senate Banking Committee — went further, calling for Lutnick to testify before the committee about what she described as "reckless mismanagement" of the Bureau of Industry and Security. Warren has separately co-signed, with Republican Senator Jim Banks, a letter pressing Commerce to pause and review all active export licences for advanced Nvidia chips and server systems destined for China and Southeast Asian markets.

The Commerce Department declined to comment when contacted by Reuters.

The immediate question is whether Lutnick will agree to testify, and on what timeline. The deeper question — whether hundreds of thousands of Blackwell-class chips already in Chinese-linked data centres elsewhere in Asia will ultimately end up accelerating the capabilities the export rules were designed to constrain — is one the new guidance cannot answer retroactively.
