White House weighs SEC and CFTC bid to revise swaps reporting rules

The SEC's headquarters at 100 F Street NE, Washington D.C., where the Form PF amendments were developed jointly with the CFTC. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The White House is reviewing a joint SEC-CFTC proposal to raise Form PF reporting thresholds sevenfold, exempting ~43% of current filers from swaps-disclosure obligations.

The White House is weighing a joint proposal from the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission that would substantially pare back swaps-related reporting obligations for private fund advisers — unwinding part of the regulatory architecture assembled after the 2008 financial crisis.

The proposal, filed with the administration for review, centres on Form PF, the confidential disclosure form that private fund managers have been required to submit under the Dodd-Frank Act. Introduced in the wake of the G20 Pittsburgh summit's pledge to pull over-the-counter derivatives into the regulatory light, Form PF channels data to the Financial Stability Oversight Council, which uses it to monitor systemic risk across the financial system.

The thresholds in question

The changes, proposed jointly by the two agencies on 20 April and published in the Federal Register four days later, would raise the general Form PF filing threshold from $150 million in private fund assets under management to $1 billion — a six-and-a-half-fold increase. For large hedge fund advisers, the bar at which more detailed quarterly reporting kicks in would climb from $1.5 billion to $10 billion in hedge fund AUM.

The agencies estimate that roughly 43% of current Form PF filers would fall below the new general threshold and exit the reporting system entirely. Among large hedge fund advisers, almost two-thirds would shed their elevated obligations. The agencies' counter-argument is that coverage of the broader market remains substantial: firms above the new general threshold hold more than 94% of private fund gross assets, while hedge funds above the higher bar account for around 81% of hedge fund gross assets.

The proposal also strips out several specific requirements — quarterly event reporting for private equity advisers, performance volatility disclosures, portfolio turnover data, rehypothecation reporting, and certain margin-default notifications. The agencies would replace the previous "as soon as practicable" language on filing timing with a firm 72-hour deadline.

SEC Chairman Paul S. Atkins said prior amendments had created "overly burdensome disclosure requirements" that diverted advisers' resources without proportional regulatory benefit. CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig aligned with the aim of cutting compliance costs while preserving effective oversight.

The current push is a deliberate reversal of the 2024 amendments, which tightened systemic-risk monitoring requirements. It traces to a January 2025 presidential memorandum directing agencies to review regulatory burdens across the financial sector.

The comment period closes 23 June. If the agencies adopt a final rule, a 12-month transition window would give affected firms time to reconfigure their reporting infrastructure. The FSOC's assessment of the narrowed data flow will be the substantive test: the council's statutory mandate — identifying risk that could destabilise the financial system — depends materially on the granularity of the information it receives from the private fund universe.
