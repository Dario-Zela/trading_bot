Nvidia bets on agentic AI at Computex as it lays the ground for the next infrastructure cycle

Jensen Huang on the Computex Taipei stage — the annual venue where Nvidia has repeatedly reframed what kind of company it is. — NVIDIA Taiwan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Jensen Huang used Computex 2026 to position Nvidia as the full-stack backbone of agentic and physical AI — from $200bn Vera CPUs to open humanoid robots — as custom-chip rivals begin to stir.

Jensen Huang walked onto the stage at the Taipei Music Center on June 1 wearing his customary leather jacket and carrying a message that has become something of a seasonal ritual: the last era of computing is over, and Nvidia is building the next one. But this year's Computex keynote was unusually specific about where that next era lives — and who will pay for it.

"The world is racing to build AI factories — the largest infrastructure build-out in human history," Huang told the crowd.

The claim sounds grand. The product roadmap behind it is detailed enough to take seriously.

The agentic bet

Nvidia's central strategic wager at Computex 2026 is that the AI market is shifting from models you query to agents that act: software that runs continuously, plans multi-step tasks, and consumes compute the way an always-on service does rather than a chatbot that waits to be asked a question. Huang put a dollar figure on the opportunity during Nvidia's May earnings call — a $200 billion total addressable market for CPUs purpose-built to orchestrate those agents — and used Computex to show the product he is betting on.

The Vera CPU is an 88-core processor with 3.6 terabytes per second of on-chip fabric bandwidth and 1.2 TB/s of memory bandwidth, engineered for the rapid token throughput that agentic workloads demand. Unlike a traditional server CPU optimised for latency across diverse workloads, Vera is designed to move vast amounts of inference data quickly between GPU accelerators. Nvidia says it has already booked $20 billion in Vera sales for 2026. The first systems were personally delivered to Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceX's AI division, and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure before Computex opened.

The Vera Rubin platform wraps that CPU into a five-rack AI factory architecture — cable-free, 100% liquid-cooled, and claiming 10x higher inference performance per watt compared with the current Blackwell generation. AWS, Google Cloud, Azure, and Oracle have committed to deployments. Alongside the hardware, Huang announced Nemotron 3 Ultra, a 550-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model offering roughly 30% lower operational costs than competing open models at comparable capability. The NemoClaw agent framework and NVIDIA Agent Toolkit give enterprise software developers the scaffolding to turn those models into autonomous, long-running systems.

Enterprise software partners stepped up to validate the stack. Palantir is integrating Nemotron into its Forward Deployed Engineer platform for air-gapped enterprise tasks. CrowdStrike is using it for agents that continuously identify and remediate security vulnerabilities — reducing manual triage rather than replacing the analyst. Cadence and Synopsys, the two dominant chip-design software vendors, are both integrating Nvidia's agent tools into their EDA workflows.

Physical AI: beyond the data centre

The back half of the keynote moved into territory Nvidia calls "physical AI" — intelligence embedded in machines that operate in the real world rather than in server racks. Three announcements gave that ambition concrete form.

Cosmos 3 is an open foundation model for physical environments, using a mixture-of-transformers architecture to handle text, images, video, sound, and action prediction within a single system. Nvidia says it compresses physical AI training cycles from months to days by giving robots and autonomous vehicles a simulation environment accurate enough for pre-deployment learning. Manufacturing partners including TSMC, Foxconn, and SK hynix are already running Cosmos-based synthetic data tools on factory lines.

Nvidia also released an open reference design for a humanoid robot — built on a Unitree H2 Plus chassis, standing close to six feet and weighing 150 pounds, with 75 degrees of freedom and dual five-finger tactile hands. Its brain is a Jetson AGX Thor T5000 chip delivering 2,070 teraflops of AI compute. Stanford, ETH Zurich, UC San Diego, and the Allen Institute for AI are among the first research adopters. The design is open; Nvidia's aim is to establish its compute stack as the default platform for humanoid development in the way CUDA became the default for GPU programming.

For autonomous vehicles, the Alpamayo 2 Super — a 32-billion-parameter driving model with 360-degree perception — ships alongside a raft of commercial partnerships: Uber is launching a Munich robotaxi programme using NVIDIA DRIVE Hyperion later this year; Foxconn's Foxtron division is targeting Kaohsiung in 2028; VinFast is deploying Level 4 systems across Southeast Asia.

The infrastructure moat — and the test ahead

The strategic logic running through all of it is vertical integration at scale. Google has no client chips and no robotics platform. Apple has neither data centre AI hardware nor physical AI. AMD has the GPUs but lacks the software stack and ecosystem depth. OpenAI — Nvidia's largest customer — is developing a custom inference chip with Broadcom targeting 2027 production and has signed a separate deal with AMD for six gigawatts of Instinct GPU capacity starting in the second half of 2026. The direction of travel is clear: the big labs want optionality, and they are investing to get it.

Nvidia's answer is to make optionality expensive. The Fluence-Siemens-NVIDIA reference architecture for a 136-megawatt AI data centre — announced in parallel with Computex — embeds Nvidia's Vera Rubin design deep into the power, cooling, and controls layer of future AI factories. The RTX Spark superchip, co-developed with MediaTek, extends that reach into Windows PCs, with 30-plus laptops from Dell, HP, Lenovo, Microsoft, Asus, and MSI shipping through the year. Huang called it "the first across-the-lineup PC reinvention in forty years."

The stock rose 3.8% on June 1 following the keynote; Nvidia's quarterly revenue stands at $81.6 billion, and the company projects $3 trillion to $4 trillion of annual global AI infrastructure spending by 2030.

The immediate test is whether agentic workloads materialise at the volume Nvidia's pricing assumes. Vera Rubin is priced for a world where continuous agent compute becomes a standard enterprise cost, not a premium experiment. The earliest signal will come from the hyperscalers' own capital expenditure disclosures over the next two quarters — and from whether the Vera CPU's $20 billion in forward bookings converts into deployed production systems before OpenAI's alternative supply chain comes online.
