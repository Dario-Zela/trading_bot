China luxury demand rebounds as equity rally lifts consumer sentiment

East Nanjing Road, Shanghai — one of the world's busiest retail corridors and a bellwether for Chinese consumer spending. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's luxury sector records its strongest Q1 in two years as a 26%-gain ChiNext rally channels fresh equity wealth into premium beauty and fashion spending.

China's luxury sector is posting its most convincing recovery signals in two years, with premium beauty and fashion brands reporting meaningful first-quarter sales gains as the country's equity rally helps restore confidence among affluent consumers.

The numbers are accumulating quickly. Ralph Lauren reported China sales jumped more than 50% in its latest quarter. L'Oréal posted mid- to high-single-digit growth in the country in the first three months of the year. On Alibaba's Tmall and Taobao platforms, combined sales of the top ten beauty labels retailing above 200 yuan — roughly $29 — rose 39% in the first four months of 2026, while cheaper brands edged lower. Brick-and-mortar is stirring too: Louis Vuitton and Burberry returned to growth in mainland China stores in Q1, Gucci's declines narrowed, and Coach accelerated.

The savings pivot

The proximate cause, analysts argue, is equity wealth. The ChiNext Index — China's Nasdaq equivalent — surpassed its 2015 bubble-era peak in May and is up roughly 26% year-to-date. That matters because Chinese household asset allocation has shifted dramatically over the past decade: property's share of household savings fell from over 90% in 2016 to about one-third last year, replaced in part by equities. Where a rising property market once underwrote consumer confidence, the equity rally is now doing that work — or beginning to.

The wealth effect is real but uneven. For many middle-class investors, a rising index does not automatically translate into portfolio appreciation; participation rates and position sizes vary widely. But among the affluent tier that luxury brands target most directly, rising equities appear to be reinforcing willingness to spend on discretionary goods.

The pattern cuts cleanly by price point. Premium beauty is outperforming mass-market beauty; and within luxury, ultra-premium is recovering faster than entry-level accessories. That selectivity, Bain & Company noted in its latest China luxury report, reflects consumers prioritising "value-driven luxury items that balance quality, exclusivity and practicality." Discounting, which eroded margins and brand equity through 2024's brutal contraction, is also easing — a signal that brands are regaining some pricing power.

Context matters here. China's personal luxury goods market contracted 3–5% in 2025, according to Bain — a sharp moderation from the 17–19% decline recorded in 2024, and one that began stabilising in the third quarter. "The market in 2025 began to stabilize, although consumer confidence remained fragile," said Bruno Lannes, senior partner at Bain. Beauty was the standout category, up 4–7% for the year; watches fell 14–17% and leather goods 8–11%, reflecting how unevenly the recovery has distributed itself across subcategories.

Beijing's stimulus mix — rate cuts, consumption vouchers, and direct support for equity markets — forms the backdrop. The policy bet is that a rising ChiNext translates into a genuine wealth effect, which translates into consumption. The luxury sector's Q1 data is the first tangible read on whether that transmission is working.

The next test arrives in July, when LVMH reports second-quarter results and L'Oréal issues its mid-year update. If Q1 momentum holds through Q2 without a fresh macro shock — a tariff escalation, a property market relapse, or an equity correction — the recovery case becomes considerably harder to dismiss.
