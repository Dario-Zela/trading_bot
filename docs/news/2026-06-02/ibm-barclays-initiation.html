IBM jumps 9% after Barclays initiates with Overweight on software transformation

IBM's corporate headquarters in Armonk, New York, where the company has been headquartered since the mid-1950s. — Mark Hillary / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Barclays' Overweight initiation on IBM, targeting $350 on a software-and-quantum thesis, sent shares up 9%—but the move's size looks more like momentum than repricing.

IBM shares gained roughly 9% on Monday after Barclays analyst Raimo Lenschow initiated coverage with an Overweight rating and a $350 price target — a move that briefly pushed IBM past its May highs and raised a question worth asking: how much of a 9% single-session rally on an analyst initiation reflects new information, and how much is momentum feeding on validation.

Lenschow's thesis centres on a structural shift that IBM has been executing — with uneven results — for more than a decade. About half of the company's revenue and the majority of its profits now come from software: Red Hat Enterprise Linux, OpenShift, and a broad suite of enterprise middleware. That segment grew 11.3% year-over-year in Q1 2026, lifting IBM's total revenue to $15.9 billion. Software annualised recurring revenue stands at $24.6 billion, up 10% from a year earlier. Barclays applies a 21x multiple to CY2027 unlevered free cash flow and arrives at a 12-month target implying roughly 18% upside from Friday's close of around $298. The PEG ratio of 0.28 — low even by large-cap technology standards — sits at the centre of the valuation argument: IBM looks cheap relative to its growth trajectory.

The structural case for why software margins hold up against AI pressure is also straightforward: IBM's largest customers are regulated enterprises — banks, insurers, government agencies — running hybrid-cloud and on-premise infrastructure that newer AI-native vendors have found difficult to penetrate. Lenschow characterises this as "sticky" demand insulated from the disruption pressures bearing down on other enterprise software categories.

The quantum footnote is starting to look like a chapter heading

The initiation's more speculative layer involves quantum computing, which Barclays frames as IBM following the "Nvidia playbook": investing more than $10 billion over five years, having already secured a $1 billion federal grant to co-build a quantum wafer foundry in Albany, and matching that with $1 billion of its own capital. Barclays' base target is $350; its bull case, contingent on quantum monetisation, is $449 — approximately 51% above Friday's close.

The Nvidia analogy is doing real rhetorical work. Nvidia's ascent was built as much on CUDA — the software ecosystem that made its hardware indispensable — as on raw chip performance. Lenschow is suggesting IBM is attempting something similar with quantum: build the tooling, the developer stack, and the enterprise relationships before the hardware is commercially mature. It is a reasonable frame, though Barclays acknowledges meaningful quantum revenue is still years away.

What makes Monday's move notable is its size relative to the informational content of the catalyst. IBM had already rallied about 30% in May 2026 — its strongest monthly performance in nearly a quarter century — on earnings beats and AI momentum. An analyst initiation, however well-argued, introduces no material non-public information. A 9% single-session response is more consistent with algorithmic and momentum-driven activity than with fundamental repricing: the note gave fast-money traders a fresh narrative hook at a moment when the stock was already running.

The next concrete test comes with IBM's Q2 2026 results, which will show whether the software segment can sustain the 11% growth rate that underpins the entire Barclays thesis.
