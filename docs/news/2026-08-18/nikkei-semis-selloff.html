Nikkei 225 slides 1.6% as semiconductor stocks tumble

The Tokyo Stock Exchange building, whose main index fell 1.6% on Tuesday as chip stocks led losses. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Nikkei 225 fell 1.6%, over 1,100 points, as semiconductor stocks led a broader Asian equity retreat.

Japan's Nikkei 225 dropped 1.6% on Tuesday, shedding more than 1,100 points, as a renewed selloff in semiconductor stocks pulled the benchmark lower even as several of the index's non-tech components held firm. The move tracked a broader retreat across Asian equity markets, extending a pattern that has defined the region's trading for much of the summer: sharp, sector-specific swings tied to how investors are pricing the durability of the artificial-intelligence spending boom.

A familiar script

This is not the first time this summer that chip names have dragged Tokyo's benchmark down on their own. The Nikkei has swung between corrections and rebounds since June, driven less by broad economic data than by shifting sentiment toward a narrow cluster of stocks — chip-equipment makers, memory producers and the conglomerates that hold stakes in them. Tokyo Electron, Advantest, Screen Holdings and Lasertec have repeatedly featured among the session's biggest losers on these down days, alongside SoftBank Group, whose valuation is entangled with Arm Holdings and its own AI infrastructure bets, and Kioxia, the memory-chip maker whose shares have been among the most volatile on the index.

The through-line across these episodes has been a question that has not gone away: whether the scale of capital expenditure going into AI data centres, chip fabrication and testing capacity is being matched by near-term revenue, or whether valuations built on that spending have run ahead of what the underlying demand can support. When that doubt resurfaces — often triggered by a disappointing forecast from a single bellwether, or by weakness in US chip stocks overnight — it tends to hit Japan's index disproportionately hard, given how concentrated the Nikkei is in semiconductor-linked names relative to peers.

Contagion or repricing

Tuesday's session raises the same question in miniature. Japan's decline arrived alongside weakness elsewhere in Asian equities, which is consistent with either explanation the market will now have to sort out. If this is contagion — a risk-off move spreading from bond markets, currency pressure or a specific geopolitical trigger — semiconductor stocks would simply be the most liquid, most heavily-owned proxy for de-risking, and the selling should broaden or fade in step with whatever is driving the wider retreat. If it is a sector-specific repricing, the losses should stay concentrated in chip and chip-equipment names while the rest of the market — retailers, banks, industrials — holds up comparatively well.

That distinction matters beyond Tokyo. Semiconductor stocks sit at the centre of the AI trade globally, and Asian chip names in particular serve as an early read on how investors are recalibrating expectations for that trade before US markets open. A repricing confined to chips would suggest markets are simply adjusting how much AI-related capital spending they believe translates into profit. Contagion would suggest something broader is unsettling risk appetite, with chip stocks merely the first and most sensitive to move.

What's missing, and what's next

The available detail from Tuesday's session does not yet settle which of the two is at work — it confirms the size of the move and its concentration in chips, but not the specific catalyst, whether a single company's guidance, an overnight move in US chip futures, or a shift in a macro variable such as yields or currency. That gap is the difference between a trade a desk fades by the close and one that reshapes a book for the week. The next reliable signal will come from Wednesday's Tokyo open: whether the same names lead again, whether the losses widen into the broader Topix, and whether South Korea's Kospi — home to SK Hynix and Samsung, the other pole of this summer's chip-sentiment swings — moves in the same direction or diverges, as it has done on several occasions already this season.
