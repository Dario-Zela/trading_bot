China's economy slows further as July retail sales, investment miss forecasts

Nanjing Road, Shanghai's main shopping thoroughfare — retail sales rose just 0.6% year-on-year in July. — Stephen Codrington / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.5)
In one sentence
China's July retail sales rose just 0.6% and industrial output slowed to 4.5%, both missing forecasts as investment fell further and the property slump deepened.

China's economy lost more ground in July, and this time the weakness showed up in the two places policymakers most needed it not to: the shops and the factory floor.

Retail sales rose just 0.6% year-on-year, the National Bureau of Statistics said Monday, down from 1% growth in June and far short of the 1.5% economists polled by Reuters had expected. Industrial output grew 4.5%, decelerating from 5.3% the prior month and undershooting forecasts that had clustered around 4.8-5%. Fixed-asset investment, which captures spending on everything from factories to roads to apartment blocks, contracted 6.7% in the first seven months of the year — worse than the 6% decline economists had penciled in.

Taken individually, any one of these might be dismissed as noise. Taken together, they describe an economy where both sides of the ledger — what China makes and what its own consumers buy — are decelerating at once, a pattern harder to wave away as seasonal drag or base effects.

In one sentence: China's July data showed retail sales and industrial output both undershooting forecasts, adding to evidence that the slowdown is broad-based rather than a temporary dip.

A second-half stumble, not a one-off

The July numbers extend a deceleration that was already visible in the second-quarter GDP print, which came in at 4.3% — the slowest pace since late 2022 and below Beijing's own 4.5-5% growth target for the year. An NBS spokesperson acknowledged the softness, saying authorities would "step up counter-cyclical policy adjustments" to shore up domestic demand, language that in China's policy vocabulary usually signals more of the same tools — equipment-renewal subsidies, consumer trade-in programmes, faster deployment of already-issued bond proceeds — rather than a fresh stimulus package.

Some of July's softness has an identifiable, one-off cause. Three typhoons made landfall during the month across China's eastern and southern manufacturing belt, prompting the relocation of millions of people and disrupting factory schedules in provinces that account for a large share of national output. That helps explain why industrial production, in particular, undershot by more than retail sales did.

But the typhoons don't explain the retail miss, and they don't explain why fixed-asset investment — a slower-moving, less weather-sensitive series — has now contracted for seven straight months of the year. The property sector remains the biggest drag: investment in property development fell 18% year-on-year in the first half, new-home prices in 70 cities fell 3.2% year-on-year in July, and the decline in monthly terms actually accelerated from June, extending a downturn now in its fourth year. China Real Estate Information Corp data cited by Bloomberg put the residential price index at its lowest level in more than two decades, down from a 2021 peak.

Why consumers aren't spending

The retail sales miss is the more structurally worrying of the two figures, because it points at household behaviour rather than a supply disruption. China's urban unemployment rate ticked up to 5.2% in July from 5% in June — a modest move, but one running in the wrong direction at a moment when policymakers are trying to talk up confidence. With home values still falling and a large share of household wealth tied up in real estate, the incentive to save rather than spend has proven durable despite two years of subsidy programmes aimed at nudging consumers toward big-ticket purchases like cars and appliances.

Economists parsing the data have started nudging their expectations toward more forceful action. Zhiwei Zhang, chief economist at Pinpoint Asset Management, said the figures raised the odds of an interest-rate cut from the People's Bank of China, while Xu Tianchen of the Economist Intelligence Unit argued that the bottleneck isn't a shortage of announced fiscal firepower but a failure to disburse what has already been budgeted — a call for officials to "be bolder about spending what they have" rather than waiting on new announcements. ING's Lynn Song had already flagged July's PMI readings as an "unpromising start" to the second half, a call the official data has now largely borne out.

What's cushioning the fall

The one bright spot remains trade. Exports rose sharply through the first half of the year, with China's trade surplus on track to top $1 trillion in 2026, as manufacturers redirected shipments away from the US and toward Southeast Asia, the EU and other markets even as Washington's tariff posture under President Trump remains unpredictable. That export strength is the main reason headline growth hasn't fallen further — but it's also a source of friction, with several trading partners now complaining that China is exporting overcapacity that its own consumers won't absorb, applying deflationary pressure abroad as well as at home.

For trading desks, the read-through is fairly direct: a China growth engine running on external demand while domestic consumption and investment both cool is a narrower, more fragile version of the story than one led by internal demand. That has implications for industrial and consumer names with China exposure, for commodities tied to Chinese construction and manufacturing activity, and for the yuan, which faces conflicting pressure from a swollen trade surplus on one side and expectations of monetary easing on the other.

The next test comes with August activity data, due in mid-September, along with any signal from the PBOC on rates following the Politburo's late-July pledge of "targeted" support. Until spending actually shows up — in bond disbursement, in a rate cut, or in a retail number that beats rather than misses — the market's working assumption is that Beijing is managing the slowdown rather than reversing it.
