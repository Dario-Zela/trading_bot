US stocks slip as oil and yields rise ahead of Fed minutes

The New York Stock Exchange on Wall Street, where major indices closed lower Monday. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Stocks fell Monday as oil topped $89 Brent and the 30-year Treasury yield hit a 19-year high, ahead of retail earnings and Fed minutes.

Wall Street closed lower on Monday as rising oil prices and long-dated Treasury yields overshadowed a week that will otherwise be dominated by retail earnings and the Federal Reserve's latest meeting minutes.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 0.5% to 53,459.78, the S&P 500 slipped a similar 0.5% to 7,745.06, and the Nasdaq Composite lost 0.3% to close at 26,644.91. The moves were modest in isolation, but they marked a retreat from the S&P 500's record high just a week earlier, and traders described the session as a reassessment rather than a rout.

Oil and yields both moved the wrong way

Crude climbed as a US-Iran memorandum of understanding covering shipping through the Strait of Hormuz expired Monday without renewal. West Texas Intermediate rose to roughly $82–83 a barrel, up about 1%, while Brent crude pushed above $89 a barrel, a gain of more than 2%. An Iranian official signalled a possible shift toward a more confrontational posture on the strait, and President Trump warned of a forceful response if Oman interfered with shipping — rhetoric that added a geopolitical premium to an already firm oil price. [[oil-nears-83-hormuz-refining]] and [[iran-hormuz-deadline-lapses]] track the same standoff from the supply side.

Treasury yields rose in tandem. The 10-year yield reached 4.72%, while the 30-year climbed to 5.31% — its highest level since June 2007, according to CNBC. Higher long-term yields raise the cost of capital for companies and compete more directly with equities for investor money, a dynamic playing out globally: benchmark yields in the UK and elsewhere have also been grinding higher this month, as detailed in [[global-bond-yields-multidecade-highs]].

The consumer test arrives this week

The more consequential news, for investors, sits a few days out. Home Depot reports Tuesday before the opening bell, followed by Target and Lowe's on Wednesday and Walmart — the country's largest retailer — on Thursday. Home Depot and Lowe's, in particular, are read as proxies for household spending on big-ticket, interest-rate-sensitive purchases, while Walmart and Target offer a broader gauge of how consumers are absorbing higher prices at the checkout.

Goldman Sachs economists have already flagged reason for caution, arguing that the burst of consumer spending seen in the spring was "the temporary byproduct of the tax refund surge" and forecasting that real spending growth will slow to an annualized 1% to 1.5% in the second half of the year. If this week's retail results confirm that deceleration, it would undercut one of the two pillars — resilient consumers, an accommodative Fed — that have been propping up valuations near record highs.

The second pillar gets tested Wednesday, when the Fed releases minutes from its July 28–29 policy meeting. Investors will parse the text for how divided officials were over the balance between still-elevated inflation and a softening labor market — a balance that rising oil prices, feeding through to gasoline and transport costs, only complicates further.

What's next

Home Depot's results Tuesday morning are the first concrete data point; Walmart's report Thursday, alongside Wednesday's Fed minutes, will do more to settle whether markets can keep leaning on consumer strength and policy patience — or whether Monday's slide was the start of a broader repricing.
