US pressures Apple to avoid Chinese memory chips

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who says he told Apple directly that Washington opposes it sourcing memory chips from Chinese suppliers. — Daniel Torok / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Washington is pressing Apple to drop Chinese memory-chip suppliers CXMT and YMTC, forcing costlier sourcing just as an AI-driven chip shortage squeezes margins.

Apple has spent months quietly testing memory chips from two Chinese manufacturers to ease a global supply crunch. Washington wants that to stop.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick said this week that he personally told Apple the administration opposes the idea of a major American company buying memory from Chinese suppliers. "The Trump administration is not in favor of that," Lutnick said, speaking after a tour of Apple's new manufacturing facility in Houston. "There have to be other solutions... it's not great American companies using Chinese memory."

The two firms in question — ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) and Yangtze Memory Technologies (YMTC) — have been in talks with Apple as it looks for ways around a memory shortage that has hit the entire electronics industry. Both companies have been flagged by the Pentagon as linked to China's military, and YMTC already sits on the Commerce Department's Entity List, which restricts the export of certain US technology to it.

A shortage with no easy fix

The root problem is capacity. AI data centre operators now absorb the bulk of global memory output, and DRAM contract prices have roughly doubled since the start of the year — analysts at TrendForce and other industry trackers put first-quarter DRAM increases near 90%, with NAND flash up more than 50% over the same stretch. Apple's own COO, Sabih Khan, told the Wall Street Journal the company was examining "all available options, including helping existing suppliers expand US production" — a carefully hedged answer that stopped short of ruling anything out.

That left Chinese suppliers as one of the few remaining sources of standard, off-the-shelf memory at workable prices, at least for devices sold within China. Under current rules Apple can buy commodity chips from CXMT and YMTC without a licence; only sharing product specifications for customised parts requires government sign-off. Apple has reportedly stayed on the commodity side of that line so far, testing the chips rather than committing to a supply deal.

Congress sets a deadline

That hasn't stopped Capitol Hill from getting involved. A bipartisan group of senators — Republican Jim Banks, Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer, and colleagues including Andy Kim, Jeanne Shaheen, Mike Crapo and Pete Ricketts — wrote to Tim Cook on July 29 demanding a written commitment that Apple will not use CXMT or YMTC memory in any product, anywhere. They set a deadline of August 21 and asked Apple to disclose what technical data, if any, it shared with the two firms during its review process. Micron, the largest US memory maker, has separately lobbied the administration to block the purchases outright, arguing they would undercut the same domestic chip manufacturing the White House says it wants to build.

Apple has not publicly responded to the letter. The company's $600 billion US investment pledge, announced a year ago, gives it a ready-made talking point about domestic manufacturing commitment — but it doesn't solve the near-term arithmetic of a memory market where supply is tight and prices are rising regardless of where the chips come from.

What it means for the numbers

For Apple, this is less an ideological fight than a margin problem. If Chinese suppliers are off the table, the company is left buying from SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron — the same three firms every other major device maker is also competing for capacity with, at prices already elevated by the AI-driven shortage. That cost pressure lands during a period when Apple has limited room to raise iPhone prices without denting demand, and it sits alongside a broader pattern: memory-chip beneficiaries of the AI boom, from Micron to SanDisk, have rallied on exactly the supply tightness now squeezing device makers on the other side of the trade.

The immediate marker to watch is August 21, when the Senate letter's deadline lands. Apple's response — or continued silence — will signal how much weight it's willing to give a congressional ultimatum on a sourcing decision it clearly considers a private commercial matter. Beyond that, the more durable pressure comes from the semiconductor market itself, where TrendForce and other trackers expect further double-digit DRAM and NAND price increases into the third quarter, regardless of which supplier logos end up on Apple's parts list.
