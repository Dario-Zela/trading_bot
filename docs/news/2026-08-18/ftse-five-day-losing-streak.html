FTSE 100 extends losing streak to five sessions

Paternoster Square in the City of London, home to the London Stock Exchange. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
FTSE 100 fell a fifth straight session to 10,750.11, but miners fell on a weather-driven copper shock while pharma's slide looks more like fatigue than a shared cause.

London's blue-chip index closed lower for a fifth consecutive session on Friday, slipping 22.56 points, or 0.2%, to 10,750.11 — a loss that left the FTSE 100 down 1.4% on the week even as smaller UK stocks held their ground. The FTSE 250 edged up 0.1% over the same five days, and the AIM All-Share added 0.3%, underlining that the pressure was concentrated at the top of the market rather than across it.

That concentration is the story. Two sectors did almost all the damage: miners and pharmaceuticals, both index heavyweights, both dragging lower for reasons that don't obviously share a root cause.

Miners: a weather problem, not a demand problem

Antofagasta led the mining retreat, falling more than 5% after cutting its 2026 copper output guidance to 625,000–655,000 tonnes, down from a prior range of 650,000–700,000 tonnes. The culprit was operational rather than macro: torrential rain and snow in July forced a shutdown at the company's Los Pelambres mine in Chile's Coquimbo region, which the Chilean government had already declared a zone of catastrophe. State-owned Codelco has trimmed its own 2026 forecast in similar fashion, to roughly 1.34 million tonnes from 1.37 million.

Glencore, Anglo American, Rio Tinto, Fresnillo and Endeavour Mining all fell between 1.5% and 2.7% in sympathy, as softer industrial metals prices compounded the single-company news. That is a supply-side shock with a traceable cause — weather knocking out a specific mine — not evidence of a broader rotation out of resources.

Pharma: harder to pin down

The pharmaceutical leg is murkier. GSK fell more than 2%, but the move looks driven by momentum and positioning rather than a fresh catalyst: the stock was already trading below its 20-, 50- and 200-day moving averages, and insider share purchases under the firm's Share Reward Plan on 11 August failed to arrest the slide. AstraZeneca, also down over 2%, has been unsettled by merger speculation rather than a hard news event. Smith & Nephew added to the sector's woes after cutting its revenue growth forecast on weaker US demand for hip and knee implants — a genuine earnings miss, but one specific to orthopaedics rather than the drugs pipeline broadly.

In other words: one heavyweight sector fell on an identifiable operational shock, the other on a blend of technical selling, one real guidance cut and unconfirmed dealmaking chatter. Treating both as a single "miners and pharma weigh on FTSE" narrative flattens two quite different stories into one.

The wider backdrop

None of this happened in isolation. The week's losses came alongside a deepening global bond rout — the 30-year US Treasury yield pushed to its highest since 2007 — and continued uncertainty over the Strait of Hormuz, which has kept a risk premium in oil prices without pricing in either escalation or a clean resolution. China's July data added a further drag on sentiment: fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% year-on-year and industrial output growth slowed to 4.5% from 5.3% in June, feeding worries about global metals demand that sit uneasily next to Antofagasta's supply-driven cut.

That combination — a genuine supply shock in copper, a soft pharma sector that reads more like fatigue than alarm, and a macro backdrop of rising bond yields and a wobbling China — is enough to explain a 1.4% weekly decline without requiring a single unifying narrative. The risk for anyone using the index mechanically is mistaking a stack of unrelated, moderate pressures for a coordinated one.

What's next

Attention now shifts to Monday's open and, more substantively, to the four-month-high UK inflation print due this week, which will shape whether gilt yields — currently steady — start moving in sympathy with the US Treasury sell-off. A further leg down in copper or a resolution to the Hormuz standoff would each do more to clarify the picture than another day of miners-and-pharma headlines.
