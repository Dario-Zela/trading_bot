Cabinet ministers' phone numbers leak online after Burnham impersonation scam

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose exchange with an impersonator of a top White House aide preceded the leak of three ministers' personal phone numbers. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street
In one sentence
Cabinet ministers' personal phone numbers leaked online days after PM Burnham was duped into texting with someone posing as a top Trump aide.

The personal mobile numbers of three senior British ministers were circulating online this week after the prime minister himself became the entry point for a scam that has now touched two governments. Andy Burnham exchanged a handful of text messages with someone impersonating Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff, before growing suspicious and cutting off contact. Within days, the private numbers of the defence secretary, the justice secretary and the Northern Ireland secretary had surfaced on the open internet.

Neither event, on its own, would be alarming. Politicians get phished; officials misplace contact details. Together, they describe something more troubling: a government whose most sensitive communications channel — the direct line to its own cabinet — turns out to be far more porous than anyone had accounted for.

A familiar voice, a fake number

The episode began, according to Politico's account, with a small number of text messages sent to Burnham's phone from someone claiming to be Wiles. The prime minister engaged briefly — the messages were later described by officials as "of no significance" — before concluding partway through the exchange that he was not talking to Wiles at all. No classified material or policy commitment changed hands. But the British embassy in Washington judged the incident serious enough to raise directly with the White House, out of concern that Wiles' phone had been compromised for a second time.

That concern had a basis. In May 2025, the FBI opened an investigation after governors, senators and business executives reported calls and texts from someone impersonating Wiles, in some cases using what recipients described as an AI-generated approximation of her voice. The impersonator appeared to be working from a genuine contacts list pulled from a prior breach of her phone, and one message reportedly asked a lawmaker to compile a list of people who might receive a presidential pardon. The White House has denied that a fresh hack of Wiles' device lies behind the Burnham exchange — a spokesperson said the incident "had nothing to do with the Chief of Staff's devices being hacked" — and the FBI has told the administration it does not suspect a foreign state is involved. Whatever the mechanism, the same identity has now been weaponised against a head of government.

Numbers in the open

The more consequential failure, from a security standpoint, is what followed. The personal mobile numbers of Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, Alex Norris, the justice secretary, and Chris Bryant, the Northern Ireland secretary, were found published online in the days after the Burnham story broke. None of the three ministers has commented publicly. Officials have since had Streeting's number scrubbed from the site where it appeared and say they are working to remove the other two, but the numbers were live and searchable long enough to matter — and there is no way to know how many people copied them down before they came offline.

Prerana Joshi, a research fellow at the Royal United Services Institute, framed the exposure in practical terms: a leaked ministerial number is not just a nuisance for the minister who owns it, but a tool. Numbers can be spoofed to place calls that appear to come from a cabinet office, used to target the minister's other contacts, or simply logged by hostile intelligence services building a picture of who talks to whom. Joshi noted the timing compounds the risk — the leak lands while the UK sits at what officials describe as an elevated threat level, with live files on Russian, Iranian and other state-linked cyber activity already open across Whitehall.

A pattern, not a one-off

British ministers' phones have been a soft target before. Liz Truss's personal mobile was reportedly compromised by Russian-linked hackers during her time as foreign secretary, with intelligence officials said to have found up to a year's worth of messages had been accessed, including sensitive exchanges about the war in Ukraine — a breach serious enough that the phone was eventually locked away rather than disclosed at the time. That history is part of why this week's leak reads as more than bad luck: it is the second time in four years that the informal, phone-based channels ministers actually use to communicate have proven to be the weak point that formal security architecture — codeword clearances, secure government devices, briefing protocols — was never built to cover.

The practical fix is not complicated. Cabinet office guidance already exists on separating personal and official numbers, verifying unfamiliar contacts through secondary channels, and rotating numbers after any suspected exposure. The harder problem is cultural: ministers, like most people who run large operations, default to speed over process, and a text from someone claiming to be a familiar name is answered before it is verified. That is precisely the gap the Wiles impersonator exploited on both sides of the Atlantic.

What happens next will say more than the incident itself. Downing Street has not said whether it will order a formal review of ministerial communications security, or simply ask the three affected ministers to change numbers and move on. The FBI's ongoing investigation into the Wiles impersonation campaign — now stretching across two governments — is the more concrete thing to watch: any arrest or attribution will determine whether this was opportunistic fraud or something with more deliberate intent behind it.
