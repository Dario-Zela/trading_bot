Gaza disarmament talks stall as Israel withholds full troop withdrawal

A map of territorial control in the Gaza Strip, including the Yellow Line that marks the boundary of continued Israeli military control. — Ecrusized / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Hamas and Israel each say the other must move first on Gaza — disarmament versus withdrawal — leaving the July peace deal unimplemented three weeks on.

The deal that was supposed to end the Gaza war has run into the same problem that has sunk every previous attempt: nobody wants to move first.

On 30 July, mediators from Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and the United States announced that Hamas had agreed in principle to a phased disarmament plan — handing weapons first to a National Committee, then to a US-backed International Stabilization Force, over a process expected to take 200 to 250 days. President Trump called it a "monumental step." Three weeks on, it has not moved past the podium.

Hamas says it will not begin decommissioning until Israeli forces withdraw beyond the so-called Yellow Line — the demarcation that currently leaves the Israeli military in control of more than 60% of the Gaza Strip — and until reconstruction funding starts flowing into a territory the United Nations estimates will cost upward of $50bn to rebuild. Israel says the opposite: no withdrawal until Hamas disarms, in full, first.

"Israel will not withdraw from the current line in the Gaza Strip and will continue to thwart any threat to our civilians and soldiers."

That was the position relayed by Doron Spielman, spokesperson for Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office, in one of the government's first detailed public responses to the plan. Spielman told the Associated Press that Israeli intelligence assessments hold that Hamas is using the framework to buy time while it rearms and rebuilds its military wing, rather than genuinely disarming. Netanyahu has separately said Israel "didn't agree" to the terms the US-led Board of Peace set out for sequencing the handover.

A deal built on a sequence neither side will start

The practical result is a standoff that resembles the original ceasefire talks more than a resolution of them: two conditional commitments, each waiting on the other to move. The Board of Peace, the oversight body Trump established alongside the deal, has said Israeli withdrawal beyond the Yellow Line should follow decommissioning, not precede it — a sequencing that lines up with Israel's position but leaves Hamas with no incentive to act first. Israeli strikes have continued through August, including a wave reported by Al Jazeera that killed at least 17 people, undercutting any sense that a de-escalation is already under way regardless of what the paperwork says.

What markets are pricing that may not hold

Oil and regional-risk trades spent much of the summer unwinding the premium built up during the Gaza and wider Middle East conflicts, on the assumption that a negotiated end was taking hold. This stand-off is a reminder that the underlying dispute — over troops, weapons and who administers Gaza once the guns are gone — has not actually been resolved, only announced. There is no new escalation here and no fresh number to trade on; the significance is that the framework markets treated as a settled de-risking event is still, three weeks in, a framework with no first mover.

The next marker is procedural rather than dramatic: whether the Board of Peace convenes to arbitrate the sequencing dispute, and whether any reconstruction financing — the $50bn figure the UN has attached to rebuilding Gaza — is released before either side blinks.
