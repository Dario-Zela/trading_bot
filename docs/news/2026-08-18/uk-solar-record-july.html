UK solar power hit record 14.4% of electricity generation in July

A solar farm in Cambridgeshire — rooftop and field installations both drove Britain's record solar month. — Hugh Venables / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK solar supplied a record 14.4% of Britain's electricity in July, up from 9.6% a year earlier, as sunshine and rooftop installations both hit new highs.

Solar panels supplied a record 14.4% of Great Britain's electricity in July, according to figures compiled by National Energy System Operator (NESO) and highlighted by industry body Solar Energy UK — the highest monthly share the technology has ever achieved on the grid.

The figure beats the previous record of 12.4%, set just two months earlier in May, and is well over half again the 9.6% solar managed in July 2025. Panels generated 3.2 terawatt-hours of electricity over the month, according to Energy UK data cited by trade press — enough, roughly, to supply 1.28 million homes for a year.

A sunny month, and a fast-growing fleet

Two forces did the work. July was exceptionally bright — England and Wales recorded their sunniest calendar month since Met Office records began, at roughly twice the seasonal average — and the amount of solar capacity connected to the grid kept climbing. Britain added 142,536 new solar installations between January and July, the strongest first-half figure since 2011, when a wave of subsidy-driven rooftop fitting swept the country. Most of this year's additions were domestic rooftop systems rather than utility-scale farms, spreading the capacity gain across millions of individual meters rather than a handful of large sites.

The monthly record had a sharper peak inside it. On Sunday 12 July, solar supplied 18.8% of the day's generation — just shy of the single-day record of 19.5% set on 24 May — and in the two hours either side of midday it briefly accounted for 44% of everything flowing onto the grid. Those windows illustrate both the promise and the awkwardness of solar's growth: for a couple of hours a fine summer weekend can look like a genuinely post-fossil grid, and for the other twenty-two it cannot.

"The more solar we generate, the less exposed families and households are to energy price shocks," said Michael Shanks, the UK's energy minister, in comments reported alongside the figures. Chris Hewett, chief executive of Solar Energy UK, put the shift in longer perspective: fifteen years ago, he said, solar was "a footnote in the electricity mix," and it is now "a major player" delivering "home-grown, cheap, clean and reliable energy."

Why the grid, not just the panels, matters now

The more interesting question is no longer whether solar can be built fast enough — the installation numbers answer that — but whether the system built to absorb it can keep pace. NESO has already logged a string of new instantaneous output records this year, including a peak of over 15,400 megawatts, alongside stretches of near-zero or negative wholesale prices when solar and wind output has swamped demand. Negative pricing is a signal that supply is temporarily outrunning what the grid can usefully do with it, whether through storage, exports or flexible demand — not a one-off curiosity but a symptom that will recur more often as capacity keeps rising.

Renewables supplied 52.1% of Britain's electricity across 2025, up from around 3% in 2000, and solar has gone from roughly a thirtieth of that renewable capacity fifteen years ago to about a third of it today. Solar Energy UK forecasts a further 5 to 5.5 gigawatts of new capacity this year. Each increment adds pressure on network operators to manage curtailment, storage and grid balancing — the unglamorous plumbing that determines whether record generation months translate into cheaper, more reliable power or simply into wasted output on the sunniest days.

What's next

Attention now turns to whether August, historically a strong solar month, can extend the run, and to how NESO manages the balancing costs that come with it. The next test of scale arrives with October's Energy Trends release from the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, which will show whether July's surge was a summer spike or a step change in solar's underlying share of the mix.
