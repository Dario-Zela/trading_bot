Economists press Burnham to sign UK up to UN inequality initiative

Nobel laureate Joseph Stiglitz, who led the G20 report proposing the International Panel on Inequality and would chair the body — Hentscheel / Munich Security Conference, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0 DE)
In one sentence
Forty economists led by Joseph Stiglitz have urged PM Andy Burnham to back a new UN body tracking global inequality, testing his government's redistributive instincts.

Forty of the world's most prominent economists have written to Prime Minister Andy Burnham urging him to put Britain's weight behind a new United Nations body designed to track and tackle global inequality — an early test of how far the new government is willing to go on redistribution, at home and abroad.

The letter, whose signatories include Nobel laureate Joseph Stiglitz, University College London professor Mariana Mazzucato, LSE vice-chancellor Larry Kramer and York University's Kate Pickett, calls on Burnham to formally back the creation of an International Panel on Inequality (IPI) — a body modelled explicitly on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the UN's scientific authority on global warming.

A climate-style panel for wealth gaps

The idea for the IPI grew out of a G20 report commissioned last year and led by Stiglitz, which warned of a global "inequality emergency" and proposed a standing scientific body to do for wealth disparities what the IPCC has done for climate: produce a single, authoritative evidence base that governments can't easily dismiss. Brazil, South Africa, Norway and Spain have already been involved in shaping the proposal, and a founding committee held its first meeting at the UN's Geneva headquarters earlier this year, with organisers aiming to have the panel operational by the end of 2026.

Its backers say the case for such a body is straightforward: national statistics agencies count income and wealth differently, tax havens obscure the true scale of concentration at the top, and policymakers arguing for redistribution too often lack a shared, credible dataset to build on. "An independent, trusted global evidence base on the causes and consequences of inequality, alongside analysis of the policies that can reduce it," is how the economists describe the panel's intended role — echoing, deliberately, the language used to sell the IPCC to sceptical governments in the late 1980s.

The ask, and the timing

The letter's authors, led by Pickett — co-author of The Spirit Level, the influential 2009 study linking inequality to a range of social ills — want Burnham to use his first appearance at the UN General Assembly next month to "signal the UK's strong support" for the panel. They point to his record as mayor of Greater Manchester, where he championed the real living wage and social housing investment, as reason to expect sympathy for the cause now that he sits in Downing Street.

The timing is pointed. The letter lands barely six weeks into Burnham's premiership, and pairs with a separate campaign in which more than 100 Britain-based millionaires — including Gary Lineker and Brian Eno — have told the prime minister "we can afford it," urging a 2% wealth tax on fortunes above £10m and the equalisation of capital gains tax with income tax, changes they say could raise roughly £36bn a year between them. Neither letter commits the government to anything, but together they signal where activist pressure on the new administration is concentrated: not on spending pledges, but on whether Labour under Burnham will treat inequality as a first-order policy problem rather than a talking point.

Why Downing Street may be receptive

Unlike a domestic wealth tax, signing up to the IPI costs the Treasury nothing directly — it is a diplomatic commitment, not a fiscal one. That makes it a relatively low-risk way for Burnham to signal intent on inequality without picking the fight over British taxation that the millionaires' letter invites. A Foreign Office minister, Stewart Wood, has already responded in broadly positive terms, saying the government would "work in partnership with other countries and international institutions to strengthen the global economy" — language that stops short of endorsement but leaves the door open.

The UK's scheduled chairmanship of the G20 in 2027 sharpens the calculation. The economists' letter frames that as an opportunity for Burnham to "demonstrate his leadership on the world stage" by championing the panel's launch during Britain's presidency — a chance to claim a legacy issue at minimal immediate cost, if the government chooses to take it.

What's next

Burnham's decision will likely come into focus at the UN General Assembly in New York next month, the moment the letter's signatories have identified as his first real opportunity to declare a position. Whether Downing Street treats that as a low-cost gesture or the opening move in a broader push on inequality — one that would eventually have to reckon with the domestic wealth-tax question the millionaires have already put on the table — will say a good deal about the shape of Burnham's premiership from here.
