Iran-Oman Hormuz deadline lapses amid mixed signals on shipping deal

The Strait of Hormuz, seen from NASA's Terra satellite — the 21-mile-wide chokepoint handles roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. — MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, NASA GSFC / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A US-brokered deadline on the Strait of Hormuz lapsed with Trump threatening to bomb Oman even as Tehran and Muscat report a tentative shipping-route deal.

The deadline came and went, and nobody quite agreed on what that meant. Monday marked 60 days since Washington and Tehran signed a memorandum of understanding meant to end their five-month war and settle, among other things, who controls traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. No follow-on deal materialised. Within hours, President Trump was on the phone to Fox News threatening to bomb Oman — the small Gulf sultanate that has spent weeks quietly brokering a shipping-route arrangement with Iran — "if Oman gets in the way." Iran, for its part, said the deadline never meant much because the US had broken the original agreement weeks earlier.

That contradiction — threats and progress in the same 48 hours — is the story. The strait, 21 miles wide at its narrowest, normally carries a fifth to a quarter of the world's seaborne oil on roughly 130 vessel transits a day. Since the war began, traffic is down about 90%.

Two tracks, one waterway

On the diplomatic track, Iran's semi-official Mehr News Agency said Saturday that Tehran and Muscat had settled on a transit map: ships would enter the strait through Iranian territorial waters and exit through Oman's, a reversal of the decades-old arrangement that ran traffic the other way. The plan, still described as an initial run of two to four months, would waive tolls at first, add them later, and set up a joint Iran-Oman coordination centre to track vessels. Iranian foreign minister Abbas Araghchi has been careful to separate that technical arrangement from the bigger question of reopening the strait itself, which he says won't happen until Washington eases sanctions and pays war reparations. Foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei went further, insisting "no talks were initiated" under the US-brokered MOU at all, and that the naval blockade Washington maintains around the strait has to lift first.

On the political track, Trump has raised the stakes twice over rather than once. Besides threatening Oman — "we'll bomb the s--- out of them," he told Fox's Trey Yingst — he has also said he intends to declare the strait itself US territory once the war against Iran is "finished," citing the US naval blockade as leverage: "No ships get through unless we want them to." Iran's deputy foreign minister dismissed the idea, saying the waterway "cannot be seized with a tweet, nor with an aircraft carrier."

What it means for the tape

Oil has whipsawed on exactly this ambiguity — Brent dropped below $79 a barrel on deal hopes in early August, then climbed back toward $90 as those hopes soured, before settling in the low-$80s this week. Analysts including naval specialist Bryan Clark argue both governments are trading maximalist rhetoric rather than negotiating in earnest, and that any durable fix likely ends up looking like the Iran-Oman technical arrangement — narrow, fee-bearing, and quietly implemented — rather than anything resembling Trump's territorial claim or Iran's demand for reparations. The next marker to watch is whether the Iran-Oman route actually starts moving tankers in the coming weeks; a real increase in transit volume would matter far more to freight and crude markets than another round of threats.
