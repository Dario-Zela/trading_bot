Reform UK's £50bn welfare-cut plan draws IFS fire over the arithmetic

Robert Jenrick, Reform UK's Treasury spokesperson, unveiled the party's £50bn welfare overhaul this week. — House of Commons / Roger Harris, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Reform UK pledged to cut £50bn from welfare by scrapping PIP for nearly 3 million people, but the IFS says the sums don't add up.

Robert Jenrick stood before reporters this week and did something Reform UK has mostly avoided since surging in the polls: he put a number on what the party would actually cut, and to whom. The figure was £50 billion a year, lifted almost entirely from Britain's working-age benefits bill, and the mechanism was the abolition of two of the system's central supports — Personal Independence Payment and the health element of Universal Credit.

It is the most detailed welfare plan Reform has produced, and by some distance its most consequential. The party that has spent two years as a protest vehicle for grievance about immigration and net zero is now publishing numbers that touch nearly three million people's incomes directly. Within a day, the Institute for Fiscal Studies had taken the arithmetic apart, and Labour had reached for a phrase — "fantasy economics" — that is likely to follow Reform through the rest of this parliament.

What Jenrick actually proposed

Speaking as Reform's Treasury spokesperson, Jenrick set out a plan to scrap PIP and the UC health element for working-age adults outright, replacing them with what he called a "health security allowance" — a new, regularly reviewed payment reserved for people who are, in his words, "gravely ill and severely challenged." Everyone else currently receiving disability or incapacity support for lower-level conditions would lose the cash payment. In its place, councils and mayors would run disability support accounts, funding specific costs such as equipment, home adaptations, transport and personal assistance rather than handing claimants money to spend as they choose.

A second plank shifts cost onto business: employers would be required to buy "return to work cover," a form of insurance underwriting the first two years of an employee's sick pay. Jenrick framed the whole package as roughly double the £23 billion of savings the Conservatives have proposed from the same benefits pool, with about £22 billion of Reform's total coming from disability-related changes alone. The party has also confirmed it would reimpose the two-child benefit cap in full, closing off one of the few areas where it had briefly diverged from Conservative orthodoxy.

The IFS pulls the numbers apart

The Institute for Fiscal Studies, Britain's most-cited independent fiscal watchdog, published a rapid response to the plan and it was not gentle. The think tank's central problem is scale: Reform is claiming £50 billion in savings from a working-age benefits bill projected to run to "a little over £200 billion" by 2030–31 — meaning the party is promising to remove roughly a quarter of the entire budget line in one parliament, an order of magnitude beyond anything either main party has attempted since the coalition-era cuts of the early 2010s.

The IFS also picked apart specific components. A proposed switch to a lower inflation measure for uprating benefits each year, one of the plan's quieter mechanisms, would save an estimated £4.8 billion by 2033–34 — a real but comparatively modest figure next to the headline £50 billion, and one that keeps compounding by shrinking the system indefinitely rather than delivering savings in one clean cut. On stripping benefits from non-UK citizens, another component of the package, the IFS's researchers, including welfare economist Tom Waters, warned the change would mean large, overnight income cuts for a group that in some cases derives most or all of its income from benefits — a hardship consequence the plan's headline figures do not account for.

Reform UK's proposals represent a substantially different vision of what the benefit system should be doing.

That line from the IFS response captures the institute's real objection: it is not simply that the sums look generous, it is that Reform is describing a fundamentally narrower welfare state — one confined to the most severe cases — while presenting the transition as a tidy fiscal exercise rather than the sweeping redesign it actually is.

Political fire from both flanks

Labour's response arrived fast and unambiguous: the £50 billion claim, a party spokesperson said, was "fantasy economics, built on stripping support from disabled people," with the additional charge that pushing sick-pay costs onto employers via the insurance mandate simply relocates the bill rather than shrinking it. The Conservatives, whose own £23 billion welfare-savings plan Jenrick explicitly tried to outbid, dismissed Reform's proposal as a "poor copy and paste" of Tory policy dressed up in bigger numbers.

That cross-fire matters for how the plan should be read. Reform is currently the largest party in most opinion polls, running narrowly ahead of Labour, which gives its policy papers a scrutiny they would not have received two years ago. But Reform holds no seats in government and no levers over the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecasting process, the body that would ordinarily test a governing party's savings claims against real behavioural and demographic data. Absent that discipline, a £50 billion figure is a political assertion rather than a costed commitment — and the IFS's line-by-line rebuttal is effectively acting as the outside check that a party of government would otherwise face internally.

What it signals, and what it doesn't

The substance of the plan is less important, for now, than what it reveals about where Reform intends to compete. By putting a number nearly double the Conservatives' own welfare-savings pitch on the table, Jenrick is staking out the most aggressive fiscal-retrenchment position of any major UK party, wagering that voters drawn to Reform's immigration and sovereignty messaging will also back a sharply smaller welfare state once it is spelled out in pounds and eligibility criteria.

Whether that wager pays off electorally is a separate question from whether the £50 billion figure survives contact with independent scrutiny — and on the evidence of the IFS's first pass, it does not, at least not at face value. The plan's near-term significance is as a marker of Reform's policy direction and a test of how the party manages the gap between headline numbers and technical credibility as its poll lead persists. The next test comes whenever Reform publishes a fuller costing document, or when the party is forced to defend these figures in a televised debate rather than a press conference where journalists cannot immediately cross-check its arithmetic against Treasury and OBR data.
