Rolls-Royce shares hit 14-year high on new engine programme

A Rolls-Royce Trent XWB engine, the powerplant family underpinning the group's civil aerospace order book. — Matti Blume / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Rolls-Royce shares hit a 14-year high after H1 profit jumped 46% and the group unveiled new engine programmes spanning narrowbody jets, combat aircraft and AI-driven power demand.

Rolls-Royce shares climbed to their highest level in 14 years on Tuesday, extending a rally built on two things a market rarely gets to price at once: a set of results that beat every estimate on the table, and a fresh signal that the company is putting real money behind the next generation of jet engines rather than just fixing the last one.

The stock's advance caps a run that began in earnest on July 30, when Rolls-Royce reported first-half operating profit up 46% year-on-year to £2.5 billion, on revenue that grew 26% to £11.3 billion. Free cash flow rose 24% to £2.0 billion. Every division — civil aerospace, defence, power systems — posted higher margins than a year earlier. Management responded by raising full-year guidance for underlying operating profit to a range of £4.7 billion to £4.9 billion, up from £4.0 billion to £4.2 billion previously, and lifted its free cash flow guidance to £3.8 billion–£4.0 billion.

From repair job to growth story

For most of the past four years, the Rolls-Royce investment case was a turnaround story: chief executive Tufan Erginbilgiç took over in 2023 talking of a "burning platform," stripped out cost, repaired a balance sheet battered by the pandemic grounding of long-haul fleets, and rebuilt margins division by division. That narrative carried the stock from under 100p in late 2022 to well over four figures by this year — a more than tenfold move that made Rolls-Royce one of the best-performing large-cap stocks in Europe.

What has changed since the summer is the register of the conversation. Rather than talking about cost discipline, Erginbilgiç has spent recent weeks describing new capacity commitments: a narrowbody re-entry via the UltraFan 30 demonstrator, continued high-thrust UltraFan 80 testing at Derby, and — most immediately relevant to this week's move — a strategic partnership announced on August 14 with Reliance Industries to design and build a sovereign combat engine for India's Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft programme.

That deal, still only a statement of intent pending India's separate selection of a foreign technology partner, envisages a dedicated "Aero Gas Turbine Complex" in India covering the full chain from design through manufacture and long-term support. "Together, with our existing partnerships and capabilities in India, this marks a major milestone towards building a robust, self-reliant aerospace ecosystem," Erginbilgiç said of the tie-up. Reliance's Anant Ambani framed it as combining "world-leading expertise in advanced propulsion with Reliance's technology, manufacturing, scale and execution capabilities."

Why the market is treating this differently

Investors have rewarded turnarounds before without pushing a stock to a 14-year high. What is different this time is that Rolls-Royce is committing capital and engineering hours to programmes that only pay off if the company still exists as a first-rank engine maker in the 2030s — the UltraFan 30 alone is a roughly £3 billion programme, for which Rolls-Royce is separately seeking up to £200 million in UK government support to re-enter the single-aisle market it exited more than a decade ago when it retired the V2500.

That is a different kind of bet than buyback-funded cost-cutting. It says management believes the civil aerospace order book — and the cash generation underneath it — is now durable enough to fund next-generation platforms without jeopardising the balance sheet repair of the past three years. Rolls-Royce has already returned £1.4 billion to shareholders this year as part of a £7 billion–£9 billion multi-year buyback programme and declared an interim dividend of 6p a share, its first meaningful payout in years.

The other leg of the story, less visible in the headline profit number but increasingly load-bearing, is power systems. Orders in the data-centre power business grew more than 50% in the first half as hyperscalers, squeezed by grid connection delays, sought on-site backup power; the division's organic revenue rose 28% and profit 72%. Erginbilgiç has said Rolls-Royce is close to a framework agreement with a major hyperscaler and is also developing small modular nuclear reactors — three in the UK, six agreed in the Czech Republic — as a longer-run answer to the same problem. It is a demand source almost entirely absent from the investment case two years ago.

What it means beyond Derby

For traders, the read-through is less about Rolls-Royce specifically than about what its share price implies for the wider aerospace supply chain. A stock re-rating on the strength of new-programme investment, rather than on cost recovery, suggests the market believes airline capacity growth and defence spending are durable enough to justify capital commitments with payoffs a decade out. If that read is right, suppliers further down the chain — forgings, castings, aerostructures — stand to benefit from the same order visibility Rolls-Royce is now pricing in.

The near-term catalysts are concrete. India has yet to select a foreign technology partner for the AMCA engine, a decision that will determine whether the Reliance tie-up converts into contracted revenue. The UltraFan 30 demonstrator is due to run in 2028, with Airbus and Boeing expected to make re-engining decisions before the end of the decade. And the hyperscaler power framework agreement Erginbilgiç described as imminent in July has yet to be announced. Any one of those would give the market its next read on whether Tuesday's 14-year high marks a plateau or a waypoint.
