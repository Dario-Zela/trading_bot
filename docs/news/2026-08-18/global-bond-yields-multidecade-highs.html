GLOBAL BOND ROUT · By M. Halloway · 2026-08-18 · 5 min read

US 30-year Treasury yield hits highest since 2007 as global bond rout deepens

The US Treasury Building in Washington, D.C., as long-dated government borrowing costs hit their highest level since 2007. — Rchuon24 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.29%, its highest since 2007, as a synchronized US-UK bond selloff signaled eroding trust in the Fed's inflation control.

The interest rate on the longest-dated US government debt rose to its highest level since 2007 on Monday, as a selloff that began in Treasuries spread through gilts, French bonds and long-dated debt worldwide. The move pushed the 30-year Treasury yield to 5.29% — a level last seen when the word "subprime" was only beginning to enter the public vocabulary and the first iPhone had just gone on sale. It is not a technical curiosity. The 30-year yield anchors mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and the discount rate used to value everything from tech stocks to pension liabilities, and its climb toward the 5.44% peak set during the 2007-08 financial crisis is forcing investors to ask whether the Federal Reserve still has inflation under control.

In one sentence: The 30-year Treasury yield hit 5.29%, its highest since 2007, as a synchronized selloff in US and UK long-dated bonds signaled markets no longer trust the Fed to contain inflation.

A Line Not Crossed Since 2007

Monday's move added roughly three basis points to Friday's close, but it capped a month in which the 30-year yield has climbed 13 basis points even as the 2-year yield — more sensitive to near-term Fed policy — fell 12 basis points over the same stretch. That divergence is the story. Short-term rates are easing on signs of a cooling labor market; long-term rates are rising because investors are demanding more compensation to hold US government debt for three decades. A Treasury auction of long-dated bonds last week priced at 5.216%, the highest level for that maturity since 2001, and a 10-year auction cleared at its highest yield since 2007.

Three forces are doing the work, according to strategists tracking the move. The federal deficit keeps widening, and the Treasury has been flooding the market with long-dated issuance to fund it. Traditional buyers of that debt — pension funds, insurers, foreign central banks — have not kept pace with the supply. And a sudden surge in corporate borrowing tied to AI infrastructure spending is competing with the government for the same pool of long-term capital. "A constructive view would require some combination of a downside fiscal surprise, slower AI-related issuance, [or] a shift in Treasury's issuance strategy," said Anshul Pradhan, head of US rates strategy at Barclays. None of those look imminent.

The Contagion Spreads to London

The US was not selling off in isolation. The UK's 30-year gilt yield climbed above 5.8% on the same day, its highest since 1998, while France's equivalent bond rose to 4.8% — a level not seen since 2008 — and Bloomberg's Global Long Bond Index yield reached roughly 4.2%, also its highest since July 2008. That synchrony matters more than any single number: it points to a broad, cross-border repricing of long-duration government debt rather than a problem specific to US fiscal politics.

In Britain, the move lands at an already fraught moment. Chancellor Rachel Reeves faces a November budget with markets pricing in doubt about whether the government's fiscal rules can hold without further tax rises or spending cuts, and the Office for Budget Responsibility watching closely. Gilt yields had already been grinding higher through the summer on those concerns; Monday's leg up folded a domestic fiscal story into a global one, making it harder for UK policymakers to argue their borrowing costs are a local problem they can fix alone.

Oil, Inflation and a Fed Under Suspicion

The bond rout is compounding a separate source of pressure: oil. Crude has been volatile for weeks as the standoff over the Strait of Hormuz drags on, and Iran's shift toward what it has called a "fully offensive" military posture after ceasefire talks with Washington broke down has kept a geopolitical premium in the price. Higher energy costs feed directly into headline inflation, and headline inflation is precisely what long-bond investors are now pricing in.

The numbers back that up. US consumer prices rose 3.4% year-over-year in the most recent reading, still well above the Fed's 2% target after five years of running hot, even as job cuts and a sharp drop in retail sales point to a softening economy — the textbook combination, weak growth alongside sticky prices, that traders call stagflation. The Fed held rates at 3.50%–3.75% in July for a fifth consecutive pause, but three FOMC members dissented in favor of a hike, and futures markets are now pricing roughly 65% odds of a rate increase in September rather than the cut policymakers had been signaling earlier in the year. Minutes from the July meeting, due Wednesday, will be read closely for how seriously the committee is entertaining that reversal. If the Fed is contemplating a hike even as growth slows, it is effectively conceding that its earlier read on inflation was too optimistic — precisely the credibility question the bond market is now pricing.

What Repricing Means Now

A 30-year yield at 5.29% is not just a headline number; it is a new floor under every model that discounts future cash flows. Stocks trading on growth-heavy valuations, real estate financed on the assumption that mortgage rates would drift down, and pension funds that matched long-dated liabilities against lower-yielding assets all now face the same arithmetic: the risk-free rate used to judge whether any of those bets were worth taking has moved meaningfully against them. That the shift is happening in Washington and London at once removes the option of dismissing it as an idiosyncratic US fiscal story.

The next test comes Wednesday, when the Fed releases its July minutes. A hawkish tone — more detail on the case for a September hike — would likely push long yields higher still and validate the market's current stance that inflation risk is being underpriced elsewhere in the curve. A more balanced set of minutes, emphasizing the softening labor market, would give investors a reason to treat this move as a supply-and-issuance problem rather than a verdict on Fed credibility. Either way, the 30-year yield's break above two decades of range is now the number every other market is being measured against.
