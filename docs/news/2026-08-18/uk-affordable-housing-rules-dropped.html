Ministers drop plan to relax affordable housing rules after backlash

Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street in July 2026, weeks before his government shelved the affordable housing rule change. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Ministers scrapped a plan letting developers skip affordable-home quotas on medium-sized sites after 41% of consultation respondents objected and campaigners cited a projected loss of 32,000 affordable homes.

Ministers have abandoned a plan to let developers pay their way out of building affordable homes on small and medium-sized housing sites in England, after a public consultation drew fierce opposition from housing charities, councils and — more surprisingly — some of the developers the change was meant to help.

The proposal, floated earlier this year, would have scrapped the requirement for private housebuilders to include affordable homes on developments of between 10 and 49 houses. In its place, developers would have made a cash payment — a so-called commuted sum — to the local council, which could spend it on affordable housing elsewhere, or not at all if the value fell below a set threshold. The Treasury under Rachel Reeves was said to be particularly keen on the idea, on the theory that lifting the obligation would unstick stalled sites and speed up housebuilding at a moment when the government is under pressure to show progress on its manifesto pledge to deliver the "biggest increase in social and affordable housebuilding in a generation."

That theory did not survive contact with the consultation responses. Housing officials received 910 submissions; 41% were strongly opposed and only 18% strongly in favour, with government analysts noting "significant opposition across stakeholder groups" — not just the housing associations who might be expected to object, but developers wary of the political fallout from being seen to profit from a rule change unpopular with the public.

Why the numbers mattered

The National Housing Federation, which represents housing associations, argued the change would have hit rural England hardest, where medium-sized sites of the kind the exemption targeted account for more than half of all affordable homes built. Its analysis projected the reform would have cost the country roughly 32,000 affordable homes over ten years — a number that gave campaigners a concrete figure to rally around rather than an abstract complaint about principle.

"It's extremely positive to see that the government has listened," said Kate Henderson, the Federation's chief executive, welcoming the reversal. The message from the sector was less about the specific mechanism than about trust: a government that had just taken office promising to build more affordable homes was proposing, within months, to make it structurally easier to build fewer of them.

A limit on how far deregulation can go

The episode is a useful marker for how much room the government actually has to loosen planning obligations in the name of speed and volume. Since Andy Burnham became prime minister in July, his administration has leaned toward direct intervention — grants for council housebuilding, low-cost loans to housing associations — over deregulatory shortcuts aimed at private developers. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner and planning minister Matthew Pennycook have kept the existing affordable-housing framework, which asks for 40% affordable provision on qualifying sites, broadly intact.

This was the government's first real test of trading that framework away for pace, and it folded within weeks of the consultation closing. For housebuilders that had priced in a friendlier commuted-sum regime as a route to fatter margins on medium-sized schemes, the reversal is a reminder that planning reform under this government is likely to run through council housing targets and streamlined approvals, not through lighter affordability obligations. The pressure to build faster has not gone away — it has simply been redirected toward mechanisms that don't require the government to be seen loosening the rules developers themselves are meant to meet.

Attention now turns to the government's New Towns decisions, due in late summer, and the national scheme of delegation taking effect on 31 October, which will shift most planning applications from committees to officers. Both are being pitched as the areas where ministers still intend to move fast — without repeating a consultation that made the political cost of deregulation impossible to ignore.
