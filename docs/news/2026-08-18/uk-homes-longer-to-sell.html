Half of British homes taking longer to sell amid mortgage volatility

A house for sale with a price-reduced sign — Zoopla data show sales are dragging in areas most exposed to mortgage-rate swings. — Geograph.org.uk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Homes are taking longer to sell in half of Britain's local markets as the average two-year fixed mortgage rate jumped to 5.61% from 4.83% since February.

The average time it takes to sell a home in Britain has not moved in a year — 42 days, same as last summer. That headline number, though, is hiding a market coming apart at the seams. In 180 of Great Britain's 363 local authorities, homes are taking longer to find a buyer than they did 12 months ago, according to data from the property platform Zoopla. The national average has held steady only because a handful of fast-moving markets are pulling it down while the rest of the country slows.

The culprit is the mortgage market's roughest patch in more than a year. The average two-year fixed residential mortgage rate stood at 5.61% on 14 August, according to the financial data provider Moneyfacts — up from 4.83% on 27 February, before the outbreak of the Iran conflict rattled global bond markets and pushed lenders to reprice or temporarily withdraw deals in March. Rates touched close to 6% in April before easing back, only to creep up again through July as tensions in the Middle East resurfaced and gilt yields stayed elevated.

A market splitting in two

The divide is stark on the ground. All ten of the fastest-selling markets in Great Britain are in Scotland, led by Falkirk at just 11 days. The quickest spot outside Scotland is a tie between Carlisle and Barnsley, both at 23 days. At the other end, it takes 76 days on average to sell a home in Melton, in the East Midlands, and 63 days in both Westminster and Teignbridge, in the south-west.

"While the national time to sell has barely moved, that stability is masking a real divide opening up between local markets," said Richard Donnell, Zoopla's executive director. Buyers in cheaper, faster-moving areas are pushing deals through quickly; those in pricier or more rate-sensitive markets are adopting what Donnell's team calls a wait-and-see approach, holding out for a better mortgage offer before committing.

That hesitancy matters beyond estate agents' windows. A housing market that slows when financing costs wobble — rather than when the economy itself weakens — points to buyer demand that is thinner and more rate-sensitive than headline transaction numbers suggest. Mortgage lenders and housing-linked insurers price their earnings on volume as much as margin; a stretched sales cycle delays fee income and completions even if house prices themselves hold up. Zoopla has said it expects activity to pick up from September, but only on the condition that mortgage rates stabilise — a condition that depends on how bond markets, and the situation in the Middle East, behave over the next few weeks.

The next test comes with UK inflation figures due this week, which will shape expectations for the Bank of England's next move and, by extension, the mortgage pricing that has been driving buyers to the sidelines.
