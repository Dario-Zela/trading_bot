Germany targets Channel-crossing smuggling gangs from secret police operation

A German federal police (Bundespolizei) patrol vehicle. — Hnordheim / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
German police disclosed an intelligence operation against Channel-smuggling warehouse networks, built on a new law that finally makes storing boats for UK crossings a crime.

German federal police have opened a window onto one of their longest-running efforts against the smuggling networks that supply small boats for Channel crossings, disclosing details of an intelligence-gathering operation built around the warehouses where dinghies, outboard motors and life jackets are staged before being driven to the French coast. The disclosure, described to the BBC, marks a rare moment of visibility into an enforcement effort that has typically stayed out of view.

Germany has functioned for years as the back office of the Channel smuggling trade. Essen, a mid-sized city in the Ruhr valley, became notorious after a 2024 BBC undercover investigation found smugglers openly advertising "packages" — an inflatable boat, a motor and 60 life jackets — for around €15,000, stored across what smugglers themselves said were roughly ten warehouses in the area, spread out deliberately so that a single police raid would not shut down the business.

A loophole closes

That resilience was, until recently, partly a function of German law. Storing boats destined for a country outside the EU — the UK, after Brexit — was not itself a crime, which meant police could seize equipment but struggled to prosecute the organisers who owned it. That changed on 19 December 2025, when the Bundestag passed legislation, negotiated as part of a broader security arrangement between Sir Keir Starmer and Chancellor Friedrich Merz, making the storage and onward supply of boats and engines for Channel crossings a criminal offence carrying sentences of up to ten years.

The new law gave the Bundespolizei a legal basis to build cases the way they would against organised crime more broadly — through sustained surveillance rather than one-off raids on tip-offs. That shift appears to be the backdrop to this week's disclosure: an intelligence operation that has been tracking the warehouse network's suppliers, financing and internal logistics over time, rather than simply waiting for a shipment to move.

A pattern of joint raids

The operation sits alongside a string of joint actions this year. In March, close to 1,000 officers — including the GSG9 tactical unit — carried out dawn raids across four German states, hitting more than two dozen properties tied to a network moving migrants from the Middle East and East Africa. In May, a further raid in North Rhine-Westphalia, run with Britain's National Crime Agency, seized more than 30 boats, 20 engines and 9,000 life jackets. Each operation has chipped away at capacity without stopping the trade: French officials reported a single dinghy carrying 230 people crossing the Channel earlier this month, evidence that smugglers are adapting by packing more people onto fewer boats rather than reducing the number of crossings.

Why the disclosure matters

German police rarely narrate their intelligence work in public, and doing so now reads as a signal — to smuggling networks and to London — that Berlin intends to use its new legal powers rather than let them sit unused. For the UK, it reinforces a security relationship that has become one of the few tangible outputs of the post-Brexit reset with Germany. What it does not yet show is results: no arrest figures or prosecution timeline under the new law have been disclosed alongside the operation.

The next marker will be whether German prosecutors bring the first cases under the December statute, and whether the Bundestag, which has been criticised for slow follow-through on the Starmer-Merz accord, keeps pace with the enforcement side. Channel crossing numbers for the current season, due from the Home Office in the coming weeks, will show whether any of this activity is denting the flow.
