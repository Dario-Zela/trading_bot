Amazon MGM wins UK film commission backing for studio expansion

Bray Film Studios on the River Thames in Berkshire, the historic Hammer Films site Amazon MGM now wants to expand into a two-production campus. — Chris Allen / Geograph.org.uk (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Amazon MGM's plan to double Bray Studios, backed by UK film-commission support and tax credits, tests whether Britain's production boom is durable or subsidy-dependent.

Amazon MGM Studios wants to more than double the sound stage capacity of Bray Film Studios, the Berkshire lot where Hammer Films once churned out Dracula sequels and where Amazon now shoots The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power. The plan — six new sound stages, four workshop buildings, a multi-storey car park and a production office inside a Grade II-listed former mansion — would take the site's stage space to roughly 20,000 square metres, enough for Amazon to run two big productions side by side. The Royal Borough of Windsor and Maidenhead is due to rule on the application, filed in July, this autumn.

What makes this more than a planning story is the backing behind it. The British Film Commission — the public-private body tasked with pitching Britain to Hollywood — counts Bray among its studio partners and has given the Amazon MGM productions filmed there, including Rings of Power, bespoke support in navigating UK infrastructure and permitting. Ministers have leaned in too: when Amazon confirmed the Bray purchase, the government welcomed it as proof of Britain's pull as "Europe's top filmmaking destination." Amazon puts the jobs number at around 3,200 across the UK, with 1,700 in Berkshire and 470 during construction alone.

The subsidy underneath the sound stages

None of this happens in a vacuum. Since April 2025, UK productions have qualified for the Audio-Visual Expenditure Credit at 25.5%, with visual-effects spend now stripped of the usual 80% expenditure cap and topped up with a further 3.75% uplift — one of the most generous VFX incentives anywhere. UK film and high-end TV spend hit £6.8 billion in 2025, up 22% on the year, and Disney has been the single largest beneficiary of Britain's tax relief regime over the past 15 years. Amazon's Bray bet sits squarely inside that pattern: a US streamer converting a favourable tax and planning environment into permanent bricks and steel, rather than a one-off shoot.

The dependency question

The same trend has a harder edge. The Commons Culture, Media and Sport Committee has flagged that Britain's reliance on US inward investment is "not without risk" — a warning borne out in 2023, when the Hollywood writers' and actors' strikes cut UK high-end TV investment by 49% and film by 35% almost overnight. Academics at the University of Reading have separately argued that Hollywood money has pushed studio and crew costs beyond what domestic broadcasters can afford, squeezing out British-financed film and TV even as the big US-backed productions thrive. A permanent Amazon campus at Bray is a vote of confidence in that system holding — but it also means more of Britain's production capacity is tied to a single overseas studio's slate, and to a tax credit regime that could yet be reshaped by a future chancellor.

The Windsor and Maidenhead planning committee's autumn decision is the next formal checkpoint; Thames Valley Police have already raised objections over a proposed second site access that will need resolving first.
