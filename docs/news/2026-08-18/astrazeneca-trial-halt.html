AstraZeneca halts lung-cancer drug trial, shares barely move

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters and R&D centre, where the company's oncology pipeline decisions are made. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca scrapped a lung-cancer trial for volrustomig but shares rose ~1%, offset by two other same-day trial wins — unlike July's Wainua failure, which erased £19bn in a day.

AstraZeneca said on Monday it was scrapping a late-stage trial of volrustomig, an experimental lung-cancer drug, after independent monitors concluded it was unlikely to beat the current standard of care. In a market still jumpy from the company's last pipeline scare, the shares moved about 1% — and not down. They rose.

The discontinued study, eVOLVE-Lung02, was testing volrustomig — a bispecific antibody engineered to hit both the PD-1 and CTLA-4 checkpoints — alongside chemotherapy in patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer whose tumours lack the PD-L1 protein. An independent data monitoring committee reviewed the interim results and told AstraZeneca the combination was unlikely to beat Merck's Keytruda plus chemotherapy on either of its two primary goals: progression-free survival or overall survival. AstraZeneca pulled the trial rather than let it run to a predictable failure.

Volrustomig isn't dead. AstraZeneca is continuing separate late-stage trials of the same drug in cervical cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma and mesothelioma, and the lung-cancer indication that was just abandoned had accounted for roughly a fifth of the molecule's projected peak sales — a real loss, but not a company-defining one. Susan Galbraith, the executive overseeing the oncology pipeline, said the team would "learn from this trial" and pointed to the rest of the programme.

Why the market shrugged

The muted reaction wasn't investors missing a setback — it was investors doing arithmetic. AstraZeneca used the same trading update to disclose two other late-stage wins: its Tagrisso-Orpathys combination hit both survival endpoints in the Phase 3 SAFFRON trial for EGFR-mutated lung cancer patients who had progressed on Tagrisso alone, and a separate Enhertu trial, run with partner Daiichi Sankyo, also met its goals. Netted against a bispecific failure in one lung-cancer subgroup, two wins in adjacent lung-cancer indications is a reasonable trade, and the tape treated it that way.

The contrast that matters

The useful comparison is five weeks earlier. On 9 July, AstraZeneca's heart drug Wainua, developed with Ionis Pharmaceuticals, failed to reduce cardiovascular deaths in its own late-stage trial — a result analysts said blindsided them, after management had sounded confident going in. That news arrived with nothing to offset it. Shares fell 9.55% in a single session, wiping roughly £19 billion off the company's market value; Ionis dropped 19%.

Same company, same broad category of news — a pipeline drug failing to clear its bar — and two entirely different market responses. The difference wasn't that one trial mattered more than the other. It was that one arrived as a surprise with no counterweight, and the other arrived bundled with information that made the net effect close to zero. For anyone pricing biotech headlines mechanically — trial halted, sell the stock — that distinction is the whole story. A discontinued trial is not, on its own, informative about which way a stock should move; it depends on what else landed in the same release.

AstraZeneca still says it's on track for $80 billion in annual sales by 2030, a target that assumes a pipeline this size will keep producing both hits and misses. The next test of that assumption is whichever trial reads out next — volrustomig's cervical and head-and-neck cancer data, still pending, will be the next chance to see whether the market keeps grading on the curve.
