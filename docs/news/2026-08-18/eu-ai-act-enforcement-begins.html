EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules on chatbots and deepfakes

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which now enforces AI transparency rules across the bloc. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The EU's AI Act now requires chatbots to disclose they're AI and deepfakes to be labelled, with fines up to €15m or 3% of global turnover for violations.

Since 2 August, if a chatbot on a European website is actually a piece of software, it has to say so — clearly, at first contact, not buried three clicks deep in a terms-of-service page. If a video, photo or audio clip has been generated or manipulated by AI and looks like it could pass for real, it needs a label. And the companies that get this wrong are now exposed to fines of up to €15 million or 3% of their global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

This is Article 50 of the EU's AI Act, and it's the first piece of the bloc's sprawling AI rulebook to move from legal text into active enforcement. Brussels has spent two years being accused of regulating a technology it doesn't fully understand. Article 50 is where that accusation gets tested against actual companies, actual chatbots and actual fines.

What the rule actually requires

Article 50 is narrower than the AI Act's headline-grabbing bans on things like social scoring or manipulative AI. It doesn't say what AI systems can do — it says they have to be honest about what they are. Three obligations matter most. AI systems that talk to people — chatbots, voice agents, customer-service bots — must disclose they're not human, unless it's already obvious from the context. Providers of generative AI must mark synthetic audio, image, video or text in a machine-readable format, so the content can be identified as artificial even after it's been copied or re-shared. And deployers who use AI to alter or fabricate footage that resembles real people, places or events — deepfakes, in the colloquial sense — have to label it as manipulated, visibly, when they publish it.

The rule applies regardless of intent. A company doesn't need to be trying to deceive anyone for the labelling duty to kick in; showing someone a synthetic image of a real person without saying so is enough. There are carve-outs for obviously artistic, satirical or fictional work, and for personal, non-published use — a deepfake shared in a private group chat isn't covered. Content generated before 2 August doesn't need retroactive labelling, though the Commission says it would prefer if companies did it anyway.

And it applies well beyond the EU's borders. A support chatbot built by a company in California is in scope the moment its output reaches a user in Warsaw or Lisbon. "Europeans have a right to know whether what they see, hear or read has been made or altered by AI, especially when such content can shape public debate," Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, said when the rules were confirmed.

Who's watching, and why this case is different

Enforcement is split. National market-surveillance authorities — one designated regulator per member state — police most of Article 50 on the ground, with power to investigate complaints and levy the fines set out in Article 99. The Commission's AI Office keeps a narrower brief, overseeing obligations tied to general-purpose AI models, the kind that sit underneath products like chatbots and image generators built by the largest labs.

What makes this moment worth watching isn't the rule itself so much as what it represents: the AI Act's first live test of whether a transparency obligation, backed by a nine-figure-scale fine, actually changes corporate behaviour rather than just generating compliance paperwork. Much of the rest of the Act — the stricter obligations on "high-risk" AI used in hiring, education, biometrics and border management — was pushed back to December 2027 under a separate Digital Omnibus deal reached in July, a concession to industry lobbying that argued the original timeline was unworkable. Article 50 wasn't touched by that delay. It's live now, which makes it the only part of the law regulators can actually point to and say: this is what enforcement looks like.

The stakes behind the paperwork are concrete. In one widely cited 2024 case, a finance worker at the engineering firm Arup authorised a $25.6 million transfer after joining a video call where every other participant — including the company's CFO — was an AI-generated deepfake. Nobody on the call was real. Regulators point to cases like that to argue labelling requirements aren't bureaucratic box-ticking; they're aimed at closing the gap between what a screen shows and what's actually there.

The doubts

Even officials involved in drafting the guidance concede the technical foundation is shaky. There is no single, industry-wide standard for marking AI content — companies use a patchwork of digital watermarks, embedded metadata and provenance systems that mostly don't talk to each other. Watermarks can be stripped out or degraded when content is re-encoded, cropped or passed through another platform, and once synthetic media has been edited a few times, tracing its origin becomes very difficult. The Commission's own assessment concedes as much, noting that "a combination of solutions is more powerful than any single measure" — an admission that no single technical fix will close the gap.

There's also a geography problem: content generated outside the EU by a provider with no EU presence can still reach millions of European users within minutes, and chasing that provider through a national regulator is a different exercise entirely from fining a company with a Brussels office. And some in the industry argue the entire framework is aimed at the wrong target. Alexandru Voica of the AI-video firm Synthesia has argued that "the real issue is a worrying lack of media literacy across large parts of Europe, which makes these technical obligations largely ineffective" — the suggestion being that labels only help if people notice and trust them, and Europe hasn't done much to ensure either.

To help providers show good faith, the AI Office is finalising a voluntary Code of Practice on labelling AI-generated content; signing up to it is expected to count as evidence of compliance, similar to the code of practice mechanism already used for general-purpose AI model obligations under the Act.

What comes next

The open question is whether any regulator actually uses the €15 million stick in the coming months, or whether Article 50 settles into the pattern common to disclosure rules elsewhere — widely ignored until a high-profile complaint forces a test case. National authorities have only just stood up their enforcement mechanisms, and the Commission has not signalled a first target. The next marker to watch is the AI Office's finalised Code of Practice on content labelling, expected in the coming months, which will show how far Brussels is willing to let voluntary compliance substitute for the machine-readable marking the law technically requires — and how it plans to reach providers who sit entirely outside its jurisdiction but not outside its market.
