Trump's approval falls to 33%, lowest of his presidency

President Trump's official 2026 portrait, taken as his approval rating slides to a presidency low. — Daniel Torok / White House, public domain
In one sentence
Trump's approval hit 33%, tying his first-term low, as Iran-war fatigue and rising gas prices erode the political capital behind wartime and fiscal decisions.

President Trump's approval rating has fallen to 33%, the lowest reading of either of his terms, according to a Reuters/Ipsos poll that closed Monday. Some 64% of respondents said they disapproved of his performance — a 31-point gap that ties the low point of his first term, set in December 2017. The four-day online survey of 1,166 US adults carried a margin of error of three percentage points.

The number itself is not the story. Approval ratings drift; markets do not trade on them directly. What matters is the mechanism connecting a weak president to policy that moves prices — and on that count, the poll points to something concrete: a White House with less room to maneuver at exactly the moment it is managing an active war, a sanctions regime, and a fragile domestic economy simultaneously.

What's dragging the number down

Two threads account for most of the decline. The first is the war with Iran, now in its sixth month with no resolution in sight — roughly 80% of respondents told Reuters/Ipsos they expect the conflict to drag on, and only 34% approve of how Trump is handling it, the lowest reading since pollsters began asking in March. The second is affordability. Gasoline has climbed from under $3 a gallon just before the war began to above $4 nationally, feeding into a broader unease about the cost of living: 62% disapprove of Trump's handling of the economy, and for the first time in roughly a decade, more voters trust Democrats than Republicans to manage it.

The two threads are not independent. Crude has pushed toward $83 a barrel on fears that Iran could disrupt shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and that price pressure is arriving at the pump in swing states ahead of November's midterms. Republican members of Congress — Sen. Josh Hawley and Rep. Anna Paulina Luna among them — have already begun floating gas-tax relief bills, a sign the party sees the political exposure as real rather than transient.

Why a weak president matters for markets

Approval ratings function as a rough proxy for a president's bargaining power — with Congress on spending bills, with allies on sanctions coordination, and within his own party on how long an unpopular war can be sustained without electoral cost. A president polling at 33% heading into a midterm year has less capacity to extract concessions from a Republican Congress already nervous about its majority, and less room to escalate a conflict that most voters want ended.

That matters for two markets in particular. Defense contractors have priced in a sustained appropriations cycle tied to the Iran conflict; a White House under pressure to show a war is winding down changes the calculus on multi-year contracts. And the same fiscal backdrop — heavy government borrowing to fund both the conflict and domestic priorities — is already showing up in bond markets, with the 30-year Treasury yield touching its highest level since 2007 this week. A president with less political capital to push through spending offsets, or to negotiate a swifter exit from Iran, is one more variable feeding into a bond market already pricing persistent deficits.

What's next

Watch two things: whether Republican leadership starts distancing itself from the administration's Iran posture ahead of November, and whether the next Reuters/Ipsos or Economist/YouGov reading — due within weeks — shows the decline stabilizing or accelerating. Gas prices, which have tracked almost exactly with the approval slide since February, remain the most immediate variable; a ceasefire or a Hormuz de-escalation would likely do more for Trump's numbers than anything the White House says.
