Reform UK holds narrow poll lead as Burnham's approval outpaces Farage

Prime Minister Andy Burnham's official Downing Street portrait, taken shortly after he took office in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Labour edges Reform 25% to 24.4% in the polling average, but PM Andy Burnham's own approval outpaces both Farage and Badenoch.

Four weeks into Andy Burnham's premiership, the party he leads has clawed back into a statistical tie with Reform UK — and the prime minister himself is doing better than either of the men who might otherwise be expected to be eating his lunch.

The poll-of-polls average maintained by britpolls.co.uk, which aggregates Ipsos, YouGov and other national surveys, puts Labour on 25% and Reform on 24.4% as of this week — a gap of just 0.6 points, well inside the margin that any single poll can be trusted to. The Conservatives trail in third on roughly 20%, with the Greens and Liberal Democrats both pushing past 10% in what pollsters are increasingly describing as a genuine five-party contest rather than the two-horse race British elections have traditionally been.

That is a marked shift from where things stood in late June, when Reform held a clear lead over a Labour party still led by Keir Starmer — in some surveys by as much as two to four points, with Starmer's own satisfaction rating sitting at a dismal net -59. Starmer's resignation and Burnham's elevation to Downing Street on 20 July appear to have done more to narrow that gap than any single policy announcement could have.

The Burnham effect

What stands out in the polling is not the topline party numbers so much as the personal ones. Ipsos data from late June — taken just before Burnham's move to Number 10 — already showed 30% of voters naming the then-Greater Manchester mayor as their preferred prime minister, comfortably ahead of Nigel Farage on 16% and Kemi Badenoch on 13%. Since taking office, Burnham's net approval has moved further into positive territory, with britpolls.co.uk's tracker putting it at roughly +15 — a rare reading for any sitting British leader in the current climate, and a sharp contrast with Farage's net favourability, which YouGov has recorded at -46, among the worst scores in the ten years the tracker has run.

Burnham's appeal has always cut somewhat across party lines — a legacy of his time overseeing Greater Manchester's Covid response and his frequent clashes with Westminster on regional funding, which built him a public profile distinct from the traditional party machine. As prime minister, that has translated into a favourability rating (38% favourable to 35% unfavourable in YouGov's first post-appointment poll) that neither Farage nor Badenoch can currently match, even as their parties out-poll or run neck-and-neck with his own.

Why the gap between the man and the party matters

The disconnect between Burnham's personal standing and Labour's still-fragile topline number is the more interesting story for anyone pricing UK political risk. A governing party sitting roughly 15 points below its 2024 general election result of 33% would normally point to instability — the kind that unsettles gilt markets and sterling. But a prime minister who polls ahead of his most likely challengers suggests the government has more room to manoeuvre than the raw voting-intention numbers imply, at least in the near term.

Reform's surge — up some 14 points since the last election, driven largely by voter concern over immigration, which 69% of Britons now call "too high" in britpolls.co.uk's tracking — has not been dented by Burnham's arrival so much as matched. Labour's recovery has come partly at the expense of the Conservatives and partly from voters parking their support with Burnham personally rather than embracing Labour as a brand. That is a fragile coalition, and one Reform will be working to break: the party's proposed £50bn welfare-cut package, unveiled this month, has already drawn a pointed critique from the Institute for Fiscal Studies over its costings.

What markets are watching

For investors, a Labour-Reform race this close, more than a year out from any confidence test in the Commons, is a source of low-grade but persistent uncertainty rather than an acute shock. Gilt yields have been more preoccupied lately with the inflation print due this week and the broader global bond sell-off than with Downing Street's occupant. But a Burnham government seen as personally more stable than its polling suggests could, over time, act as a dampener on the kind of sterling volatility that a Farage-led alternative would likely invite, given Reform's more radical fiscal platform.

The next test comes with the UK's autumn party conference season, when Reform, Labour and the Conservatives will each set out how they mean to close — or widen — a gap that currently sits inside the statistical noise. Until then, Burnham's personal ratings look like the more durable asset his party has.
