Ukraine launches 600-drone barrage on Moscow as Russia strikes Kyiv

The aftermath of a Russian strike on a postal distribution centre in Kyiv earlier this year — the kind of damage repeated overnight as Moscow retaliated for Ukraine's drone barrage. — State Emergency Service of Ukraine (DSNS), via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Ukraine hit Moscow with roughly 600 drones in one of the war's largest barrages, killing at least one person, as Russia struck back with missiles on Kyiv.

Just after dark on Saturday, Moscow's air defence crews began what would become one of the largest single nights of drone interception in the war's four-and-a-half-year history. By dawn, Russian officials said they had brought down more than 800 Ukrainian drones nationwide — and still, at least one of them found its way through to a private home in the Moscow region, killing an 83-year-old man. Hours later, Russian ballistic missiles were falling on Kyiv.

Neither side blinked. That, more than the body count, is the story.

In one sentence: Ukraine hit Moscow with roughly 600 drones in one of the war's largest such barrages, killing at least one person, while Russia answered with ballistic missiles on Kyiv — neither side showing any sign of running short of ways to hurt the other.

A barrage sized for the record books

Moscow Mayor Sergei Sobyanin said roughly 600 drones were launched toward the Russian capital between Saturday evening and Sunday morning, with 201 intercepted over the Moscow region alone. Russia's Defence Ministry put the nationwide total far higher — 822 drones destroyed across multiple regions — a figure that, if accurate, would make this one of the single largest waves of the drone war to date, rivalling the peaks of the 2023 Moscow attacks and surpassing most of what has come since.

Governor Andrei Vorobyov of Moscow Oblast said an elderly man was killed when a drone struck a residential property in the Ramensky district; three more people were wounded in the capital region. Further south, in Russia's Rostov Oblast, local officials reported five deaths and one injury from a parallel wave of strikes — bringing total Russian casualties from the night's attack to six dead, according to regional reporting, even as the Moscow toll alone stayed at one.

The drones were not scattered at random. Ukraine's Defence Ministry said the strike destroyed or damaged facilities belonging to Wildberries, Russia's dominant e-commerce platform, including the company's largest logistics hub — a 250,000-square-metre warehouse complex at Koledino, south of Moscow — and a second site at Domodedovo. Kyiv says it has now taken seven of Russia's ten largest Wildberries centres out of operation this year alone, part of a stated campaign to disrupt the freight and fulfilment networks the Kremlin leans on to move military as well as civilian goods. In Rostov, Ukrainian forces also targeted Kombinat Kamensky, a state facility producing solid rocket fuel for Russian artillery — a more conventional military target, and a reminder that the drone campaign runs on two tracks at once: economic disruption and direct attrition of Russia's arms-production base.

Kyiv does not go unanswered

Russia's retaliation arrived on schedule. Ballistic missiles and drones struck Kyiv overnight, hitting non-residential sites in the Obolonskyi and Holosiivskyi districts and wounding at least one person in the capital. In Kryvyi Rih, President Volodymyr Zelensky's hometown in central Ukraine, a Russian strike killed one woman and wounded six more. A separate NATO incident underscored how far the war's edges now reach: a Spanish F-18 shot down a Russian drone that had drifted into Romanian airspace by way of Moldova — the fourth such infringement recorded this year.

The exchange fits a pattern that has hardened over recent weeks rather than eased. Russia has been firing more than 100 ballistic and hypersonic missiles a month at Ukraine, according to Western estimates, and appeared to draw on stockpiled Iskander and Oreshnik-family weapons to launch dozens more in the first week of August alone. A strike on Kyiv on 1 August killed nine people and wounded 33, with Ukrainian air defence intercepting just one of 27 incoming ballistic missiles — a reminder that even a well-resourced, NATO-supported air-defence network struggles against weapons that spend only minutes in flight. Ukrainian officials have said they expect Russia may attempt a large-scale strike on Kyiv around the country's Independence Day on 24 August, a date Moscow has marked with heavy bombardment in past years.

Why the front lines of the sky matter more than the front lines of the map

Ground combat in Ukraine has, for over a year now, moved in increments measured in villages and tree lines. The war's more consequential axis has shifted upward and inward — Ukrainian long-range drones reaching 800 miles or more into Russian territory to hit refineries and rocket-fuel plants, Russian ballistic missiles arcing back to hit Kyiv within the hour. Ukraine has carried out more than 300 strikes on Russian oil infrastructure since the 2022 invasion, with a marked escalation since mid-2025; this August alone has brought more than a dozen hits on refineries in Ryazan and Volgograd oblasts, part of what Ukrainian drone-forces commander Robert "Magyar" Brovdi has described as a shift from broad refinery strikes toward narrower attacks on hard-to-replace components — the idea being to keep facilities offline for months rather than days.

That neither side's capacity to strike appears to be degrading is itself a data point. Diplomatically, it lands awkwardly. Zelensky said on 12 August that Kyiv had sent Washington a new set of proposals to end the war, and that envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner were preparing fresh contacts with both capitals. But there is no ceasefire, no territorial framework, and — as of this weekend — no sign that either Moscow or Kyiv is negotiating from a position of diminished military option. A war in which both sides can still reach deep into the other's heartland on demand is a war in which neither has much incentive to sign something short of what it set out to win.

What to watch

Ukraine's Independence Day, 24 August, is the next plausible flashpoint — officials on both sides have flagged it as a likely date for a large Russian strike, mirroring past years. Beyond that, the trajectory of Ukraine's refinery campaign will show up in Russian fuel prices and export volumes before it shows up in any battlefield map, while the fate of Witkoff and Kushner's renewed shuttle diplomacy will determine whether nights like this one become the war's new normal or its last gasp.
