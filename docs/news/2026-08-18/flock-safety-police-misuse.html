Flock Safety boss admits surveillance firm was slow to act on police misuse

A Flock Safety automated license plate reader camera, part of a network now covering roughly 120,000 cameras and 5,000 police agencies nationwide. — Tony Webster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Flock Safety's CEO admitted the company knew police were misusing its license-plate camera network — including to stalk women — but was too slow to act.

Garrett Langley built Flock Safety on a simple pitch to nervous city councils: put automated cameras on street corners, read every passing license plate, and let police search the results to solve crimes. Five thousand law enforcement agencies signed up. Roughly 120,000 of the cameras now watch American roads. This month, the company's own boss admitted the system had a hole in it big enough for officers to stalk women through — and that Flock knew and moved too slowly to close it.

"Hindsight tends to be very clear," Langley told CBS News on 14 August. "And I think, of course, we could have done more earlier." Of one specific case, he added: "I apologize. It kills me that she went through that."

What officers actually did with it

The admission followed a Washington Post investigation, published 2 August, that traced at least 50 cases in which police officers were accused of turning Flock's camera network — built, in the company's words, to fight crime — into a tool for tracking women. In 46 of those cases the officers were using Flock specifically; the rest involved rival products from Axon and Motorola. Twenty-six cases involved officers searching for wives, girlfriends, ex-partners, or their exes' new partners.

The specifics are ugly. A police chief in Braselton, Georgia, allegedly used the system to search for his ex-girlfriend's license plate and her teenage daughter's roughly 600 times. In Wisconsin, an officer allegedly ran a search to check whether his ex-girlfriend had visited an abortion clinic. In Kansas, a police chief tracked an ex-partner's movements and then confronted her while she was with another man. None of this required a warrant, a supervisor's sign-off, or — in most departments, at the time — even a stated reason. An officer with network access could type a plate into a search box and see where that car had been.

Langley pushed back on the idea that Flock created the problem. "I don't think that Flock created police abuse," he said. "I think we're the first company to ever shine a light on it." That defence rests on the fact that Flock's audit logs are what let investigators reconstruct these searches after the fact — but after the fact is the operative phrase. The logs existed before the Post published; the abuse did not stop until the story ran.

The fix, and its limits

Flock's response, unveiled 13 August, tightens rules that were previously optional. Officers must now enter a case number before running a search, rather than being able to skip the field; the default data-retention window drops from 30 days to seven, with a new "Evidence Mode" letting departments preserve records tied to active investigations for longer with sign-off from a governing body; and agencies can now restrict which categories of case — homicide or arson, say, but not immigration enforcement — outside departments are permitted to search for in their camera data. An automated system flagging abnormal search patterns, which Flock says about a third of its agencies already use, is due to roll out company-wide by the end of the year.

The gaps are already visible. The American Civil Liberties Union has documented officers typing generic terms like "investigation" into the new case-number field, or simply ignoring the prompt where enforcement is lax — Flock does not verify what officers enter. Nor has the company opened its auditing system to independent outside review; the flagging tool that is meant to catch misuse is itself unaudited. Since the Post's reporting, NPR has tallied at least 30 cities dropping their Flock contracts over roughly a year, with the advocacy group DeFlock counting more; Flock calls the losses a small fraction of its 5,000 agency contracts, and some of those departments are simply switching to competitors rather than abandoning the technology altogether.

Who's actually accountable

The deeper problem the episode exposes is structural, not just a matter of Flock tightening a few settings. A private company built a searchable, cross-jurisdictional surveillance network, sold it to thousands of police departments with minimal state or federal rules attached, and then became — by default — the only entity positioned to notice when officers abused it. Flock could see the misuse in its own logs. It chose, by its own CEO's account, to wait for state and local governments to write the guardrails rather than impose them itself. Those guardrails never fully arrived, and only a newspaper investigation forced the company's hand.

That leaves the accountability question exactly where it was before the apology: with a vendor that profits from broad, low-friction access to its network, is asked to police that access itself, and has just admitted it was too slow to do so. The next test is whether the new mandatory fields actually stop abuse or merely give officers a box to lie in — a question that will be answered by whichever local reporter or advocacy group runs the next records request, not by Flock's own audit.
