UK gilt yields hold steady as markets brace for four-month-high inflation print

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK gilt yields hold near 4.98% as economists expect headline CPI to hit a four-month high of 2.9% even as core inflation keeps cooling, muddying the BoE's rate path.

Ten-year gilt yields sat at 4.98% on Tuesday, a few basis points below the highs above 5% touched earlier this year but still elevated by any recent historical measure. Traders had a fresh growth number to chew on — UK GDP grew 0.4% in the second quarter, matching forecasts — and largely shrugged. The reason: Wednesday's inflation report is the number that matters, and economists expect it to complicate, rather than clarify, the Bank of England's next move.

The headline rate is forecast to rise to 2.9% in July, according to the median estimate in a Bloomberg survey of economists — the first acceleration in four months and a four-month high after headline CPI eased steadily from 3.3% in March to 2.6% in June, its lowest reading since December 2024. Core inflation, by contrast, is expected to keep drifting down, having already fallen from 3.1% in January to 2.6% in June, unchanged from May. It is an unusual split, and it leaves the Bank's rate-setters with two stories to reconcile rather than one.

A GDP beat that didn't move the market

Tuesday's growth figures were, on paper, encouraging. Quarterly output slowed to 0.4% from the first quarter's 0.6%, exactly as forecast, but June's monthly reading of 0.3% beat expectations for no growth at all, helped by a run of warm weather and a bump in hospitality and entertainment spending tied to the football World Cup. Services output rose 0.5% on the quarter, with computer programming up 3.7% and advertising up 4.3%.

None of it moved gilts much, and that is the tell. Sterling and yields are trading on what happens Wednesday and on the price of oil, not on last quarter's growth data. A market that priced in comfortable rate cuts a year ago is now waiting to find out whether inflation is turning back up.

Chips, planes and energy bills

The forecast uptick in headline CPI has identifiable causes, and none of them are demand overheating in the usual sense. A global shortage of memory chips, driven by the scramble to build AI data-centre capacity, is pushing up prices for consumer electronics — laptops, tablets and games consoles are all said to be getting more expensive as the cost gets passed through to retail. Separately, the conflict around the Strait of Hormuz has lifted jet fuel and airfares, and fed into household energy bills after Ofgem's latest quarterly price cap reset captured higher wholesale gas costs.

Those are supply-side shocks, not signs that British consumers are suddenly spending beyond their means — which is precisely why the core figure can keep falling even as the headline number rises. Services inflation, the Bank's preferred gauge of domestically generated price pressure, is still running around 3.6%, well above target but no longer accelerating.

The Bank's narrowing path

That split puts the Monetary Policy Committee in an uncomfortable spot. It held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, the fifth straight hold, but the vote told its own story: three of nine members — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and chief economist Huw Pill — voted for an immediate quarter-point rise to 4.00%, up from two dissenters in June. Governor Andrew Bailey noted that inflation had fallen faster than expected, to June's 2.6%, but warned that Middle East-driven energy volatility complicated the outlook; the Bank's own projections now see CPI running "a little under 3%" in the third quarter and "a little over 3¼%" in the fourth.

Pill has been the most direct of the hawks. Asked in July whether rates would need to rise over the coming year, he said: "The short answer is yes," adding that he was "concerned that we've been running the economy a little bit hotter than the supply side." Swaps markets are listening — pricing now implies Bank Rate could reach 4.00–4.25% within six to twelve months if services inflation stays sticky.

It is a sharp reversal from where consensus stood at the start of the year. Goldman Sachs, in its 2026 outlook published in January, had forecast gilt yields falling to 4% and Bank Rate being cut to 3% by summer on the back of inflation settling near 2.3%. Instead, yields are almost a full point higher than that call and the Bank spent July fending off hawkish dissent rather than debating further cuts.

What's next

The Office for National Statistics publishes July's CPI at 7am on Wednesday. A print at or above the 2.9% consensus would harden the case for the hawks ahead of the 17 September MPC meeting, where the Bank is still expected to hold — but where a further narrowing of the vote would signal that a hike, not a cut, is now the more live risk into year-end. A softer core services number inside that release would give the doves room to argue the headline move is transitory. Either way, gilt investors will get their answer well before the Bank's rate-setters have to give theirs.
