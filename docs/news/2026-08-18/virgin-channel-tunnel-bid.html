Virgin edges closer to running Channel Tunnel rail services

Eurostar trains at St Pancras International — the London terminus Virgin hopes to share by 2030. — Oxyman / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
UK regulators pre-approved Virgin Trains to run up to 20 daily Channel Tunnel services from 2030, challenging Eurostar's 30-year rail monopoly.

Virgin Trains took its clearest step yet toward breaking Eurostar's three-decade monopoly on Channel Tunnel rail services, after Britain's Office of Rail and Road pre-approved the company's access to the high-speed line linking London St Pancras to the tunnel.

The decision, issued on Monday, grants Virgin a framework agreement to run up to 20 return services a day on High Speed 1 between October 2030 and December 2040. The ramp-up is gradual: six London-Paris round trips daily at launch, rising to 13 by October 2031, plus four London-Brussels services from February 2031 and three to Amsterdam from September 2031 — the same three cities Eurostar already serves from the same London terminus.

"Our plans for a new London-Europe rail service from 2030 are moving at pace," a Virgin Group spokesperson said, welcoming the pre-approval and pointing to the prospect of "competition and Virgin's award-winning customer experience" on the route.

What's still missing

A pre-approval is not a launch date. Virgin and HS1 Ltd, the company that owns the London-to-tunnel track, have until September 4 to convert the framework into a binding access agreement. Beyond that, Virgin still needs sign-off from Eurotunnel operator Getlink to actually enter the tunnel, safety certification from UK and EU regulators, and running rights on the French, Belgian and Dutch networks at the other end — none of which it yet holds. It also needs trains: Virgin has said it is working with Alstom on 12 Avelia Stream high-speed sets, an order likely worth upward of £500 million once signed. Eurostar and rival bidders reportedly objected to the pre-approval during the ORR's review, an early sign of how contested each subsequent stage is likely to be.

The incumbent isn't standing still

Eurostar, jointly owned by SNCF and other state and infrastructure investors, carried a record 20 million passengers across its network in 2025, of which 11.8 million travelled to or from London — up 5% on the London-Paris route alone. Net profit reached £200 million, up 49% year on year. The company's public response to Virgin's progress was measured rather than defensive: it pointed to "huge potential for growth in international rail" and said its own focus remains on "investing in our fleet and carrying 30 million passengers a year," an ambition rather than a current figure. Virgin is not the only challenger circling — Spanish start-up Evolyn and UK-based Gemini Trains are also pursuing tunnel access, though neither has matched Virgin's regulatory progress.

The tunnel itself operates on an open-access basis, meaning any qualified operator has an equal legal right to use it. Whether that principle survives contact with the practical hurdles — depot capacity, cross-border safety sign-off, and an incumbent with a 30-year head start and £200 million of annual profit to defend — is the real test of Europe's rail liberalisation ambitions. The next marker is September 4, when Virgin's access agreement with HS1 is due to be finalised.
