SanDisk and Micron surge as AI memory-chip demand builds

A SanDisk NAND flash memory chip, the kind of component now central to AI data storage. — Raimond Spekking / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
SanDisk jumped 10% and Micron 6% as investors bet AI's real bottleneck is memory chips, not GPUs.

Memory chipmakers, long treated as AI's supporting cast, took a turn in the spotlight on Monday. SanDisk shares rose 10%, Micron Technology gained 6%, and Western Digital added roughly 6% as investors piled back into the companies that supply the NAND flash and DRAM chips underpinning large-scale AI infrastructure.

The move extends a rally that has been building for months but accelerated after a weekend exchange on X. Entrepreneur Peter Diamandis posted that "memory, not compute, is the rate limiter of the Agentic Era." Elon Musk replied simply: "Few realize this." The comment reinforced a thesis Musk had already laid out on SpaceX's second-quarter earnings call, where he described memory supply growing roughly 20% a year against demand increasing by 200% or more — a gap he called the single biggest constraint on AI's expansion.

Why memory, and why now

For two years, AI capital spending has been read almost entirely through the lens of GPUs — Nvidia's order book, TSMC's foundry capacity, the race for compute. Memory chips got comparatively little attention, in part because DRAM and NAND are commodity products that have historically traded on boom-bust pricing cycles rather than structural growth stories.

That framing is starting to look incomplete. Training and running large language models requires enormous amounts of high-bandwidth memory to feed data to processors, and enterprise SSDs — the NAND-based drives that store the datasets AI systems learn from — took 48% of NAND shipments last quarter, up from 26% a year earlier, according to industry data cited in recent coverage. Micron's chief business officer, Sumit Sadana, has said customers increasingly name DRAM, not power or data-centre capacity, as their primary constraint.

Micron chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra put it plainly on the company's fiscal third-quarter call: "DRAM and NAND industry demand continues to significantly exceed industry supply." He added that tight conditions are likely to persist beyond calendar 2027, since neither Micron nor its rivals expects meaningful new capacity online until late 2027 or 2028.

The market reaction

That supply-demand mismatch has translated into pricing power, and Wall Street has responded accordingly. Micron carries a Strong Buy consensus, with UBS's Timothy Arcuri holding an $1,625 price target and Bank of America projecting fiscal 2030 earnings per share of $200–$250, well above the Street's $160–$170 consensus. SanDisk, spun off from Western Digital last year, has drawn similarly bullish coverage — Goldman Sachs lifted its price target to $2,200 in July — with chief executive David Goeckeler telling investors the company is an "unbelievable franchise" targeting 80% gross margins by fiscal 2028–2030.

The scale of the re-rating shows up in fund flows too: the Roundhill Memory ETF, which launched in early April, is up more than 100% since its debut.

What it signals

A sustained rally in memory names, as distinct from a one-day pop, would suggest something more than momentum trading. It would indicate that investors are pricing AI infrastructure spending as a broader capital cycle — one that runs through storage and interconnect, not just accelerator chips — and that memory suppliers, who spent years in oversupply-driven price wars, have finally regained pricing leverage as AI demand outstrips their ability to add capacity.

It is also, awkwardly, a story with a geopolitical subplot: Washington has separately pressed Apple to avoid sourcing Chinese-made memory chips for its devices, a reminder that the memory supply chain sits inside the broader US-China tech rivalry even as Western suppliers benefit from the demand surge.

The next test comes with Micron's fiscal fourth-quarter results, expected in late September, which will show whether pricing gains are showing up in margins or merely in analyst spreadsheets. Until then, the market's working theory is that the AI buildout needs somewhere to put its data — and someone is finally getting paid for supplying it.
