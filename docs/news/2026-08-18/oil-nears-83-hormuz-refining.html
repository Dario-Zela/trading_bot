Oil nears $83 a barrel as Hormuz risk and refining bottlenecks bite

The Strait of Hormuz, once the transit point for roughly a fifth of the world's oil, from 35,000 feet. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Crude neared $83 a barrel and refining margins hit records as the Iran war shuts Hormuz shipping and Ukrainian strikes cripple Russian refineries at once.

Crude oil pushed toward $83 a barrel this week, with US benchmark West Texas Intermediate settling at $83.20 and international marker Brent crude closing in on $89 — up roughly 24% since late February, when US and Israeli strikes on Iran set off the chain of events now squeezing global fuel supply from two directions at once. The war shut down normal shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. A separate, unrelated campaign of Ukrainian drone strikes has crippled Russian refineries. Together they have done something crude supply shocks alone rarely manage: they have made refining itself, not just the oil going into it, the tightest link in the chain.

That distinction matters for anyone paying for fuel. Refining margins — the spread between what a refiner pays for crude and what it earns selling gasoline, diesel and jet fuel — have hit record highs even as crude has pulled back from the $110-plus peak it touched in May. The NYMEX 3-2-1 crack spread, a standard industry gauge, has traded near decade-plus highs this summer, roughly triple where it stood in January. Gasoline prices are up 98% year-to-date, more than double the 44% rise in WTI itself. The bottleneck has shifted downstream.

In one sentence: Crude is climbing toward $83 a barrel and refining margins are at records, as war in Iran and drone strikes on Russian refineries squeeze the world's capacity to turn oil into usable fuel.

The Hormuz chokehold, still unresolved

Before the war, roughly 130 vessels a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz, carrying close to 20 million barrels of oil — about a fifth of global supply. On Monday, ship-tracking data showed just 10 transits. The seven-day average flow has fallen to 7-9 million barrels a day, and Iran has said the strait will not reopen on normal terms until its conditions are met by Washington. The International Energy Agency has called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market, and OPEC+ spare capacity — the cushion the cartel usually leans on to offset exactly this kind of shock — has fallen to a historic low, with a large share of it stranded behind the closure. "OPEC crude production can only increase once there is a normalcy in flows," said June Goh, a Singapore-based oil analyst, capturing why the cartel's usual lever isn't working this time.

There is a narrow path back to normal. Iran and Oman have spent August finalizing a temporary shipping arrangement — a two-to-four-month corridor routing a significant share of traffic through Iranian waters — with Tehran's foreign ministry saying this week that a joint statement is in its final drafting stage. Washington has pushed for full reopening rather than a managed, temporary fix, and Iran's foreign ministry has yet to confirm timing. "Markets have not completely lost hope for a deal, but confidence is clearly eroding," said Tim Waterer, an energy analyst who has tracked the talks closely. The gap between hope and confirmation is where the risk premium lives.

Russia's refineries become the second front

The Hormuz closure would be enough on its own to tighten fuel markets. It has been compounded by a campaign that has nothing to do with the Gulf: sustained Ukrainian long-range drone strikes on Russian refining infrastructure, concentrated this month on facilities in Ryazan and Volgograd regions. Russian refinery runs have fallen to their lowest level in two decades. Moscow has responded by banning diesel exports outright and imposing broader fuel-export restrictions through the end of the year, moves aimed at keeping product at home amid domestic shortages, price spikes and rationing in several regions.

"Russia is transitioning from one of the world's largest exporters of transportation fuels to a potential importer of selected products," said Sumit Ritolia, an analyst at commodities tracker Kpler — a reversal that removes a major swing supplier of diesel and gasoline from the global market at the precise moment Gulf capacity is also constrained. The IEA estimates global refining capacity fell 4.5 million barrels a day, a 5.4% decline, in the second quarter alone. In the US, seven major refinery closures since 2019 have already removed 1.2 million barrels a day of processing capacity that isn't coming back.

Refiners cash in, consumers absorb the rest

The capacity squeeze has been extraordinarily good business for the companies that survived it. Marathon Petroleum and Valero shares have nearly doubled this year; Phillips 66 is up 66%, a third of those gains booked in the past month alone. The VanEck Oil Refiners ETF climbed more than 21% in July. Wall Street analysts are penciling in triple-digit percentage earnings growth for the sector's next reporting round. Atlantic Basin refining margins hit all-time highs in July as diesel, jet fuel and gasoline cracks all surged together — an unusual alignment that leaves refiners collecting a premium on nearly everything they produce, not just their most profitable product line.

The other side of that ledger falls on drivers and industrial buyers. The AAA national average for gasoline stood at $4.06 a gallon, up 17 cents on the month, a rise that outpaces the move in crude itself. That gap between crude and pump prices is the practical meaning of "structural premium": even a resolution in Iran would not immediately restore the refining capacity lost to closures and drone strikes, so the fuel-price cushion that once existed to absorb a second shock has largely gone.

What to watch

The Iran-Oman shipping statement, once finalized, is the next concrete test of whether Hormuz traffic can normalize on any timeline — the IEA's working assumption is not before early 2027. Washington's response to a partial, managed reopening rather than the full one it has demanded will shape how much of the current premium markets treat as temporary. On the refining side, upcoming earnings from Marathon, Valero and Phillips 66 will show how much of the crack-spread windfall is being reinvested in capacity versus returned to shareholders — a signal for whether the bottleneck eases or hardens into the market's new normal.
