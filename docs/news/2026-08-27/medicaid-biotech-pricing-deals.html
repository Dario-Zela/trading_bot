White House to unveil fresh Medicaid drug-pricing deals with biotechs

The White House, where the administration has been rolling out most-favored-nation drug pricing agreements since early 2026. — Ed Brown / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The White House is extending its Medicaid drug-pricing push from Big Pharma into midsized biotechs, a tier with far less room to absorb price cuts.

The White House is set to unveil a fresh round of Medicaid drug-pricing agreements this week, this time aimed at midsized biotechnology firms rather than the large drugmakers that have dominated the administration's pricing push so far, according to Bloomberg.

The structure mirrors deals already struck with 17 major pharmaceutical companies — including Pfizer, Eli Lilly, Amgen and, most recently, Regeneron — since the White House's most-favored-nation (MFN) initiative got underway. Participating manufacturers agree to discount outpatient drugs for state Medicaid programs so that the prices states pay track what the same companies charge in a basket of peer countries: Canada, the UK, Germany, France, Italy, Japan, Switzerland and Denmark. Bloomberg's report, citing people familiar with the plans, did not name which biotechs would sign on, and state participation in the Medicaid discounts remains optional.

From Big Pharma to the middle tier

Until now, the MFN campaign has run almost entirely through the industry's largest names — a roster that also includes AstraZeneca, Novartis, Merck, GSK, Gilead, Bristol Myers Squibb, Novo Nordisk and Sanofi. The administration says those agreements, alongside existing Medicaid drug provisions, could save $529 billion over ten years — a figure it has said could rise to $733 billion using a broader 2025 drug cohort. The savings are concentrated in a handful of therapeutic classes: antipsychotics, antiretrovirals, antineoplastics, and treatments for inflammatory disease and diabetes.

Moving into midsized biotech is a different proposition. Large pharma companies can absorb a Medicaid price cut on one product by leaning on a diversified portfolio; many midsized biotechs cannot. That asymmetry has already produced organized pushback: ten companies — Acadia Pharmaceuticals, Madrigal Pharmaceuticals, Travere Therapeutics, Alkermes, Alnylam, Ardelyx, BioMarin, Exelixis, Incyte and Neurocrine Biosciences — formed the Midsized Biotech Alliance of America earlier this year specifically to contest the policy's extension to their tier of the market.

"MFN would destroy biotech innovation in this country," the group's spokesperson, Alanna Temme, has said, adding that for single-product firms the policy "could wipe them off the map."

The alliance notes its members collectively spend about $7 billion a year on US R&D, employ 12,000 people, and have brought 30 therapies to market with nearly 100 more in the pipeline — output it argues took an average of 25 years to reach profitability and would be jeopardized by a Medicaid price shock landing on a one- or two-drug company rather than a diversified conglomerate.

What it means for the trade

For markets, the read-through is about where pricing risk now sits. Big pharma has largely priced in MFN exposure — share prices across the 17 companies already under agreement have been broadly resilient, with some, like Regeneron, seeing the deal treated as a de-risking event rather than a shock. Midsized biotechs, by contrast, often trade on the strength of one or two approved assets, meaning a Medicaid discount tied to European or Canadian list prices could compress margins in a way that shows up directly in the multiple the market assigns those single-product names. Even Eli Lilly's chief executive, David Ricks, has warned that the policy's mechanics are unpredictable once they move through the legislative process rather than staying voluntary: "When you throw it into the congressional process, what goes in is not what's going to come out."

The scale of this next round is unclear until the administration names participants, which Bloomberg's sourcing suggests could come as soon as this week. What to watch: whether the deals are voluntary or come paired with regulatory leverage, which state Medicaid programs opt in, and whether the Midsized Biotech Alliance secures the sort of carve-outs — smaller reference baskets, phase-in periods, exemptions for single-asset firms — that its member companies have been lobbying for since forming the coalition in February.
