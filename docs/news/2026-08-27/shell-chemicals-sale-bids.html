Shell draws up to $8bn in bids for US chemicals arm

Shell's petrochemical complex in Monaca, Pennsylvania — built for roughly $14bn and now part of a division up for sale. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell has drawn non-binding bids of up to $8bn for its US chemicals unit from Exxon, LyondellBasell, Apollo and Kuwait Petroleum, a steep discount to what it spent building the plants.

Shell is testing the market for its US chemicals business, and the early answer is that four industrial giants and one sovereign buyer think it's worth having a look at — even at a price that would leave Shell nursing a substantial loss on the capital it sunk into the plants.

The oil major has drawn non-binding indicative offers worth up to $8 billion for the division, according to the Financial Times, with Morgan Stanley advising on the process. ExxonMobil, LyondellBasell, Apollo Global Management and the chemicals arm of Kuwait Petroleum Corporation are among the parties that submitted approaches last month, some for the entire portfolio and others for individual plants.

What's on the block

The portfolio spans four sites in Louisiana, Texas and Pennsylvania. Its centrepiece is the sprawling Monaca complex outside Pittsburgh, which began producing polyethylene in 2022 with capacity for 1.6 million tonnes a year — and which cost Shell roughly $14 billion to build, according to prior company disclosures. The rest of the network includes plants at Norco, Geismar and Deer Park along the Gulf Coast, which supply feedstock and produce alpha olefins, ethylene oxide and other chemicals used in everything from detergents to plastics.

Even a top-of-range $8 billion sale would represent a steep discount to that cumulative outlay — a gap that says as much about the state of the US chemicals industry as it does about Shell's execution. Ethylene and polyethylene margins have been ground down for years by a wave of new Gulf Coast and Chinese capacity built during the last cycle's expansion boom, leaving crackers across the industry running below the returns their owners once underwrote.

The Sawan calculus

The sale slots into a strategy chief executive Wael Sawan has been pushing since taking over in 2023. Sawan has said Shell had roughly $45 billion of capital tied up in businesses — chemicals and renewables chief among them — that were "underperforming" against the returns he wants from the wider group, and has made clear the company no longer considers itself the natural long-term owner of a large chemicals footprint. Shell is separately marketing chemical assets in Europe, though those are expected to fetch considerably less.

For the bidders, the logic runs in the opposite direction. Exxon and LyondellBasell are scale operators who can absorb distressed capacity and extract cost synergies that a standalone owner cannot; Apollo represents the private-capital bet that margins eventually recover; Kuwait Petroleum's interest reflects a state producer's appetite for downstream integration in the world's largest chemicals market. A deal, should one emerge, would concentrate US ethylene capacity further in the hands of fewer, larger owners.

Nothing here is signed. The offers are non-binding, Shell has not committed to a sale, and the FT notes several structures remain on the table, from a single buyer taking the whole unit to a break-up among bidders. The next marker will be whether Shell moves to a binding round this autumn — and whether the price holds up once due diligence meets the reality of margins that show little sign of turning before the end of the decade.
