Hot July PCE print dents hopes for a September Fed cut

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC will decide rates on September 15–16. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
July PCE inflation held at 3.7%, hotter than forecast, cutting September rate-cut odds to 39% from 58% a week earlier and reviving hike talk.

Wall Street had spent the summer nursing a comfortable assumption: that the Federal Reserve, under a new and more market-friendly chair, was drifting toward a September rate cut. The Bureau of Economic Analysis spent Wednesday morning quietly dismantling it.

The personal consumption expenditures price index, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, rose 3.7% in July from a year earlier, a tenth of a point above the 3.6% consensus and unchanged from June's pace. Core PCE, which strips out food and energy, held at 3.3% year-on-year — in line with forecasts, but still 1.3 percentage points above the Fed's 2% target with no clear sign of narrowing. Both the headline and core index rose 0.2% on the month.

It was not a shocking number. It was worse than that, in the way inflation data can be worse: unremarkable, stubborn, and just hot enough to matter. Futures tied to the September 15–16 policy meeting moved accordingly. The implied probability of a quarter-point cut, which had stood at 58.4% a week earlier and 46.7% the day before the release, slid to 39.2% by Wednesday afternoon. Odds of a hike, which would have sounded eccentric a month ago, ticked up to roughly 44%.

A Fed chair who won't say what he'll do

The backdrop makes the numbers sting more than they otherwise might. Kevin Warsh was sworn in as Fed chair on May 22, in a White House ceremony that broke with decades of central-bank tradition, after the Senate confirmed him 54-45 — the narrowest margin for the post in history. President Trump nominated Warsh, a former Fed governor who cut his teeth at Morgan Stanley and in the Bush White House's crisis-response effort in 2008, explicitly as someone who would move rates lower. Markets took the hint and priced in easing.

Warsh has so far declined to confirm that read. He has explicitly rejected the forward-guidance language his predecessors relied on, telling reporters after the July meeting that he would rather let the data speak than commit the Fed to a path. That approach has left investors doing the interpretive work themselves — and in July, the work looked different than they expected. The Federal Open Market Committee voted 9-3 to hold rates at 3.50%–3.75%, but the three dissents came from the hawkish side: Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan all wanted an immediate 25-basis-point increase. Four more votes would flip the committee to a hiking majority. The June meeting, Warsh's first, had been unanimous; by July, cracks were showing, and the Fed had already revised its year-end PCE forecast up from 2.7% to 3.6%.

Stocks slip, but the bigger story is bonds

Equities absorbed Wednesday's data with more of a shrug than a flinch — traders had one eye on Nvidia's earnings, due after the close. The Nasdaq Composite eased 21 points to 26,130.20, the S&P 500 dipped 1.58 points to 7,675.70, and the Dow fell 113 points to 53,463.88. None of that constitutes a selloff. The more telling reaction was in rates, where Treasury yields firmed as traders trimmed cut bets, a move consistent with a market recalibrating not just the timing of easing but whether it is coming at all this year.

That distinction matters for how the rest of 2026 prices out. Equity valuations, particularly in growth and technology names, have leaned on the assumption that borrowing costs would fall from here, compressing the discount rate applied to future earnings. A Fed that holds — or worse, hikes — pulls that support away gradually rather than all at once, which is part of why Wednesday's reaction was a wobble rather than a rout. The risk is less a single bad day than a slow re-rating if the data keeps arriving this way.

What's actually driving the stickiness

The income and spending detail underneath the inflation print adds some texture. Personal income rose 0.4% in July and disposable income 0.5%, but real consumer spending was essentially flat — nominal outlays rose 0.2%, with an $86.2bn gain in services spending largely offset by a $49.9bn pullback in goods. The personal saving rate climbed to 3.0% from a four-year low of 2.6% in June, suggesting households banked some of that income gain rather than spent it. That is not the profile of an overheating consumer driving prices higher through demand; it looks more like a supply-side or cost-push story, which is consistent with the FOMC's own diagnosis in June, when officials pointed to AI-driven demand for compute and materials, tariffs, and Middle East-linked energy costs as the forces keeping inflation elevated regardless of what households are doing.

That framing matters for what the Fed can actually do about it. Rate policy works on demand, not on tariff schedules or chip shortages. If the committee believes the stickiness is structural rather than cyclical, a cut becomes a bet that slower growth is an acceptable price for signaling accommodation — a bet Wednesday's hawkish dissenters have already signaled they're unwilling to make.

What's next

August's employment report, due in the first week of September, and the August CPI print in the second week will land just before the Fed's meeting and are likely to matter more than usual, given how thin the margin between a hold and a hike vote has become. Warsh's refusal to pre-commit means the September 15–16 decision — and the press conference that follows — is now a genuine toss-up rather than a formality, with the University of Michigan's revised inflation-expectations survey another data point worth watching for signs of whether the public still trusts the 2% target.
