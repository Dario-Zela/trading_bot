Algeria wildfires kill at least 12 as heatwave sweeps North Africa

Smoke from wildfires rises over forested hills near Bejaia, Algeria, in a comparable outbreak captured by satellite in August 2021. — Pierre Markuse / modified Copernicus Sentinel data, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
At least 12 people are dead as more than 150 wildfires burn across Algeria's olive-growing Kabylie region amid a severe North African heatwave.

More than 150 separate wildfires were burning across three provinces in northeastern Algeria on Wednesday, as an intense regional heatwave pushed temperatures toward record levels and turned tinder-dry forest and farmland into fuel. At least 12 people are dead and 54 injured, six critically, according to Algeria's interior minister, Said Sayoud.

The fires are concentrated in Jijel, Bejaia and Tizi Ouzou — the mountainous Kabylie region east of Algiers, and the heartland of Algeria's olive economy. Sayoud told reporters five deaths were recorded in Jijel, four in Bejaia and three in Tizi Ouzou; among the dead were an eight-year-old boy and a 22-year-old woman, both in Bejaia. Civil Protection crews logged 154 fire outbreaks nationally by Wednesday evening, with 36 in Bejaia province alone, where 18 hotspots remained active. In Jijel, fires were burning across 12 municipalities. Three national highways linking Bejaia, Jijel and Setif have been closed.

A second wave in one summer

This is not an isolated flare-up. In late July, a separate outbreak of fires near Annaba forced the evacuation of more than 100 patients from a hospital in Seraidi after flames reached its perimeter, and killed six people. That episode, coming barely a month before this one, points to a fire season running longer and hotter than usual even by the standards of a country accustomed to summer blazes.

The immediate driver is heat. Tunisia, next door, has recorded temperatures near 49°C this summer, prompting the state utility to cut power in some neighbourhoods to protect the grid. A study of Algerian heatwaves between 1979 and 2023 found average peak temperatures during those events had risen from roughly 44.9°C to 46.8°C — evidence, researchers say, of a hotter baseline climate compounding drought and longer dry seasons, not a single freak event.

Kabylie's olive economy in the path of the fires

Jijel, Bejaia and Tizi Ouzou together produce roughly 42 percent of Algeria's olive output, alongside figs, grapes and cork oak forestry that anchor the regional economy. This is not primarily grain country — Algeria's cereal belt lies further inland on the high plains — but it is a dense, tree-based agricultural economy that recovers slowly from fire. When wildfires tore through the same region in August 2021, in what remains Algeria's deadliest fire season on record with roughly 90 deaths, they destroyed thousands of hectares of olive groves and fruit trees and left oil mills and dairies idle for want of raw material months afterward, according to local reporting at the time.

It is too early to size the damage from this week's fires to olive groves or livestock; authorities are still fighting active fronts in Bejaia and Jijel. But the pattern is familiar enough that Algerian olive producers and cooperatives will be assessing losses as soon as crews report the blazes contained, with harvest season only weeks away.

What's next

Firefighting teams have already contained fires in five other provinces — Skikda, El Tarf, Bouira, Tizi Ouzou and Tebessa — and dozens of families have been evacuated from threatened areas. The next markers to watch are the official damage assessment once the active fronts in Bejaia and Jijel are under control, and whether the heatwave gripping Algeria and Tunisia breaks in the coming days or drags the fire season further into September.
