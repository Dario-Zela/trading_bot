Major US banks weigh joint stablecoin as fintech rivals encroach on payments

Jamie Dimon, JPMorgan Chase's chief executive, has gone from stablecoin skeptic to acknowledging the bank must respond to fintech competition in payments. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
JPMorgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and others are weighing a joint stablecoin, reversing years of resistance as fintech rivals push into bank payments turf.

For most of the past decade, the executives running America's largest banks treated stablecoins the way a maître d' treats a diner without a reservation — politely, but with no intention of finding them a table. Jamie Dimon has said more than once that he does not personally see the appeal. JPMorgan's own chief financial officer, Jeremy Barnum, called the idea of stablecoins paying yield to holders "dangerous and undesirable" earlier this year.

That posture is now bending. According to a Wall Street Journal report published August 26 by Gina Heeb and Vicky Ge Huang, JPMorgan is weighing whether to issue its own stablecoin, while more than a dozen other institutions — Bank of America, Wells Fargo and Santander among them — are separately advancing plans for a jointly operated stablecoin that could start in dollars and eventually stretch to the euro and other G7 currencies. A JPMorgan spokeswoman was careful with her hedge: "While we have no plans to issue a stablecoin, depending on customer demand and the evolution of the regulatory landscape, we would of course evaluate all options in the future." That is not a denial. It is a bank keeping its options open in public for the first time.

The competitive squeeze

The timing is not coincidental. In June, more than 140 companies — Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Stripe, BlackRock, Coinbase, Google and Shopify among them — unveiled Open USD, a dollar stablecoin governed by an independent entity called Open Standard rather than any single issuer, with no fees and no volume caps for partners minting or redeeming the token. Circle's USDC and Tether's USDT already circulate in the hundreds of billions; total stablecoins in circulation stand near $263 billion, and business-to-business stablecoin payments reached roughly $226 billion in 2025. Analysts at TipRanks and elsewhere have flagged forecasts that stablecoins could handle 3% of all US dollar payments this year and 10% by 2031.

That is a slice of the settlement and liquidity business banks have run largely unchallenged since Fedwire and the Clearing House Interbank Payments System were built. Bank of America chief executive Brian Moynihan has put a number on what's at stake, estimating deposit outflows of up to $6 trillion if stablecoins pull meaningfully away from bank accounts. Dimon, in more recent remarks, has acknowledged the shift plainly: the regulatory environment is opening up, and JPMorgan faces growing competition from financial technology firms in a business it once considered its own.

The scale of the industry's response, revealed the same week, is hard to miss. On August 25, thirty-nine state banking associations — representing 3,283 banks and a combined $21.8 trillion in assets — announced the BankChain Alliance, a shared blockchain network aimed at giving community and regional banks the same tokenized-deposit and stablecoin infrastructure the giants are building for themselves, targeting a 2027 launch under interim chair Kathy Kraninger. From money-center banks down to state associations, the entire industry is moving on the same problem in the same month.

Two tracks, one motive

What's notable is that the big banks are not betting on a single vehicle. Alongside the stablecoin talks, JPMorgan, Citigroup, Bank of America and Wells Fargo are separately building a Tokenized Deposit Network through the Clearing House, targeting a first-half 2027 launch for corporate clients. Tokenized deposits stay on bank balance sheets, remain FDIC-insured up to $250,000, retain access to the Fed's discount window, and — critically — can pay interest, something stablecoins are barred from doing under current law. JPMorgan's own JPMD deposit token already processes roughly $7 billion in daily volume and more than $4 trillion cumulatively since launch, a scale that dwarfs the bank's public stablecoin ambitions to date.

The distinction matters because it maps onto an unresolved fight in Washington. The GENIUS Act, signed into law in July 2025, created the first comprehensive federal framework for stablecoin issuers, requiring full reserve backing and barring issuers from paying interest directly to holders. But the follow-on CLARITY Act, still being negotiated, has become a battleground over whether stablecoin "rewards" programs are functionally the same as interest. Dimon has been blunt about where he stands: "Rewards are the same as interest," he has argued, adding that any firm holding balances and paying returns on them "is the bank" and should be regulated like one. Banks want the growth of stablecoins channelled into instruments — tokenized deposits — that preserve their funding advantage, even as they hedge with stablecoin plans of their own in case that fight goes the other way.

A crowded field, and a mixed record

Banks have tried joint infrastructure ventures before, and the record is not encouraging: trade-finance consortia such as we.trade, Marco Polo and Contour all wound down after competing institutions found it easier to talk about shared ledgers than to actually share exposure to each other's balance sheets. A stablecoin consortium asks JPMorgan, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and rivals to agree on governance, reserve custody and liability in a way that has defeated similar efforts in the past — and to do so while facing a well-funded field of 140-plus companies that already has an eighteen-month head start with Open USD.

Still, the discussions described to the Journal are real enough that talks are described as advancing, not merely theoretical. What happens next runs through Congress: the fate of the CLARITY Act's yield language will determine whether banks lean harder into tokenized deposits or into stablecoins proper, and the Clearing House network's first-half 2027 target gives the industry a concrete date to build toward. Traders watching bank equities and stablecoin-adjacent names — Circle, Coinbase, the card networks — should expect the next signal to come from Capitol Hill rather than from any single bank's press office.
