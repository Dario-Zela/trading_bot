Trump escalates Canada tariffs to 50%, Carney vows retaliation

President Trump meets Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney in the Oval Office, May 2025 — months before this week's tariff escalation. — Daniel Torok / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Trump's 50% Canada tariffs took effect and Carney pledged matching dollar-for-dollar retaliation starting September 8, hitting steel, dairy and autos.

Trade talks between Washington and Ottawa collapsed last Friday, and just after midnight the United States began charging 50% tariffs on a swathe of Canadian goods worth roughly $20-28bn a year. Prime Minister Mark Carney did not wait long to answer. Canada, he said, would match the tariffs "dollar for dollar," with its own duties on American goods taking effect September 8.

"You're at war when you get attacked. We got attacked," Carney told reporters, a notably blunt line from a prime minister more associated with central-banker restraint than trade-war rhetoric. He called the collapse of talks the result of "last-minute changes" to U.S. terms he described as "unfair" and "uneconomic," and labelled Trump's move "a miscalculation."

What gets hit

Canada's retaliatory list, unveiled through Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc, covers about $20bn of annual U.S. imports at rates ranging from 15% to 50%. The targets read like a tour of politically sensitive American export sectors: steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, electronics, motorcycles and seafood. Washington's own list leans on Canadian metals, autos and agricultural goods — and Trump has separately flagged that tariffs on Canadian cars, trucks, auto parts and steel are set to climb further, to 50%, on January 1, 2027, layering a second escalation on top of this one.

The currency market has already made its judgment. The Canadian dollar slid to roughly 1.38 per U.S. dollar, down from a three-month high near 1.376 struck on August 21, the day before talks broke down. That is a modest move in percentage terms, but it marks a clean reversal of the loonie's recent strength and puts it back on a weakening path that has now tracked every tariff escalation since the dispute began.

The read for markets

This is the fourth round of tariff escalation in this dispute since last year, and each has followed the same pattern: a deadline, a breakdown, a retaliatory list, a currency wobble. What is different this time is the size — 50% on both sides now covers a broad enough basket of goods that supply chains in steel, autos and food processing will need to reprice inputs rather than simply absorb the cost. Cross-border manufacturers with thin margins are the most exposed; dairy and steel producers on both sides of the border are bracing for the September 8 start date.

The next hard marker is that date itself, when Canada's countermeasures take legal effect. Between now and then, watch for any fresh signal from the White House — a partial rollback, as seen recently with beef tariffs, is not out of the question if exporter lobbying intensifies. Absent that, USD/CAD and trade-exposed equities on both sides of the border look set to trade on tariff headlines rather than fundamentals for the next two weeks.
