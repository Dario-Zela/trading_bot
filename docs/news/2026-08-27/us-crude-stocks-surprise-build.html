US crude stocks post surprise build as distillates draw down

The Whiting Refinery on Lake Michigan, one of the largest US refining complexes processing crude into distillates and gasoline. — Pi.1415926535 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
EIA crude stocks rose just 95,000 barrels against forecasts for a much bigger build, while distillates plunged to 40-year seasonal lows.

US commercial crude oil inventories rose by a mere 95,000 barrels last week, the Energy Information Administration said Wednesday — a rounding error next to the multi-million-barrel swings that have rattled the oil market this month, but a surprise all the same. Traders and the EIA's own private-survey cousin, the American Petroleum Institute, had been braced for something bigger: API's Tuesday tally pointed to a 4.2 million-barrel build, well above the roughly 1.8-1.9 million barrels the market had priced in. Instead, the government's harder count came in almost flat, nudging total stocks to 428.9 million barrels — about 1% above the five-year seasonal average.

Distillates told a different story entirely. Stocks of diesel and heating oil fell 2.228 million barrels to 103.4 million, a steeper draw than the 1.6 million barrels forecasters expected. That leaves distillate inventories roughly 14% below their five-year average and, per EIA figures, the lowest for any August week since the agency's weekly series began in 1982.

A split signal

The divergence is the story. Crude sitting near seasonal norms says nothing alarming about upstream supply — refiners are still finding barrels to run, and imports have been erratic enough this month (a 17.4 million-barrel crude build in the week of August 7 was the largest since January 2023) to make any single week's crude number noisy. Distillates are a cleaner read on downstream demand, and they are being drawn down hard. That points to refiners struggling to keep pace with diesel and heating-oil demand even as the summer driving season winds down — usually a period when distillate stocks stabilize, not shrink toward four-decade lows.

The price action reflected that split. WTI slipped to around $80.42 a barrel and Brent to roughly $86.32 on Wednesday morning, both extending a weekly decline driven mostly by geopolitics rather than the inventory data — Washington held off on the harsher secondary sanctions on Iran's oil buyers that traders had feared, and President Trump said roughly 10 million barrels had transited the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday without incident, easing supply-disruption fears. Retail diesel, meanwhile, has kept climbing: AAA put the national average at $5.62 a gallon this week, up from about $3.70 a year ago.

What it means for positioning

For traders who lean on inventory prints to time entries, the crude miss — expecting some kind of move and getting essentially nothing — is a reminder that headline builds and draws can mask the more informative product-level detail. Gasoline stocks also fell, down 2.5 million barrels and now 6% below their five-year average, adding a third data point to a complex where every refined product except crude itself looks tight. The Strategic Petroleum Reserve, still refilling gradually, added roughly 3.7 million barrels to reach 289.7 million barrels for the week.

The next EIA release, covering the week ending August 28, lands September 3 and will show whether the distillate drawdown is accelerating into autumn heating-oil demand or was a one-week anomaly tied to refinery maintenance schedules. Refiners ran at 97.4% of operable capacity last week, near the top of the seasonal range, which argues against a supply-side explanation and toward genuine demand pull on the product side.
