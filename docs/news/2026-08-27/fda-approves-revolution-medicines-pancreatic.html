FDA approves Revolution Medicines' first-in-class pancreatic cancer pill

A model of a mutant RAS oncoprotein bound to a targeted inhibitor — the same family of molecular switch that daraxonrasib blocks in pancreatic tumors. — Wikimedia Commons (public domain, PDB 6OIM)
In one sentence
The FDA approved Revolution Medicines' daraxonrasib, the first RAS-targeted pill for pancreatic cancer, after it nearly doubled median survival to 13.2 months in trials.

For the roughly 67,000 Americans diagnosed with pancreatic cancer this year, the numbers have barely moved in a generation. The five-year survival rate sits at 13%, the worst of any major cancer, and for the majority who present with disease that has already spread, median survival after chemotherapy has hovered around six to seven months for decades. On Tuesday, the Food and Drug Administration approved a drug that, for the first time, changes that arithmetic in a randomized trial.

Rasonque (daraxonrasib), made by Redwood City-based Revolution Medicines, is a once-daily pill that blocks multiple mutated forms of the RAS protein — the molecular switch stuck in the "on" position in the overwhelming majority of pancreatic tumors. It is the first approved medicine to directly target that switch in this cancer, and the trial data behind it are, by the standards of a disease this stubborn, striking.

What the trial showed

The approval rests on RASolute 302, a Phase 3 trial of 500 adults with metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma who had already progressed on standard chemotherapy. Patients randomized to daraxonrasib had a median overall survival of 13.2 months, against 6.7 months for those who received chemotherapy alone — a reduction in the risk of death of 60%, with a hazard ratio of 0.40 and a p-value below 0.0001, according to results Revolution Medicines presented and later published in the New England Journal of Medicine. Median progression-free survival was 7.2 months on the drug versus 3.6 months on chemotherapy.[^1][^2] Notably, the FDA approved the drug for patients regardless of whether their tumor carries an identified RAS mutation, and without requiring a companion diagnostic test — a broader label than KRAS-targeted drugs in other cancers, such as Amgen's sotorasib for lung cancer, have typically received.[^3]

"It will be transformative in the way we treat pancreas cancer," Andrew Ko, a gastrointestinal oncologist at the University of California, San Francisco, told STAT News. "It's the biggest development we've had in pancreas cancer in decades."[^4]

The trade-off is a distinctive side-effect profile. Roughly 90% of patients on daraxonrasib developed some degree of rash, a known class effect of RAS inhibitors, and about 12-15% had grade 3 rash or stomatitis (mouth sores) severe enough to require dose interruption or topical and systemic treatment. Oncologists running the trial recommend prophylactic steroid creams and doxycycline from the outset.[^5] Set against that, serious grade 3-or-higher adverse events overall were less common on daraxonrasib than on chemotherapy — 43.6% versus 57.5% of patients — which is one reason investigators describe the safety profile as manageable rather than merely tolerable.[^2]

A decade of betting on RAS

Revolution Medicines was founded in 2014 by physician-scientist Mark Goldsmith, who remains chief executive, on a premise that had frustrated drug hunters for thirty years: RAS proteins, discovered as cancer drivers in the early 1980s, were widely considered "undruggable" because their smooth surface offers few pockets for a small molecule to grip. Amgen's 2021 approval of sotorasib for a single RAS mutation in lung cancer cracked that assumption open; Revolution Medicines built a broader platform designed to hit several mutant forms of RAS at once, betting that pancreatic cancer — where KRAS mutations appear in roughly 90% of tumors — was the harder and more valuable prize.[^6]

Daraxonrasib is the company's first approved product after more than a decade with no drug on the market, a gap it funded through public offerings, including a $1.5 billion follow-on stock sale in April 2026 that ranked among the largest in biopharma history.[^7] Shares of Revolution Medicines (Nasdaq: RVMD) closed little changed on approval day, up less than 1% to around $213, with trading volume about 1.5 times the 20-day average — a reaction consistent with a market that had already priced in a widely expected approval after strong trial data were disclosed months earlier, rather than a surprise.[^8]

Price, access, and what comes next

Rasonque will list at $39,800 for a 30-day supply in the US, with co-pay assistance intended to bring out-of-pocket costs to zero for many commercially insured patients — a price point that will draw scrutiny given the Trump administration's parallel push, detailed elsewhere in this edition, for fresh Medicaid drug-pricing deals with biotech firms.[^9] The approval arrived roughly six and a half months ahead of the FDA's regulatory deadline, reflecting the agency's Breakthrough Therapy designation for the drug, granted in mid-2025.[^10]

For investors watching biotech more broadly, the approval is being read as evidence that a decade-long, capital-intensive bet on a single mechanism can still pay off — a signal likely to sustain interest in Revolution Medicines' broader RAS pipeline, including an earlier-stage inhibitor selective for the G12V mutation, and in rival programs chasing the same biology at Amgen, Mirati and elsewhere. Revolution Medicines is also running RASolute 304, testing daraxonrasib earlier in the disease course, after surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy in patients whose tumors have been removed — a trial that, if positive, would extend the drug's reach from the roughly 90% of pancreatic cancer patients who present with metastatic disease to the smaller group eligible for surgery. Results are not expected before 2027, and until then, Tuesday's approval marks a ceiling raised, not a cure found.
