Trump bars some foreign equipment from the US power grid

High-voltage transmission towers carrying power along the Pacific DC Intertie in Oregon. — Stefan Andrej Shambora / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Trump declares a national emergency giving the Energy Department power to bar or remove foreign-made grid equipment, without naming countries, vendors or a start date.

President Trump signed an executive order on Wednesday declaring a national emergency over foreign-made equipment on the US electricity grid, giving the Energy Department broad new power to block — or force the removal of — components it deems a security risk. The order names no countries, no companies and no specific parts. It doesn't need to: the subtext, as with most recent Washington action on hardware supply chains, is China.

The order invokes the International Emergency Economic Powers Act and the National Emergencies Act, citing an "unusual and extraordinary foreign threat" from bulk-power equipment built abroad. Its reach is wide. Transformers, generators, battery storage systems, grid-connected inverters, turbines and industrial control systems are all in scope, along with the software, firmware and remote-access tools that run them. Crucially, it isn't limited to future purchases — the Energy Secretary can order existing foreign-made equipment identified, isolated, monitored or physically ripped out if it's judged to pose an "unacceptable security risk" tied to a "covered foreign entity," a term the order leaves undefined for now.

A sequel, not a debut

This is a revival, in sharper form, of ground Trump covered before. In May 2020 he signed a similar order — Executive Order 13920 — barring bulk-power equipment from designated foreign adversaries. Biden suspended it within his first 24 hours in office and let the underlying emergency declaration lapse the following spring. Utilities that lived through the first attempt found compliance messy: vague criteria, unclear timelines, and no clean list of what counted as risky. The new order gives the Energy Department 120 days to publish implementing rules, and 180 days to recommend changes to federal procurement rules favoring US-built infrastructure — deadlines meant to avoid a repeat of that ambiguity, though the substance of "covered foreign entity" still awaits definition.

Where the cost lands

The timing matters because the grid equipment market is already strained. Large power transformers, the workhorses of substations, carry lead times of two to four years industry-wide, and distribution transformer backlogs run more than a year — roughly double historical norms. China supplies an estimated 85% of global solar supply chain capacity and is a significant transformer manufacturer, meaning any enforcement that reaches those product lines collides directly with capacity utilities are already struggling to secure.

Domestic manufacturers stand to benefit either way. Hitachi Energy has committed more than $1 billion to US plants, including a facility in South Boston, Virginia due online in 2028; Siemens has put $421 million into a transformer factory in Charlotte, North Carolina. The National Electrical Manufacturers Association has called related federal moves "a step in the right direction" while warning that impact hinges on funding — a separate Defense Production Act allocation for grid equipment runs to only about $323 million for the current fiscal year, which analysts have called inadequate to reshape supply chains at scale.

None of that resolves the immediate question for utility planners: which vendors, and which parts, actually fall under the ban. Until the Energy Department's rulemaking lands in late December, procurement teams are left pricing risk against a policy whose edges haven't been drawn.
