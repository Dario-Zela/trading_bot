'Forever chemicals' surge during Thames Water drought-relief pilot

Teddington Weir on the River Thames, near where Thames Water's drought-relief scheme would abstract water and discharge treated effluent — Nigel Cox / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Pilot tests for Thames Water's drought-relief scheme found a 'forever chemical' at up to 13 times the legal limit, undercutting the utility's safety assurances.

Thames Water built the Teddington Direct River Abstraction scheme to solve one problem: London running short of drinking water as droughts become more frequent. The pilot testing meant to prove the technology safe has instead surfaced a different one — elevated levels of a toxic "forever chemical" in the very water the scheme would return to the river.

According to Thames Water's own pilot data, obtained and reported by the Guardian, concentrations of PFOS — perfluorooctane sulfonate, one of the most closely regulated PFAS compounds — reached 6.3 nanograms per litre in one test and 8.7 in another. The legal limit for surface waters is 0.65 nanograms per litre. That puts the readings up to 13 times over the threshold, reached, campaigners say, within less than a month of the pilot plant running. The same tests found iron concentrations high enough that Thames Water's own technical notes described a "significant detrimental impact" on the river if replicated at scale.

A scheme built to fix scarcity, not chemistry

The Teddington project is meant to let Thames Water pull up to 75 million litres a day from the Thames at Teddington during drought, then replace that volume with treated effluent piped from the Mogden works in Isleworth — one of the largest sewage treatment plants in the country — through a new tunnel discharging just above Teddington Lock, near the river's first designated bathing site at Ham.

Conventional wastewater treatment was never designed to remove PFAS — a family of synthetic compounds used for decades in firefighting foam, non-stick coatings and industrial processes, prized for not breaking down and now blamed for that same durability building up in rivers, soil and human blood. Mogden's effluent, like that of most treatment works, carries a PFAS load through largely unchanged. The Teddington scheme's advanced treatment stage was supposed to strip it back out before the water re-entered the Thames. The pilot suggests that stage struggled almost immediately.

Thames Water has told regulators that keeping PFOS within legal limits at full scale would be "operationally challenging" and carry "significant additional operational expense" — an admission, in the company's own technical language, that the economics of clean compliance and the economics of drought relief may not point the same way. Publicly, the company has taken a different line, telling reporters that "the data from the pilot plant study is not indicative of what would be released back into the river when the project is operational," since the pilot used simplified, smaller-scale equipment rather than the full treatment train planned for the finished scheme.

The Environment Agency, which must issue an environmental permit before the scheme can proceed, said only that "strict legal requirements" are in place and that the Teddington site "will continue to undergo rigorous environmental assessments." That is a holding position, not a verdict. Solar, the local campaign group that has opposed the scheme since it was first unveiled, was blunter: "In essence, they can't make it work properly. Test failures were rapid."

A company already juggling one crisis

The timing matters. Thames Water spent much of the past two years edging toward special administration, kept out of it only by a creditor-led recapitalisation plan that Ofwat's own board has been split over approving. Under that plan, reported by Bloomberg in April, fines for past failures would be waived until 2030 and pollution and performance targets loosened — terms environment secretary Emma Reynolds has publicly criticised as failing to protect customers or the environment. Ofwat opened a formal investigation into the company's financial resilience in July.

Against that backdrop, a drought-relief scheme meant to demonstrate Thames Water can still deliver serious infrastructure has instead produced evidence that its engineering ambition is outrunning its ability to control what comes out the other end of the pipe. Ofwat's own draft decision on the scheme, issued in July, already ordered Thames Water to model how Teddington's abstraction would interact with its other strategic water projects and to compare it against smaller, cheaper alternatives — a sign the regulator has doubts that predate the PFAS findings. Costs have moved as well: originally billed at roughly £350m, the scheme is now expected to exceed £500m after engineers found they would need additional mechanical pumps sited within a nature reserve, and separate analysis has suggested the true cost per litre of water produced could run several times higher than Thames Water's business case assumed, since the plant would likely operate only a few weeks a year.

What the pilot exposes

None of this means London's drought risk goes away if the scheme is shelved — the underlying pressure on supply, driven by a growing population and drier summers, is real and Thames Water did not invent it. What the pilot exposes is narrower and more damaging: a company under acute financial strain, negotiating away its own pollution penalties with creditors, now also asking regulators to accept operational trade-offs on PFAS as the price of drought resilience. Each crisis on its own might be defensible. Managing both at once, without one undermining the other, is the test Thames Water has yet to pass.

Thames Water is expected to lodge its full Development Consent Order application with the Planning Inspectorate in early 2027, following a supplementary public consultation that closed in June. Before then, the Environment Agency's permit decision — and whether it accepts Thames Water's argument that full-scale treatment will outperform the pilot — will be the next concrete signal of whether regulators are willing to take the company's assurances on trust.
