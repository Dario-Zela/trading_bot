Burnham won't rule out tax rises as gilt yields hold near cycle highs

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, photographed at 10 Downing Street shortly after taking office. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham won't rule out tax rises before his first Budget, and gilt yields near 5% suggest markets already expect them.

In one sentence: Andy Burnham has declined to rule out tax rises ahead of his first Budget as prime minister, a fiscal-uncertainty signal that has helped push 10-year gilt yields to levels not sustained since 2008.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for five weeks. He has not yet said how he intends to pay for the government he leads, and on Thursday he made clear he isn't about to start. Pressed repeatedly on whether taxes will rise in the autumn Budget, Burnham gave the answer chancellors and prime ministers reach for when the arithmetic hasn't come together yet: everything is on the table.

It's a small, careful sentence with a large shadow. Bond markets have already been trading as though the answer is yes.

A prime minister with no room to manoeuvre

Burnham took the keys to Downing Street on 20 July, four days after winning the Labour leadership following Keir Starmer's departure, and appointed John Healey — until then defence secretary — as his chancellor. Healey inherits a Treasury with less headroom than any of his recent predecessors enjoyed, and it has narrowed further since he arrived. The Institute for Fiscal Studies has put the gap the Budget needs to close at somewhere around £25bn, and that figure carries its own downside risk: government economists have separately modelled that an escalation of the Iran conflict could open an additional £11bn hole, rising to £35bn in a worst case, through higher energy costs and weaker growth.

Against that backdrop, Burnham's refusal to rule anything out is less a strategy than an admission. His government inherited fiscal rules it has pledged to keep, a spending base already committed under the previous administration, and an Office for Budget Responsibility forecast — due alongside the Budget on 28 October — expected to show growth of only around 1.4%. Something has to give, and revenue-raising measures are the least politically costly place to start once spending cuts and growth have been squeezed for what they're worth.

Burnham told reporters his government would "make full use of any flexibility" within the UK's existing fiscal rules — a formulation vague enough to cover borrowing more, taxing more, or both.

That comment, made on his first day in office, was enough on its own to send gilt yields to a G7 high: 10-year yields jumped to 5.04% and 30-year yields to 5.75%, the long end's worst levels since 1998. The market took "flexibility" as a euphemism for debt, and priced it accordingly.

The gilt market was already primed to punish uncertainty

The timing compounds the problem. UK 10-year yields were trading at 5.03% as of Thursday — barely below the cycle highs hit in late July — while the Bank of England continues to run down its balance sheet at a pace of £70bn a year under its quantitative tightening programme. That programme, agreed under the previous chancellor's watch, is steadily removing a buyer that has absorbed enormous volumes of government debt since 2009. It leaves private investors to absorb more of a record gilt issuance remit, with the Debt Management Office having planned to sell some £302bn of bonds in the current fiscal year — the largest annual programme since the DMO was created in 1998.

Goldman Sachs strategists, among them rates strategist David Curtin, have tied the recent selloff explicitly to fiscal uncertainty rather than to inflation or monetary policy alone — a distinction that matters, because it implies the yield premium is one Burnham's government could remove with clarity, not one the Bank of England can address through its own instruments. Long-dated gilts, most sensitive to questions about the sustainability of UK debt, have borne the brunt: 20- and 30-year yields have reached levels not seen since 1998, a generation before the 2008 financial crisis reset assumptions about what "normal" borrowing costs looked like.

The mechanics are straightforward even if the politics aren't. The Bank is a shrinking buyer just as the Treasury needs more buyers than ever, and every unresolved fiscal question — including whether income tax, VAT or National Insurance thresholds might move — adds a term premium that shows up first in the long end and eventually feeds through to mortgage pricing and corporate borrowing costs.

What the Budget has to resolve

Healey has said the Budget will comply with the government's existing fiscal rules and will lean toward devolving spending power away from Westminster — a priority that speaks to Burnham's own history as mayor of Greater Manchester but does little to close a £25bn gap on its own. Between now and 28 October, the chancellor has to reconcile three things that don't naturally fit together: a fiscal rule he says he'll keep, a spending base he says he won't cut sharply, and a revenue picture that requires either growth surprising to the upside or taxes rising. Growth forecast at 1.4% doesn't leave much room for the first option.

For traders, the read-through is fairly mechanical. Sterling and gilts have been pricing a probability-weighted mix of tax rises and further borrowing since Burnham's first day in office; each subsequent hedge — "won't rule out," "full use of flexibility" — nudges that probability without resolving it. The premium embedded in 30-year yields will not fully unwind until the Budget removes the uncertainty one way or the other. Equity investors exposed to UK domestic demand, particularly retail and housebuilders, face the mirror image: tax rises that close the fiscal gap credibly could stabilise gilts and ease borrowing costs even as they weigh on consumer spending forecasts.

The next scheduled catalyst is the Budget itself on 28 October, but the OBR's pre-release forecasts and any autumn fiscal statement from the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee — which next meets in September — will offer earlier signals of how much fiscal slack Healey actually has to work with.
