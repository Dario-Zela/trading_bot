Nvidia's $96bn quarter and bullish guidance calm AI-bubble fears

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, whose company posted record quarterly revenue and guided to 70% growth for fiscal 2028. — Pronoia / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Nvidia's $96.2bn quarter, up 106% year-over-year, and a $108bn guide for next quarter eased fears the AI capex cycle was stalling.

Nvidia posted $96.2 billion in quarterly revenue on Wednesday, more than double what it made in the same period a year ago, and told investors to expect $108 billion in the current quarter. Both numbers beat what Wall Street was modelling, and both landed at a moment when the market badly wanted a clean answer to a nagging question: is the artificial-intelligence spending boom still accelerating, or has it started to plateau under the weight of its own hype?

For a few hours, at least, Nvidia supplied the answer. Shares jumped 4.7% in after-hours trading, and futures on the Nasdaq 100 rose about 0.8% into Thursday's session, with S&P 500 futures up 0.5%. Salesforce, which also reported that evening, added its own jolt — a 12% postmarket pop on stronger-than-expected quarterly revenue of $11.35 billion — reinforcing the sense that this was a good night for big tech generally, not just one chipmaker.

The numbers behind the headline

Revenue of $96.2 billion was up 106% from $46.7 billion a year earlier and came in ahead of the roughly $92.2 billion analysts had penciled in. Adjusted earnings per share of $2.22 more than doubled the $1.05 posted a year prior. Gross margin held at 75%, with the company guiding to 74%, plus or minus 50 basis points, for the quarter ahead.

The forward guidance is where the story sharpens. Nvidia told analysts to expect $105.8 billion to $110.2 billion in revenue for its fiscal third quarter — a midpoint of $108 billion that implies year-over-year growth of roughly 89%. Chief financial officer Colette Kress went further on the earnings call, guiding to approximately 70% revenue growth for fiscal 2028, against a Street consensus closer to 44%. Nvidia's order backlog, she said, now exceeds $2 trillion.

"AI has reached its inflection point," chief executive Jensen Huang told analysts. "Compute is revenue, and demand is accelerating."

On the fiscal 2028 forecast specifically, Huang added a line that traders seized on: "We have supply for 70% growth. Our demand is much higher than that."

Data centers are still doing the heavy lifting

Strip out the headline figure and the growth is concentrated almost entirely in one segment. Data center revenue reached a record $89.0 billion, up 116.6% year-over-year and 18.3% from the prior quarter, driven by what Nvidia described as the ramp of its Blackwell Ultra systems. Sales to hyperscale cloud customers alone more than doubled to $48.7 billion.

That concentration is precisely why Nvidia's results function as a proxy for the broader capex cycle. Kress told analysts she expects combined hyperscaler capital spending to top $800 billion in 2026 and reach $1.3 trillion in 2027 — a forecast that, if it holds, would validate the spending Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta have already flagged. The four hyperscalers spent a combined $166 billion on capex in the June quarter alone, up 87% year-over-year. Amazon Web Services separately confirmed it will buy 2 million Nvidia GPUs and deploy the company's new Vera CPU, some of it paired with Nvidia's forthcoming Rubin chip.

China remains the one variable Nvidia can't guide around

The quarter's cleanest results came despite, not because of, China. Nvidia confirmed it sold no H20 chips — the cut-down processor designed to comply with US export rules — to China-based customers during the period, though it did recognize $180 million from releasing previously restricted H20 inventory to a customer outside China. Kress noted that a shift in the geopolitical picture could unlock an additional $2 billion to $5 billion in H20 revenue this quarter, but the company is not counting on it, and neither is its guidance. That Nvidia can post 106% growth while effectively writing China off as a swing factor rather than a base-case assumption says something about how much of the AI buildout is now happening in the US, the Gulf and Europe.

Why the relief rally matters, and its limits

Nvidia's stock has a recent habit of falling even after strong prints — a "sell the news" pattern that had repeated for four consecutive quarters as investors treated any result short of extraordinary as a disappointment against sky-high positioning. This time the pattern broke, which is itself a data point: it suggests expectations had reset lower after a summer of chip-sector wobbles, including a broad sell-off in late July that wiped more than $1 trillion from chip stocks amid capex-return anxiety.

That anxiety hasn't vanished. Some analysts continue to flag the growing web of financing arrangements between Nvidia and its own customers — deals in which the company backstops or invests in the build-out of data centers that will, in turn, buy its chips — as a structural risk if AI monetization disappoints. Nvidia's numbers this week don't resolve that debate so much as buy it more time: a $2 trillion backlog and a 70% growth guide are hard figures for skeptics to argue against in the near term, even if the ultimate return on the hundreds of billions being committed to AI infrastructure remains, by definition, unproven until the tokens get sold.

The next checkpoints are already on the calendar. Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet and Meta report again in late October, and their capex guidance will show whether the $800 billion hyperscaler spending figure Kress cited holds up in practice. Any resolution on China export licensing would also move the needle, given the $2-5 billion in H20 revenue Nvidia says is sitting on the table. Until then, Wednesday's numbers stand as the clearest signal yet that the capex cycle, whatever its eventual reckoning, is not slowing down now.
