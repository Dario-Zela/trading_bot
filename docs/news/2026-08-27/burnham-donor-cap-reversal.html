Burnham drops pledge to cap political mega-donors

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, official portrait, July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham has dropped his May pledge to cap political mega-donors at £500,000 after union lobbying, angering the campaigners he'd promised.

There is a particular kind of political promise that dies not with a bang but with a government source, speaking anonymously, telling reporters the votes "aren't there." That is roughly how Andy Burnham's pledge to cap political mega-donors met its end this week — quietly, through unnamed briefings, rather than any minister standing up to explain the change of heart.

The backstory is not complicated. In May, Burnham — then still building his leadership campaign — emailed Shaun Bowler, a democracy campaigner who runs the group WakeUpGB, to say he backed a cap on individual political donations, floating a figure of £500,000. "This would guard against the perception of any one party being unduly influenced or swayed by one person or organisation," he wrote, adding that he'd want the cap to fall further over time. Bowler took it as a commitment worth publicising. Byline Times ran it as news. Momentum built around amendments to the elections bill already working through Parliament.

From pledge to retreat

That momentum has now collapsed. Government sources confirmed Burnham will not back the amendments — tabled in various forms by backbenchers Stella Creasy (who proposed £100,000, then revised it upward to £500,000 in an apparent bid to meet ministers halfway) and Alex Sobel (£1 million) — that would have written a donations cap into law. The bill, as it stands, will still lower the voting age to 16 and cap foreign and overseas-British donations at £100,000. The provision curbing large domestic donors is the piece that has been dropped.

The pressure that moved Burnham appears to have come chiefly from his own side. The GMB union wrote to roughly 80 union-affiliated Labour MPs in July warning that a cap could have "significant unintended consequences," arguing that unions already face donation-specific statutory regulation that other organisations do not. Labour's structural dependence on union funding — a relationship dating back to the party's founding — gives that lobbying unusual weight inside government.

The reaction

Campaigners have not been quiet about it. Bowler called it a broken promise directly: Burnham, he said, had assured him of a "wholesale culture change" in how Westminster is funded. Creasy warned colleagues that "the public won't forgive us if we miss this chance to clean up Westminster because we are squabbling with each other." Darren Hughes, chief executive of the Electoral Reform Society, put the stakes in blunter terms: "Huge donations have a corrosive effect on trust in politics as they leave the public wondering what donors expect in return for their money. A donations cap is a crucial step to ending the impression that our politics is for sale to the highest bidder."

The timing sharpens the criticism. Burnham's first register of interests, published earlier this month, showed he personally accepted more than £350,000 in donations and gifts in the run-up to entering Downing Street — including large sums from the businessman Sacha Lord and the billionaires David Sainsbury and Gary Lubner, £50,000 from a television production company, and smaller union contributions plus perks like Glastonbury and Brit Awards tickets. None of that is unusual by the standards of British political fundraising, but it does mean the man now declining to cap such donations is himself a fairly direct beneficiary of the system as it exists.

Downing Street has not offered a public rationale beyond the vote-counting explanation relayed through unnamed sources, and has not disputed the substance of Burnham's earlier email to Bowler. Watch the elections bill's remaining committee stages for whether Creasy or Sobel force a vote on their amendments regardless of government support — and whether enough Labour MPs are willing to defy the whip on a issue the party has spent years arguing it wanted to fix.
