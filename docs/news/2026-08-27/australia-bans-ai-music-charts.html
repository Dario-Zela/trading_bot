Australia bans AI-generated songs from its official music charts

A recording studio mixing console — the kind of human production step ARIA's new rule still allows AI to assist with. — Unsplash
In one sentence
ARIA banned wholly AI-generated songs from Australia's official charts and awards, converting a voluntary global labelling standard into binding local rules.

From Friday, a song made entirely by artificial intelligence cannot appear on an Australian music chart. The Australian Recording Industry Association, which compiles the country's official singles and albums charts and hands out the ARIA Awards, has updated its code of practice to exclude tracks that are wholly AI-generated — and to bar them from award eligibility too. Songs where AI played a supporting role, rather than the starring one, can still chart.

The trigger was hard to ignore. In July, Queensland DJ Josh Fawaz released a reworking of Madonna's 1989 single "Like a Prayer" built around AI-generated vocals and drums. It became the most-played song on Australian radio, spent four weeks atop the national airplay chart and reached number four on two ARIA charts, all before Fawaz quietly added AI credits to the Spotify listing after listeners and other musicians worked out — and objected to — how it had been made. "I use AI as a tool, it's not that deep," he wrote in response to the backlash. ARIA's new rule is, in effect, a direct answer to that shrug.

Where the line sits

ARIA's code now requires that eligible recordings be "substantially human-made": humans have to write the song and perform the lead vocal and the primary instruments, and the track must raise no concerns about stream or chart manipulation. AI tools used in mixing, mastering or minor production touches don't disqualify a song; AI standing in for the vocalist or the band does. ARIA chief executive Annabelle Herd framed it as a matter of clarity rather than prohibition: "This gives everyone who releases music in Australia a clear rule to work to," she said, while calling on radio networks and streaming platforms to adopt similar standards.

ARIA also adopted the labelling scheme a coalition of global music bodies — including the IFPI, the RIAA, the Recording Academy and SAG-AFTRA — introduced in July: a black square marked "AI" for fully AI-generated tracks, a white one marked "ai" for AI-assisted work. Under the new code, ARIA can strip an ineligible track from the charts, adjust rankings retroactively, or revoke an award, and artists can contest a ruling.

A shared framework, uneven enforcement

What makes Australia's move notable isn't that it broke from a global consensus — the IFPI had already published international eligibility principles for AI recordings on official charts. It's that ARIA is among the first national bodies to convert a voluntary global framework into binding local rules with real teeth: songs pulled, chart history rewritten, awards revoked. Sweden's charts have already removed at least one track after reporters found it was substantially AI-generated, but enforcement elsewhere remains patchy, and definitions of "substantially human-made" are left to each chart body to interpret and police on its own.

That gap matters because the underlying volume is no longer trivial. Deezer has reported that AI-generated tracks account for 44% of new uploads to its platform; Apple Music has said more than a third of tracks it carries are "100% AI." A shared label doesn't settle who checks the label is accurate, or what happens when a track charts in one country under human credits and another treats it as machine-made.

What's next

ARIA's rule takes effect this week, and the next test is whether Fawaz's Madonna cover — now credited with AI vocals and drums — would fail the new eligibility bar outright if resubmitted. Beyond music, the same argument is already surfacing in publishing and film, where AI-assisted scripts and ghost-written manuscripts face no equivalent global labelling standard at all. If charts, awards bodies and streaming platforms each draw their own line on what counts as "substantially human," the practical effect for anyone releasing creative work — or investing in the platforms that distribute it — is a patchwork of eligibility rules that shift at the border, not a settled definition of authorship.
