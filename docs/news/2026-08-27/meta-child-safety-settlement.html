Meta to pay up to $18bn in landmark child-safety settlement

Meta's headquarters campus in Menlo Park, California. — LPS.1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Meta will pay up to $18bn and impose mandatory screen-time limits after 52 state AGs argued it knowingly built Instagram and Facebook to addict children.

Meta Platforms has agreed to pay as much as $18 billion and rewire how Instagram and Facebook treat teenage users, ending a set of lawsuits that accused the company of building its apps to be addictive to children and then hiding what it knew about the harm. The deal, announced Wednesday, resolves claims brought by 52 state attorneys general and comes with a legal document nearly as significant as the money: a settlement record that treats Meta's engagement-driving design — the infinite scroll, the algorithmic feed, the notification pings — not as an unfortunate side effect of building a popular product, but as a choice the company made with its eyes open.

In one sentence: Meta will pay up to $18bn and impose mandatory screen-time limits after 52 state AGs argued it knowingly built Instagram and Facebook to addict children.

A number built to move markets, not just states

The headline $18 billion figure is a composite, not a single check. The core interstate agreement — covering the bulk of the 52 attorneys general — runs to roughly $17.1 billion over ten years: a $12.1 billion base commitment plus up to $5 billion more that only becomes payable if Meta fails to persuade rivals to match its new safeguards. Texas, which negotiated separately rather than join the multistate coalition, adds another $1 billion. Participating states are set to collect about $12.7 billion of the total directly, roughly 70%, with the remainder funding an independent research foundation on teen wellbeing and five years of external auditing of Meta's compliance.

Wall Street's initial read was relief rather than alarm. Meta shares jumped as much as 4% in early trading, touching an intraday high near $593, before gains moderated to roughly 1.5% as investors weighed a $10 billion litigation charge Meta will book in the third quarter. The context matters: the states had been seeking closer to $200 billion in damages, and Meta's own lawyers had reportedly modeled a worst-case verdict as high as $1.4 trillion. Against that backdrop, an $18 billion settlement — spread over a decade, against a company with roughly $180 billion in annual revenue — reads to most analysts as a number Meta can absorb without altering its underlying economics.

What the trial exposed before it ended

The settlement arrived one day after Instagram head Adam Mosseri took the stand, and eight days into a trial in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, before Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey were serving as bellwether plaintiffs for the broader coalition, which alleged Meta violated child-protection and consumer-fraud laws by designing features specifically to hook young users while its internal research documented the mental-health toll.

Mosseri's testimony did Meta few favors. He acknowledged that take-up of "Take a Break," a feature meant to interrupt compulsive scrolling, sat in the low single digits among teenagers before Meta made it a default rather than an opt-in — a data point states used to argue that safety tools existed internally long before they were switched on for users. Meta did not admit wrongdoing as part of the settlement, and the underlying liability findings were never reduced to a final verdict. But attorneys general in Washington, D.C. and Colorado both described the deal, in public statements, as validating claims that Meta's own research confirmed the platforms were addictive and harmful — language the company chose to pay to avoid contesting before a jury.

The controls, and what they leave out

The operational core of the settlement is a set of mandatory product changes, most of which must stay in place for ten years: a default two-hour combined daily limit on Instagram and Facebook for users under 18, with usage alerts and prompts at 15, 60 and 90 minutes; a "night mode" blocking access from midnight to 6 a.m.; a "school mode" muting notifications from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m.; hidden like-and-reaction counts for teen accounts; new limits on appearance-altering filters; strengthened age verification; and expanded parental controls. If Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube adopt comparable measures, the daily limit for Meta's apps actually tightens further, to 60 minutes per platform — a clause plainly designed to pressure the rest of the industry into matching Meta's concessions rather than let it absorb a cost advantage alone.

Not every state AG involved considers the deal sufficient. New Mexico's Raúl Torrez, who won a separate jury verdict against Meta in March worth roughly $942 million, pointed out that the nationwide settlement omits two protections his state secured at trial: an outright ban on romantic or sexualized AI chatbot interactions with minors, and stronger safeguards against adults contacting children through private messaging. "We had hoped a nationwide settlement might echo the full strength of the protections New Mexico secured in court," Torrez said, adding that the gap is now a matter for Congress to close.

Why the sector is watching the fine print

For investors in the broader platform economy, the settlement's real function is as a pricing signal. It establishes, for the first time at this scale, a dollar figure and a design standard for what regulators consider unlawful engagement optimization aimed at minors — and it does so through a negotiated instrument rather than an appealable verdict, which means it can be cited as precedent without ever being tested on appeal. TikTok, Snap and YouTube now face a straightforward choice: adopt Meta's controls voluntarily and blunt the argument that they're outliers, or decline and become the more obvious next target for the same coalition of attorneys general, several of whom have said publicly they intend to keep pressing platforms that haven't settled.

The more durable question is what mandatory two-hour limits and muted engagement signals do to the metrics that underpin platform valuations — daily active users, time spent, ad inventory — once they bind for a full product cycle rather than a single feature test. Meta's third-quarter earnings call will be the first place to look for early guidance on how the company expects the changes to touch usage among its youngest cohort, and it will also carry the $10 billion charge onto the balance sheet for the first time. Congress, prodded by Torrez and others, remains the wildcard that could turn a state-by-state settlement pattern into a federal statute — a shift that would matter far more to the sector's risk profile than any single company's payout.
