FTSE 100 slips as oil-linked stocks fall and Sage tumbles on AI fears

Sage Group's Newcastle headquarters; the company led FTSE 100 fallers after Intuit's cautious guidance renewed AI-driven doubts about software valuations. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Sage sank nearly 4% after Intuit's cautious AI-era guidance, while Shell and BP fell as oil extended losses on Strait of Hormuz de-escalation, leaving the FTSE 100 lower.

London's blue-chip index drifted lower, with two unrelated stories doing the damage from opposite ends of the market. Sage Group led the fallers, down as much as 3.8% at one point, after Intuit — the American maker of QuickBooks and TurboTax — trimmed its growth guidance and reignited worries that artificial intelligence is squeezing the software industry's economics rather than expanding them. At the other end of the index, Shell and BP slipped as crude extended a third straight day of losses on signs that shipping through the Strait of Hormuz could resume more freely. The FTSE 100 closed down 8 points, or 0.1%, at 10,878.12, snapping a six-session winning run.

Software's AI reckoning

The Sage sell-off traces directly to Silicon Valley. Intuit beat Wall Street's fourth-quarter estimates comfortably — adjusted earnings of $4.03 a share against a $3.58 consensus — yet its own stock fell 12% after it guided fiscal 2027 revenue growth to just 9–10%, down from 14% in fiscal 2026. TurboTax, the tax-prep business that generative AI threatens most directly, is pencilled in for growth of only 2–3%. Capital expenditure, meanwhile, jumped 79% to $221 million as the company builds out AI infrastructure for QuickBooks.

Chief executive Sasan Goodarzi framed the caution as discipline rather than alarm: "We are being prudent with how we're thinking about our guidance… because I want to make sure that our say-do is there and I want to make sure that we build a durable model going forward." Investors read it differently — as confirmation that AI features cost more to build than they currently return in revenue, at least in the near term.

That read travelled straight to Newcastle. Sage has spent two years selling its own AI copilots to small-business bookkeeping customers as the next leg of growth, alongside steady 9%-plus organic revenue expansion. Intuit's guidance did nothing to alter Sage's own numbers, but it reframed how the market prices software peers exposed to the same question: does AI expand what customers will pay for, or does it let them do more with less and pay less for it? Goldman Sachs cut Intuit to Sell back in June on similar grounds. Thursday's move suggests that scepticism has stopped being an Intuit-specific call and started being read across the sector.

Oil eases as Hormuz risk fades

The energy side of the ledger was a more familiar story running in reverse. Brent crude fell to around $86.93 a barrel, its third consecutive daily decline, as Iran and Oman edged toward an arrangement on managing traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, including a proposed joint navigational corridor and mine-clearing agreement. Donald Trump said 10 million barrels had passed through the strait on Tuesday and repeated claims that mines in the waterway had been cleared. Separately, fresh US sanctions on Iran landed softer than markets had feared, sparing Tehran's trading partners from tougher measures. BP fell more than 2% and Shell around 1.4% as the geopolitical premium that had underpinned prices for weeks continued to unwind.

Taken together, Thursday's session was a market pricing out one risk while pricing in another. The Hormuz de-escalation is tactical and largely priced by now; the software repricing is not. Intuit's guidance lands roughly a year into the sector's AI capex build-out, the point at which investors start asking for revenue to show up rather than roadmaps. Sage reports again in the coming months, and its own AI monetisation numbers — not Intuit's — will decide whether Thursday's fall was a read-through or a one-day scare.
