US-CHINA TRADE · By C. Tanaka · 2026-11-07 · 4 min read

US presses China to meet Busan trade terms as GE Aerospace books $12.9bn of Chinese orders

In one sentence
Bessent and Greer publicly told China to honour its Busan commitments immediately, as GE Aerospace announced a record $12.9bn of Chinese deals at the Shanghai import expo.

Washington has begun to police the trade truce it once celebrated. On Friday, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer held a call with Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng. They told him that Beijing must fully and immediately honour the commitments it made at the Busan summit in October 2025. The warning was made in public, which is unusual. Washington has typically settled compliance disputes behind closed doors. This one was put on record, on the same day a US engine-maker announced its largest China haul on record.

The warning

Bessent said on X that he stressed the need for China to honour its commitments fully and immediately, and that progress depends on concrete results. Greer's office was more specific. He pressed for "concrete actions" on "agricultural purchases, rare earths, and other critical minerals". The Treasury readout said Washington will closely assess China's implementation. It also said both sides "reaffirmed the value of maintaining direct and candid communication", which is the diplomatic phrase for a conversation that was not entirely friendly.

The triage summary refers only to unspecified "recent Chinese actions". The published readouts do not give a list of violations. They name three areas where the US wants delivery: farm purchases, rare earths and other critical minerals. These were central to the original bargain. In Busan, Washington obtained a one-year pause on new Chinese export controls and commitments to buy American agricultural goods. In return it offered tariff relief. A reminder aimed at precisely these items implies that deliveries are lagging, though the readouts stop short of saying so.

Beijing's account runs the other way. On Thursday, China said it was open to buying more US farm goods if the two countries implemented the reciprocal tariff cut. According to Xinhua, it also raised "serious concerns" about recent US actions. Each side is therefore asking the other to move first. That is the usual way truces fray: neither party disputes the deal, but each reads the sequence differently.

A very different headline

The commercial news from Shanghai points the other way. On 6 November GE Aerospace said it had signed more than $12.9bn of sales and service agreements in China at the China International Import Expo, which it described as a record. The anchor customer is China Eastern Airlines, which plans to take 202 LEAP-1A engines for 101 Airbus A320neo-family aircraft, with spares and long-term service support. China Eastern also agreed to buy 14 further spare engines across the LEAP-1A, LEAP-1C and CFM56-7B families. GE also signed supply and repair agreements with MTU Maintenance Zhuhai, a joint venture involving China Southern and MTU Aero Engines. GE shares rose on the news; the size of the move was not available.

Two cautions apply. Reuters-based coverage noted that China Eastern had not immediately confirmed the agreements. And the Expo is a showcase at which Chinese buyers have long signed large, headline-friendly deals, many of them framework agreements that unfold over years. The figure measures intent and service value, not cash received this quarter.

Still, the juxtaposition is useful. Aircraft engines are among the clearest cases of American industrial exports that Beijing can reward or withhold at will. GE is also reporting strong momentum elsewhere: its third-quarter orders were $19.0bn, up 49%, and it cites a commercial services backlog above $170bn. The China deals suggest that, at the level of individual airlines and suppliers, the commercial relationship is working. The complaint in Washington is about different items: soybeans, magnets and minerals.

What the strain means

The two stories are not contradictory. Beijing can sign engine contracts at a trade fair while moving slowly on the less visible items Washington cares about. Equally, the US can publicise a company's success while pressing on its own list. Both sides have reasons to keep the truce alive. Neither wants a return to the triple-digit tariffs and export bans of early 2025.

The stakes reach beyond the two capitals. Importers have planned around the tariff reductions. Chipmakers and other manufacturers depend on uninterrupted flows of rare earths and critical minerals. Commodity buyers, farm exporters especially, are watching whether Chinese purchases materialise. If compliance slips far enough that Washington reverses its concessions, or Beijing restores the export controls it suspended, the pricing that markets have built on the truce would have to be revised.

The call also tests the Busan deal as a framework. Further talks, on a broader package or on future leader-level meetings, assume that the first round was honoured. A public demand for "immediate" action shows that the US wants to settle that question before moving on.

The next indicators are concrete. The US has said it will assess implementation closely; the data to watch are Chinese purchases of US farm goods, licensing of rare-earth exports and any Chinese confirmation of the Shanghai contracts. The one-year pause on new export controls agreed at Busan expires around the end of next October, which sets the clock for the larger negotiation.
