What a fall in share prices means for UK savers, pensions and ISAs

In one sentence
A fall in global share prices hits UK savers mainly through pensions and stocks-and-shares ISAs, with time to retirement, not the headline move, deciding how much it matters.

A sharp fall in global share prices has prompted the familiar question from British households: what does it do to the money they have put aside? The BBC's business desk has published an explainer on the knock-on effects for pensions, ISAs and everyday finances. The scale of the latest selloff is not set out in the material available for this piece, so what follows deals with the mechanisms, which hold whatever the size of the move.

The short answer is that the exposure is large, uneven and mostly indirect. Most UK adults hold no shares directly. They hold them through a workplace pension, a stocks and shares ISA or a fund inside one of those wrappers. How much a falling market hurts depends less on the headline index than on what a person owns and how soon they need the money.

Pensions: age and structure decide the damage

Most workplace pension savers are in a defined contribution scheme, usually a default fund. Those funds typically hold a high share of equities, much of it global, while the saver is young. They shift gradually towards bonds and cash as the retirement date nears. This "lifestyling" is designed for the moment a selloff arrives. A saver 30 years from retirement can treat a fall as a discount on future contributions, because monthly payments buy more units when prices are lower. A saver in the final few years is shielded in part by the bond and cash allocation, though not entirely.

The group most exposed is the one that has retired, or is about to, but keeps its pot invested through drawdown. Selling units to pay income during a downturn locks in losses. That is the "sequence of returns" risk that advisers cite, and it is why the standard guidance is to hold a cash buffer of a year or two of spending rather than sell into weakness.

Savers close to retirement face a second effect. Annuity rates move with government bond yields. When a selloff pushes investors into gilts, yields fall and the income bought by a given pot falls with them. An earlier BBC piece on market turmoil, citing Hargreaves Lansdown, estimated that falling shares and lower annuity rates together could cut the income from a £100,000 pot by £926 a year for a 65-year-old. That figure comes from a previous episode and is an illustration, not a measure of the current fall.

Final salary schemes are less direct. The member's benefit is promised, though falling asset values can widen the scheme's deficit and put pressure on the sponsoring employer.

ISAs: the wrapper does not protect the contents

A stocks and shares ISA shelters gains and income from tax. It does not shelter the holder from price falls. Value drops one-for-one with the underlying funds, and losses within an ISA cannot be set against gains elsewhere for tax purposes.

A cash ISA is a different matter. Its balance does not move with equities, and the effect of a selloff is indirect: if weak markets lead the Bank of England to cut rates sooner, savings rates drift lower. Cash savers still hunting for yield can find it. Nationwide, for one, has launched a savings account paying 5.3%, which is covered elsewhere in this edition.

The practical advice that recurs in every episode is unglamorous. Paper losses become real only on sale. Stopping regular contributions after a fall means missing the cheaper purchases. Checking that a fund's risk level still matches the holder's timescale is reasonable, but doing so in a panic usually means selling after the drop and buying back after the recovery. BBC business editor Simon Jack has made the point in past episodes that pensions are a long-term game that will see volatility like this.

Household finances: rates, mortgages and confidence

The wider household effect runs through interest rates and sentiment. Equity falls tend to pull bond yields down, which feeds through to fixed-rate mortgage pricing, though with a lag. A selloff driven by growth fears raises the odds of rate cuts. One driven by inflation or bond-market stress can have the opposite effect. The distinction matters more to borrowers than the size of the equity fall.

There is also a confidence channel. Households that feel poorer tend to spend less, and those with large investment holdings are generally the better off, so the effect on aggregate spending is modest compared with a house-price fall. For most families, the pension statement that arrives in the post is the only place the selloff will ever show.

What to watch

Two things would turn an ordinary correction into a policy question. The first is persistence: pension providers publish quarterly statements, and a fall that lasts through the next reporting cycle will start to alarm members of default funds. The second is the cause. A selloff tied to a named UK pension or tax issue, rather than to global tech valuations or US data, would warrant a different kind of coverage. For now the evidence in hand describes a market move whose size has not been confirmed in this reporting, and whose consequences for most savers depend on time horizon rather than on this week's index level.
