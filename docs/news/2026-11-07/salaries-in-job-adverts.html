PM tells firms to publish salaries in job adverts ahead of new law

In one sentence
The Prime Minister is urging employers to publish salaries in job adverts before a planned legal duty, still at consultation stage with no start date, takes effect.

The Prime Minister has asked employers to start putting salaries in their job adverts now, rather than waiting for the law to require it. The request, reported by BBC Politics, turns a rule still working its way through government into an early test. The question is whether firms will disclose pay before they have to.

The BBC report's detailed wording was not available to verify. This article therefore does not quote the Prime Minister or give a start date for the request. What can be confirmed is the policy behind it.

The rule behind the request

The government is consulting on a statutory duty for employers to include pay and conditions information in job adverts. Where there is no advert, employers would have to give candidates the information in writing before interview. The consultation closed on 27 October 2026, according to a Lewis Silkin analysis published in July.

The proposal would apply to all employers, with no size threshold mentioned in that analysis. It would be introduced through regulations, and the government has signalled a long implementation process. No commencement date has been set and the regulations have not been made. The government says it wants a low burden on business and does not plan new reporting requirements.

Much is still undecided. The consultation does not fix how wide a quoted pay range may be. It leaves open exactly what "pay information" must cover, with salary, collective bargaining terms, annual adjustments and other financial benefits given as examples. It also does not clearly say where the duty falls when a recruitment agency places the advert.

Why ask early

A voluntary push is cheap for ministers and informative for employers. If many firms comply, the eventual rule looks like a codification of existing practice. If few do, that is evidence about the compliance gap the regulations will have to close.

The gap is real. An Adzuna analysis found only about six in ten job adverts stated the salary on offer. Government has tried persuasion before. In 2022 it urged employers to publish pay to draw more women into higher-paid roles, alongside a two-year pay transparency pilot. A Commons select committee has also recommended considering compulsory publication of starting salaries.

The UK proposal is narrower than the EU's Pay Transparency Directive. The Lewis Silkin analysis notes that it lacks the Directive's ban on asking candidates about current or previous pay, its pay-comparator information rights, and its duty to make pay-setting criteria accessible. Employers operating on both sides of the Channel may therefore face two different regimes.

What to watch

The next marker is the government's response to the consultation, which will show how wide a pay range employers must publish and whether agencies are covered. Until regulations are laid and a start date is set, the Prime Minister's request carries moral weight rather than legal force. How many large employers change their adverts in the coming weeks will show how much that weight counts for.
