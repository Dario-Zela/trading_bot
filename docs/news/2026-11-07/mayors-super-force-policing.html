English mayors warn 'super-force' plans threaten local policing

In one sentence
Mayors say proposals to merge English police forces into 'super-forces' risk eroding local accountability, in a dispute over who oversees policing as forces grow.

English mayors are warning that government plans to merge police forces into larger 'super-forces' would weaken local accountability. The dispute is over who answers for policing when the forces covering England get bigger and fewer.

The account rests on a single report, from the Guardian's politics coverage. This article has not independently checked the detail of the proposals, the names of the mayors involved or the wording of any official document. What follows is the shape of the argument rather than a record of who said what.

The argument over scale

The case for consolidation is usually about capacity. England has 43 territorial police forces in its current structure, and they differ greatly in size. Supporters of mergers argue that bigger forces can pool specialist work, such as serious organised crime, cyber-enabled fraud and major investigations. Small forces often struggle to staff these functions, and larger ones can spread the cost.

The mayors' objection is about distance. Policing in England is held to account locally, and over recent years that oversight has increasingly passed to elected mayors in areas that have them. A force covering several counties or city regions would have several local politicians with a claim on it and no one with clear authority. The mayors' warning, as reported, is that local priorities such as neighbourhood patrols, antisocial behaviour and town-centre crime could lose out to the demands of a much larger organisation.

Who controls what

The underlying question is a constitutional one. Local policing has been an elected role since police and crime commissioners were introduced in 2012. Where a combined authority has a mayor, that mayor can take on the commissioner's duties: setting the force's priorities, holding the chief constable to account and shaping the budget. A merger would change the geography those powers cover. A mayor whose area is one part of a larger force could end up with a share of the oversight rather than the whole of it.

The reported details leave several things open. They do not say how many forces would be combined or whether mergers would be compulsory. They also do not say how oversight would work across a merged force, whether through a joint board, a lead mayor or something else. Those design choices will decide whether the mayors' objection is a matter of principle or one that can be met with safeguards.

What to watch

The story matters mainly if the consolidation plans are confirmed in detail and the mayors' objections gain traction. The first test is whether the government publishes a concrete map of proposed forces and a governance model for them. The second is whether mayors and other local leaders, including those from the government's own party, press the case in public. A formal consultation or a bill would give those objections a place to be heard, and ministers would then have to say who holds the chief constable to account in a force that spans several mayoral areas.
