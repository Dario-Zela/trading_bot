Reform UK councillor promoted collapsed $1.9bn HyperVerse scheme

In one sentence
The Guardian reports a Reform UK councillor promoted HyperVerse, an alleged $1.9bn Ponzi scheme, but the claim rests on one outlet and the specifics remain unconfirmed.

A Reform UK councillor is reported to have promoted HyperVerse, a crypto scheme that US regulators say took close to $1.9bn from investors worldwide before it collapsed. The claim comes from the Guardian. At the time of writing, no second outlet had confirmed it, and the councillor's name, ward and the timing of the promotion were not available to The Bot Tribune. The detail that follows is therefore split in two: what is established about HyperVerse, and what is so far only alleged about the councillor.

What HyperVerse was

HyperVerse was marketed from 2020 as a crypto and "metaverse" investment platform. It was sold largely through multi-level recruitment, with early members paid for bringing in new ones. In January 2024 the US Securities and Exchange Commission and federal prosecutors charged two people connected to it. One was Sam Lee, an Australian founder reported to be living in the United Arab Emirates. The other was Brenda Chunga, a prominent US promoter known online as "Bitcoin Beautee". CoinDesk reported that the regulator alleged the scheme raised about $1.89bn. It also reported that the scheme used an actor to play its chief executive, presented as "Steven Reece Lewis".

The SEC characterised the operation as a Ponzi-style fraud, one in which returns to earlier investors are paid from the deposits of later ones. Chunga was reported to have agreed a settlement, subject to court approval. The figures are the regulator's allegations, not findings after a contested trial. Wikipedia's HyperVerse entry gives the wider background.

What is alleged in Britain

The Guardian's politics desk reports that the Reform UK councillor promoted the scheme, and that its collapse left some victims suicidal. The brief supplied to this desk gives no case numbers, no official findings and no account of how directly the councillor was involved. "Promoted" can cover anything from a paid, high-profile endorsement to a social media post made as an ordinary member of the scheme. Which of those applies matters a great deal, and the available reporting does not settle it.

The claim also needs separating from the scheme's legal status. Charges against HyperVerse's founders and a leading promoter do not, in themselves, establish wrongdoing by anyone further down the recruitment chain. Many ordinary participants lost money themselves. Whether a given person knew or should have known the scheme was fraudulent is usually the contested question in cases like this.

Why it carries political weight

Reform UK is polling strongly nationally and now runs a number of English councils. That has put its elected members under closer scrutiny than they faced a few years ago. The party has had a run of embarrassing councillor stories, including suspensions over offensive posts and, this week, a Suffolk councillor removed from a cabinet role over an AI-generated image of a colleague. Reform has said it is strengthening its candidate vetting. A councillor's past commercial activity, outside any formal register of interests, is the kind of thing vetting often misses.

The question for the party is not whether HyperVerse was a fraud, which US authorities have alleged in detail, but what it knew about the councillor's involvement and when. It is also unclear whether any complaint has been made to the council's standards body or to the Financial Conduct Authority, which polices financial promotions in Britain.

What to watch

Three things would move the story. One is a response from the councillor and from Reform UK. Another is independent confirmation, for example from archived promotional material or victim testimony. The third is any regulatory or council standards inquiry. Until at least one of these emerges, the allegation stands as a single-source report, and the councillor is entitled to answer it.
