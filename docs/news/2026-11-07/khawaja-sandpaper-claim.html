Khawaja says whole Australia team knew of sandpaper use

In one sentence
Usman Khawaja reportedly says the whole Australia team knew of the 2018 sandpaper plan, but without names or evidence it does not yet contradict the official findings.

Usman Khawaja has said the whole Australia team knew about the sandpaper used on the ball in Cape Town in 2018, according to BBC Sport. The claim cuts against the account Cricket Australia gave at the time. That account held that the plan was confined to three men.

The quote has not been checked against the original interview. The BBC Sport report is the only source behind it, and the wording and context matter a good deal here. "Knew" could mean knew in advance or knew afterwards. It could also mean that the dressing room should share responsibility, not that it was in on the plan.

What the official record says

In the Newlands Test against South Africa in March 2018, Cameron Bancroft was caught on camera working on the ball with an abrasive object. It turned out to be sandpaper. Steve Smith, the captain, and David Warner, his deputy, each received a one-year ban from international and domestic cricket. Bancroft received nine months.

Cricket Australia's investigation concluded that only Smith, Warner and Bancroft knew of the plan. No other player or member of staff in the touring party was found to have known. The four bowlers in that Test have consistently denied any knowledge of it.

Why Khawaja's word carries weight

Khawaja was in the squad that tour. Australian media reported at the time that he had urged the group to accept collective responsibility, and that the suggestion was rejected. The claim also arrives as he prepares a memoir. Reports from October 2026 say it includes a chapter on the affair, though one outlet said it was not known whether the book covers the episode at all. Those accounts conflict.

Pat Cummins, the current Test captain, said Khawaja had given him a heads-up about the book and that he was unbothered. "As a group, we've moved on," he said.

What would change the picture

As reported, the claim names no one beyond the three already sanctioned. It supplies no new evidence and no account of who knew what, or when. Without those, it is an old suspicion in a new voice, and it falls well short of a contradiction of the findings.

It would become more than that if Khawaja names names, or if the book gives dates and conversations. That would put Cricket Australia's inquiry and its conclusion under fresh scrutiny. Some of those named might also respond, particularly the bowlers who have denied knowing.

The next test is the book and any fuller transcript of the interview. Both should show whether Khawaja is describing a shared secret or a shared atmosphere.
