Nasdaq 100 rebounds 2% after its worst day since 2020 as strong payrolls lift chips and push bonds lower

In one sentence
A strong US payrolls report lifted the Nasdaq 100 about 2% after a sharp selloff, while pushing bond yields up as traders weighed the chance of a December Fed hike.

The Nasdaq 100 rose about 2% on the back of a stronger-than-expected US jobs report, recovering part of a slide that had been described as its worst day since 2020. Chip and AI shares led the bounce. Bonds went the other way, with yields rising as traders cut the odds of easier Federal Reserve policy. The two moves were the same message read two ways: equities took the data as evidence of growth, and the rates market took it as a reason for the Fed to stay put.

Some details of that account are not yet pinned down. Payroll figures and the consensus estimate, the size of the yield move and the exact closing levels were not available at the time of writing, and are not given here. The timing matters too. Today, 7 November, is a Saturday, and US markets are shut. The payrolls release and the rebound therefore belong to an earlier session, most plausibly Friday 6 November, the usual first-Friday release date. The selloff came on a separate, earlier day. The material gathered for this piece places it after the 3 November midterm elections but does not date it.

Two sessions, one theme

The sequence matters because the rebound can look larger or smaller depending on what is measured against. A 2% gain in the Nasdaq 100 is a solid day but not an extraordinary one. Against a drop that reportedly ranked as the index's worst since 2020, it is a partial repair at most. Without the selloff's size, the net position since the slide cannot be stated, and a one-day recovery should not be read as the damage being undone.

The earlier selloff was reported to have spread from US AI stocks to Asian and European markets, with talk of trillions of dollars in market value wiped out. Those figures are unconfirmed. Reports also tied the slide to the midterms, on the theory that the result raised the risk of tougher policy toward artificial intelligence. That causal link is the weakest part of the story. Markets often fall around political events without the event being the cause, and no analysis reviewed here established the connection. One analyst, cited by Benzinga and not named in the material available, has argued that the post-election selling priced in more policy risk than the situation warrants. That is one view, offered after the fact. It is not a measurement of anything.

What the jobs data did

Payrolls beat economists' estimates, according to Bloomberg and Benzinga. The reaction ran along two tracks. Technology and semiconductor shares, which had been sold hardest, rallied. Bonds sold off, pushing yields higher.

The bond move is the more informative of the two. A normal read of a hot labour report is that it lowers the chance of rate cuts. Reports said it did more than that: bets on a Fed rate hike in December rose after the release. How far those odds moved is not stated in the material gathered, and a rise from a very low base is a different thing from a coin flip. Rate-hike odds are also quoted in several ways, and the underlying futures pricing should be checked before the shift is characterised.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent entered the argument with a figure of his own. He cited nearly 400,000 private-sector jobs added over three months. The source for that number was not identified. It could come from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, from ADP or from another dataset, and the three can differ substantially. It reads as a case that the economy is strong, which is an awkward position for anyone arguing that the Fed needs to ease.

The Fed's view of the volatility

Mary Daly, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, addressed the turbulence on CNBC. According to the account relayed through CNBC and Benzinga, she called the market volatility painful but said that, stepping back, it looked like the kind of rebalancing policymakers would hope for. The remark has not been checked against a transcript, and the wording above is a paraphrase rather than a quotation.

The comment is a tone signal, not a policy decision. It suggests, at most, that at least one senior official does not see the selloff as a threat to financial stability or a reason to change course. Combined with a strong labour market, that supports the pricing the bond market showed: the Fed has little reason to rush to cushion equities.

What the pairing implies

In the usual pattern of the past few years, equity investors cheered weak data because it brought rate cuts closer. Friday's reaction, as reported, ran the other way. Stocks rose on good news about growth, while bonds treated the same news as a reason for higher yields. That is consistent with a market that is, for now, giving growth more weight than rates when it values technology shares.

That reading has limits. It rests on a single session, and on figures not yet verified. Higher yields are a known headwind for long-duration assets such as growth stocks. If yields keep rising while the Fed leans against easing, the trade-off can reassert itself. One strong day for chip shares does not show that investors have stopped worrying about rates. It shows only that, on this day, the jobs number outweighed that worry.

The European side of the story remains thin. European indices and ADRs were reported lower in the earlier selloff, but the figures for the FTSE 100 and 250 and for gilt yields were not available, and none are asserted here.

What to watch

The next checks are concrete. The first is the Friday closing level of the Nasdaq 100 and the size of the earlier drop, which together would show how much of the loss has been recovered. The second is the next US inflation report, which will show whether a strong labour market is feeding through to prices and whether December rate-hike odds rise further. The third is the Fed's own communication ahead of its December meeting, including whether Daly's relaxed framing is shared by her colleagues. If hike odds keep climbing, the pairing of rising stocks and rising yields becomes harder to sustain.
