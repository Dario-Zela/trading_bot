After the midterms: four takeaways for 2028, and the limits of Musk's money

In one sentence
Democrats took both chambers on 3 November, losing Republicans Texas seats despite roughly $450m of Musk-linked spending, which sets the terms of the 2028 argument.

Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in the US midterm elections on Tuesday 3 November. That gives each party a first draft of its 2028 argument. It also hands a verdict, of sorts, to the country's richest man, who spent close to half a billion dollars trying to prevent it.

Exact seat counts were not available in the reports reviewed for this piece, and the takeaways below are interpretation, not settled fact.

What the results suggest

1. The Republican map is less safe than assumed. Al Jazeera reports that Republicans lost in traditionally safe states, Texas and Kansas among them. In Texas, Democrat James Talarico beat Republican Ken Paxton for the Senate, and Democrat Gina Hinojosa unseated Governor Greg Abbott. A party that has to defend Texas has fewer places to expand in a presidential year.

2. Democrats have a template that is not about Donald Trump. Talarico campaigned for tighter regulation of data centres, and Al Jazeera reports that voters rejected Paxton's plan to make Texas a hub for AI data-centre construction. If that message travels, 2028 Democratic hopefuls will have a local, economic argument about AI infrastructure alongside the usual anti-incumbent one.

3. Republicans face a succession problem sooner than expected. Trump cannot run again. Midterm defeats usually set off a contest over who owns the party's direction, and a loss in the biggest red state sharpens it. Whether the 2028 field will treat this as a verdict on Trump or on weak candidates is the open question.

4. Control of Congress turns 2027 into a pre-campaign. Investigations, spending fights and confirmation battles will double as audition stages for both parties' ambitions. The Senate, which confirms appointments, matters most to any White House.

The limits of one donor's money

The sharpest test of the first takeaway is the money. Reports put Elon Musk's spending at between roughly $446m and $474m, and Forbes puts it at no less than $458m. The totals differ, and the precise figure should be checked against Federal Election Commission filings. By any count he was the largest individual donor of the cycle.

Al Jazeera reports that the money went through America PAC, his Republican-leaning committee, and the Senate Leadership Fund, a Republican-aligned super PAC. The fund drew $158m of his donations in September to back Paxton, who lost. Musk also gave $5m to the Republican side in the governor's race, and Abbott lost too.

The caveat is that giving to a group that spends in a race does not show the money decided it. Forbes made that point while concluding that his return on the spending was low. The losses show that money cannot overcome a candidate or message that voters reject. They do not show that the spending was irrelevant.

Musk himself treated the defeat lightly. "Guess my election-interfering space lasers didn't work this time," he wrote on X, adding that "data centers in space looking pretty appealing right now." Talarico took the opposite tone in his victory speech, saying his campaign had taken on "goliaths," including "the richest man in the world," and won.

What to watch

The next data points are the final seat counts and the FEC filings that fix Musk's total. After that comes the question of whether he commits money again for 2028, which reports say remains unclear. He has warned that a Democratic sweep in 2028 could change the country's political system, so a retreat looks unlikely. The more telling signal will be whether Republican candidates still want his backing in the Texas-style races where it failed.
