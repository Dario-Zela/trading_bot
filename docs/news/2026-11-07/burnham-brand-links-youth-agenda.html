Burnham pressed on Labour's past links to Russell Brand as Met chief expects more allegations

In one sentence
Andy Burnham answered BBC questions on Labour's past Russell Brand ties and promised radical youth support, as the Met chief said more Brand allegations may emerge.

Andy Burnham has been asked, in a BBC interview, to account for the Labour Party's past association with Russell Brand. He used the same exchange to set out what the BBC headline calls "quite radical change" in support for young people. On the same day the Metropolitan Police commissioner said he would not be surprised if more allegations about Brand emerged. Two separate stories have landed on the same name.

This account rests on headlines and summaries from the BBC and the Guardian. It does not rest on the full text of Burnham's interview. His exact words on Brand, and the detail of his youth agenda, are not reproduced here because they could not be confirmed.

The Brand question

Labour's history with Brand is public. In 2015 the comedian, then a prominent anti-establishment voice, urged his large young following to back the party at that year's general election. Commentators at the time presented the move as an attempt to reach disengaged, largely young voters. Since 2023 Brand has been the subject of allegations of sexual misconduct, and the endorsement now reads very differently.

The question put to Burnham therefore works on two levels. The narrow one is whether the party, or figures within it, should have been more careful about whom it courted. The broader one concerns judgment. A politician who wants to speak for young people has to explain how he would avoid the kind of celebrity alliance that looks awkward in hindsight.

The headline-level reporting says Burnham answered the BBC's questions on the point. It does not say whether he apologised, defended the party, or declined to relitigate the past. That is the detail on which the story turns.

The youth offer

Burnham paired his answer with a promise of "quite radical change" in how young people are supported. The phrase fits a familiar line of his. He has long cast himself as a politician who wants to reconnect Labour with people who feel shut out, and in his 2010 leadership campaign he made that reconnection his central theme.

The available coverage gives no costings, timetable or policy specifics. Without them the pledge cannot be judged. Housing, education costs and job prospects are the usual ground on which such promises are tested. Which of them Burnham meant, and how far he would go, awaits the full interview.

The policing thread

The second strand comes from Scotland Yard. The Met commissioner said policing must be reformed to cope with modern criminals. Asked about Brand, he said he would not be surprised if further allegations came to light. The BBC carried the remarks.

The reform argument is a recurring one. Sir Mark Rowley has previously described the current policing model as a tweaked version of a design drawn up by a 1962 royal commission. He has also complained that bringing a case to court now takes far more work than it did three decades ago. The Brand aside is the newer element. Even so, it is a statement of expectation, not an announcement of new charges or a new investigation, and it should be read as such.

What ties the two together

The two stories meet at the question of institutional judgment. Burnham is being asked whether Labour's vetting and associations failed in the past. The commissioner is arguing that policing's structures have not kept pace with how offenders operate. Both turn on whether bodies that deal with the public learn from what they missed.

For Burnham the practical test is specific. He needs an answer on Brand that does not become a distraction, and a youth programme concrete enough that it is not remembered only for the headline phrase. Watch for the full interview text and any follow-up detail from his team on costs and timing. Further statements from the Met on Brand would show whether the commissioner's remark was a passing comment or a signal of something coming.
