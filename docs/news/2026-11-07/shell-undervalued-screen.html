Shell among British stocks flagged as potentially undervalued

In one sentence
Simply Wall St. has flagged Shell and two other UK stocks as 'potentially undervalued', but gives no figures, date or criteria, so the label is a model output rather than news.

A stock screen has put Shell on a list of British shares described as "potentially undervalued". The label comes from Simply Wall St., a retail-investing platform that generates company analysis from a quantitative model. It has been circulated alongside material from Zacks, another provider of rules-based stock ratings. Nothing has happened at Shell. The oil major has issued no new guidance and no deal, and no event prompted the list. The story is the screen itself, and how a recurring formula comes to be read as a headline.

What the screen says

The item names Shell and two other London-listed stocks as potentially undervalued. It adds a separate list of UK healthcare names. Shell is a FTSE 100 constituent, and that is the only background detail the piece offers.

The item does not say which valuation measure was used. It gives no fair-value estimate, no discount to that estimate, no date on which the screen was run, and no criteria for inclusion. A reader cannot tell whether "undervalued" means a price below a discounted-cash-flow figure, a low multiple of earnings, or a ranking against sector peers. These are different tests, and a stock can pass one while failing another.

How a model output becomes news

Automated screens of this kind are cheap to produce and easy to refresh. A model ingests reported financials and analyst forecasts, then sorts companies by how far the price sits from the model's estimate. The output is a list, and a list can be published on a schedule whether or not anything in the market has changed. A "potentially undervalued" list that reappears every few weeks reflects the model's inputs and the share price. It is not independent evidence about the company.

The word "potentially" carries most of the weight in the label. It signals that the result is conditional on assumptions the reader cannot see.

For an energy company the assumptions matter more than usual. Shell's earnings follow oil and gas prices and refining margins, so any discounted-cash-flow model depends heavily on the commodity price path it assumes. Two models fed different long-run crude forecasts can disagree about the same stock in opposite directions. A screen that flags Shell as cheap is partly a statement about one set of price forecasts.

What a reader can test

On the published material, very little. A claim of undervaluation is testable only if the method is stated. That means the metric, the benchmark, the date of the price used and the forecasts behind the estimate. Without them the label is an assertion. Past screens of this type could be checked against later share-price performance, but the item cites no earlier list to compare with.

The risk is mostly one of nudging. Retail investors who meet "undervalued" in a headline may take it as a judgement from an analyst who has studied the company. It is the output of a formula, and the formula's authors would typically say it is not personal advice. The healthcare list attached to the item carries the same caveat.

The broader backdrop for UK shareholders, including how share-price moves feed through to savings and pensions, is covered in our piece on what a fall in share prices means for UK savers.

What to watch

The practical test for Shell is its next quarterly results, which will show whether earnings and cash returns support the valuation any model assumes. A screen can be rerun at any time. Reported figures are the check on it.
