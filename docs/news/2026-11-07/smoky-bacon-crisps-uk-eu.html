Smoky bacon crisps become a new flashpoint in UK–EU rules rows

In one sentence
A draft EU ban on meaty names for non-meat products could inadvertently catch bacon-flavoured crisps, testing how the UK's food-rule alignment plays out politically, though nothing is final.

A dispute over the labelling of smoky bacon crisps is turning into a small test of how far the UK's food-rule alignment with the EU will be argued out in public. The reporting so far shows friction, not a rupture. The rule in question is still being drafted in Brussels, and the terms under which Britain would be bound by it are unsettled.

The rule behind the row

The UK and EU agreed a 'reset' at a leaders' summit in May 2025. It includes negotiations on aligning rules for food, agriculture and animal and plant health, known as SPS. Alignment of that kind generally means Britain follows EU rules as they change, which is why a technical EU text can quickly become a British political question.

The text at issue is a revision of the EU's Common Market Organisation for agricultural products. According to trade-press reporting, negotiators from the European Parliament, Council and Commission agreed earlier this year to reserve 31 meat-related terms, including 'chicken', 'bacon' and 'steak', for products that actually contain meat. The aim is to stop vegetarian products using them. 'Burger' and 'sausage' were left out of that agreement. Further proposals would add them and extend the restrictions to products not made wholly of meat. As of 27 October, those proposals had not been agreed.

Crisps were not the target. Olivier Devaux of the European Snacks Association told Politico that flavour descriptions using meaty names 'may be inadvertently included in scope' of the regulation, as reported by Bakery&Snacks. An amendment from Stefan Köhler and three other centre-right MEPs would exempt snacks, sauces, seasonings and condiments, which it says are 'clearly understood as distinct from meat or meat substitutes'. Whether that amendment survives is not yet known.

Why the UK is in the frame

Britain's link to the dispute is the alignment deal. One report says the Food Standards Agency has indicated that Britain would be bound by the new labelling requirements unless negotiators secure exemptions for specific products. I could not confirm that from a primary FSA document. A Daily Sceptic piece on 4 November claimed the reset deal puts the crisps at risk. That outlet is partisan, and I found no named minister, official statement or published text backing the claim. The BBC Politics report that prompted this piece was not available to check.

An older episode is easy to confuse with this one. In 2024 the EU moved to phase out certain smoke flavourings. Because Great Britain sits outside EU food law, that mainly affected Northern Ireland, which still follows EU rules. PepsiCo said Walkers Smoky Bacon and Frazzles would not be affected. That was a Northern Ireland Protocol question. The current question would arise from a deliberate UK choice to align.

What it tests

The crisps are a minor commercial matter, since the rule is unfinished and may well exempt them. The more useful point is the pattern. Dynamic alignment means rules written in Brussels, sometimes with unintended reach, arrive in British shops. Each such case gives opponents of the reset an easy example and gives ministers a reason to press for carve-outs. Smoky bacon is a ready-made example because it is unmistakably British and easy to mock.

Two things will show whether this grows. The first is whether the EU's final text exempts flavour descriptors. The second is whether the SPS agreement, with a UK–EU summit reported for 20 November, spells out how Britain handles rules adopted after signing, including any right to seek exceptions. A text that leaves crisps untouched would make the row a footnote. A text that binds Britain with no carve-out mechanism would make it a template for later disputes.
