Commons Speaker calls Parliament restoration delays 'frustrating'

In one sentence
The Commons Speaker called Westminster restoration delays 'frustrating' in the same week MPs put the cost of waiting at £420m a year, with no decision yet on how to proceed.

The Speaker of the House of Commons has described the continuing delays to the restoration of the Palace of Westminster as "frustrating", according to a BBC report. The remark is a mild one. It lands in a week when the cost of those delays has been set out in unusually stark terms by a committee of MPs.

The BBC item as summarised carries no date for the Speaker's comments and no new figures, timetable or decisions of its own. This account therefore does not put words in the Speaker's mouth beyond the single adjective reported. The substance comes from the context around it.

What the committee said

On Wednesday, the Commons Public Accounts Committee warned that continued postponement risks a "catastrophic failure" that would damage the UK's "morale and reputation", Euronews reported. Its report cites crumbling masonry, ageing electrics, frequent leaks and high asbestos levels in a complex of which parts are more than 900 years old.

The figures are the new element:

  • Weekly maintenance costs are expected to rise from £1.5m to £2m.
  • Each year of delay adds about £420m to the programme's lifetime cost.

The committee's chair, Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, said inaction could leave the Commons chamber in darkness during the state opening of Parliament. He also warned of a fire on the scale of Notre Dame, which he called a "real and rising" risk. The building has burned before, in 1512 and 1834.

The options, and the unmade decision

MPs voted in 2018 to move out of the building so that the work could be done. Parliament has since failed to settle how far to go. The choices now before it are far apart:

  • Full decant: both Houses vacate for most of the works, at up to £15.6bn and as long as 24 years.
  • Enhanced maintenance and improvement plus: a partial decant, with no more than 30% of staff on site at any time, projected at £39bn and up to 61 years.

Work is currently scheduled to start in 2032. No decision has been taken. Both Houses are expected to hold a series of votes later this year on how deep the renovation should go, according to the same report.

Why the Speaker's irritation is not new

Sir Lindsay Hoyle has long chaired the House of Commons Commission, which oversees the administration of the Commons, and has pressed the programme's managers before. In December 2023 he asked restoration chiefs not to pay performance bonuses while the project struggled to make progress, after awards totalling £168,000 over two years to the Delivery Authority's chief executive drew criticism. The Commission has also backed abolishing the separate Sponsor Body and starting significant works sooner.

That history is the reason the latest remark reads as a continuation rather than a turn. The Speaker does not control the outcome; the decision rests with votes in both Houses, and the delay he calls frustrating is largely the product of MPs' own failure to choose between options.

What to watch

The test is the votes expected before the end of the year. If the Commons and Lords back the full decant, the 2032 start date and the £15.6bn ceiling become the numbers to hold the programme to. If they favour the partial approach, the £39bn estimate and its 61-year horizon will define the argument. If no vote produces a clear result, the committee's £420m-a-year figure keeps running.
