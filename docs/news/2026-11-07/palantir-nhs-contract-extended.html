Government extends Palantir NHS contract while drawing up a British alternative

In one sentence
Ministers will reportedly keep Palantir's £330m NHS data contract running while planning a UK-built replacement, with a decision on the March 2027 break clause due by December.

The government is set to keep Palantir's contract to run the NHS's central data system alive while it works on a British-built replacement, according to reports this week. Ministers have not yet said what they will do. That leaves one procurement decision doing a lot of work. It now tests whether the UK can run its own health data without leaning on an American contractor.

The detail is thin, and some of it is secondhand. The Guardian's reporting was not available to this desk in full. Accounts of an extension of roughly 12 months, and of a UK alternative, come from secondary outlets citing earlier reporting, and they differ on timing and framing. The point that is solid is the direction of travel: keep the incumbent running, and prepare an exit.

What the contract is

Palantir's Federated Data Platform (FDP) was awarded by NHS England in 2023 and is widely reported at £330m. It links hospital systems so that trusts can see waiting lists, theatre use and bed capacity in one place. About two-thirds of NHS hospitals have adopted it. Palantir says it has helped enable 110,000 additional surgeries. The Department of Health says the platform is helping "thousands more patients" each month.

The NHS says patient data stays in Britain, is not owned by Palantir and is not used for commercial purposes or for training AI. Palantir argues it was appointed under public procurement rules and that NHS data remains under NHS control.

Opposition rests on trust and dependence more than on any proven misuse. The British Medical Association has urged doctors not to use the platform, and its chair, Tom Dolphin, has called for "a complete break from Palantir technologies in the NHS." Critics point to Palantir's work with the Israeli military and US immigration authorities. A July YouGov poll found 65% of Britons backed ending the contract. Lib Dem MP Martin Wrigley has argued that US services could be switched off for political reasons, and has called for a "sovereign" alternative.

Why extend at all

The case for keeping Palantir running is practical. Reports say an internal evaluation warned that ending its involvement abruptly could disrupt NHS data systems. This is a familiar pattern. In 2023 NHS England extended Palantir's earlier pandemic-era contract by six months at £11.5m, saying that changing contractor at that stage would cause "significant inconvenience" and duplicated cost. A later 12-month, £25m transition deal was awarded without an open tender.

The contract also has a break clause. Campaigners want it used so the deal is not renewed beyond March 2027. Ministers are reported to be due to decide by December. An extension would run alongside that clock rather than replace it. It buys time for whatever comes next.

What a British alternative could look like

Who would build it is the open question. Reports name several routes. One is an in-house NHS platform. Another is a UK-linked supplier; Quantexa has been mentioned as a frontrunner, pitching a "sovereign" framework hosted in NHS-controlled cloud. Parliament's science and technology committee chair, Dame Chi Onwurah, has urged the government to invest in an in-house option or back a UK provider. None of this is confirmed as a formal procurement, and no timeline has been published.

There is a working precedent. NHS Greater Manchester has spent about a decade building its own data platform, which can do some of what the FDP does. Its chief data officer, Matt Hennessey, says the local system is "better" for patients. Whether that scales nationally is untested, and migration is the hard part. Hospitals have spent three years wiring workflows to Foundry, which is Palantir's core software product.

The politics are crowded. Prime Minister Andy Burnham, in office since July, has not commented publicly on the contract. Elsewhere, Sadiq Khan blocked a £50m Metropolitan Police contract with Palantir in May, and the company launched legal action. France has moved its intelligence agencies off Palantir software this year.

What to watch

The December decision is the next marker. If ministers confirm an extension, the terms matter more than the headline: its length, its price, and whether it includes exit and data-portability provisions. The second test is whether the replacement becomes a funded, competitive programme with dates, or stays a statement of intent. A credible plan would change how every UK public body weighs a foreign supplier. A vague one would leave Palantir's position stronger than before.
