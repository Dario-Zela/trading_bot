US rig count falls again as oil and gas drilling activity softens

A shale gas drilling rig tower — the kind of onshore rig Baker Hughes counts each week to gauge US drilling activity. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US rig count fell 5 to 588 last week — oil rigs down 3 to 452 — but remains up about 9% from a year ago despite WTI near $84.

US drillers idled rigs again last week, extending a soft patch that has become the summer's quiet undertone in the oil patch. Baker Hughes' weekly count, released Friday, showed the total US rig count down five to 588 for the week ended August 21 — oil-directed rigs off three to 452, gas rigs down one to 127, and miscellaneous rigs down one to 9.

On its own, that is a small number in a slow-moving series. The count had actually risen five rigs the week before, to 593, its highest mark since March 2025, so last week's pullback erased almost exactly what the prior week added. Read across a month rather than a week, the trend is closer to flat-with-a-downward-lean than a rout: the total remains roughly 9% above where it stood a year ago, when the US was running around 539 rigs.

Prices are up. Rigs mostly aren't.

What makes the flatness notable is the backdrop it's happening against. West Texas Intermediate has been trading near $84 a barrel this month, propped up by the standoff over Iran and repeated disruptions to tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. In an earlier cycle, prices at that level would typically have pulled a wave of new rigs into the field. This time the response has been muted, and that gap between price and activity is the more useful story than the weekly rig tally itself.

The explanation operators and analysts point to is capital discipline that has outlasted the price spike. Since the investor revolt against shale's growth-at-all-costs era, producers have leaned on longer laterals and more efficient completions to keep output climbing even as rig counts stay well below their 2022 peak — Lower 48 crude production has been running near record highs, above 13.7 million barrels a day, on fewer rigs than a year ago. Wall Street has told management teams it wants free cash flow and buybacks, not a repeat of the drilling binges that wrecked returns the last time crude ran hot.

The basin-level detail from last week fits that pattern more than it contradicts it. The Permian and the Eagle Ford both added rigs even as the national total fell, while the gas-heavy Haynesville lost two and the Granite Wash lost one — a split that looks more like reshuffling toward the most productive oil acreage than a broad retreat from drilling.

None of this settles whether the softening is capital discipline holding firm or the first sign of demand-side hesitation creeping into investment plans. That distinction won't come from any single Friday print. It will come from whether the Permian keeps adding through the autumn even if headline prices soften, and from the next round of EIA production revisions, due in the Short-Term Energy Outlook next month, which will show whether output is still climbing on fewer rigs or beginning to plateau.
