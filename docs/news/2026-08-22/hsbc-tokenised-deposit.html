HSBC completes first live cross-border interbank tokenised deposit transaction

In one sentence
HSBC and Standard Chartered settled a cross-border payment using bank-issued digital tokens on Swift's blockchain ledger, a step toward bank-controlled 24/7 settlement.

HSBC and Standard Chartered have moved money between each other using bank-issued digital tokens instead of the usual correspondent-banking plumbing — a small transaction with a large ambition behind it.

The two banks said on 19 August they had completed the first live cross-border interbank transfer using tokenised deposits, settled via Swift's blockchain-based shared ledger. Neither disclosed the amount or currency. What mattered, in their telling, was that it happened at all: two separate institutions, each running its own token system, moved value between them without falling back on the decades-old network of correspondent accounts that normally handles cross-border payments.

How it actually worked

A tokenised deposit is simply a bank's own ledger entry — the money in a corporate account — represented as a digital token rather than a row in a database. HSBC has offered this through its Tokenised Deposit Service since May 2025, initially in Hong Kong and since expanded to the US, UK and several Asian currencies including the yuan, dollar, euro and Emirati dirham.

The innovation this month was interoperability. HSBC's token system and Standard Chartered's separate one don't talk to each other directly. Swift's ledger — live since July after nine months of development — sat in between, passing payment messages that let each bank record matching obligations on its own books. Those obligations were then netted and settled through existing payment rails, the same ones banks have used for years. Nothing radical changed under the hood; what changed is that it happened around the clock, in principle, rather than only during the overlapping business hours of two national payment systems.

"Digital money issued by banks can be interoperable across institutions while maintaining regulatory oversight of the existing financial ecosystem," said Lewis Sun, HSBC's head of digital currencies.

Standard Chartered's Mark Willis, head of emerging payments, called tokenised deposits "a key pillar" of the bank's digital assets strategy for cross-border liquidity.

The bigger picture

Seventeen banks across six continents — including Citi, BNP Paribas, UBS, ANZ and DBS — are lined up to pilot transactions on Swift's ledger. Separately, a group of large US banks including JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citi and Wells Fargo is building its own tokenised deposit network through The Clearing House, targeting a 2027 launch. Two rival infrastructures, in other words, both racing toward the same idea: banks want to keep settlement inside the regulated banking system rather than cede it to public blockchains or crypto exchanges.

That's the quiet subtext. Stablecoins and crypto rails have spent several years arguing they can move money faster and cheaper than banks can. HSBC and Swift's answer is to borrow the plumbing — distributed ledgers, always-on settlement — while keeping the money itself inside conventional bank deposits, with all the deposit insurance, capital rules and compliance that implies. If it works at scale, it undercuts one of crypto's core pitches to institutional treasurers: that only public blockchains can offer genuinely continuous settlement.

It is, for now, one transaction of undisclosed size between two banks that already work together constantly. The test is whether it stays a curiosity or becomes routine plumbing — the next marker will be whether disclosed volumes on Swift's ledger start showing up, and whether other banks in the 17-strong pilot group follow HSBC and Standard Chartered's lead before The Clearing House's rival network arrives in 2027.
