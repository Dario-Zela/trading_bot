US-Canada trade talks stall as Ottawa vows to match tariffs 'dollar for dollar'

Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who said Ottawa would match new US tariffs "dollar for dollar." — Daniel Torok / The White House, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US-Canada trade talks collapsed, triggering 50% US tariffs on up to $28bn of Canadian goods; Carney vows to match them dollar for dollar, but hasn't said how.

Trade talks between Washington and Ottawa collapsed just before a midnight deadline on Friday, triggering 50% US tariffs on a swathe of Canadian goods and prompting Prime Minister Mark Carney to promise a retaliation that matches the levies "dollar for dollar."

The tariffs took effect at 12:01 a.m. Saturday, hitting an estimated $20-28bn of Canadian exports — reporting varies on the precise figure — spanning plywood, cement, liquor, electrical equipment, clothing and hockey gear. It is the second time this year Washington has escalated against its northern neighbour after steel, aluminium and auto tariffs imposed earlier in the trade dispute.

The breakdown came after three straight days of talks in Washington, where Canada's trade minister Dominic LeBlanc and chief negotiator Janice Charette met US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick for nearly two hours on Friday without emerging with a deal. The two sides gave conflicting accounts of why. Greer said in a statement that Canada "declined to finalize the trade deal under the terms agreed earlier this week," accusing Ottawa of "new demands and walk backs of other commitments" that "upended the careful balance reached in the past days." Carney's office countered that "last-minute changes in the US proposed terms were unfair, uneconomic, and called into question the reliability of any deal," and said Carney had ordered Canadian negotiators to return to Ottawa.

"Canada will match those tariffs dollar for dollar to protect our workers and businesses," Carney said in a statement Friday night. Ottawa said the retaliation comes on top of nearly C$25bn in relief and adjustment measures it has rolled out for affected sectors over the past 18 months.

The gap traders are pricing blind

What Carney has not said is which Canadian tariff lines get raised, by how much, or when — the retaliatory list itself has not been published. That leaves autos, energy and agricultural supply chains, the sectors most exposed to a tit-for-tat spiral, in limbo: known cost on the US side, unknown cost on the Canadian side. Steel and softwood lumber were the sharpest sticking points in the failed talks — Washington had reportedly offered to cut the top-line tariff on Canadian vehicles to 15% from 25% and halve steel and aluminium duties to 25%, an offer that lapsed with the deadline. The S&P/TSX Composite was still up 0.88% in Friday morning trading, before the deadline passed, suggesting markets had not yet priced a full collapse.

Neither government has confirmed when talks might resume. The next concrete signal will be Ottawa's own tariff list, which would let markets size the hit to specific sectors rather than guess at it — until then, this is a story about brinkmanship, not yet one about numbers.
