MPs accused of 'failing to learn' after accepting £400,000 in hospitality

The Palace of Westminster, seat of the House of Commons, where MPs' declared hospitality has again topped £400,000 for the year. — Hartmut Schmidt Heidelberg / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
MPs across parties declared close to £400,000 in tickets and hospitality this year, reviving the freebies row that dogged Starmer's early premiership.

There is a familiar rhythm to these disclosures now. A concert, a cup final, an awards ceremony — an MP's name against a four-figure sum, filed quietly in the Register of Members' Financial Interests, then gathered up months later into a headline figure large enough to make people wince. This time the number is just under £400,000, the freebies span January to July 2026, and the reaction from transparency campaigners is closer to weary than shocked.

"Some ministers and MPs appear to have short memories of the freebie-gate scandal that engulfed the first few months of Starmer's time in No 10," said Sue Hawley, director of Spotlight on Corruption.

In one sentence: MPs across parties declared close to £400,000 in tickets and hospitality this year, reviving the freebies row that dogged the start of Keir Starmer's premiership.

The list

The entries read like a summer social calendar rather than a scandal ledger. Education Secretary Lucy Powell took £858 tickets to see Harry Styles at Wembley, courtesy of the music industry body PRS. Defence Secretary Wes Streeting and Attorney General Ellie Reeves each declared hospitality worth over £1,300 — the Chelsea Flower Show for Streeting, courtesy of Lloyds Banking Group, and the Ivor Novello Awards for Reeves, alongside Louise Haigh. Chief Whip Anneliese Midgley notched up roughly £4,300 across the Brit Awards, Cheltenham Festival and the Grand National. Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander watched the Wimbledon men's final courtesy of a £1,480 hospitality package, and Andy Burnham's declarations for the year run to around £6,000, including a £3,800 Brit Awards appearance in Manchester.

The Conservatives are not spared the comparison. Leader Kemi Badenoch accepted £1,360 of Wimbledon hospitality from the All England Club, while shadow chancellor Mel Stride and shadow business secretary Andrew Griffith took Chelsea Flower Show and Formula One tickets worth similar sums. None of it, on the record so far, breaches parliamentary rules — hospitality above £300 simply has to be declared within 28 days, and by that narrow measure the system worked exactly as designed.

Why the déjà vu

That is precisely the campaigners' complaint. Rules that require disclosure rather than restraint tend to produce disclosure rather than restraint — and Westminster has been here before. Starmer's own early months in office were consumed by the row over free clothing and football tickets from Labour donor Waheed Alli, a controversy serious enough that the prime minister eventually stopped accepting personal gifts of clothing altogether. The expectation, inside and outside Parliament, was that the episode would chasten members across the House. The 2026 numbers suggest it mostly chastened them into finding new categories of freebie.

Duncan Hames of Transparency International UK put the underlying tension plainly: "Just because something is permitted it is not necessarily appropriate." It is a distinction the current disclosure regime is not built to enforce. The Parliamentary Commissioner for Standards can investigate breaches of the rules, but accepting a declared, permitted gift is not a breach — it is compliance. Critics argue that is exactly the problem: a threshold-and-declare system polices paperwork, not judgment, and judgment is what kept failing.

What's next

No new sanctions or rule changes have followed the disclosures, and none appear imminent — the Committee on Standards has not signalled a fresh review. The more likely test comes the next time a high-value gift lands close to an unrelated vote or contract decision, at which point "permitted" and "appropriate" will be forced apart in public rather than in a campaigner's quote. Until then, the register will keep filling up, and the retrospective tallies will keep landing every few months, each one bigger than campaigners would like and each one, technically, within the rules.
