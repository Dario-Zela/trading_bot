European heatwave death toll passes 25,000 as wildfires burn 250,000 hectares

Aid workers respond to wildfires in Ávila province, Spain, one of the largest blazes in the country's history. — World Central Kitchen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Europe's heat deaths have passed 25,000 and kept climbing toward 30,000 as wildfires burn over 500,000 hectares, exposing how far official tolls lag the real one.

Europe is closing out its deadliest summer in over a decade, and the final number is still moving. Heat-linked deaths across the continent passed 25,000 in early August, according to Bloomberg's tally of national mortality data — and by this week, Europe's excess-mortality monitoring hub was already reporting more than 30,000. The gap between those two figures, three weeks apart, is itself the story: official heat-death counts are structurally built to lag the events that cause them, and this summer has stretched that lag further than usual.

Alongside the deaths, more than 500,000 hectares have burned across the European Union by the most recent count from the EU's fire information system — well above the 20-year average of under 200,000 hectares for the same point in the season, and running ahead of even last year's record pace. France and Spain have each endured the largest single wildfires in their modern history. Both countries have carried out mass evacuations, and one crisis — a European river running dry — has quietly connected the heat deaths, the fires and a very different casualty: the continent's cruise and energy infrastructure.

Counting the dead, weeks behind

Heat mortality is never announced in real time. It is reconstructed afterward, by comparing actual deaths against a statistical baseline of what would normally be expected — a method that requires weeks of national registry data to settle. EuroMOMO, the pooled European monitoring platform, put excess deaths at 14,260 for a single week in late June alone, with more than 12,000 of those among people over 65. Belgium's excess-mortality rate hit 48% that week, ahead of France at 23% and the Netherlands at 13%. Germany's Robert Koch Institute has separately estimated close to 12,000 heat-linked excess deaths since April, the highest national toll on the continent.

By the time Bloomberg's compiled figure crossed 25,000 in early August, the underlying week-by-week data it drew on was already a month old. That is the pattern epidemiologists flag every heatwave season: the headline number is real, but it is a floor, not a ceiling. This week's 30,000-plus reading from EuroMOMO suggests the eventual, fully reconciled total — the kind of number that arrives only after autumn, once every national registry has caught up — will land meaningfully higher again. Whether 2026 ultimately exceeds 2022's estimated 61,600 European heat deaths, still the modern benchmark, is a question the data cannot yet answer.

Fire follows the heat

The wildfires are the visible half of the same event. In France, a blaze near Bordeaux burned through the Gironde and Landes, growing large enough to generate its own lightning and weather — a pyrocumulonimbus event never previously recorded in the country, and one more commonly associated with fire behaviour in North America or Australia. It forced evacuations that peaked above 250,000 people in France alone, with the Cap Ferret peninsula cleared entirely by boat and road. France has now burned more than 115,000 hectares this year, a national record.

Spain declared a national emergency after a fire in Ávila province, west of Madrid, burned roughly 50,000 hectares — described by officials as the largest in the country's recorded history. Madrid deployed nearly 2,700 personnel, plus reinforcements from Greece, Italy and Portugal, a reminder that Europe's firefighting capacity is now routinely pooled across borders rather than handled nationally.

When infrastructure meets its limit

The same heat that fed the fires has been drying out Europe's rivers, and that has turned into an economic story of its own. The Danube fell toward record-low levels in August, exposing sandbanks and shipwrecks from Belgrade to Budapest. In Hungary, the Paks nuclear plant — which depends on river water for cooling — was forced to run at roughly a tenth of its normal capacity, tightening the country's power supply during the same weeks that air-conditioning demand was peaking.

On the same stretch of river, TUI's river cruises ran into a smaller but telling version of the same problem. Passengers on Danube sailings described broken air conditioning, sewage smells and non-functioning toilets through the peak of the heat — one traveller reported "big blowers" installed in place of proper cooling, pushing hot air through cabins already at 40°C outside. TUI has acknowledged that low water levels compounded the strain on its onboard systems and says it will invest in fleet-wide air conditioning upgrades this winter. It is a small case study in a larger question the summer has posed to industries well beyond travel: infrastructure built for the climate of a decade ago is now routinely tested by conditions it wasn't designed to survive.

What comes next

The reconciled, final mortality figures for this summer won't be settled until national statistics offices close out autumn registry data — the point at which 2022's 61,600 figure was itself confirmed, months after the season ended. Insurers are watching the same lag from the other direction: wildfire and business-interruption claims from France and Spain are still being filed, and reinsurers will use this season, alongside 2022's, to judge whether 25,000-plus heat deaths and half a million hectares burned is becoming Europe's new one-in-ten-year event rather than an outlier. The next firm marker is EuroMOMO's autumn reconciliation, expected to either confirm or push past this week's 30,000-plus reading.
