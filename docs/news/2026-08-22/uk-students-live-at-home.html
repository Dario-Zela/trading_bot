More UK students plan to live at home as university costs turn 'insane'

Grand Central, a Unite Students halls of residence in Liverpool — the operator is cutting its national portfolio from 72,000 to about 55,000 beds. — Alan Fairweather / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK 18-year-olds choosing to live at home for university rose 7% this admissions cycle as rents outpace loans, forcing operators like Unite Group to shrink their portfolios.

The maths that once made leaving home for university straightforward — take out a maintenance loan, rent a room near campus, pay it off over a working life — is breaking down for a growing share of UK school leavers. UCAS figures show 89,510 UK 18-year-olds who secured a place for the 2025 intake said they intended to live at home while studying, up from 83,705 the year before, a 7% rise in a single admissions cycle. The longer trend is sharper still: 30% of UK 18-year-olds in 2024 said they planned to live at home during their studies, up from 25% in 2019 and just 21% in 2015.

The proximate cause is rent. Save the Student's national accommodation survey puts average UK student housing costs at around £563 a month; in London the figure regularly clears £1,000 for a basic en-suite room, with some central studios exceeding £2,000. Students in Bristol, Glasgow and Guildford now pay more than £300 a week on average. At the University of York, students told the BBC their weekly rent had risen by £50 to £60 in a single year, with some paying around £800 a month to share a house with five others. In south Wales, 18-year-old Meg Campbell chose to study media at Swansea and live at home rather than take a place at Winchester, telling the BBC the accommodation costs there were simply too high to justify.

The loan doesn't cover the rent

The funding gap does most of the explanatory work. For 2026/27 the maximum maintenance loan is £10,830 a year for students living away from home outside London, £14,135 in London, and £9,118 for those living at home — but most students don't receive the maximum, since the loan is means-tested against household income. Save the Student estimates the average student now spends around 30% of their loan on rent alone, with one in five spending 50% or more, leaving little for food, transport or course materials before term has properly started. Against that arithmetic, a free room at home and a season ticket looks less like a compromise and more like the only workable option.

Operators are already repositioning

The shift is showing up in the numbers of Britain's largest listed student landlord. Unite Group — which owns and manages purpose-built student accommodation across the UK — reported a £417m first-half loss and is now cutting its portfolio from roughly 72,000 beds to about 55,000, pulling out of nine cities over the next two years to concentrate on the 20 universities where demand remains strongest. Management points to a mix of factors, including an expected decline in postgraduate numbers and rising competing supply, but has explicitly named the rise in live-at-home students as one of the drivers reshaping occupancy. The company still expects occupancy of 94–96% for the coming academic year in its remaining core cities and forecasts rental growth of 1–2%, arguing that a tightening of supply elsewhere — fewer new developments, older halls closing — will support pricing where it chooses to remain. That is a bet that demand is concentrating rather than disappearing, not that the underlying pressure on students is easing.

Who feels it first

The shift is not evenly spread. Research from the Sutton Trust and UCL found that students from families who had used a food bank in the past year were nearly twice as likely to plan on living at home as their peers — 31% versus 19% — even before accounting for whether they applied to university at all. That points to the accommodation squeeze as a widening-participation problem as much as a real estate one: it is disproportionately shaping the choices of students with the least financial cushion, potentially narrowing their choice of course and institution to whatever is commutable.

UCAS has responded with a budget calculator letting applicants estimate living costs across more than 100 towns and cities, an acknowledgment that cost has become as central to the choice of where to study as course rankings. The next marker to watch is UCAS's end-of-cycle data in December, which will confirm whether this year's Clearing intake — still being finalised this month — extends the live-at-home trend further, and whether Unite's repositioning proves to be a one-off adjustment or the start of a broader retreat by accommodation operators from cities where the economics no longer work.
