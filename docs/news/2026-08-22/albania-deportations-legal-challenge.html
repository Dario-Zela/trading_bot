Home Office could be blocked from deporting trafficking victims to Albania

The Royal Courts of Justice in London, home to England and Wales's senior courts including immigration appeals. — David Castor / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A tribunal ruling shielding Albanian male trafficking victims from deportation threatens to slow the Home Office's Albania-driven asylum enforcement strategy.

A ruling from a panel of three senior judges in London this month has opened a fresh legal front against one of the Home Office's most heavily relied-upon deportation routes — and could force ministers to rethink how they treat some of the asylum system's most vulnerable claimants.

The Upper Tribunal's Immigration and Asylum Chamber found that a 21-year-old Albanian man, trafficked into cannabis cultivation as a teenager, remained at "real risk" of being retrafficked if returned to Albania — despite five years having passed since he first fled his exploiters. In doing so, the judges issued new country guidance that the Home Office must now factor into every future decision on whether to remove male trafficking victims to Albania. It is a narrow ruling on paper. In practice, lawyers say it strikes at the foundation of a returns strategy the government has leaned on for four years to bring down Channel crossing numbers and clear the asylum backlog.

A well-worn deportation pipeline hits a wall

Albania occupies a peculiar place in Britain's asylum system. It is not a country in conflict, and the Home Office has long treated it as broadly "safe" — a classification that underpins a 2022 returns agreement, struck after more than 10,000 Albanians arrived by small boat that year alone, roughly a quarter of all Channel crossings. Rishi Sunak's government responded with a dedicated Home Office unit to fast-track Albanian claims and a bilateral deal with Tirana to put failed applicants on removal flights. It worked, in volume terms: Albanians have been the single largest nationality among enforced removals from the UK for five consecutive years, with 2,247 deported in the twelve months to the end of March, roughly a quarter of all enforced returns.

But the same period saw a quieter, parallel trend. Referrals of Albanian males into the UK's National Referral Mechanism for suspected trafficking victims overtook referrals of Albanian women for the first time — 4,107 men and boys flagged in 2022, against 502 women, with the pattern holding in 2023 at 3,463 to 588. Most were trafficked not for sex but for labour, typically cultivating cannabis in rented houses under the control of organised crime networks that operate on both sides of the Channel. The government's "safe country" framing and the reality facing this specific population — recognised trafficking victims, disproportionately young men — have been on a collision course for some time. This ruling is where they met.

What the tribunal found

The case centred on a young man first exploited by an Albanian gang while visiting Belgium with his father at 16, then trafficked onward to the UK and forced into cannabis cultivation. He escaped, was formally recognised by the Home Office as a trafficking victim through the National Referral Mechanism, and was subsequently denied asylum on the basis that Albania was safe for him to return to.

The tribunal disagreed. Its judgment, examined in detail by three senior judges, concluded that although Albania has made genuine progress against corruption, it remains "widespread," that there are no dedicated shelters for adult male trafficking victims anywhere in the country, and that the risk of retrafficking for men in this position is significant — regardless of how much time has elapsed since the original exploitation. Because it was issued as country guidance rather than a one-off decision, the finding binds future tribunals and obliges Home Office caseworkers to grapple with it directly, rather than treating each Albanian male trafficking case in isolation.

"[This] measured judgment reflects the significant and detailed evidence... about the organised criminal networks that target and control boys and young men."

That assessment came from Esme Madill, a solicitor at Islington Law Centre who represented the claimant and has spent years working on Albanian trafficking cases through the Breaking the Chains project, a joint initiative with the Shpresa Programme focused on young Albanian asylum seekers. Home Office sources indicated they were weighing whether to appeal.

The stakes for enforcement policy

The political backdrop makes the timing awkward for the government. The headline asylum numbers have been moving in the direction ministers want: the initial-decision backlog stood at 35,744 applications at the end of March, down 58% since June 2024 and the lowest level since 2020. Albanian removals have been central to that progress, both as a volume driver and as a deterrent signal to prospective Channel crossers. A country-guidance ruling that requires individualised scrutiny of trafficking history before any Albanian male can be removed complicates that machinery considerably — it does not halt removals outright, but it raises the evidential bar for each one and hands defence lawyers a binding precedent to invoke.

It also lands as the broader system strains elsewhere: the appeals backlog, distinct from the initial-decision queue the government has been trumpeting, nearly doubled last year to a record 80,000 cases. Every case where a trafficking claim now requires fresh consideration under this guidance is a case that risks feeding directly into that queue rather than clearing out the front door.

The deeper tension the ruling exposes is not really about Albania specifically. It is about whether a general "safe country" designation can survive contact with a well-evidenced, population-specific protection claim — in this instance, that organised crime in Albania targets and re-targets trafficked men in a way generic country assessments miss. Courts have shown, in this judgment, that they will not defer to enforcement targets when the evidence points the other way.

What happens next

The immediate question is whether the Home Office appeals the ruling to the Court of Appeal, a decision expected in the coming weeks. Either way, caseworkers must now apply the new country guidance to pending and future Albanian male trafficking claims, which is likely to slow individual case processing even as the government continues to publicise backlog reductions elsewhere. Immigration lawyers expect a wave of fresh challenges from Albanian men already served with removal directions, testing how far the ruling's protection extends beyond the specific facts of this case.
