Labour accused of stalling on Jackdaw and Rosebank North Sea projects

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch has accused Labour of stalling on North Sea approvals. — House of Commons / Roger Harris, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Kemi Badenoch says Labour's 'heart isn't really in' approving the Jackdaw and Rosebank North Sea fields, as ministers sit on a decision with no set deadline.

Kemi Badenoch has a straightforward diagnosis of why two of the North Sea's largest undeveloped oil and gas fields are still waiting for a green light: the government doesn't really want to give them one.

"If this decision had been made earlier in the summer, we could have had gas from Jackdaw in people's homes by this winter, by Christmas," the Conservative leader said, accusing Labour of dragging its feet on approvals for the Jackdaw gas field and the Rosebank oil field. Their "heart's not really in it," she added — a jab less at process than at motive.

The timing is pointed. Public consultations on both projects have now closed — Jackdaw's just over a week ago, Rosebank's more recently — which normally would clear the way for a ministerial decision. Instead, officials have declined to set a deadline. "We've not put a particular deadline on it, but we have gone as fast as we can through every stage of this process," one government source said when asked whether a call would land in September.

Two fields, two fates

Jackdaw, a Shell-operated gas field roughly 150 miles east of Aberdeen, was first approved back in 2022. Rosebank, west of Shetland and jointly owned by Equinor and Shell through the Adura venture, is the larger prize — up to 500 million barrels of oil, and the UK's biggest untapped field. Both had their consents quashed last year after a legal challenge succeeded in the courts, forcing the fresh consultation round that has just concluded.

Reporting in recent days suggests the government may be preparing to split the difference: Prime Minister Andy Burnham is said to be "minded to approve" Jackdaw, while Labour figures have privately signalled that Rosebank — facing fiercer climate objections — may be rejected. One option under discussion, according to reports, is delaying any announcement until public attention has moved past the summer's heatwave coverage, to blunt the political fallout either way.

The final sign-off rests with Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, who has previously opposed new North Sea licensing but said in July she intended to be "pragmatic" on the question. That shift alone tells its own story about the pressure the government is under from unions and industry, who point to 3,500 construction jobs and 880 permanent roles the two fields could support.

The pressure from the other side

Badenoch's complaint is not the only one landing on Burnham's desk. Ninety MPs and MSPs, organised by the environmental campaign group Uplift, wrote to the prime minister this month urging him to reject Rosebank outright, arguing the field would produce more than 250 million tonnes of CO2 over its lifetime — equivalent to roughly 70% of Britain's annual emissions — while adding barely 1% to the country's energy security, per an International Energy Agency assessment cited in the letter.

That squeeze — jobs and tax revenue on one side, emissions accounting and manifesto commitments on the other — is precisely why no deadline has been set. Every month of delay is itself a decision of sorts, just one nobody in government has to defend on the record.

For operators, the uncertainty carries a real cost: investment decisions, rig contracts and workforce planning don't wait comfortably on political timetables. Watch for Fahnbulleh's formal response to the consultations, expected in the coming weeks, and for whether Jackdaw and Rosebank are announced together or peeled apart — a split verdict would tell its own story about how Burnham is trying to have this both ways.
