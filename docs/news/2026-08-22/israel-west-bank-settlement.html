Israel re-establishes closed West Bank settlement, defying international protest

In one sentence
Israel reopened the Ganim settlement and advanced the E1 project despite a 14-nation rebuke from the UK, Canada and Australia, none of which has yet imposed real costs.

Two decades after Israeli bulldozers flattened it, the settlement of Ganim is standing again. On August 13, families began moving into around two dozen prefabricated homes on the hillside near Jenin in the northern West Bank — the site Israel evacuated in 2005 under Ariel Sharon's disengagement plan, alongside Gaza's Jewish settlements. It is the third of four settlements dismantled that year to be rebuilt this year alone, after Homesh in March and Sa-Nur in April.

Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who doubles as the cabinet minister overseeing settlement policy, called the reopening the erasure of a "disgrace," referring to the 2005 pullout. Defence Minister Israel Katz was blunter: "Here, this is the Land of Israel. Here, we are staying forever." Yossi Dagan, who heads the regional settler council, declared "the era of evacuations is over." Palestinian stores near the site were demolished and access roads to neighbouring villages restricted ahead of the move-in, according to reporting from the ground.

A pattern, not an incident

Ganim's reopening did not happen in isolation. A week later, Israel opened bidding for the E1 project — roughly 3,400 housing units on land between East Jerusalem and Ma'ale Adumim that would, if built, sever East Jerusalem from the rest of the West Bank. Smotrich called E1 a plan that "practically erases the two-state delusion." UN Secretary-General António Guterres described it as an "existential threat" to a contiguous Palestinian state.

The response from Western governments was immediate and unusually broad. On 20-21 August, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Canada, Norway, Australia, New Zealand, Sweden, Belgium, Spain, Austria and Greece issued a joint statement branding the E1 tenders "unacceptable" and warning that "international law is clear that Israeli settlements in the West Bank are illegal." It went further than past statements, urging companies not to bid on the tenders and warning of "legal and reputational consequences" for firms that did. The same governments had already, in May, signed a separate statement over what they called "unprecedented levels of violence by settlers against civilians" in the West Bank.

Protest without cost

What stands out is less the criticism than the absence, so far, of anything behind it. Britain, Canada and Australia have condemned settlement expansion and demolitions repeatedly this year — over E1, over settler violence, over Israel's refusal to investigate the killing of aid workers in Gaza — without imposing sanctions, trade restrictions or arms measures tied specifically to West Bank policy. The corporate warning attached to the E1 statement is the sharpest tool used yet, and it relies on companies self-policing reputational risk rather than any government enforcement mechanism.

That gap is the story here for now: three settlements rebuilt, a fourth-largest settlement project advancing, and a coalition of Western governments whose criticism has grown louder each time without translating into material pressure. For markets, that is precisely why the episode has registered as noise rather than signal — oil prices and Israeli equities have shown little reaction to a dispute that remains, for now, rhetorical.

The next test is whether words turn into action. Israel's Higher Planning Committee is expected to advance further E1 construction approvals in the coming weeks, and the fourth evacuated settlement, Kadim, remains a likely candidate for reopening. Whether any signatory to the August statement follows through with concrete measures — rather than another joint letter — will determine whether this remains background noise in a two-decade-old conflict or becomes something markets need to start pricing.
