Gold hits fresh highs above $4,600 as Bitcoin tops $77,000 in risk-on move

Gold bullion bars — the metal is on pace for its best monthly gain since 1999, closing near $4,662 an ounce. — Stevebidmead / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Gold's best August since 1999 and Bitcoin's push past $77,000 both trace to one event: Treasury's bond buyback and the dollar's slide, not a risk-on shift.

Gold closed last week at $4,661.60 an ounce, up more than 14% for August and on pace for its best month since 1999. Bitcoin, in the same stretch, tore back above $77,000, adding close to 25% in a week — its strongest run since 2023. Two assets that are supposed to move in opposite directions, one bought when investors are scared, the other bought when they are not, rose together. That is the part worth explaining, because it did not happen by accident.

The trigger was not a Federal Reserve decision. It was the U.S. Treasury. On August 19, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced the department would at least double its buyback operations for longer-dated debt — from $2 billion to $4 billion per operation — running from September through early November. The stated goal was to relieve pressure on 10- and 30-year yields, which had been climbing toward multi-year highs as the federal government's total debt crossed $40 trillion for the first time. The 30-year had touched levels not seen since 2007.

A buyback that spooked the dollar more than it calmed bonds

Yields did fall initially — the 10-year dropped more than 5 basis points to 4.65%, the 30-year tumbled 9 basis points to 5.196% — but the relief did not hold. By the following session the 30-year was back above 5.2%, and the 10-year went on to touch a 20-month high days later. What did move, and stayed moved, was the dollar: it fell nearly 1% against a basket of major currencies on the announcement alone.

That is the mechanism linking gold and Bitcoin. Neither rallied because investors got more confident about growth or more relaxed about risk. They rallied because a large, discretionary intervention by the Treasury — buying back debt with billions in newly directed purchasing power, on top of a $40 trillion debt load — read to markets as an admission that Washington intends to manage its way out of a funding problem rather than let yields find their own level. "There has to be a price to pay," Scotiabank's chief FX strategist Shaun Osborne put it, "either in the form of higher yields, or they're going to get a concession from the U.S. dollar." Markets chose to price the second option. Gold and Bitcoin, for different reasons, are both trades against a weaker dollar.

Two different trades wearing the same trade

Gold's leg is the more conventional one. Central banks bought 288.9 tonnes in the second quarter alone, up 62% year-on-year and the strongest Q2 on record, and that accumulation has continued as a hedge against exactly this kind of fiscal-dominance scenario — a government leaning on the bond market and its currency rather than raising taxes or cutting spending to close the gap. Real yields falling, or credibility falling faster than yields, both push in the same direction for bullion.

Bitcoin's leg is messier and partly mechanical. Roughly $2.7 billion in crypto short positions were liquidated as the rally accelerated, a short squeeze layered on top of the dollar move. But the move outlasted the squeeze: spot Bitcoin ETFs pulled in $606 million on August 20 alone, a fourth straight day of net inflows, and traders also pointed to a last-ditch legislative push on the CLARITY Act — the digital-asset market-structure bill that would give Bitcoin and Ethereum unambiguous statutory classification — as a secondary tailwind, even though the bill's actual odds of passing this year sit closer to a third, down from above 80% in February. Strip out the squeeze and the ETF flows, and what is left is the same dollar-debasement logic gold traders are running, applied to an asset some investors now treat as a parallel, harder-to-debase store of value rather than a straightforward risk asset.

What Jackson Hole has to answer

The timing sharpens the stakes for next week. New Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole address since taking office in May, at a symposium running August 27–29, and the Federal Open Market Committee is unusually split — July's meeting produced a 9-3 vote split, the widest in a generation. Markets are pricing around 40 basis points of cuts by October; some economists argue the number should be closer to 100. Warsh has so far leaned hawkish and said his remarks will address "long-term structural questions" rather than near-term guidance, which leaves the market's dollar-debasement read largely unchallenged going into the speech.

That is the actual test for this trade. If Warsh uses Jackson Hole to reassert the Fed's independence from Treasury's balance-sheet management — pushing back on the buyback logic, signalling fewer cuts than the market has priced — the dollar could find support and both gold and Bitcoin would have to answer for gains built substantially on a single week's fiscal intervention. If he stays vague, or leans dovish, the debasement trade gets another leg. Either way, the next data point is not a jobs report or an inflation print. It is one speech, in Wyoming, from a Fed chair three months into the job.
