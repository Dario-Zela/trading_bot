FDA approves Bristol Myers Squibb's Zenbexus for multiple myeloma

Bristol Myers Squibb's Princeton, New Jersey campus — Jonathan Schilling / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
FDA approved BMS's Zenbexus for relapsed multiple myeloma — a first-in-class drug, but one facing a crowded field and a still-unproven commercial ceiling.

The FDA has approved Bristol Myers Squibb's Zenbexus, a new drug for adults with multiple myeloma who have relapsed after at least one prior round of treatment. It is a small approval in the sense that the patient population is narrow — this is not a first-line therapy — but it is a large one in the sense that Zenbexus is the first of an entirely new drug class, called CELMoDs, to reach the market for this cancer.

That distinction matters more than it might sound. Zenbexus, known chemically as iberdomide, is approved for use alongside two existing drugs — Johnson & Johnson's Darzalex (daratumumab) and the steroid dexamethasone — in patients who have already tried a proteasome inhibitor and an immunomodulatory drug, common building blocks of frontline myeloma regimens. The FDA granted the approval on August 13, based on results from the Phase 3 EXCALIBER-RRMM trial, in which 41% of patients on the Zenbexus combination reached a minimal-residual-disease-negative complete response, versus 21% on the comparator regimen — a statistically significant gap (p<0.0001), according to BMS's filing[^1].

A new mechanism, and a new yardstick

CELMoD stands for cereblon E3 ligase modulator — drugs that hijack a cell's own protein-disposal machinery to degrade proteins tumor cells depend on. It is a lineal descendant of the older "IMiD" class that includes BMS's own Revlimid, but engineered to bind cereblon more tightly and drive a broader set of proteins to destruction. Cristian Massacesi, BMS's chief medical officer, called it the arrival of "a new treatment class," adding that the approval "opens venues for many more potential combinations"[^2].

The approval is also notable on regulatory grounds: it is the first new multiple myeloma drug cleared using minimal residual disease — a blood test sensitive enough to detect a handful of cancer cells among a million healthy ones — as the primary evidence of benefit, rather than the traditional yardstick of progression-free survival. That is faster for patients and companies alike, but it comes with a catch: like other accelerated approvals, Zenbexus's continued marketing is contingent on BMS confirming real clinical benefit — meaning it actually helps patients live longer or better — in follow-up trials[^1].

Why BMS needed this

The approval lands as BMS's existing myeloma franchise is being hollowed out by generics. Revlimid sales fell 49% year-over-year to $425 million in the second quarter of 2026; Pomalyst, hit by generic entry earlier this year, dropped 71% to $204 million^3. Zenbexus, priced at $29,500 per 28-day cycle, is one of the assets BMS is counting on to offset that decline. William Blair analysts project U.S. sales could top $1 billion annually by 2031^4 — a number that assumes both a smooth rollout and no material erosion of the eligible patient pool in the meantime.

That second assumption is the one worth watching. Multiple myeloma has become a crowded field: CAR-T therapies Carvykti and Abecma, and bispecific antibodies including Tecvayli and Talvey, already compete for the same relapsed patients, and daratumumab-based regimens are increasingly used as early as first-line treatment — which, if the trend continues, could shrink the later-line population Zenbexus targets before it fully ramps^4. BMS shares rose roughly 1.5% on the approval day and have since traded in the mid-$60s, with Citi analysts framing the approval as a sign of the company's broader shift away from legacy-drug dependence^4 — a reasonable read, though one that says more about portfolio narrative than about Zenbexus's standalone commercial ceiling.

The next checkpoint is less about the label and more about the label's durability: BMS's confirmatory trial data, and whether real-world uptake tracks the EXCALIBER-RRMM numbers once Zenbexus is competing for prescriptions rather than headlines.
