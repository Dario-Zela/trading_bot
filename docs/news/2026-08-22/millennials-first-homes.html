Millennials finding it easier to buy first homes as market turns

A street of terraced housing in Loughborough, England — the kind of first-time buyer stock at the centre of the affordability shift. — Duncharris / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK house prices have fallen from nine times to 7.6 times average income since 2021, giving millennials their best affordability window in years — though deposits and housing supply remain binding constraints.

For most of the past two decades, the story of millennial homeownership in Britain has been a one-way ratchet: prices up, deposits up, the age of the first-time buyer creeping later with each cohort. New figures suggest that ratchet may finally be loosening.

The headline number comes from a comparison of birth cohorts. Britons born in 1961-65 had a 47% chance of owning a home by age 28. Those born in 1991-95 — the youngest millennials — managed just 27% by the same age. That gap is the definitive story of a generation locked out. But the more recent trend line has turned: house prices, which stood at roughly nine times average income in 2021, have eased to about 7.6 times income this year, according to data cited in BBC reporting this week. It's not a return to the affordability of the 1990s. It is, for the first time in a long while, movement in the right direction.

What's actually changed

Three things have shifted since the post-pandemic peak, and none of them is dramatic on its own.

First, wages have outpaced house prices for several consecutive years, narrowing the price-to-earnings ratio mechanically rather than through any single policy intervention. Second, mortgage products have adapted: some lenders now offer 5% deposit mortgages, down from the 10-15% that became standard after the 2008 crisis, and terms have stretched to 40 years from the traditional 25-30, lowering monthly payments even as headline rates stay elevated. Two-year fixed mortgage rates were averaging 5.63% and five-year fixes 5.66% in early August, with markets pricing in further Bank of England cuts from the current 3.75% base rate at the September and November meetings.

Third, and less visibly, the burden has fallen. Mortgage payments for a typical first-time buyer now consume about 32% of take-home pay, close to the long-run average of 30% and well below the 45% peak reached in 2007. Private renters, by contrast, are still handing over roughly 32% of income in rent — meaning buying and renting now cost, in relative terms, about the same. That parity is new, and it changes the calculation for anyone who has been sitting on the fence.

The deposit still bites

None of this erases the deposit problem, which remains the single biggest barrier cited by prospective buyers. A 10% deposit on a typical first-time buyer property costs £44,800 in London, £32,800 in the outer metropolitan ring, and £13,100 in the north of England — a near fourfold regional spread that means the "easier" story is really a regional and income-dependent one. Over a third of first-time buyers now receive help from family, whether as a gift, a loan or an inheritance, according to lender data — a dependency that widens the gap between buyers with property-owning parents and those without.

Supply remains the binding constraint behind all of it. England needs roughly 300,000 new homes a year to keep pace with demand, by the government's own estimate; only around 208,000 were completed last year. Building costs have risen 50% since 2015 against 35% general inflation, partly a legacy of the energy price shock that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which pushed construction energy costs up 15% in a single year. A home that cost £150,000 to build in 2015 costs roughly £230,000 today, with a further 15% rise projected over five years — a cost base that limits how far builders can pass on price relief even as the government pushes planning reform and looser green-belt rules to unlock sites.

Why markets should care

A millennial cohort re-entering the housing market matters beyond the individual balance sheets involved. Housing transactions pull through demand for furniture, white goods, renovation and removals spending — a multiplier effect that shows up in retail and construction earnings before it shows up in GDP data. A sustained pickup in first-time buyer activity would also support materials and housebuilder equities, which have spent much of the past three years pricing in a structurally weak demand backdrop.

The interest-rate read-through cuts the other way, and matters more to the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee. Improving affordability driven partly by wage growth outpacing prices is not obviously disinflationary — it reflects income gains that could keep services inflation stickier than the Bank would like, one reason six of nine MPC members opted to hold rates at 3.75% in July against three dissenters pushing for a rise. Whether the September 17 meeting delivers the cut markets are pricing will hinge on whether that private-sector wage growth, running at its weakest pace since 2020 by some measures, continues to cool.

The honest reading of the data is that this is a partial, uneven recovery rather than a regime shift: the youngest millennials are still buying homes at little more than half the rate their parents' generation did at the same age. But after years in which every affordability metric moved the wrong way, a price-to-earnings ratio falling from nine to 7.6 and a rent-versus-mortgage gap closing to near-parity are real, measurable improvements. The next data points worth watching are the Bank's September rate decision and the autumn house-price indices from Halifax and Nationwide, which will show whether this year's affordability gains survive contact with the traditionally busier back-to-school buying season.
