UK fiscal squeeze persists: gilt yields hold above 5%, July borrowing overshoots

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London, whose policy path is one factor markets are weighing against gilt yields near 18-year highs. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields hit 5.05%, an 18-year high, as July borrowing overshot forecasts and eroded fiscal headroom before Healey's 28 October Budget.

The 10-year gilt yield sat at 5.05% this week — the highest since April 2008 — as investors priced in a government that may need to borrow more than it currently plans to. That level, revisited repeatedly since a Middle East energy shock rattled markets in February, has become the backdrop against which Chancellor John Healey will deliver his first Budget on 28 October. The market is not waiting for the announcement to render a verdict; it has already started pricing one in.

Healey inherited the job in unusual circumstances. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July after Keir Starmer's resignation, and one of his first acts was to sack Rachel Reeves and install Healey — until then defence secretary — at the Treasury. Reeves had left behind a fiscal buffer of £23.6bn against her own rules, set at the March spring statement. Since then, economists estimate that £10bn to £12bn of that headroom has been eroded by the same forces pushing gilt yields higher: sticky inflation, a hawkish Bank of England, and higher borrowing costs that compound as more debt matures and rolls over at today's rates rather than the near-zero rates of a decade ago.

July's numbers make the arithmetic harder

Into that already-tight picture, the Office for National Statistics added a complication this week. Public borrowing in July came in at £1.8bn, against an Office for Budget Responsibility forecast of a roughly £500m surplus — a miss of about £2.3bn in a single month. The financial year to date is not disastrous by comparison with last year: borrowing for April through July, at £56.7bn, is still £6bn (9.6%) lower than the same stretch in 2025. But it remains £2.3bn above where the OBR expected it to be, and every month that runs hot narrows the room Healey has to manoeuvre before October.

The two data points reinforce each other. Higher gilt yields raise the government's own debt-servicing bill, which mechanically worsens the borrowing figures; worse borrowing figures raise the perceived risk of gilt issuance, which markets can, in turn, demand a higher yield to hold. Reeves did leave one lever Healey can pull: a change to the current-budget fiscal rule, permitting a deficit of up to 0.5% of GDP by the end of the forecast horizon, worth an estimated extra £17bn in headroom. Whether Healey uses it, and how markets read that choice, is now the central question hanging over the autumn Budget.

The test before the test

For traders, the 5% threshold matters less as a number than as a signal of what the market expects to see on 28 October. Gilt yields easing in the weeks after a Budget typically mean investors judge the numbers credible; yields holding near current levels, or climbing further, would read as a vote of no confidence delivered before Healey has even stood at the despatch box. The next major data points — August borrowing figures in September, and any pre-Budget signalling from the Treasury — will show whether the gap between market expectations and government plans is narrowing or widening.
