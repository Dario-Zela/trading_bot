UK small businesses hit by 1,500% invoicing software price hike

Invoicing paperwork on a small business desk — the kind of workflow now tied to a single software vendor — Unsplash
In one sentence
A UK consultancy's Harvest invoicing bill rose 1,500% after Bending Spoons' takeover, exposing SMBs' lack of leverage over locked-in SaaS vendors.

Richard Haldenby has used the same invoicing software for fifteen years. He runs Salentis, a consultancy with sister operations in the UK, US and Australia, and until recently his monthly bill for Harvest — a time-tracking and invoicing tool popular with small consultancies and agencies — was $130. His renewal notice this month asked for $2,110. That is an increase of roughly 1,523%.

Haldenby is not alone. A US-based customer told the BBC their annual charge jumped from $2,800 to $23,000, a 721% rise. Others describe increases running into the tens of times their old bill. "It's a classic example of corporate greed over valuing customers," one affected user said. Another simply called it daylight robbery.

What changed

Harvest was bought in mid-2025 by Bending Spoons, the Milan-based acquirer that floated on Nasdaq on 1 July this year at an $18.4bn valuation. The firm's model is well established: buy software with a loyal, somewhat captive user base, then restructure the economics. It has done the same with Evernote, WeTransfer and Vimeo, tightening free tiers and raising prices after each deal.

With Harvest, the change was structural, not just a bigger number on the same bill. The old plan was a flat per-seat monthly fee, some of it grandfathered in from as far back as 2011. The new one bills against usage — active projects, clients, tasks and the revenue a business invoices through the platform. Many customers pay annually, so the shock has landed only now, as 2026 renewals come due.

Mark Peacock, who runs the UK consultancy PriceMaker, put it bluntly: "Harvest has completely failed at the transparency test. There is no way for a customer to work out how much it's going to cost them until they get their bill at the end of the month." He said reports of ten-fold increases were "entirely unacceptable."

The lock-in problem

What makes this more than a pricing squabble is how little room affected businesses have to move. Invoicing and time-tracking tools sit at the centre of a firm's billing cycle — client histories, project records, integrations with accounting software. Switching costs are real, and Bending Spoons appears to be pricing that in. Haldenby negotiated his bill down to $1,309 a month by prepaying annually — still a tenfold rise on his original rate, and a discount he only secured by threatening to leave.

There is, notably, no UK rule stopping a software vendor from repricing a contract this aggressively mid-relationship. Consumer protection law constrains subscription pricing for individuals in some respects; business-to-business SaaS contracts are largely left to the market. For a five-person consultancy with its books running through one platform, that market has exactly one seller with any leverage.

What to watch

Bending Spoons has run this playbook before without drawing regulatory scrutiny, and nothing so far suggests the Competition and Markets Authority is looking at this case specifically. The more interesting signal is whether other acquired SaaS tools — the CMA is already probing Microsoft's business software bundling on a separate track — start pulling the same lever now that one has shown it can be done with limited backlash. For SaaS investors, that is the read-through: pricing power over locked-in SMB customers is proving durable, right up until a regulator or a mass exodus decides otherwise.
