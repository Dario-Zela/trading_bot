TikTok to pay $400m in one of largest US child-privacy settlements

The TikTok app icon on a smartphone home screen. — Solen Feyissa / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
TikTok and ByteDance will pay $400m to settle DOJ claims they collected children's data without consent — one of the largest child-privacy recoveries on record.

TikTok and its parent company ByteDance have agreed to pay $400 million to settle a US Justice Department lawsuit accusing the platform of harvesting children's personal data without parental consent — one of the largest recoveries the government has ever secured under federal child-privacy law.

The settlement, announced Friday, closes a case the DOJ filed in 2024 alleging TikTok violated the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) by letting millions of under-13 users onto the platform and collecting their data anyway — despite, prosecutors said, having actual knowledge many of those accounts belonged to children. The suit also claimed TikTok kept that data for targeted advertising and altered its registration flow in ways that made it harder to catch underage sign-ups.

The math behind $400 million

The payment breaks into two pieces. TikTok will hand over $300 million immediately. The remaining $100 million is tied to a separate, older matter: it's the price of vacating a 2019 consent order the Federal Trade Commission imposed on Musical.ly, the lip-syncing app ByteDance bought and folded into TikTok. That 2019 case ended with a $5.7 million fine — at the time the largest COPPA penalty on record. The new settlement is more than 70 times that figure, a gap that says less about inflation and more about how far the DOJ believes the underlying violations reached once TikTok had scaled to over a billion users.

Associate Attorney General Stanley E. Woodward Jr. framed the outcome bluntly: "This settlement is a major victory for American children and parents." He added that the department's priority is "ensuring that children are protected online and that companies entrusted with their personal information meet their legal obligations." TikTok did not admit wrongdoing as part of the deal — standard practice in settlements of this size, and a reminder that the $400 million buys the company an exit from litigation, not a verdict.

What TikTok has to change

Beyond the money, the settlement requires TikTok to strengthen age-verification controls, add new safeguards specifically for younger users, and give parents more direct oversight of their children's accounts and data. Those terms echo what regulators have demanded of other platforms in past COPPA cases — the mechanism is not novel — but the scale of enforcement against a company TikTok's size is.

The timing adds an edge to the story. Days before the settlement was announced, reports surfaced that TikTok had knowingly switched off algorithmic safety guardrails for roughly 10% of US users as part of an internal experiment, drawing bipartisan criticism in Washington. That disclosure sits awkwardly next to a company simultaneously agreeing to tighter child-safety commitments, and it suggests the DOJ's oversight of TikTok's practices is unlikely to end with this check.

The read for the rest of the sector

What makes this settlement worth watching beyond TikTok itself is the precedent it sets for cost. Every major platform with a large teenage and pre-teen user base — YouTube, Instagram, Snapchat — operates under the same COPPA framework and the same basic exposure: undisclosed data collection from users too young to legally consent to it. A $400 million number, arrived at through negotiation rather than trial, gives regulators a fresh reference point for what "substantial recovery" looks like the next time one of TikTok's rivals ends up in the DOJ's sights. For an industry that has treated child-privacy fines as a rounding error against ad revenue, this is the first one big enough to actually register on a balance sheet.

The next marker to watch is enforcement of the settlement's behavioral terms — how the DOJ verifies TikTok's new age-checks and parental controls actually work, not just that they exist on paper.
