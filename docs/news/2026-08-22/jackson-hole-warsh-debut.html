Jackson Hole looms as Treasury's bond buyback draws skepticism ahead of Warsh's debut

In one sentence
Treasury doubled its bond buybacks to $4bn per operation after debt hit $40tn, but critics call it a Band-Aid — putting Warsh's Jackson Hole debut under pressure to reassure.

The number landed on August 18 with the flat, unarguable weight of an odometer: US federal debt crossed $40 trillion. It took less than five months to add the previous trillion, and less than a year to add the one before that. Two days later, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent did what Treasury does when the bond market gets restless — he announced more buying.

The department said it would double the size of its liquidity-support buybacks for longer-dated coupon securities, from a $2 billion cap per operation to at least $4 billion, starting September 9 and running through November 4. Bessent has since suggested the ceiling could go higher still. It is, on paper, a technical fix: Treasury using cash on hand to retire older, thinly-traded bonds and smooth a market that had started to seize up at the long end. It is also, on the calendar, a decision made one week before Kevin Warsh walks to the podium at Jackson Hole for the first major speech of his chairmanship — and market voices are already treating the two events as one story.

A Band-Aid, not a bond

The proximate cause of the buyback expansion was ugly auction results. A $25 billion sale of 30-year bonds on August 13 cleared at 5.216%, the highest yield on a long bond auction since 2001. Days later the 30-year touched 5.34% in secondary trading, a 19-year high, while the 10-year auctioned at 4.683%, its worst level since 2007. Inflation running above target, a widening war footprint in the Middle East, and a wall of AI-driven capital spending have all been cited as pressure points; the debt load sits underneath all of it, a slow leak that widens every deficit into a bigger financing problem.

Buybacks do not touch that arithmetic. Treasury is not retiring debt — it is swapping illiquid old bonds for cash it already holds and reissuing at the short end, a liquidity operation, not a deficit operation. That distinction is the crux of the skepticism. Peter Schiff, the economist and dollar bear who has spent two decades warning about US debt sustainability, was blunt about it, arguing the move was less a fix than a panic: an admission that the Treasury market itself was showing signs of strain. His view, laid out on his radio program and in market commentary this week, is that a $4 billion buyback ceiling is a rounding error against $40 trillion of debt and does nothing to change the trajectory investors are pricing into 30-year paper. He has gone further, arguing that if long yields keep climbing, Treasury will eventually need a buyback far larger than what has been announced — and that the Federal Reserve may ultimately be pulled into outright quantitative easing to backstop it, an outcome that would collide directly with the inflation-fighting credibility Warsh has spent his short tenure trying to build.

That is the sharper version of a more measured concern showing up across bond desks: buybacks improve market plumbing, they do not change the volume of paper the government needs to sell. With the deficit still running large and no fiscal consolidation on the table, the supply problem simply reappears at the next auction.

Warsh's inheritance

Kevin Warsh took over as the Fed's 17th chair on May 22, confirmed by the narrowest Senate margin in the institution's history, 54–45. He arrives at Jackson Hole on August 28 having deliberately dialed back the forward guidance markets grew used to under Jerome Powell — a shift that makes this speech carry more information risk than a typical chair's debut, because investors have fewer breadcrumbs to work from. The symposium's official theme, financial innovation and the future of payments, points toward a speech built around structural questions — the architecture of monetary policy in an economy reshaped by AI investment and shifting trade patterns — rather than a line-by-line response to the week's yield moves.

But the market will not let him stay above the fray entirely. Surveys of fund managers ahead of the speech show a plurality — roughly 69% in one poll — expecting a deliberately neutral tone, neither hawkish nor dovish. That expectation itself is telling: it suggests investors do not expect Warsh to either validate or directly rebuke Treasury's buyback strategy, leaving the credibility question that Schiff and others have raised essentially unanswered from the podium. Whether that neutrality reads as prudent independence or as a chair declining to engage with a market that is visibly nervous will depend on the specifics of what he says about debt issuance, if he addresses it at all.

The credibility gap

The fiscal and monetary tracks were never fully separable, but the buyback timing has fused them into a single test. Treasury's move is meant to be reassuring — a signal that Washington is managing the mechanics of a $40 trillion balance sheet competently. Instead it has been read by at least part of the market as confirmation that the mechanics are under real strain, arriving days before the one official positioned to calm markets steps to a podium with, by design, less to say than his predecessors offered.

The next data points arrive fast. The expanded buyback operations begin September 9 and run through November 4, giving the market a live read on whether $4 billion per operation actually narrows the yield gap between old and new long bonds — the thing the program is explicitly designed to fix. Before that, Warsh's August 28 keynote will be parsed line by line for any reference to debt issuance, term premium, or the central bank's appetite to backstop Treasury if yields keep climbing. If both land as reassurance, the credibility gap narrows. If either reads as improvisation, the next 30-year auction will say so first, and loudest.
