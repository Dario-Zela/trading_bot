Royal Mail misses delivery targets again despite 'encouraging' signs

A Royal Mail delivery van in Norfolk. The company missed both first- and second-class delivery targets in the June quarter. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Royal Mail missed Ofcom's newly lowered delivery targets again in Q1, even as it points to year-on-year gains and faces a fresh regulatory investigation.

Royal Mail delivered 85% of first-class post the next working day in the three months to June, against an Ofcom target of 90%. Second-class fared little better: 91.4% arrived within three days, versus a 95% requirement. Both are misses. Both are also, by the company's own arithmetic, improvements — a year earlier the same quarters produced 76% and 89.3% respectively.

That gap between "still failing" and "getting better" is where Royal Mail wants the conversation to sit. Ofcom, which is a year into scrutinising a company now on its fourth consecutive year of missed universal service targets, has less patience for it.

A lower bar, still not cleared

The 90% and 95% figures are themselves new — Ofcom cut them down from 93% and 98.5% on 1 April, part of a wider reset of the universal service obligation. Even against the softer targets, Royal Mail fell short in the first quarter under the new regime. It did clear the parallel "backstop" thresholds — 98.1% of first-class and 98.4% of second-class arriving within two days of the original deadline, against a 99% bar — which the company and Ofcom both treat as evidence the service isn't collapsing so much as running persistently late.

Jamie Stephenson, Royal Mail's chief operating officer, said first-class performance was "well ahead of where we expected to be at this stage of our improvement plan," with second-class "tracking in line with the plan." The plan in question is a £500m, five-year overhaul that includes a new delivery pattern — first-class letters six days a week, second-class and other mail delivered only every other weekday — being rolled out across the group's 1,200 delivery offices, with a target of full compliance by May 2027.

The fines keep escalating

Ofcom's patience has a paper trail. It fined Royal Mail £5.6m in November 2023, £10.5m in December 2024, and £21m in October 2025 — each time for missing first- and second-class targets the year before, and each time citing repeat failure as an aggravating factor. The most recent penalty covered a year in which just 75.7% of first-class mail arrived the next day, against the then-target of 93%. Ian Strawhorne, Ofcom's enforcement director, put it bluntly at the time: "Millions of important letters are arriving late, and people aren't getting what they pay for when they buy a stamp... that means making actual significant improvements, not more empty promises."

In June, Ofcom opened a fresh investigation into the 2025/26 performance, the second year running it has done so. A ruling, and any further fine, is still pending.

Why the miss keeps landing softly

Royal Mail's habit of pairing a missed target with an improving trend has so far bought it time rather than trouble beyond the fines — the company's parent, International Distribution Services, was taken private by Czech billionaire Daniel Kretinsky's EP Group in 2025, insulating it from the shareholder pressure that might otherwise sharpen scrutiny of quarter after quarter of shortfalls. Consumer groups are less convinced by the trajectory argument. Ofcom itself lowered the targets it polices rather than wait for Royal Mail to hit the old ones, a decision critics read as the regulator adjusting the test to the pupil's marks.

The next checkpoint is Ofcom's ruling on the 2025/26 investigation, alongside Royal Mail's half-year results, which will show whether the improvement in first-class delivery Stephenson described is holding into the autumn parcel and letter volumes — traditionally the period when service levels come under the most strain.
