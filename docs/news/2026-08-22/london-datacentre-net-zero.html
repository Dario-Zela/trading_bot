Proposed London datacentre deemed 'incompatible' with net zero over 1m-tonne carbon footprint

A data centre server hall, illustrative of the type of facility proposed for North Ockendon — BalticServers.com / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A £14.7bn London datacentre would emit 1.2m tonnes of CO2 a year, and even its own developer admits that's incompatible with UK net-zero goals.

A single planning application in outer London has put a number on a question the government has spent months avoiding: can Britain build the datacentres its AI boom demands and still hit net zero?

The East Havering Data Centre Campus, a proposed £14.7bn complex on 218 hectares of green belt farmland near North Ockendon, would be one of the largest datacentres in Europe. According to planning documents filed by its developer, Digital Reef, it would also generate more than 1.2 million tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year once fully operational — roughly the same as 27,000 return flights from London to New York. Over its projected 60-year lifespan, the total comes to more than 72 million tonnes.

"It's crop-producing farmland, in an age when we are threatened by security, it's the loss of wildlife habitats, and there's the pollution cost."

That's the North Ockendon Residents Association, one of several local groups objecting to the scheme. But the more striking admission comes from the developer itself. Digital Reef's own submission concedes the project "does not align with a science-based 1.5C compatible trajectory and achieving net zero by 2050." Few applicants volunteer that their flagship project contradicts the government's core climate law.

The numbers behind the objection

The scale is what makes East Havering unusual. The site would house up to 340,000 square metres of data centre space and draw around 2.65 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity annually — comparable to the demand of more than a million UK households, according to planning documents reviewed by the Guardian. By 2038, the campus's operational emissions alone would consume nearly 20 times the entire carbon budget allocated to the London borough of Havering, and close to 1% of the UK's national carbon budget.

Digital Reef has offered £77.6m toward the borough's carbon offset fund as mitigation. Campaigners, including the group Foxglove, argue that offsetting cash is the wrong tool for a problem this size. "It's a big threat to the ability to decarbonise in the UK," said Foxglove's Donald Campbell, who wants operators required to build dedicated renewable generation rather than pay into a fund.

Not an isolated case

East Havering is the largest single scheme by disclosed emissions, but it sits inside a much bigger pipeline. Roughly 50 to 60 of the 100-plus datacentres currently working through planning in the UK are destined for London, with Havering, Tower Hamlets, Newham and Brent identified as the boroughs under most pressure. London Assembly members have been told that every ten new datacentres built could add around 2.7 million tonnes of emissions — a proliferation that assembly members warn could undermine the mayor's own climate targets even before East Havering is decided.

At Westminster, the discomfort runs upward too. The Commons Environmental Audit Committee, chaired by Labour MP Toby Perkins, has pressed energy secretary Ed Miliband on whether datacentre growth was ever factored into the plan to reach net zero. Miliband's written reply called the climate impact of the datacentre boom "inherently uncertain" — an answer that satisfied few of the MPs who asked the question.

What happens next

Digital Reef is pursuing the scheme through a Local Development Order, a route that grants planning permission for a defined category of development while still requiring public consultation. An initial consultation closed in April; a revised proposal is expected before Havering council in autumn 2026, with a further consultation period to follow. The developer has also floated the possibility of applying for status as a Nationally Significant Infrastructure Project, which would shift the final decision away from the local council entirely and toward central government.

That procedural fork matters as much as the emissions figures. If ministers end up deciding East Havering's fate directly, the case becomes a test of whether the government will hold a project to its own net-zero commitments when the alternative is turning away £14.7bn in investment and the AI infrastructure capacity that comes with it. The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero has said datacentre emissions are already factored into its climate modelling and that its next carbon budget, covering the period to the 2040s, is due by the end of 2026 — the point at which this tension either gets resolved on paper or simply deferred again.
