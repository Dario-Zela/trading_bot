HSBC clears out 130+ senior bankers in biggest purge since 2010

HSBC's headquarters tower at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC cut 134 senior 'material risk takers' at a cost of $68m — its biggest such purge since the financial crisis — despite a 23% jump in first-half profit.

HSBC spent close to $68m last year paying off more than 130 of its most senior bankers — the deepest cut into its upper ranks since the 2008 financial crisis. The unusual part is the timing: the bank did this while making more money than it has in years, not while nursing losses.

First-half profit for 2026 came in at $19.5bn before tax, up 23% on the same period last year, beating analyst forecasts of $18.9bn. Revenue rose 11% to $37.7bn. Return on tangible equity, the measure investors watch most closely, climbed to 18.2% from 14.7%. HSBC used the moment to raise its full-year guidance for net interest income to at least $46bn and announced a fresh $1bn share buyback alongside a second interim dividend of $0.10 a share. None of that reads like an institution under pressure to shed risk.

What actually happened

According to Financial Times reporting picked up across the trade press, HSBC removed 134 employees classified as "material risk takers" during 2025, at a total severance cost of $67.5m — call it $68m once rounding and related charges are included. That is roughly 10% of the bank's entire material-risk-taker cohort in a single year, and by a wide margin the largest such reduction since the crisis-era retrenchment of 2008-10.

The scale becomes clearer set against peers making similar cuts over the same period. Santander removed 49 risk-takers, Deutsche Bank 48, BNP Paribas 39 and Barclays 32 — HSBC's total is nearly three times the next-largest cull among major European lenders, and reportedly the biggest cut to senior, highly paid banking staff since the pandemic year of 2020. Average severance packages elsewhere ran higher on a per-head basis — Société Générale's departing risk-takers averaged €870,000, Santander's €736,000 — suggesting HSBC's cuts were broader but somewhat less front-loaded with individual payouts.

Why "material risk taker" is a specific label, not a euphemism

The term is not corporate softening for "senior banker." It is a formal regulatory designation under the UK and EU remuneration codes, applied to staff — traders, underwriters, senior executives, anyone holding a PRA Senior Manager Function — whose individual decisions can materially move the firm's risk profile. Historically that status came with teeth: a bonus cap limiting variable pay to 100% of salary (200% with shareholder sign-off), and deferral periods on bonuses stretching up to seven years for the most senior staff. The UK scrapped the strict bonus cap in 2023, but deferral, malus and clawback rules still apply, and the category still functions as the regulatory register of exactly who at a bank can blow a hole in its balance sheet.

That is what makes the number notable. Trimming material-risk-taker headcount by a tenth isn't cost-cutting in the ordinary sense — it's a bank narrowing the list of people authorised to take on concentrated risk, at the exact moment its income statement gives it every excuse not to bother.

The Elhedery overhaul

The cuts sit inside a wider restructuring under chief executive Georges Elhedery, who took over in September 2024 promising to make HSBC "a simple, more agile, focused bank built for a fast-changing world." Managing director headcount is down a net 15% since he arrived, much of it from what the bank calls deduplication — collapsing overlapping roles across HSBC's newly consolidated four-part structure. The investment bank has retreated from Western advisory work entirely, closing its mergers-and-acquisitions and equity capital markets businesses in the UK, continental Europe and the US to redirect roughly $1.5bn toward the bank's stronger franchises in Asia and the Middle East.

Elsewhere the bank has been quietly exiting markets that don't fit the narrower footprint — retail banking in Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and Bahrain, UK life insurance, German custody and fund administration, a stake in Argentina's Grupo Financiero Galicia, French loan portfolios, its Uruguay business. It completed the privatisation of Hang Seng Bank in Hong Kong in January. HSBC set a target of $1.5bn in annualised savings by the end of 2026; by the first quarter it had already identified $1.4bn of that and taken $1.2bn in charges, most of it severance, putting the programme roughly six months ahead of schedule.

A signal beyond one bank's balance sheet

The interesting question is what this says about risk appetite more broadly, not just about HSBC's cost base. Leverage across the banking sector has climbed steadily since the deleveraging of the early 2010s, and the material-risk-taker regime was built precisely to track — and constrain — the individuals capable of re-inflating it. A bank cutting a tenth of that cohort during its best earnings run in years is choosing discipline it doesn't obviously need yet, which is a different signal than a bank cutting risk-takers because a trading loss forced its hand.

Read generously, it's simply Elhedery finishing a structural simplification that happens to coincide with a strong quarter. Read more cautiously, it's a bank pre-positioning its risk governance ahead of conditions it expects to get harder — rate normalisation, tighter credit, a less forgiving capital-markets backdrop — while it still has the earnings cushion to absorb the severance bill comfortably. Peers moving in the same direction, if more modestly, adds some weight to the second reading.

The next test of which interpretation holds is HSBC's full-year results early next year, alongside first indications of how rivals' 2026 bonus pools and risk-taker rosters shift once the annual disclosures land. If the reduction proves to be a one-off tidy-up tied to Elhedery's reorganisation, the number fades into a footnote. If Santander, Deutsche Bank and the rest keep trimming their own material-risk-taker cohorts through the back half of the year, it starts to look less like one CEO's project and more like an industry quietly bracing.
