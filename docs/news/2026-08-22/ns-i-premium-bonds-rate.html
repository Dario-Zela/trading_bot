NS&I raises Premium Bonds prize fund rate again, improving odds of a win

A novelty money box modelled on ERNIE, the machine that has picked Premium Bonds winners since 1957. — Science Museum, London / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
NS&I is lifting the Premium Bonds prize fund rate to 4.35% for September — its second rise this year — as deposit competition squeezes even risk-free savings products.

Premium Bonds have never paid a fixed return — that has always been the point, and the source of a certain low-grade national anxiety every time the prize draw lands and the text message doesn't come. But the odds of getting anything at all just got a little better, for the second time this year.

National Savings & Investments said the prize fund rate will rise to 4.35% from the September draw, up from 3.8% in July and 3.3% in April. The chance of any single £1 bond winning something shortens to 1 in 21,000, from 1 in 22,000. It sounds like a modest tweak. In practice it means NS&I is adding roughly £63 million to the monthly prize pot and creating more than 308,000 extra prizes compared with August — everything from an additional dozen jackpots at £100,000 down to thousands more £25 payouts.

Why NS&I keeps moving the dial

Premium Bonds are, formally, a Treasury-backed savings product with no interest at all — savers forgo a guaranteed return in exchange for a shot at tax-free prizes, picked monthly by ERNIE, the Electronic Random Number Indicator Equipment that has done the job in various incarnations since 1957. What has changed is not the mechanism but the incentive behind it. NS&I operates under an annual net financing target set by the Treasury — currently £15 billion, give or take £4 billion — meaning it needs to pull in (or hold onto) a specific volume of retail deposits each year. When commercial banks and challenger apps are paying more to attract savers, NS&I has to sweeten Premium Bonds or watch the money walk.

That competitive pressure has been intense. Some easy-access accounts are advertising rates as high as 5% including introductory bonuses, and top cash ISAs are paying up to 4.56%, tax-free and guaranteed — no dice roll required. April's rate cut to 3.3% now looks, in hindsight, like NS&I misjudging how sticky that competition would be; the reversal back up to 4.35% in five months suggests the correction was more urgent than a routine review.

The gap between headline and lived experience

The prize fund rate is a statistical average across the roughly £132 billion held in Premium Bonds by more than 22 million holders, not a promise to any individual saver. Someone holding the maximum £50,000 will, over enough draws, land close to the advertised rate. Someone holding a few hundred pounds may well go years without winning a penny, luck being what it is. That asymmetry is precisely why financial advisers tend to steer smaller savers toward an ISA or a fixed-rate bond, where the return is contractual rather than probabilistic — and it is why, even at 4.35%, Premium Bonds remain a product bought as much for the tax-free lottery thrill and capital security as for expected value.

What it says about the wider savings market

None of this moves markets. But it is a useful barometer: even a government-backed product with no obligation to compete on headline rates is being forced to, twice in five months, because deposit competition among banks and NS&I's own financing needs leave it no room not to. The next test comes whenever the Bank of England next adjusts its base rate — a cut would ease the pressure on commercial banks' savings rates and could, in time, give NS&I room to ease off the accelerator again.
