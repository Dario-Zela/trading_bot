Battery fires at UK recycling centres now costing industry £1bn a year

Batteries collected for recycling — a growing share end up crushed or punctured in general waste, triggering fires. — Mauro Cateb / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lithium-battery fires now cost UK waste and recycling operators roughly £1bn a year, and the industry wants manufacturers, not handlers, to pay.

The economics of Britain's waste industry are being rewritten by something most households toss out without a second thought: the battery inside a broken vape, an old toothbrush, or a dead set of earbuds.

New estimates from the Environmental Services Association (ESA), the trade body for UK waste and recycling operators, put the cost of lithium-battery fires in the waste system at over £1 billion a year — up from roughly £150 million in 2021. The jump reflects not a single catastrophe but a steady accumulation of thousands of small ones: refuse trucks gutted mid-round, sorting lines shut down for days, insurance premiums climbing, and recycling centres rebuilt after blazes that often start with a single crushed cell.

An epidemic, in the industry's own words

UK fire services tackled 1,760 lithium-ion battery fires in 2025 — one every five hours, and a 147% rise in three years, according to figures compiled by fire and waste bodies. More than 1,200 of those fires broke out specifically in bin lorries and waste facilities, a 71% jump on the year before. Roughly half of all waste fires are now attributed to lithium-ion cells.

"Waste fires caused by batteries not only endanger the lives of people working in essential frontline services but also destroy vital infrastructure," said Jacob Hayler, the ESA's executive director, in the trade body's most recent warning on the issue. The language — "epidemic levels" — is the ESA's own, and it is doing real work: it signals an industry group that has stopped treating this as an operational nuisance and started treating it as an existential cost problem it cannot absorb alone.

The mechanics are mundane and that is precisely the danger. A vape, a laptop battery or a cordless toothbrush thrown in with general rubbish gets compacted in a collection vehicle or shredded on a sorting line. If the cell is punctured or crushed, it can enter thermal runaway — a chemical reaction that ignites within seconds and is very hard to extinguish with water alone. Material Focus, the electricals-recycling body, estimates 1.6 billion batteries are discarded in household bins each year, more than a billion of them hidden inside everyday electricals rather than sold as standalone batteries.

Who pays, and who doesn't

The striking feature of the £1bn figure is where it lands. Recycling and waste operators absorb the repair bills, the insurance hikes, the lost vehicles and the business interruption. The manufacturers whose products contain the batteries that start the fires do not currently bear any of that cost. That mismatch — cost falling on the handler of a battery's end-of-life, not the maker of its beginning-of-life — is the crux of what waste operators are now asking the government to fix.

London's four largest waste authorities wrote to the minister for nature, Mary Creagh, in November 2025 urging faster action. The ESA's own policy ask is more specific: mandatory kerbside collection of batteries and small electricals across England, funded through a reformed Extended Producer Responsibility scheme rather than left to supermarket bring-banks and household recycling centres. Independent analysis commissioned from the consultancy Eunomia suggests such a scheme, retrofitting collection vehicles with external battery containers at an estimated capital cost of around 70p per household, could save more than £6 billion over the next decade by cutting fires at source rather than fighting them after collection.

What's next

A cross-party Lithium-ion Battery Safety Bill, introduced in the House of Lords in 2024, remains under debate and would tighten rules on the sale and disposal of battery-powered products, including a crackdown on non-compliant online sellers. Whether the government moves on the ESA's producer-funding ask — and how quickly — will determine whether the £1bn cost keeps rising or starts to fall. For battery and electronics manufacturers, the direction of travel is the same one now familiar from packaging and electricals EPR reform elsewhere: costs currently externalised onto waste operators are being eyed for redistribution back up the supply chain, with implications for anyone pricing battery-dependent products, EV components included, on the assumption that end-of-life costs stay someone else's problem.
