Britain's railways prepare for more extreme weather as intense El Niño develops

Repair works at the storm-breached sea wall in Dawlish, Devon — the 2014 closure cost an estimated £35m to fix and up to £1.2bn in regional economic disruption. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Network Rail is spending roughly £2.8bn on drainage and earthworks this funding period, five times the prior allocation, as forecasters flag the strongest El Niño in a century.

Two trains derailed within 24 hours of each other on Britain's railways this month, during the fifth heatwave of the summer — an unremarkable-sounding statistic that Network Rail engineers describe as "exceptional." It is the kind of exceptional that forecasters now expect to become routine. The Met Office says an El Niño event now building in the Pacific is on track to be the strongest in more than a century, with sea-surface temperatures projected to peak more than 3°C above normal — well above the 1-2°C of a typical event.

"We're expecting the biggest El Niño for over a century," said Prof Adam Scaife, the Met Office's head of long-range forecasting. For the UK, the winter signature that follows is a familiar one: milder air and wetter, stormier weather. The Met Office's November-to-January outlook puts the probability of above-average temperatures at 55-65%, strongest in the south-east, and gives a 60% chance of an above-average storm season — ten or more named storms making landfall.

A budget line that keeps growing

For Network Rail, the state-owned infrastructure manager, weather resilience has quietly become one of the fastest-growing items in its capital programme rather than an occasional emergency cost. In its current five-year funding round, Control Period 7, running to 2029, the company is directing roughly £2.8bn toward drainage and earthworks — reinforcing more than 480km of embankments and cuttings, laying over 600,000 metres of new drainage, and recruiting some 400 specialist engineers. That figure represents roughly five times the equivalent allocation set five years earlier, a jump that followed a taskforce review, led by geotechnical engineer Lord Robert Mair, commissioned after the fatal Stonehaven derailment in 2020.

The scale of that reallocation illustrates the underlying problem: infrastructure sized for one climate is being asked to perform in another. Britain's railway was engineered for a temperature band of roughly -10°C to 20°C, said Xueyu Geng, professor of ground engineering at the University of Warwick — a tolerance dwarfed by systems in Spain (0°C to 45°C) or Saudi Arabia (10°C to 55°C), where infrastructure was designed with far wider swings in mind from the outset. "I believe Network Rail has done a great job to maintain our railway network, but we still need to be able to adapt more in order to cope with this changing climate," Geng said.

The cautionary precedent is Dawlish. When a storm surge breached the sea wall at the Devon coastal town in February 2014, tearing away 80 metres of track, it took eight weeks and roughly £35m to reopen the line — the only rail connection into the South West — with the closure estimated to have cost the regional economy up to £1.2bn. Network Rail has since spent £165m reinforcing that stretch of coastline alone, a single-location bill that underscores how expensive reactive repair is next to planned adaptation.

That arithmetic is now the operative question for this winter: whether drainage clearance, culvert inspections and earthwork monitoring, the maintenance measures Network Rail is racing to complete before the wetter season arrives, prove cheaper than another storm-forced closure. The answer will show up first in delay-minute data and infrastructure-fault reports once the named storms start arriving, and later in whether Network Rail's regulator, the Office of Rail and Road, judges the current funding settlement adequate when Control Period 8 planning begins.
