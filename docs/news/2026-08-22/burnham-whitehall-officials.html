Burnham to dispatch 100 Whitehall officials to bolster regional mayors' offices

Andy Burnham, pictured in his official portrait taken at 10 Downing Street after becoming Prime Minister in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham will send 100+ Whitehall staff into regional mayors' offices from autumn, but critics say the money — and control — still sits in London.

There is a particular irony in Andy Burnham running Whitehall now. For a decade he was the mayor who needled London for hoarding power over Manchester's buses, its housing, its growth funding. On Friday, a month into his premiership, he sat in his northern outpost and announced that Whitehall would start sending its people north — not the other way round.

The plan: more than 100 civil servants seconded from central government departments into the offices of regional mayors, five to 10 apiece, across a pilot covering 18 mayoralties from the autumn. Secondments run six to nine months, and mayors get to request the skills they need — a planner here, a finance specialist there. The government has billed it as the "largest transfer of Whitehall capacity to local leaders yet."

"The days of Westminster hoarding power are over." — Louise Haigh, First Secretary of State

Haigh's framing — Whitehall giving up ground rather than reasserting it — is the government's line, and it fits the broader project. Burnham has already relocated a slice of the Prime Minister's operation itself to Manchester, branding it No10 North, working out of Heron House while a permanent campus is built on Great Ancoats Street. He calls it "the nerve centre of a rewired Britain." Chancellor John Healey has called growth a "dual mission between No 11 and No 10 North."

Whose officials are they, really

The secondment model cuts the other way too. The 100 officials being placed inside mayoral offices remain Whitehall employees, on loans of under a year, reporting up a Whitehall chain even as they sit at a mayor's table. Critics inside and outside the civil service reform world have been blunt about what that tends to produce. Writing in City AM, one commentator argued that since devolution began in 2014, Whitehall's own headcount has grown by almost 120,000 — the pattern being that the centre doesn't shrink when it hands out responsibility, it "metamorphoses into a regulatory state," trading direct control for outcomes frameworks, program boards and reporting lines that still run through London.

IPPR North's Mirte Boot put the more sympathetic version of the same doubt more gently: the plan "establishes the plumbing, but the water isn't flowing yet" — meaning the money hasn't moved, only the staff have. That matters because roughly 95% of UK tax revenue still flows to the Treasury, the highest centralisation ratio in the G7. Scotland's John Swinney dismissed the announcement as rhetoric without fiscal substance; Wales's Rhun ap Iorwerth warned it risked becoming "Whitehall with a different postcode."

Burnham's team says the real test comes in October, when the Budget is expected to set out how much local income tax revenue mayors get to keep and control — the piece of devolution that would actually change who decides how the money is spent, rather than who sits in the room while it's decided. Until then, 100 secondees moving north look less like a transfer of power than a transfer of personnel, with the ledger still kept in London.
