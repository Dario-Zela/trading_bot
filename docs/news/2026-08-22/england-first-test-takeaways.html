England's first Test takeaways as 'Fab Four' era faces new challengers

Joe Root, restored as England's Test captain following Ben Stokes's retirement, led the side to an innings victory over Pakistan at Headingley. — Ben Sutherland / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
England crushed Pakistan by an innings and 103 runs in Joe Root's first Test back as captain, but the real test of England's new-look batting order starts at Lord's.

England did not so much win the first Test against Pakistan as demolish it. An innings and 103 runs, wrapped up before tea on day three at Headingley, is the kind of scoreline that flatters nobody's uncertainty — and after a torrid few months for English cricket, uncertainty was exactly what hung over this match before a ball was bowled.

The game arrived as the first assignment of a rebuilt England: Joe Root back in the captaincy eight weeks after Ben Stokes's shock retirement from international cricket mid-way through a losing series against New Zealand, Brendon McCullum gone from the red-ball coaching post, and Kiwi veteran Stephen Fleming installed alongside him. If that sounds like a lot of change to absorb before Pakistan even turned up, it was. Root's response was to skittle the tourists for 171, pile up 409 in reply, and bowl them out again for 135 — the kind of statement that buys a new regime some breathing room.

A captaincy return built on substance, not nostalgia

Root led England 64 times between 2017 and 2022 with a reputation, fairly or not, for over-thinking the job. This time the tone was different. Ollie Robinson and Josh Tongue took five-wicket hauls in the same innings — the first England pace pair to do it against Pakistan since 1998 — with Robinson removing the ninth wicket and Tongue the tenth on consecutive deliveries, a first in Test history. Root left out a frontline spinner at Headingley, backed four seamers instead, and it worked before lunch on day one.

"Judge me not on how I was last time, but how I am now," Root told Michael Atherton afterwards. "It was never trying to be Ben Stokes. It was just Joe Root being Joe." Former England captain Nasser Hussain, watching from the commentary box, called it composure across "decision-making, batting and field management" — a pointed contrast with a player who, by his own admission, used to find the captaincy a weight rather than a tool.

The personal milestones piled up alongside the team performance. Root's fielding effort to remove Pakistan opener Azan Awais took him to 219 Test catches, moving him past Steve Smith's 218 to become the outright leading catcher, wicketkeepers included, in the format's history. He also became the first captain to pass 3,000 runs in the World Test Championship era. At 35 and 166 Tests into a career now closing in on Sachin Tendulkar's all-time run tally, Root remains the last man standing from cricket's celebrated "Fab Four."

The Fab Four's long goodbye

That quartet — Root, Virat Kohli, Steve Smith and Kane Williamson — was coined by Martin Crowe more than a decade ago to describe the batters who would define an era. The era, on current evidence, is ending in instalments. Kohli has retired from Tests and T20Is. Williamson has stepped away from international cricket entirely. Smith no longer plays ODIs and, at 37, is into the twilight of his Test career. Root, still scoring and now captaining again, is the exception rather than the rule — which is precisely why his second stint carries more scrutiny than his first. He is no longer simply England's best batter; he is the last of a generation still auditioning as its own successor.

The candidates to inherit the mantle are increasingly named in the same breath: India's Shubman Gill and Yashasvi Jaiswal, New Zealand's Rachin Ravindra, and England's own Harry Brook, who made 91 at Headingley and was dismissed — like three other England batters — by Pakistan debutant left-armer Ali Usman, who claimed five wickets on his first day in Test cricket. Kane Williamson himself has floated Brook's name as a natural heir. Root, for his part, has called Brook "far and away the best batter in the world" on current form. Jordan Cox, uncapped before this year, made 73 in a 150-run stand with his captain and looks like the next name England will build an order around.

What Headingley actually proved, and what it didn't

The caveat, and it is a significant one, is Pakistan. A touring side that has been in near-permanent turmoil off the field found four of its top six dismissed for single figures across the two innings, and only Mohammad Rizwan (41) and opener Shafique (61 in the first innings) offered sustained resistance. An innings win over an out-of-sorts Pakistan attack tells England less about its depth than a contest with India or Australia would. The batting collapse that undid the previous regime against New Zealand two months ago has not been retested here.

What Headingley does confirm is personnel: Cox and Emilio Gay pushing for permanent middle-order and opening slots, Brook established as vice-captain and lead run-scorer in waiting, and a four-man pace attack — Robinson, Tongue, Jofra Archer and Gus Atkinson — that looks capable of bowling out most sides twice inside three days on a helpful surface. Whether that holds on flatter pitches, against sharper batting orders, is the question this series was designed to start answering, not finish.

The second Test begins Thursday at Lord's, with Pakistan hoping to have captain Babar Azam back from a finger injury — a return that would test England's bowling attack against opposition rather better equipped to survive it. That, more than anything at Headingley, will show whether this is a genuine changing of the guard or simply one bad Pakistan performance flattering a team still finding its post-Stokes shape.
