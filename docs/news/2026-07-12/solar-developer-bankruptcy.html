Renewable energy developer collapses into bankruptcy months after signing new deals

Solar photovoltaic panels at a utility-scale solar farm, the kind of asset at the center of the GoldenPeaks collapse. — Kevin Kiefuik / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
GoldenPeaks Capital signed a landmark solar deal with Hankook Tire in February; by May it had filed Chapter 11 with under €1.1 million in cash against $952 million in debt.

In February, GoldenPeaks Capital was closing one of the largest corporate solar deals in Hungary's history — a ten-year agreement to supply Hankook Tire's Rácalmás plant with 430 gigawatt-hours of power from 30 megawatts of new photovoltaic capacity, enough to cover a fifth of the tire maker's electricity use there. It followed a run of similar contracts with Nestlé, Cargill, Mars, Mondelez and Auchan, the kind of blue-chip roster that lets a renewable developer claim it has arrived. GoldenPeaks, by then one of the largest solar owner-operators in Poland and Hungary with a construction pipeline exceeding a gigawatt, looked like a company built for the corporate clean-power boom.

Five months later it was in Chapter 11.

A liquidity crisis measured in weeks, not years

GoldenPeaks Poland Holding, the Malta-domiciled parent of the group's Polish and Hungarian solar assets, filed for bankruptcy protection on May 29 in the US Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of Texas, along with 39 affiliated entities. The filing listed roughly $952 million in funded debt against a cash position of less than €1.1 million — a company that had, at its peak, run the largest utility-scale solar portfolio in Poland and employed more than 250 people, reduced to unable to meet payroll.

Edward Manning of Alvarez & Marsal, the restructuring adviser brought in to manage the collapse, told the court the deterioration was "precipitous": liquidity "evaporated" within weeks, a payroll cycle was missed, staff walked out, and events of default cascaded across more than twenty separate financing facilities almost simultaneously. Court filings describe a company with no direct employees of its own by the time of filing, running entirely on three outside contractors — Alvarez & Marsal for restructuring, Ergy for asset management, and PwC for back-office functions — a structure that left GoldenPeaks with strikingly little internal capacity to manage a crisis once one began.

The immediate trigger was the January collapse of Spectris Energy, the contractor that handled essentially all of GoldenPeaks' operations, maintenance, engineering and construction work. Spectris entered its own remedial insolvency proceedings after component costs and currency swings made its contracts unworkable, and Polish tax authorities froze its bank accounts — disrupting both the solar farms already running and the ones still being built. "GoldenPeaks had nobody left to run its solar farms," as one account of the case put it. Layer on top of that: curtailment orders from Poland's grid operator limiting how much power the company's plants could actually sell, construction and financing costs that ran ahead of projections through 2025 and 2026, a paused equity raise in the first quarter of this year for lack of investor interest, and — according to filings — internal accounting so disordered that standalone financial statements hadn't been produced and ledgers were unclosed since December.

The Brookfield fight

What has made the case unusually contentious is the role of Brookfield Asset Management, GoldenPeaks' controlling shareholder since a roughly €8.5 million investment years earlier that grew into a roughly $294 million junior secured lending position. Brookfield holds two of five board seats, extended a $12 million emergency bridge loan in early May as the crisis peaked, and has now proposed a $162.8 million debtor-in-possession loan to fund the four-month restructuring — while simultaneously positioning itself as the stalking-horse lead bidder for GoldenPeaks' assets in the court-supervised auction that loan is meant to finance.

That dual role drew a sharp objection from Berenberg, the German merchant bank whose Alternative Assets Fund II sits as a mezzanine lender structurally senior to some of Brookfield's positions. Berenberg's lawyers accused Brookfield of trying to "jump ahead of structurally senior creditors" and of "secretly establishing US jurisdiction" over the case by routing a European asset group through a Texas courtroom, and flagged the DIP loan's terms — cross-collateralization without carve-outs, no adequate protection for existing lenders — as tilting the eventual sale in Brookfield's favor before other bidders got a look. US Bankruptcy Judge Alfredo Perez rejected the challenge on July 6, approving Brookfield as both DIP lender and lead bidder and clearing the way for a $114.8 million debt-for-assets bid to anchor the auction. Berenberg has reserved its right to contest jurisdiction and venue as the case proceeds.

What it says about corporate clean-power deals

The uncomfortable detail in the GoldenPeaks case is not that a leveraged developer ran into trouble — that happens. It's the compression of the timeline. A company signing new long-term supply contracts with a Fortune Global 500 tire manufacturer in February was insolvent by May. Corporate power purchase agreements have become the preferred vehicle for companies like Nestlé, Cargill, Mars and Hankook to hit their own climate targets without owning generation assets themselves: the buyer locks in a price, the developer takes on the construction and operating risk, and everyone gets a line for the sustainability report. GoldenPeaks' PPA counterparties are not directly on the hook for its debt, and the underlying solar assets — panels already spinning revenue under contract — are exactly what Brookfield is bidding to keep running. But the speed of the collapse is a reminder that a signed PPA is a claim on a counterparty's future solvency, not just its megawatts, and that the credit quality behind these deals doesn't always get the scrutiny that the headline contract announcement does.

For Brookfield, the case is a small entry in a much larger book: the firm has raised over $40 billion for decarbonization investing since 2021 and already holds a stake in Poland's Polenergia. For GoldenPeaks' other lenders and its corporate offtakers, the next milestone is more immediate. The court-supervised auction Brookfield is now positioned to win is expected to run through the four-month DIP financing window, meaning a sale — or a further fight over who controls the assets — should land by late autumn.
