EasyJet takeover battle pulls private equity out of its comfort zone

An EasyJet Airbus A319 takes off from Amsterdam Schiphol — the airline is now the target of a rare private equity bidding war. — Alf van Beem / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Two U.S. private equity firms are locked in a bidding war for loss-making EasyJet — a sign capital has nowhere better to go, not that airlines got safer.

EasyJet has spent two decades being the airline private equity firms politely avoided. Thin margins, brutal fuel exposure, a habit of losing money whenever the world does something unexpected — none of it fit the buyout playbook. Over the past twenty years, PE firms globally have announced only about $8 billion in airline deals, roughly 2% of all transport and logistics spending, according to Bloomberg Intelligence data. That history is why the fight now unfolding over EasyJet is being read on trading floors less as an airline story than as a private equity story.

EasyJet said on Friday it had agreed in principle to a cash offer from Apollo Global Management worth £5.7 billion, or £7.15 a share — trumping a £6.90-a-share bid from fellow U.S. fund Castlelake that the airline's board had accepted just days earlier, on July 7. Apollo's offer is an 81% premium to the £3.94 EasyJet shares fetched on May 28, the last trading day before Castlelake's interest became public. The stock jumped 13% on the news. Apollo now has until August 7 under U.K. takeover rules to make a firm offer or walk away.

Why airlines, why now

The honest answer has less to do with EasyJet than with the roughly $2.5 trillion in private equity dry powder sitting uninvested worldwide, about $1 trillion of it in the U.S. Buyout funds typically have a five-year window to deploy committed capital, and the clock is running out on money raised in the fundraising boom of the early 2020s: 24% of global buyout dry powder has now sat unspent for four years or more, up from 20% in 2022, according to industry data. Limited partners want distributions, not idle commitments, and the inventory of conventional buyout targets — mid-market industrials, software roll-ups, healthcare services — has been picked over.

"Nearly every decade seems to bring an unexpected shock of some kind," Paolo Battaglia of Lazard said of the airline sector. "That makes underwriting airline risk difficult for buyout firms." That risk hasn't gone away — EasyJet's pre-tax loss widened to £552 million in the first half of 2026, with the airline citing higher fuel costs and weaker forward bookings tied to the Middle East conflict. What has changed is the tolerance for it, because the alternative is capital that earns nothing at all.

Apollo brings a track record here that Castlelake, an aviation-leasing specialist better known for financing aircraft than running them, does not: it has restructured distressed carriers before, steering Aeromexico back to profitability by 2023 and doing similar work at Sun Country, largely by stripping out premium features and running a leaner cost base. That playbook — treat an airline like any other overleveraged industrial asset, cut hard, sell ancillary revenue aggressively — is presumably the pitch Apollo is making to EasyJet's board about why this time is different.

The EU complication

Both bidders face the same regulatory obstacle: EU law requires airlines flying within the bloc to be majority-owned and effectively controlled by EU member states or qualifying European nationals, a rule that predates Brexit but now applies to EasyJet's continental operations in a way that bites harder for two American funds. Castlelake had proposed satisfying it by installing two Irish aviation executives — Peter Bellew, a former EasyJet chief operating officer, and Mark Breen, chief executive of Dublin-based Oneiros Aerospace — as controlling shareholders through an EU-based vehicle. Apollo has said only that it will take "all necessary steps" to secure merger clearance and satisfy the EU's Foreign Subsidies Regulation, without yet detailing how it clears the ownership bar.

Bloomberg Intelligence's Conroy Gaynor noted that EasyJet "has generally traded at a significant discount since travel fully resumed after the pandemic" — the kind of valuation gap that makes a takeover premium easier to justify on paper, whatever the underlying business is doing. Jefferies' Sheila Kahyaoglu offered the more optimistic frame: European budget carriers are, on their own operating merits, a segment that "tend[s] to do really well."

What it signals

A bidding war for a loss-making, low-margin airline is not, by itself, evidence that airlines have become safer investments. It is better read as evidence that the buyout industry's usual hunting grounds are thinning out, and that firms sitting on aging, unspent capital are being pushed toward assets they would once have passed on. If Apollo can prove out its restructuring thesis on EasyJet the way it did at Aeromexico, the deal recasts the sector as investable. If not, it becomes the latest reminder — after SAS, after Alitalia, after a long list of carriers that burned outside capital — of why private equity avoided airlines in the first place.

The near-term marker is August 7, Apollo's deadline to convert its indicative offer into a firm one under U.K. takeover rules. Watch also for whether Castlelake returns with a sixth revised bid, and for the first concrete detail from Apollo on how it intends to satisfy the EU's ownership-and-control test — the one obstacle that neither fund's balance sheet can simply outbid.
