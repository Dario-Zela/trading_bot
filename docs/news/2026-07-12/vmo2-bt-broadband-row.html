Virgin Media O2 accuses BT of 'choking off' broadband competition

An Openreach engineer works on the telecoms infrastructure that still underpins most of Britain's broadband connections. — Alcook101 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Virgin Media O2 has accused BT of using rival-discount schemes to "choke off" broadband competition just as it builds its own wholesale challenger network.

Britain's second-largest broadband company has picked a public fight with the operator of the network most of the country still depends on. Virgin Media O2 has told the regulator Ofcom that BT's infrastructure arm, Openreach, is trying to "choke off" broadband competition — not through outright refusal, but through a wave of discounts aimed at keeping rival internet service providers loyal to Openreach's pipes.

The discounts in question are aimed at companies such as Vodafone, Sky and TalkTalk, which buy wholesale access to broadband networks and resell it to households under their own brands. Openreach wants those providers to keep routing new full-fibre customers through its network rather than switching to Virgin Media O2's, which is trying to establish itself as a genuine wholesale alternative for the first time. In a submission to Ofcom, which must approve the pricing plan before it can take effect, Virgin Media O2 argues the offer is a calculated move to lock in customers before a rival network can get off the ground.

A rebate war over rival loyalty

The scheme is an extension of Openreach's existing "Equinox" wholesale pricing programme, which already discounts full-fibre rental for providers who commit most of their new orders to Openreach rather than legacy copper lines or competing fibre builders. The newest addition, due to run from October 2026 for six months, offers a £50 connection rebate to internet providers that sign up incremental new-to-Openreach fibre customers specifically inside Virgin Media O2's own coverage areas — but only to providers already placing at least 1,000 fibre orders a month nationwide, a threshold that effectively limits the offer to the largest resellers.

Virgin Media O2's chief executive, Lutz Schüler, put the complaint in blunter terms than a regulatory filing usually allows. "For BT, it seems Ofcom's conclusions were heard as a dinner bell to start feasting on fibre challengers," he said, calling the discounts "a prime example of a dominant force flexing its muscles to squeeze out emerging competition." Openreach's chief executive, Katie Milligan, rejected the framing entirely: "Ofcom has said that Openreach should be allowed to compete, and we agree," she said, describing the targeted rebates as an effort to "test the waters" in areas where a rival network already exists — precisely the areas Virgin Media O2 needs to win business to make its wholesale ambitions pay off.

Building a challenger while fending one off

The timing explains the sharpness of the complaint. Virgin Media O2 is midway through a £2 billion acquisition of altnet operator Netomnia, engineered through nexfibre — its joint venture with parent companies Liberty Global and Telefónica — that would knit together roughly 8 million premises of full-fibre coverage by the end of 2027. The pitch to regulators and investors has been consistent: this is meant to be the first wholesale network with the scale to genuinely challenge Openreach's dominance, rather than another niche builder Openreach can wait out.

That deal is itself under regulatory strain. The Competition and Markets Authority referred the Netomnia acquisition for an in-depth Phase 2 investigation in early July, fast-tracked at the parties' own request, citing roughly 80% overlap between the two companies' networks. A verdict is due by 15 December under the CMA's statutory deadline. Virgin Media O2 is therefore arguing on two regulatory fronts at once — trying to persuade the CMA that combining with Netomnia will sharpen competition rather than dull it, while telling Ofcom that Openreach is exploiting the exact period of vulnerability created by that unresolved merger review.

What Ofcom decides next

Ofcom's position is that Openreach earned more commercial freedom through its own wholesale market review concluded earlier this year, which set the current rules for how the dominant network operator can compete on price. A spokesman said the regulator is now assessing whether the specific discounts raise competition concerns serious enough to warrant intervention, with a consultation due for publication later this month.

The UK broadband market has roughly 100 alternative network builders jostling for a shrinking pool of undecided customers, and consolidation among them has accelerated as smaller altnets run short of capital. Openreach retains by far the largest network footprint and, crucially, the deepest relationships with the resellers who decide where new customers actually get connected. Whether Ofcom treats Openreach's rebates as legitimate competitive response or anti-competitive foreclosure will shape how much room is left for challengers like Virgin Media O2 to build scale — and that answer, alongside the CMA's Netomnia ruling in December, will do more to determine the shape of Britain's fibre market than any discount headline on its own.
