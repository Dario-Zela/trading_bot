Ukraine creates long-range strike command to hit Russian energy infrastructure

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, who signed the decree creating the new long-range strike command. — President.gov.ua / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Zelenskyy has folded Ukraine's deep-strike drone and naval-drone units into a single new command as Kyiv's campaign already has Russian refining capacity and Sea of Azov shipping under sustained strain.

Volodymyr Zelenskyy signed a decree this week creating a dedicated "long-range impact" command inside Ukraine's armed forces — a bureaucratic-sounding move with a blunt purpose. It consolidates the drone units, planning staff and targeting intelligence behind more than a year of deep strikes into a single chain of command, one built to keep hitting the refineries, pipelines and depots that fund Russia's war.

"Today, I signed a decree establishing a special command within the Armed Forces," Zelenskyy said in his evening address, "a command aimed at a long-range and, in effect, global impact on Russia in response to this war." Ukrainian officials have described the new structure's mandate in blunter terms still: to put 100% of available resources behind reducing Russia's capacity to sustain the fighting.

A war economy already under strain

What makes the announcement more than symbolic is the campaign it now formalizes. Ukrainian long-range drones — chiefly the Fire Point-built FP-1, a one-way attack aircraft with a stated range of 1,600 kilometers and a warhead of up to 120 kilograms — have hit more than a dozen major Russian refineries and fuel terminals since the start of the year, some more than 2,500 kilometers from the border. On July 6, drones reportedly struck the Gazprom Neft refinery at Omsk, Russia's largest, disabling equipment responsible for as much as 40% of the plant's output. Nizhnekamsk and Saratov were hit within the following 48 hours.

The cumulative effect shows up in Russian fuel markets rather than any single dramatic strike. Independent estimates suggest the attacks have knocked out somewhere between a fifth and two-fifths of Russian refining capacity, leaving domestic gasoline output running roughly 20% below demand. Moscow has already banned exports of petrol and jet fuel and is weighing the same for diesel — a step it took in part because of a lingering fuel shortage in occupied Crimea, where rationing at filling stations has become routine. The International Energy Agency has trimmed its forecast for Russian oil output by 85,000 barrels a day this year and 150,000 next.

The sea campaign nobody predicted

The newer front is maritime. Robert Brovdi — known by the callsign "Madyar," and commander of Ukraine's Unmanned Systems Forces — has reported a rapid escalation of strikes on the so-called shadow fleet of aging tankers Russia uses to move sanctioned oil. In a single 72-hour stretch this month, Ukrainian forces claimed to have hit 21 vessels in the Sea of Azov, most of them tankers, alongside tugboats and cargo ships supporting Crimea's supply lines. Russia has responded by suspending navigation through the Don–Azov canal and telling shipping firms it would stop processing passage requests through the Kerch Strait — a disruption that has also snarled grain exports from southern Russia, a secondary effect Kyiv has not been shy about advertising.

Taken together, the refinery strikes and the tanker campaign are two expressions of the same theory: that Ukraine, unable to match Russia's manpower or match it missile-for-missile, can still bleed the revenue and logistics that keep the war funded. Officials in Kyiv have taken to calling it a form of "long-range sanctions," a phrase meant to underline that the target is Russia's war chest rather than its front lines.

What the new command changes, and what it doesn't

It is worth being precise about what this week's decree actually does. It does not introduce a new weapon or a new capability — the FP-1 and its naval-drone counterparts already existed, and Brovdi's forces were already running the tanker strikes. What changes is organizational: authority over long-range operations moves under one roof, with a mandate that outranks competing claims on drones, intelligence and production capacity. Military analysts caution that it remains unclear whether even a more efficient campaign can alter the war's trajectory on its own — Russia continues to launch missile and drone barrages of its own, and Ukraine's air defenses remain stretched.

For markets, the relevant question is less about a single battlefield decision and more about durability. A one-off refinery fire is noise; sustained double-digit reductions in Russian refining capacity, an export ban that spreads from diesel to crude products, and a shadow fleet that operators increasingly regard as uninsurable are the kind of frictions that show up in global diesel and gasoil spreads even without moving Brent much on their own. The IEA's downward revisions to Russian output are an early signal of that shift, small in absolute terms but pointed in direction.

The next things to watch are concrete rather than rhetorical: whether Moscow extends its export ban to diesel, whether the Kerch Strait disruption starts showing up in Black Sea grain-shipment data, and whether the new command's first weeks bring a change in strike tempo or simply a change in the org chart signing off on it.
