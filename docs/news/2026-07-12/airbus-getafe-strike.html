Airbus deliveries slip as Getafe strike drags on

Airbus's engineering and manufacturing plant in Getafe, near Madrid, where a strike began July 1. — Wikiberto / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A month-long strike at Airbus's Getafe plant is slowing inspections feeding aircraft deliveries, adding fresh risk to a 2026 guidance number analysts already doubted.

Airbus's problem this month is not demand. It is getting airplanes out the door. At the company's Getafe engineering and manufacturing site outside Madrid, roughly 3,000 of the plant's 9,000 workers have been on strike since July 1, and the walkout has begun to snarl the aircraft inspections and engineering checks that sit between a finished airframe and a delivered one — the paperwork-and-signoff stage that airlines and lessors will not skip, strike or no strike.

The action was called by SIPA, the second-largest union at Airbus's Spanish operations, and has since drawn in UGT, the country's national trade union confederation. Getafe is Airbus's third-largest site worldwide and handles horizontal stabilisers and rear-fuselage sections for the A320, A330 and A350 families, along with military and space engineering work — not glamorous, but structurally load-bearing in the literal and financial sense. Participation across Airbus's roughly 14,000 employees in Spain has reportedly reached 80% on strike days, according to Spanish outlet Getafe Central, spreading beyond Getafe to Airbus Defence and Space and Airbus Helicopters España sites in the same municipality.

What the strikers want

The grievances are the familiar shape of a labor dispute at a company posting record profits — Airbus reported €5.22 billion in net income for 2025 — while asking staff to absorb tighter terms. Airbus set 2026 and 2027 pay increases unilaterally at 3% and 2% after talks stalled, figures workers say lag inflation. From September, office staff will be required in four days a week rather than three, cutting a day of remote work. The company has also moved to end the 100% salary top-up during short-term sick leave, introduced a stricter absenteeism-tracking method known as the Bradford Index, and fixed three weeks of August vacation that were previously negotiated with more flexibility. None of these are the kind of number that shows up in a 10-K. All of them are the kind of number that shows up in a picket line.

SIPA has called the action through July 31. More consequential for anyone modeling the rest of the year: Comisiones Obreras, or CCOO, the largest union at Airbus's Spanish plants, sat out this round but has said it will call an indefinite strike from September 7 if talks fail, warning that deliveries would be "seriously affected" across all Spanish plants in the last four months of 2026. That is the scenario worth tracking, since a month-long July strike and an open-ended one starting in the autumn are different orders of risk entirely.

A guidance number already under strain

The timing lands badly. Airbus has told investors to expect around 870 commercial aircraft deliveries in 2026, a target that was already sitting on shaky ground before a single Getafe worker walked out. The company delivered at a run rate of roughly 71 aircraft a month in the first half of the second quarter, according to analysis cited by AskTraders, but that pace had slipped to about 42 a month by mid-May — well short of what's needed. Hitting 870 for the year now requires roughly 97 deliveries a month for the remainder of 2026, a pace mwb Research analyst Jens-Peter Rieck called "the strongest second half in company history."

Rieck has gone further, describing Airbus as caught in a "credibility squeeze": the company is maintaining its 870-unit guidance in public while simultaneously freezing 10% of non-industrial spending internally — a combination he called difficult to reconcile, and evidence that Airbus's existing LEAD cost-reduction program, launched in 2024, has "proven insufficient." His house view is that a formal cut toward roughly 840 deliveries is "a matter of when, not if." That call predates the Getafe strike; the walkout adds a fresh, difficult-to-quantify drag on top of an already-strained production system, one already complicated separately by Pratt & Whitney's reluctance to commit firmly to engine delivery numbers for the year, per comments from chief executive Guillaume Faury in February.

Why a stabiliser plant matters to a delivery count

The mechanics matter here. Getafe does not build complete aircraft — final assembly happens in Toulouse, Hamburg, Mobile and Tianjin — so the strike will not directly halt an assembly line the way a walkout at those sites would. But horizontal stabilisers and fuselage sections built at Getafe feed those lines, and the engineering sign-offs performed there are gating steps for aircraft further along in the process. Airbus has reportedly already begun shifting some engineering work to other subsidiaries to keep programs moving, according to Spanish outlet El Independiente — a workaround that buys time but does not replace lost capacity for free. Delivery slippage of this kind rarely shows up as a single dramatic cancellation; it shows up as aircraft sitting a few weeks longer in pre-delivery configuration, revenue recognition pushed a quarter later than modeled, and a delivery count that quietly misses guidance by enough to move a rating.

For a company whose backlog stretches past nine years of production at current rates — 9,247 aircraft as of the end of May, split heavily toward the A320neo family — the strike is not an existential threat to the order book. It is a test of execution at exactly the moment execution was already the thing analysts were worried about.

What comes next

The SIPA-led strike runs through the end of July, after which the more consequential date is September 7: the deadline CCOO has set before calling an indefinite strike across all Spanish plants. Airbus's next scheduled update on production and delivery pace will show whether the run rate has recovered toward the roughly 97-a-month pace needed to hit 870 for the year, or whether Rieck's predicted guidance cut toward 840 arrives first. Either way, the Getafe dispute has turned a spreadsheet debate about delivery cadence into a labor negotiation with a hard deadline attached.
