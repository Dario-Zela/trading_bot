C3.ai's revenue slide collides with Tom Siebel's return and a bigger Shell deal

Thomas Siebel, founder and CEO of C3.ai, pictured in 2013 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
C3.ai's revenue fell 35% and losses widened even as founder Tom Siebel returned as CEO and Shell expanded its AI contract.

Tom Siebel has run C3.ai twice now, and both times he came back to a company whose numbers didn't match its narrative. The first time, in the late 1990s echo of enterprise software, the story was growth. This time, the enterprise AI vendor he founded in 2009 just posted a 35% revenue decline for the fiscal year, a widening loss, and a leadership saga that ended with the 73-year-old founder reclaiming the chief executive's chair he'd handed off nine months earlier. Three weeks after that, Shell — one of C3.ai's oldest and largest customers — signed up for more.

The two facts sit awkwardly together. A company whose revenue is falling by more than a third is not, on its face, a company energy majors should be expanding their bets on. Yet that is exactly what happened, and the contradiction is the story: nobody, including the analysts paid to model this stuff, seems to agree on whether C3.ai's enterprise AI software actually works well enough to justify what customers and investors are being asked to pay for it.

A revolving door in the corner office

Siebel stepped back from the CEO role in mid-2025 after disclosing an autoimmune condition that had damaged his eyesight. Stephen Ehikian, a Salesforce veteran and former acting administrator of the U.S. General Services Administration, took over as CEO on September 1, 2025, while Siebel stayed on as executive chairman. That arrangement lasted eight months. On May 8, 2026, C3.ai announced Siebel was resuming the CEO title, with Ehikian shifting to president.

"While my vision remains impaired, it is improving," Siebel said in the announcement. "The health issues I encountered during 2025 are largely resolved." He called himself "energized to be back in the CEO role."

The reinstatement came bundled with a signal investors don't often get: Siebel personally bought 6.17 million newly issued shares directly from the company at $11.16 apiece, putting roughly $69 million in fresh cash on C3.ai's balance sheet and lifting total cash and marketable securities to about $673 million. It's a notable reversal — earlier in 2026, while serving as executive chairman rather than CEO, Siebel had sold several million dollars of stock, including a $7.6 million sale in January and a $4.4 million sale in March. Insiders selling into weakness and then buying back in at a founder's return is not unheard of, but it does little to settle the question of what Siebel actually believes about the stock's near-term prospects versus its long-term one.

The numbers Siebel came back to

Whatever confidence the share purchase was meant to convey, the fiscal year he inherited was rough by any measure. C3.ai's total revenue for the fiscal year ended April 30, 2026 came to $250.3 million, down 35% from the prior year. The fourth quarter alone told a starker story: $51.6 million in revenue, down from $108.7 million in the same quarter a year earlier — a decline of more than half. GAAP loss from operations for the full year widened to $498.5 million; on a non-GAAP basis, the operating loss was $217.8 million.

C3.ai has responded with a restructuring plan the company says will generate about $135 million in annualized non-GAAP cost savings and cut cash burn by a similar amount. That's a meaningful retrenchment for a company whose pitch to Wall Street has for years rested on enterprise AI as a growth category, not a cost-cutting one. Investors, for their part, were unmoved by the leadership change: C3.ai shares fell roughly 7% on the news of Siebel's return, part of a stock that was already down around a fifth for the year.

What Shell sees that Wall Street doesn't

Against that backdrop, Shell's decision to deepen its relationship with C3.ai reads almost like a counter-argument. The two companies have worked together since 2018, when Shell adopted C3 AI's predictive-maintenance software to monitor more than 13,000 pieces of equipment across upstream, midstream and downstream operations on six continents — refineries, offshore platforms, pipelines. Shell renewed the partnership for five years in 2021. On June 4, 2026, the companies announced a new multi-year expansion: Shell will layer AI agent-based root-cause analysis and remediation, built on C3.ai's Agentic AI Platform and running on Microsoft Azure, on top of the existing reliability monitoring.

Shell is not a customer prone to sentimentality about vendor relationships; oil majors run tight procurement processes and can walk away from underperforming software with little friction. That it chose to expand — after seven years of production use across a genuinely large industrial footprint — is real evidence that C3.ai's product works for at least one of the hardest use cases in enterprise software: physical asset reliability at global scale. It just isn't evidence that the product is selling well enough, broadly enough, to keep the company's revenue line moving in the right direction.

The valuation question nobody has answered

Wall Street's read on all this is, fittingly, split down the middle. The consensus analyst rating on C3.ai is a Hold, with price targets scattered from roughly $11 to $16 a share. A price-to-sales comparison against peers suggests the stock is still priced for a growth rate it isn't delivering, and one widely cited discounted-cash-flow estimate puts it as much as 35% overvalued. Gross margins remain below 40%, well short of what a mature enterprise software business typically carries.

That gap — between a marquee customer doubling down and a market that can't decide what the stock is worth — is the real takeaway. Enterprise AI vendors have spent three years selling Fortune 500 boards on the idea that predictive models and AI agents pay for themselves in avoided downtime and labor savings. Shell's expanded contract is a data point that the technology can deliver on that promise in at least one demanding setting. C3.ai's own income statement is a data point that turning technical wins into a durable, profitable business is a separate and much harder problem. The next test comes with C3.ai's first-quarter fiscal 2027 results, expected in early September, when investors will find out whether the restructuring and the founder's return have done anything to slow the revenue slide — or whether Shell's vote of confidence remains an outlier rather than a signal.
