Labour MPs speak out against Rosebank as a decision looms

In one sentence
Labour backbench opposition to the Rosebank oil field is growing, but no decision date is set, and the reported size of the revolt is unverified.

The government has not yet decided whether to approve Rosebank, Britain's largest undeveloped oil field. A group of Labour backbenchers is now lobbying against it in public. The field lies about 80 miles north-west of Shetland. A second consent would be tested against the Labour government's climate pledges, and against a court ruling that already struck down the first.

The Labour revolt is real, but its size is contested. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, said in the Commons that 50 Labour MPs oppose approval, led by the Energy Secretary. That is her claim, not a verified count. Reporting on a draft letter to the prime minister, Andy Burnham, says more than 60 of roughly 400 Labour MPs have indicated support for it. At the time of reporting the letter had not been sent. Neither figure has been independently confirmed.

How the field got here

Rosebank is operated by Equinor, which holds 80%, with Ithaca Energy holding the other 20%. Company estimates put recoverable oil at roughly 300 million barrels of oil equivalent. Equinor has said the first phase would target about 245 million barrels, at an estimated cost of $3.8bn.

In January 2025 the Court of Session quashed the field's consent. The environmental assessment had not properly accounted for Scope 3 emissions, meaning those released when the oil is burned. The consent process had to be re-run, so any approval now would be a second decision on the same project.

The courts have already shaped how ministers handle the two North Sea projects now in play. Burnham pointed to the Jackdaw gas field approval and said the previous government's attempt to take that decision was struck down for being overtly political. He has described the Rosebank decision as quasi-judicial. That means it must be taken on the evidence and the law, not announced as a political choice.

Where the cabinet and the backbenches stand

The split is not only on the backbenches. Reports describe tension in cabinet. Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh is reportedly minded to oppose Rosebank, while Burnham and the Chancellor, John Healey, are reportedly in favour. These are press accounts of private positions. Neither side has confirmed them on the record.

Burnham's public line is that the government will "take a further decision on Rosebank in due course". That phrase does not signal an imminent ruling, and no date has been set. The premise that a decision is imminent could not be confirmed. Badenoch has suggested the delay is partly about avoiding a row while the UN climate summit is under way in Antalya, Turkey, until 20 November. Burnham is due to speak at its leaders' summit. That reading is hers, and ministers have not endorsed it.

Opposition to approval is not uniform inside Labour. At a Westminster Hall debate, about a dozen Labour MPs raised concerns. One described the field as incompatible with the UK's climate commitments. Others stress jobs. Jonathan Brash, whose Hartlepool constituency has ties to the industry, argued for the benefits of approval for British workers. Scottish Labour MPs have previously argued that Rosebank and Jackdaw should proceed.

There is a fiscal wrinkle as well. Treasury documents published alongside the Chancellor's tax and spending plans priced in future revenue from the field, though it has not been approved. Badenoch asked why Rosebank was in the Budget if it was not going to be approved.

What a decision would settle

The two tests are separate but overlapping. The legal test is whether a rebuilt environmental assessment now handles Scope 3 emissions adequately. The political test is whether a government committed to net zero can approve a large new oilfield, and then defend the decision to its own MPs. One piece of research cited in Labour opposition estimates the field's lifetime output at about 250 million tonnes of CO2. That figure comes from campaigners and has not been verified here.

The outcome would also set a precedent. One approval would be hard to square with refusing similar projects. A rejection could draw compensation arguments from the operators. Reports point to a split outcome as the most likely result: Jackdaw gas approved, Rosebank refused. That remains unconfirmed.

What to watch next: whether the backbench letter is actually sent to Burnham, and whether a decision comes before or after COP ends on 20 November.
