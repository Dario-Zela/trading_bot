Chancellor says Budget will lift growth as the OBR finds no evidence, and UK GDP posts its fastest rise in over two years

In one sentence
The Chancellor says the Budget will lift growth though the OBR reportedly found no evidence, as ONS data showed 0.7% Q3 growth, the fastest in over two years.

The Chancellor has said the Budget will lift growth. The Office for Budget Responsibility, the official fiscal watchdog, has said it could not find evidence for that. On the same day the Office for National Statistics reported the fastest quarterly expansion in more than two years. The two items point in different directions, and the first is the one that matters for fiscal policy.

Only the GDP number could be checked. The Guardian's account of the Chancellor and the OBR was not available in full, so this article cannot quote the watchdog's wording. It also cannot say which measure the OBR was assessing, or whether its comment concerned the Budget's effect on growth over the forecast period. Readers should treat that half of the story as reported, not confirmed.

The data

The ONS said the UK economy grew 0.7% between July and September, according to LBC's report of the release. That was the fastest rate in more than two years, and it follows 0.5% in the second quarter. Economists had expected 0.7%, so the figure was strong without being a surprise.

The triage notes said one summary put the pace as the quickest since 2024, which sat awkwardly with "more than two years". The wording used here is "more than two years", which is what the sourced report says. The ONS release itself should settle which quarter the comparison reaches back to.

The composition is what stands out. Business investment led the quarter, particularly technology, infrastructure and defence. The ONS said AI spending contributed to July's figures, with many of the highest-turnover businesses involved in AI and cloud computing. Sanjay Raja, chief UK economist at Deutsche Bank, pointed to data centres and IT, defence and aircraft, and added that "consumers have also been out in force, despite the energy shock". The growth came despite higher energy prices linked to the Iran war.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham called the figure "welcome news" and said the economy "is growing, bringing hope for the future".

The dispute

The OBR's forecasts are the arithmetic behind every Budget. Its growth assumptions feed directly into projected tax receipts, borrowing and the headroom against the government's fiscal rules. A Chancellor who claims a policy package will raise growth is, in effect, asking the OBR to put that into its numbers. If the watchdog says it cannot find evidence that the package does so, the claim has no place in the forecast, and any extra headroom it would have created is absent.

There is precedent. At the November 2025 Budget, a Commons debate heard an MP's reading of the OBR's Blue Book as saying nothing in that package would do much for growth. That is one MP's characterisation of paragraph 3.13 and should be checked against the document. It does show the same argument recurring: ministers talk up growth effects and the OBR declines to score them.

What the numbers do and do not settle

A strong quarter does not rescue the Chancellor's argument. One quarter of 0.7% is evidence about the past three months, not about the effect of measures that have yet to take effect. The drivers cited, AI-related investment and defence spending, are not obviously products of a Budget that had not yet been delivered. Government could point to the data as momentum. The OBR's position, as reported, is that it cannot attribute future growth to policy.

The data does help in one narrower way. Faster underlying growth tends to improve tax receipts and can ease the arithmetic the OBR must work with, independent of any policy claim.

What to watch

The LBC report did not mention the Bank of England, and the triage claim that stronger growth tests its ability to keep cutting rates is unsupported by any cited rate decision or market move. It is a reasonable question, since firmer growth and energy-driven price pressure both argue for caution, but it remains a question. The first tests are the OBR's published Budget forecast and its stated reasoning on growth, then the reaction in gilt yields and sterling. Whether the watchdog revises its growth path upwards after a 0.7% quarter will show how much of the Chancellor's case it is willing to accept.
