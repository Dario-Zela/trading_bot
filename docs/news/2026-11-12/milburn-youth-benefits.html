Milburn review set to recommend fewer benefits for some under-25s

In one sentence
Alan Milburn's youth review is reportedly set to cut benefit entitlements for some under-25s, but no saving has been costed, which leaves the fiscal effect for gilts unclear.

Alan Milburn's review of young people who are not in work or education is set to recommend that some under-25s receive less in benefits than they do now. The Treasury has no costing for that yet, and the numbers are what gilt investors will look for when the review is published.

The details so far come from newspaper reports of a Times interview with Milburn, and from a BBC report on the same lines. They have not been checked against the final text. Milburn's review is due to publish its second part, and one report puts that at next week. The published recommendations may differ from the leak.

What is reportedly proposed

According to those reports, under-25s would lose access to two disability-related benefits: the health element of Universal Credit and Personal Independence Payment (PIP). They would instead receive a new youth allowance at the standard Universal Credit rate. Many current PIP recipients would move to a new "additional cost allowance". A separate benefit would be created for a smaller group whose disabilities mean they can never be expected to work.

Milburn has acknowledged the trade-off. He told the Times it "could mean that some people will get less financial support at the beginning of their benefit claim than under the current system", and said: "I'm not saying that any of this will be easy or that it will be pain-free because it won't." He has also argued that the "menu of opportunities has got to increase", with a personal adviser for each young claimant. The report is expected to call for billions of pounds more for employment support, though no sum has been reported.

The numbers that exist, and those that don't

The government commissioned the review in November 2025 under Keir Starmer. Its interim findings supply the baseline. About one in eight 16-to-24-year-olds is not in education, employment or training, and the interim report warned the proportion could reach one in six by 2031, or about 1.25 million young people. It also found that in 2024/25 roughly £25 was spent on benefits for young people for every £1 spent on employment support.

The figure that matters for the fiscal arithmetic is missing: neither the leak nor the coverage gives a projected saving, or a cost for the extra employment support. The reports give no Treasury estimate either. Without them, the plan cannot be placed against the fiscal rules, and it is unclear whether it would be a net saver in its first years. Up-front spending on coaching and training would come before any reduction in benefit bills. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said an overhaul will require the government to "invest up front in young people", which points to a package with costs in the early years.

Why gilts care

Welfare cuts carry a long record of missed savings and political attrition. Plans that clear the Commons on paper have been diluted when backbenchers objected. A government with limited headroom against its fiscal rules needs credible savings, and markets tend to price the gap between announced and delivered ones. A package that is visibly front-loaded in cost and back-loaded in savings would add to borrowing before it subtracted from it. Any such shift would be small beside the gilt market's broader concerns, but it would feed into the same debate over the Budget's growth claims.

Charities have already raised concerns. Harriet Edwards of the disability charity Sense said it was "deeply concerned that, if these proposals are taken forward without proper funding for specialist employment support" they would fail, and asked who would qualify and who risked missing out.

What to watch

The published second part of the review is the first test: whether it carries a costed saving, and whether the Treasury publishes its own assessment. After that comes the legislative timetable. The government has said major welfare reforms are planned for 2027, with one report pointing to legislation early next year. The size of any backbench rebellion would then show how much of the package survives.
