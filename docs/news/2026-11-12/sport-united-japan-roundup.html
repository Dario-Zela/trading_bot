Man Utd weigh Pochettino and Carrick; Jones eyes Twickenham upset

In one sentence
A BBC Sport column casts United's succession as Pochettino versus Carrick with no confirmed development, while Eddie Jones wants Japan to make 'the sky fall in' at Twickenham.

Two stories from BBC Sport's pages sit together today. One is a football argument about who should run Manchester United next. The other is a rugby coach talking up an underdog's chances at Twickenham. Neither reports a decision, and both are best read as argument and expectation rather than development.

United's succession debate

The United piece, headlined "Pochettino a decade in Man Utd's making, but Carrick deserves better", frames the club's managerial future as a contest between Mauricio Pochettino and Michael Carrick. The headline carries the argument: Pochettino has long been linked with the job, and Carrick is being overlooked.

The framing is the piece's own. The material available for this report contains no appointment, no shortlist confirmed by the club, no statement from United and no change in policy. A column that says a candidate "deserves better" is making a case, not breaking news. Without those facts, "Pochettino versus Carrick" is a description of the debate rather than of the process.

The question is real to United supporters, and managerial vacancies at big clubs tend to generate this sort of positioning. But a reader should treat the two-horse race as a columnist's construction until the club says otherwise.

Jones wants the sky to fall

The rugby item has a quote, which helps. Eddie Jones, coach of Japan, told BBC Sport that his side want "the sky to fall in" at Twickenham, where they face England in an autumn international.

It is a vivid line, and a useful one for a coach whose side is widely expected to lose. Jones has long been fluent in the language of provocation, and the phrase manages two things at once. It lowers the stakes by admitting the odds, and it raises the ambition by naming the upset as the aim. It does not amount to a prediction, and nothing in the source material offers one.

The fixture's likely lopsidedness is the context for reading the quote. Japan arrive as outsiders and Jones is setting out what a good day would look like: an England side unsettled in its own stadium.

What ties them together

Both pieces are about expectation. United's debate concerns who ought to be given a job, and Jones's concerns what an outsider can hope to take from a favourite. In each case the stated position runs ahead of the evidence.

The next real data points are simple. For United, it is any formal word from the club on its search. For Japan, it is the result at Twickenham, which will show whether the sky obliged.
