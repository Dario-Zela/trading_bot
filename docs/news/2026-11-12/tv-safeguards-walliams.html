Government threatens mandatory safeguards for TV after Walliams allegations

In one sentence
Ministers have warned the TV industry it must tackle abuse or face a crackdown, with mandatory membership of its standards body CIISA reportedly on the table after the Walliams allegations.

The government has told the television industry to get its house in order on abuse or face a crackdown. The warning follows allegations against the entertainer David Walliams, which he denies. It turns one man's case into a test of whether British broadcasters can police themselves, or whether Parliament will do it for them.

The core facts, as reported by the BBC and the Guardian, are these. Ministers have warned the entertainment industry to bring in better protections for women and children, or face a "crackdown". Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy is reported to be summoning leaders from broadcasting, publishing, film and entertainment to "draw a line" in the sand. The BBC reports that she is prepared to compel participation in the industry's standards body if the response falls short. The conditional matters: this is a threat, not yet a policy.

The allegations

The catalyst is a joint investigation by the Guardian and ITV News. It reported that several women had accused Walliams of sexual misconduct and of abusing his position. Walliams denies the allegations. He has said he is "not a sexual predator" and has not "broken the law". None of the claims has been tested in court, and nothing here should be read as a finding against him.

This is not the first time his conduct has been in the news. Late last year he faced allegations of inappropriate behaviour towards junior female staff, which his spokesperson said he "strongly denies". The BBC's handling of his work then drew criticism. It had cut ties with him, yet a Boxing Day programme still aired. The reporting I could retrieve did not give consistent dates for these episodes, so the sequence is best treated as approximate.

The body at the centre

The mechanism ministers have in mind is the Creative Industries Independent Standards Authority, or CIISA. It was set up in 2022 to deal with bullying and harassment across film, TV, theatre and music. Taking part is voluntary, and it has limited power to impose sanctions. It relies on industry funding and has no statutory footing.

The campaigner Zelda Perkins has argued that membership should be mandatory. Deadline has separately reported that the government is weighing a legal requirement for UK broadcasters to fund CIISA regularly. Nandy has previously said she takes a "dim view" of large industry players who do not back it financially.

The approach has a precedent. After the Gregg Wallace affair in late 2024, Nandy said: "if the industry can't get its own house in order, we are prepared to step in." Nearly two years on, the threat is back in nearly the same words, which says something about how much has changed in between. Bectu, the entertainment union, has found that 74% of those it surveyed said behaviour that would be unacceptable elsewhere is tolerated in TV.

What mandatory would mean

The distinction is between a club and a rulebook. A voluntary body can be ignored by anyone who prefers not to join. A statutory duty would reach every broadcaster and production company, including the small independents where much of the sector's work, and much of its informality, sits. That is why the precedent would outlast the Walliams case.

The details are still missing. Nothing in the reporting gives a draft duty, a timetable or a penalty regime. The BBC's account of the planned meeting with industry leaders, which I could not date precisely, is the next marker. The industry's response there will decide whether the warning becomes legislation. A convincing offer on funding and membership would give ministers a reason to hold off. A weak one would give them the case for a mandate.

This piece draws on secondary summaries of the BBC and Guardian reporting, not the original articles, so quotes and the meeting date should be checked against those sources.
