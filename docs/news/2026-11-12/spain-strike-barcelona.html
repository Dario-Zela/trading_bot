Spain general strike turns violent in Barcelona

In one sentence
Spain's first general strike in 14 years, over housing and living costs, saw masked groups clash with police in Barcelona, with turnout figures disputed ahead of the 29 November election.

Spain held its first general strike in 14 years on Wednesday. By evening, the largest of the day's marches had turned into running clashes between police and masked groups in Barcelona. The unions behind the walkout framed it as a stand over housing costs and the cost of living. Employers and the political right called it an electoral gambit.

The strike was called by Spain's main unions and drew participants in the tens of thousands at least, with some reports putting the national figure in the hundreds of thousands. Public transport ran on minimum-service rules, which were enough to keep some commuter trains, metros and buses moving but not to prevent disruption, especially in and around Barcelona. Shopfronts on a main shopping boulevard in the city were boarded up before the marches began, a sign that businesses expected trouble.

A dispute over numbers

The turnout figures in Barcelona are far apart. Organisers said 400,000 people took part in the city. The city police put the figure at 55,000. Gaps of this size are normal in Spanish protest reporting, where unions and police have competing incentives, and neither number can be independently checked from the reports available. Both are best read as the edges of a range.

The violence came later in the day. Groups of masked men clashed with police, and bins were set alight, leaving small fires in the streets. The reports available do not give figures for arrests or injuries, so none are stated here. A person interviewed by the BBC described the protest overall as mostly peaceful and even joyful. That fits a pattern in which the bulk of a march passes without incident while a smaller group draws the cameras. How large that group was is not yet established.

Why it is landing now

Spain votes in parliamentary elections on 29 November, which gives the strike a political edge it might not otherwise have had. Business groups and the right rejected it as an election-season manoeuvre rather than a genuine labour dispute. The unions called it a success and employers called it a failure, the usual split after such a day.

Barcelona has form here. A 2019 general strike in Catalonia, held after the sentencing of separatist leaders, led to days of clashes, cancelled flights and an estimated €1.57m in damage to the city. A nationwide strike in 2012 over labour reform ended with a burned-out Starbucks in Barcelona and 176 arrests across Spain. The current dispute is about housing, not constitutional questions or labour law. But a housing protest in the city in early October was also reported to have ended in clashes with police, which suggests the issue has been running hot for weeks.

What to watch

The immediate questions are factual. Police and the interior authorities will publish arrest and injury counts, and the city will tally damage. The larger test is political. If the unions can turn a day of mass turnout into a housing commitment from the parties, the strike will have had an effect. If the lasting image is burning bins in Barcelona, it may instead help the parties that framed the walkout as electioneering. The campaign's remaining weeks before 29 November will show which reading takes hold.
