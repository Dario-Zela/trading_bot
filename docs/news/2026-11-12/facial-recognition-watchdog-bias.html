Police watchdog warns facial recognition errors put ethnic minorities at risk of wrongful arrest

In one sentence
England and Wales's police inspectorate warns that retrospective facial recognition can wrongly flag ethnic minorities and is over-trusted by some officers; the report does not cover live systems.

A report from England and Wales's police inspectorate has put in writing a warning that police facial recognition can wrongly flag people from ethnic minorities as suspects, and that some officers are treating its output as more certain than it is. One point matters for how the story is framed. The inspection covered retrospective facial recognition, where police run a still image, such as a CCTV frame, against a database of custody photos. It did not cover live facial recognition, the camera-van technology that scans passers-by in real time and is the part of the system now being expanded.

That distinction bears on the angle that the report shows live rollout raising the risk of wrongful arrest. The report does not say that. It does say something related and serious about the retrospective tool, and it lands while the government is consulting on a legal framework for both.

What the inspectors found

His Majesty's Inspectorate of Constabulary published the report on Wednesday 11 November, according to coverage of the Guardian's reporting by Vikram Dodd. The findings, as relayed in that coverage, are these:

  • Some officers did not know that a facial recognition result is "intelligence rather than evidence" and needs further checks before action is taken.
  • Some investigators did not carry out enough corroborating enquiries before acting on a potential match. In "a small number of cases", officers relied too heavily on the result.
  • The algorithm still shows bias, including a higher likelihood of wrongly flagging people from ethnic minorities as suspects.
  • Poor-quality or unsuitable images, such as blurred photos or pictures containing several faces, reduced the system's ability to return reliable matches.
  • Custody images that should have been deleted were still being kept.
  • Legislation has not kept pace with the technology, and no comprehensive statutory framework governs its use.

The coverage reviewed gives no error rates, case counts or retention figures, and no confirmed wrongful arrests are reported. The warning is about risk. It describes how a biased tool and an over-trusting officer could combine, not a tally of people wrongly detained. Until the full report is read directly, the figures behind the bias finding remain unchecked.

Why bias and over-trust compound

Facial recognition software does not identify anyone. It returns a ranked list of possible matches with a similarity score. If the underlying algorithm produces false matches more often for some groups, those groups will see more false leads. If officers then treat the lead as proof, the error passes straight into a stop or an arrest.

That is the mechanism the inspectors appear to be describing. Kathryn Stone, HM Inspector of Constabulary, said forces must show that safeguards are in place, that bias and data-retention risks are managed, and that human judgment still applies. She called for a clearer statutory framework, stronger national standards and better governance.

Big Brother Watch's Jasleen Chaggar said treating facial recognition as "bona fide evidence rather than a probability score is alarming". She said police are not logging their use of the retrospective tool, and that the algorithm is more likely to misidentify women, Asian and Black people. Prof Pete Fussey of the University of Southampton, a former police adviser, said "meaningful human scrutiny of AI systems is a legal requirement", and pointed to examples of investigators accepting computer suggestions without further checks.

The wider evidence on bias is mixed rather than one-sided. A 2023 National Physical Laboratory study of the live system found no statistically significant differences in true-positive identification across gender and ethnicity at the settings tested. Separate testing of the retrospective search tool found a lower false-positive rate for white subjects than for Asian ones. The Met Police's own annual figures listed 10 false live alerts, of which seven involved Black men, which is a small sample but not a comfortable one. Essex Police paused its live system earlier this year after studies suggested it identified Black people disproportionately.

Police response and the legal gap

Ben Russell, the National Police Chiefs' Council lead on facial recognition, said policing remains committed to ethical, proportionate and responsible use and welcomed the report's recognition of the technology's potential. No Home Office response appeared in the coverage reviewed.

The legal gap is the most concrete point of agreement. The Home Office's January 2026 policing white paper announced more live facial recognition vans and opened a national consultation on a legal framework. The only significant case law remains the Court of Appeal's 2020 ruling in Bridges v South Wales Police. Forces are in effect operating under a patchwork of data-protection, equality and human-rights law, with guidance rather than a dedicated statute.

What to watch

Three things will show how much weight the report carries. The first is whether the Home Office's framework consultation produces legislation that covers retrospective searches as well as live deployments, since the inspectors' findings concern the former. The second is whether forces start logging retrospective searches and training officers on what a match does and does not prove. The third is the separate inspection of the Police National Database search tool, which the government asked for after independent testing. Its publication date has not been confirmed. Whatever it finds will be the nearest test of whether the bias concern reaches live deployment too.
