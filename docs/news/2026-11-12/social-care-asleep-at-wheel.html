Social care review head says governments were 'asleep at the wheel'

In one sentence
A social care review chair has told the BBC that governments were 'asleep at the wheel' on adult social care reform, though the full remarks could not be verified.

Adult social care in England, the help given to older and disabled people with washing, dressing, eating and living independently, has been the subject of reviews, white papers and promised reforms for more than a quarter of a century. The head of a social care review has now said that governments of all stripes were "asleep at the wheel", according to BBC Politics. The remark is a rebuke of the whole run of administrations rather than of one party.

What has been said, and what has not

This article rests on a headline and a short summary from the BBC. It does not rest on the full text of the interview, and the details below should be read with that limit in mind. The BBC attributes the phrase "asleep at the wheel" to the review's chair. The target of the criticism is successive governments' failure to reform adult social care. The reporting available does not give the chair's full remarks, any figures they cited, or any new policy proposals. This piece therefore does not infer what the review will recommend.

It is also unclear which review is meant. The best-known current exercise is the independent commission on adult social care in England. Health Secretary Wes Streeting set it up under the crossbench peer Baroness Louise Casey, and it is not due to report until 2028. Searches did not confirm that the quoted remark came from that commission's chair, so this article does not attribute it to anyone by name.

A long history of deferral

The context is well documented, even if this particular quote is not. The Dilnot commission reported in 2011 with proposals to cap individuals' lifetime care costs. A version of the cap was legislated for and then shelved. Reform has since been promised in successive manifestos and green papers without a settled outcome. Andrew Dilnot has himself said the government is letting down families on this issue. Layla Moran, who chairs the Commons health and social care committee, has warned against "kicking the can down the road".

The government's position is that it wants cross-party consensus, which it argues takes time. Critics reply that a report due in 2028 pushes any legislation well into the next parliament. Ministers deny that the commission is a delaying device.

Why the rebuke matters

A chair's public criticism of those who commissioned the work is unusual, and it carries political weight. Social care sits awkwardly between local council budgets, NHS pressures and household finances. Delay in one place tends to show up elsewhere, most visibly in hospital beds occupied by patients who are medically fit to leave but have no care package to go to. A strongly worded intervention keeps the subject on the political agenda, but on its own it commits no government to anything.

What to watch

The next test is the evidence the review publishes and whether ministers respond to it with timetables and funding rather than further consultation. Readers should check the chair's exact words and any supporting figures in the BBC's full report. Those details were not available to this writer.
