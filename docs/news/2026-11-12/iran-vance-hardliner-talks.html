Vance weighs direct talks with an Iranian hardliner as unverified claims of a closed Hormuz circulate

In one sentence
The WSJ reports Vance is weighing direct contact with an Iranian hardliner to end the war, while claims of a closed Hormuz and a relocated Bessent remain unverified.

Washington is reportedly weighing a route around Iran's usual diplomatic channels. The Wall Street Journal reports that Vice-President JD Vance is considering direct contact with an Iranian hardliner as a way to end the war between the two countries. The report reached markets through the news aggregator Benzinga, and it has not been independently confirmed. It names no Iranian figure, gives no date or venue, and carries no on-the-record confirmation from either government.

Two other claims have circulated alongside it, and both are weaker still. One says a Revolutionary Guards general has declared the Strait of Hormuz closed. The other says Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been moved into military housing after Iranian threats. Neither has been verified. Taken together they show how much of the war's information environment currently runs on headlines that have yet to be checked.

What the Vance report would mean

Diplomacy normally runs through designated officials: foreign ministries, intermediaries, and negotiators whose standing is understood by both sides. An approach to a hardliner skips that structure. If it is real, there are two plausible readings.

The first is that Washington thinks the mainstream channels have stopped being useful. It may believe the officials who usually speak for Tehran cannot deliver, or will not. The second is that it wants to talk to someone who can. A hardliner who agreed to a settlement would be harder to attack at home as having sold out than a moderate who did the same.

Either way, the reported contact implies the US wants a way out. Officials do not usually explore unorthodox routes to people on the other side when they expect the existing approach to work. That is an inference from the shape of the report, not a statement from anyone involved, and the thin sourcing limits how far it can be pushed.

The obvious difficulty is the one named in the report's framing: who actually decides in Tehran. A direct line is only worth as much as the authority behind it. If the hardliner in question speaks for the people who control the military and the final call on war and peace, the channel could matter. If not, it risks producing assurances that no one is bound to honour. The Hormuz claim, discussed below, points at the same problem: the person with ultimate say is the Supreme Leader.

The Hormuz claim

The second thread is an assertion, attributed to a Revolutionary Guards brigadier general and passed around among traders, that the Strait of Hormuz is closed and will stay closed until the Supreme Leader orders otherwise. The only cited source is Benzinga. It is not presented as an official statement from the Iranian government or the Guards, and it has not been matched against wire reports or official sources.

The strait carries a large share of the world's seaborne oil, so any credible suggestion of closure moves energy risk pricing quickly. A claim does not have to be true to do that. It only has to be believed for long enough to trade on. The sentence about waiting for the Supreme Leader is also worth reading closely. It places the decision at the very top of the system, which is consistent with the question of who can speak for Iran, but it is a quoted fragment of unknown provenance and should not be treated as policy.

The Bessent headline

The third item is a Bloomberg report, relayed by Benzinga, that Bessent was relocated to military housing after threats from Iran. The relayed version gives no date, no description of the threat and no named source. There is no response from the Treasury, the White House or Tehran. If confirmed, it would carry the conflict into the household arrangements of a cabinet official who sets economic policy. As it stands, it is a headline awaiting its original text.

What to watch

The first test is whether either government acknowledges the Vance report, even to deny it. A named Iranian counterpart, or a statement from the White House or the vice-president's office, would turn a secondhand account into a fact that could be assessed. The second is whether any official Iranian or Guards statement on Hormuz appears, and whether shipping data show any change in traffic through the strait. Absent those, the strongest reading of the day is that Washington is reportedly looking for an exit, while the questions of who in Tehran would meet it, and with what authority, remain unanswered.
