Indian mega library accused of stocking AI-generated books

In one sentence
A newly opened Coimbatore library is accused of stocking error-ridden, possibly AI-written books under invented authors, a claim now under anti-corruption investigation.

A grand new public library in southern India has become the centre of a row over whether some of its shelves were stocked with books written by machines, under names that may not belong to anyone. The allegations are unproven. They have nonetheless turned into a political fight in Tamil Nadu and an early test of how public institutions check what they buy.

The Periyar Arivulagam, a seven-acre library and science centre in Gandhipuram, Coimbatore, was opened on 16 October by Chief Minister C. Joseph Vijay. Planned under the previous DMK government, it carries an estimated price tag of Rs 300 crore, according to The Federal. Of that, Rs 245 crore went on the building, Rs 50 crore on physical books and Rs 5 crore on e-books.

The shelf walk

The row began on 6 November, when the Tamil writer Perumal Murugan published an article on the site Sindhai. He had counted nearly 1,800 titles from one publisher, Agalvilakku Pathippagam, on the fourth floor, plus more than 100 from a second, Phoenix Pathippagam. He described what he found as a major scam.

His examples were specific, if not all conclusive. One book on the classical Tamil text Naladiyar was priced at Rs 1,445 and treated the work as though it were an individual poet. Comparable titles cost about Rs 350. Another named Muthuvelu Subbaiah Iyer as Tamil Nadu's first chief minister, which Murugan called historically wrong. He also pointed to ISBNs he could not verify, missing publisher contact details and a Nandi sculpture photo presented as a find from Keezhadi, an archaeological site. The AI suspicion rests on that pattern of errors and on author names that appear not to correspond to real writers.

School Education Minister A. Rajmohan went further. He alleged that AI-generated text was credited to invented authors, among them "Thamarai Murali", "Kamalambal Karunanidhi" and "Trisha Vijay", and called it "India's first artificial intelligence scam". He put the Agalvilakku purchase at about 1,500 books, bought in February 2026. Those claims are the minister's and Murugan's. Neither the publishers' side nor an independent technical check of the text appears in the coverage reviewed.

Who ordered what

The politics are tangled. Vijay's TVK government blames the DMK, pointing to a government order of 5 November 2025 that set up the committee recommending titles. The DMK counters that some disputed books carry date stamps of 22 June 2026, after it left office in May, and notes that Murugan himself sat on the committee. One member, S. Tamilselvan, has said it met about once, recommended books online and was never told what was actually purchased.

Rajmohan filed a complaint with the state's anti-corruption bureau, the DVAC, on 7 November. He has said questionable orders will be cancelled, that some payments have been made and others remain pending, and that purchases planned for other libraries will be reviewed. Reports say similar buying involved other institutions, including the Connemara Public Library.

Opposition and allied parties have piled in. The CPM wants a special investigation team and a panel of Tamil scholars to purge unreliable titles. The CPI warned that factual errors could mislead students preparing for competitive exams, and the VCK called for a commission of inquiry.

What to watch

The distinction that matters is between fraud and carelessness. Padded prices and untraceable publishers are procurement problems that long predate AI. Whether the text itself was machine-written is a separate question that only a proper review of the books can settle. The DVAC inquiry and the promised scholars' review will show whether the library has a bulk-buying scandal or something more novel. Cancelled orders would be the first sign the government thinks the claims hold.
