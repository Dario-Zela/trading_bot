Significant drop in 'forever' chemicals in England's rivers

In one sentence
The Environment Agency says PFOS and PFOA in English rivers fell about 30% between 2016 and 2025, but the fall covers two legacy chemicals and PFAS remain widespread.

The amount of two of the best-known "forever chemicals" in England's rivers has fallen by roughly 30% in under a decade, according to a new Environment Agency assessment. The result is unusual in a field that has mostly produced bad news. It is narrower than the headline suggests, though, because it covers two long-regulated compounds and not the whole PFAS family.

What the data show

The report, published on 12 November, compares monitoring from 2016 with that from 2025. Over that period:

  • PFOS in English rivers fell by around 30%.
  • PFOA in rivers fell by about 29%.
  • PFOS in freshwater fish fell by roughly 49%.

The agency uses "around" and "approximately" for these figures, and the published summary gives percentage changes, not absolute concentrations. How far the rivers sit above any safety benchmark is therefore not clear from the release alone. The assessment updates the agency's 2021 report and draws on a much larger body of data, with sampling now spanning rivers, lakes, groundwater, estuaries, coastal waters and landfill leachate.

Why the fall happened

PFAS, or per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, are a family of thousands of synthetic chemicals used in non-stick coatings, waterproof clothing and firefighting foams. They break down extremely slowly, hence the nickname. PFOS was largely banned in the UK from 2010 and PFOA from 2020. The decline fits what regulators would expect once manufacture and use are curtailed, though the release does not attempt to attribute the drop to specific measures.

The limits of the good news

The fall applies to these two legacy compounds. The release does not show comparable trends for newer PFAS, which have in many cases replaced them. The report itself says PFAS presence "continues to be widespread" because of historic use and continuing releases from some sources.

That sits uneasily beside earlier findings. Analyses of official data in 2025 found that most English water bodies tested exceeded proposed safety limits, and the government has conceded that England will miss its 2027 target for good chemical status in waters, in part because of PFAS. A 30% fall from a high baseline can leave concentrations well above a threshold.

Dr Robert Bradburne, the agency's chief scientist, called the reductions in some older PFAS "encouraging" while saying there is "more to do". Emma Hardy, the environment minister, pointed to tougher rules on use, updated guidance and support for safer alternatives. Dr David Megson of Manchester Metropolitan University said data from thousands of samples is essential and can help target the worst pollution.

What comes next

The agency argues the findings strengthen the case for monitoring and regulating PFAS as a group, a position the chemicals industry opposes. Planned steps include testing of estuaries and coastal waters, guidance on legacy PFAS on contaminated land, a possible restriction on PFAS in firefighting foams, and legislation to prohibit further persistent organic pollutants. The regulator is also testing for dozens of additional PFAS. Whether the group-regulation argument gains ground will depend on what that wider monitoring shows about the newer compounds.
