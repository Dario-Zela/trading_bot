Marvell's S&P 500 inclusion set to trigger wave of passive ETF buying

Marvell Technology's Santa Clara campus, where the company is headquartered. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Marvell joins the S&P 500 on June 22, triggering billions in forced ETF buying — but research suggests the premium from passive flows has largely been front-run away already.

Marvell Technology will join the S&P 500 on June 22, replacing Campbell Soup — a swap that captures something essential about where the American economy has moved. The chipmaker's inclusion was announced on June 5, and shares jumped roughly 10% on the news, adding to a year-to-date gain that has already exceeded 250%.

At around $300 a share and a market capitalisation north of $230 billion, Marvell is a substantial addition. The mechanics of what happens next are largely automatic.

Programmatic at scale

Approximately $13 trillion in assets track the S&P 500 passively. Every fund benchmarked to the index — from the largest ETFs like the SPDR S&P 500 Trust and the Vanguard 500 to institutional pension allocations — must build a position in Marvell proportionate to its weighting before the effective date. The three largest S&P 500 ETFs alone account for more than $2 trillion in combined assets. None of that buying is discretionary. It arrives regardless of what a portfolio manager thinks about Marvell's valuation.

And the valuation is the thing worth examining. Marvell currently trades at approximately 65 times forward earnings — a premium well above peers including Broadcom and Taiwan Semiconductor. The company earned its place in the index by clearing the profitability hurdle in March, when it posted GAAP earnings of $0.46 per share for the fourth quarter. Revenue in the most recent quarter came in at $2.42 billion, up 28% year on year, with data-centre business — the AI infrastructure play — accounting for 76% of that figure. Full-year guidance stands at $11.5 billion.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang called Marvell "the next trillion-dollar company" at Computex in early June. The stock rose 33% in a single session on that endorsement. Then fell 16% on the Friday before the S&P announcement. Then surged 13.7% on the Monday when inclusion was confirmed. The amplitude of those moves says something about how sentiment, not cash flow, is doing most of the price-setting work at the moment.

The front-run problem

Research by Harvard Business School economists Robin Greenwood and Marco Sammon suggests the mechanical buying windfall may be smaller than it appears. The "index inclusion effect" — the abnormal return associated with joining a major index — averaged 7.4% in the 1990s. By 2010–2020, it had fallen to roughly 1%, statistically indistinguishable from zero. The culprit is anticipation: as passive investing has grown, so has the army of traders who position ahead of the forced buyers, absorbing the premium before the ETFs arrive at the door.

The critical data will emerge in the week of June 16, when trading behaviour around the June 19 close should indicate whether passive demand is still being accumulated or whether it was front-run almost entirely during the announcement pop. For a stock already trading 160% above its 200-day moving average, the direction of that answer matters.

Q2 earnings — guided at $2.7 billion in revenue, roughly 35% growth year on year — will be the next fundamental checkpoint after inclusion mechanics have cleared.
