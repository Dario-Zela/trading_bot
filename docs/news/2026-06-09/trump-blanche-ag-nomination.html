Trump nominates personal attorney Todd Blanche as attorney general

Todd Blanche, official portrait as Deputy Attorney General, March 2025 — Ryan M. Biller / U.S. Department of Justice
In one sentence
Trump nominates his personal criminal-defence lawyer as AG, triggering Senate Republican doubts and questions about DOJ independence under a loyalty-first administration.

Donald Trump formally nominated Todd Blanche — the lawyer who spent eighteen months defending him through three criminal prosecutions — to be the next Attorney General of the United States. The nomination was sent to the Senate on June 8. If confirmed, Blanche would graduate from Trump's most trusted personal counsel to the nation's top law enforcement officer.

The appointment cements a pattern that critics argue defines Trump's second term: loyalty as the primary hiring criterion. Blanche, 51, has no prior experience running a major federal institution, never served as a United States attorney, and spent most of his post-federal career in white-collar criminal defence. What he does have is a record of standing beside Trump through a felony conviction and two further failed federal prosecutions — eventually switching his own party registration from Democrat to Republican in 2024. He became acting AG in April after Trump abruptly fired Pam Bondi, having been confirmed as deputy attorney general in March 2025.

The fund that won't quite die

The immediate complication is a $1.776 billion fund — labelled the "Anti-Weaponization Fund" — that the Justice Department created as part of a settlement in which Trump dropped a $10 billion lawsuit against the IRS. The fund was designed to compensate Americans who claimed Biden-era government had targeted them politically. Critics, including a clutch of Republican senators, feared it would function as a slush fund for Trump allies and, more pointedly, for some of the 1,500-plus people prosecuted in connection with the January 6 attack on the Capitol.

Facing a revolt from senators whose votes he needs, Blanche testified before a House panel on June 2 that the DOJ was "not moving forward" with the fund. Senate Republican leadership had made clear the legislative agenda would be held hostage to the controversy. But Blanche declined to put the commitment in writing — a hedge that has not gone unnoticed on Capitol Hill. Sen. Thom Tillis said he would oppose the nomination if Blanche equivocated on condemning January 6; Senate Majority Leader John Thune told reporters it was "hard to say" whether Blanche could win confirmation. With Republicans holding a slim majority, Blanche can afford to lose just four votes on the floor.

One further wrinkle: despite the fund's ostensible death, Trump, his family, and the Trump Organization retain immunity from IRS tax audits and enforcement actions — a protection that travelled through the original settlement and remained intact after Blanche's testimony.

Watching briefs

For markets and those watching corporate enforcement, the nomination carries a signal beyond the political theatre. An attorney general shaped primarily by personal loyalty to the executive may approach antitrust enforcement, corporate investigations, and financial regulation differently than a career prosecutor would. Blanche's prosecutorial roots — eight years in the Southern District of New York's violent crimes division — do not obviously translate into experience running the DOJ's civil and regulatory divisions. Whether that gap produces weaker oversight of concentrated industries, or simply selective enforcement, is a reasonable question rather than a foregone conclusion.

The confirmation hearing is the next data point. Blanche's handling of direct questions on January 6, DOJ independence, and the future of the fund will tell both markets and wavering senators whether the acting-AG mode carries over to the permanent role.
