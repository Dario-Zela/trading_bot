IRAN-HORMUZ · WATCH · By C. Tanaka · 2026-06-09 · 3 min read

US forces seize tanker in Gulf of Oman; Austin says Hormuz could be forced open

In one sentence
US forces disabled a seventh tanker off Iran as ex-Pentagon chief Austin warned that fully reopening Hormuz would be costly and require allied burden-sharing.

A US Navy fighter jet disabled an oil tanker in the Gulf of Oman on Monday — the seventh vessel to be immobilised since Washington imposed a naval blockade on Iran's ports in April — a concrete display of American enforcement reach that arrived alongside a more measured assessment of its limits.

The Palau-flagged M/T Marivex, travelling without cargo toward an Iranian port, was struck by a precision munition fired from an F/A-18 Super Hornet off the USS Abraham Lincoln after its crew failed to comply with directions from US forces. The strike targeted the ship's engineering and steering spaces. US Central Command confirmed the vessel "is no longer sailing to Iran."

Since the blockade launched on April 13, US forces have redirected 134 compliant ships while allowing 42 vessels carrying humanitarian aid to pass unmolested. The operational cadence — one more non-compliant tanker stopped, no dramatic escalation — has become the rhythm of a standoff that entered its fourth month this week.

What it would cost to hold it open

Overlapping in timing with the Marivex action, former Defense Secretary Lloyd Austin offered the clearest public assessment yet from a credible American voice of what it would take to reopen the Strait of Hormuz entirely — and where the constraints lie.

"Certainly, the United States Navy could open the Strait of Hormuz," Austin said in an interview with Bloomberg's Ruth David at the HSBC Gulf Cooperation Council Exchanges Conference in London on Sunday. The qualification came immediately: "Holding it open for a long period of time would be pretty costly and so we'd like to see an international effort, if that's the case."

Austin, who led the Pentagon from 2021 to 2025 under the Biden administration, framed the objective in explicitly global terms. "If we get it open, we want to get allies and partners involved in that because freedom of navigation is not just important to the United States of America, it's important to the entire globe."

The nuance matters for energy markets. The strait carries roughly 20% of global crude oil and LNG exports through a corridor barely 33 kilometres wide at its narrowest point. Since Iran closed the waterway in the aftermath of US and Israeli airstrikes in late February — which killed Supreme Leader Khamenei — Brent crude has surged from around $80 to peak above $126 a barrel. Petroleum flows through Hormuz fell approximately 30% year-on-year in the first quarter of 2026, to 14.6 million barrels per day.

Austin's remarks are calibrated realism rather than new policy. He holds no operational authority; his weight is institutional credibility. That a senior former Pentagon official frames indefinite military control of Hormuz as an unsustainable posture — expensive, requiring allied burden-sharing — signals that the US defence establishment views maximum escalation as cost-constrained, not freely available. For energy traders pricing regional risk, the takeaway is that the blockade's current enforcement rhythm is more tenable than a full reopening campaign, and therefore more durable.

The allied piece of Austin's equation is what to watch. Whether Washington can assemble a coalition of European and Asian partners — whose dependence on Gulf hydrocarbons runs deeper than America's own — will determine whether the current enforcement posture can evolve into something sustainable. The next GCC diplomatic summit will be an early test of that appetite.
