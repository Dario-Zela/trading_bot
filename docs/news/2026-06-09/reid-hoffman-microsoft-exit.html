Reid Hoffman steps down from Microsoft board a decade after the LinkedIn deal

Reid Hoffman at the Defense Innovation Board's fall meeting at the Pentagon, October 2022 — Lisa Ferdinando / U.S. Department of Defense
In one sentence
LinkedIn's Reid Hoffman is leaving Microsoft's board after nearly a decade to focus on Manas, his AI drug-discovery startup — a departure the company calls amicable.

Reid Hoffman, the LinkedIn co-founder who has sat on Microsoft's board since the company absorbed his social network a decade ago, will not stand for re-election at this year's annual shareholder meeting. He told Microsoft on June 2. He will remain a director until the meeting is held later this year.

The official explanation is clean: Hoffman is stepping down to go "founder mode" with Manas, his AI-powered drug-discovery startup. He made the announcement on his "Possible" podcast, recorded with Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella — an exit that, in its staging, left little room for acrimony. The SEC filing is equally anodyne: the departure is not the result of any disagreement over the company's operations, policies, or practices.

A lot has happened in ten years

When Microsoft closed the $26.2 billion LinkedIn deal in December 2016 — then its largest acquisition — Hoffman came with it, joining the board formally in March 2017. Microsoft stock has risen 570 percent since that close, against a 230 percent gain for the S&P 500 over the same span. The LinkedIn integration absorbed quietly into Microsoft's enterprise stack and long ago passed into the category of "good deal" rather than "complicated bet."

Since then the board's AI agenda has grown considerably more tangled. Hoffman was on the board when Microsoft made its first $1 billion investment in OpenAI in 2019. He was also a co-founder of Inflection AI, and he remained a director in 2023 when Microsoft structured a $650 million acqui-hire deal to absorb Inflection's talent — including co-founder Mustafa Suleyman, who now runs Microsoft AI. That arrangement drew regulatory scrutiny and raised pointed governance questions. Earlier that same year Hoffman had stepped down from OpenAI's own board, citing conflicts between his director role and his investment portfolio at Greylock.

The pattern is less a scandal than a straightforward map of how entangled the AI ecosystem's early power brokers have become. Hoffman has been simultaneously a Microsoft director, an OpenAI board member, an Inflection co-founder, a Greylock investor, and now a Manas chairman. At some point the chairs run out.

Manas has raised more than $50 million across two seed rounds backed by General Catalyst. Its CEO is Dr. Siddhartha Mukherjee — oncologist and Pulitzer Prize-winning author of The Emperor of All Maladies — and Hoffman frames its ambition in terms of "Move 37 AI": systems that surpass human creativity in chemistry the way AlphaGo's infamous nineteenth move surpassed human intuition in Go. Whether that framing describes near-term progress or an investor-pitch metaphor will become clearer as the company moves toward clinical-stage work.

For Microsoft, the practical question is board composition after the 2026 annual meeting — and whether the open seat goes to someone whose primary AI relationship is with Microsoft itself, rather than spread across the whole map.
