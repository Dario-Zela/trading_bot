Airbus pushes A321neo deliveries into 2027 and 2028 as supply chain strain deepens

A Wizz Air Airbus A321neo on the apron at Budapest Ferenc Liszt International Airport — DavidivardiIL / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Airbus is pushing A321neo deliveries into 2027-28 as Pratt & Whitney's engine backlog deepens a structural supply crisis that already cut Q1 narrow-body output 24% year-on-year.

Airbus has begun notifying customers that A321neo deliveries scheduled for 2027 and 2028 will slip by a few months — the latest indication that supply-chain constraints on the planemaker's most commercially important product are structural rather than cyclical. Bloomberg reported the customer notifications on 7 June; Reuters was unable to immediately verify the accounts.

The A321neo, the longest and highest-margin variant of the A320 family, is the most affected. That delays are now reaching this far into the order book matters: previous schedule slips had largely clustered near the nearer end of delivery commitments. Their spread into 2027 and 2028 suggests the production ramp-up Airbus has been promising since 2024 is encountering deeper resistance than the company has publicly acknowledged.

A numbers problem that won't resolve quickly

The quarterly data are unambiguous. In the first quarter of 2026, Airbus delivered 81 A320-family jets — down from 106 in the same period of 2025, a 24% year-on-year drop on the product line that drives roughly three-quarters of its commercial revenues. Total commercial deliveries were 114, against 136 a year earlier. Group revenues fell 7% to €12.7 billion; adjusted EBIT dropped 52% to €300 million. The company posted a €2.5 billion cash outflow for the quarter.

The central constraint is Pratt & Whitney. The American engine-maker halted new GTF production in 2024 to address manufacturing quality defects, and the backlog has since ballooned to more than 1,500 engines across the installed fleet and order book. By May 2026, airlines were cannibalising near-new A320neo aircraft to recover GTF engines — a measure that says more about supply scarcity than any disclosure could. Airbus filed a damages claim against Pratt & Whitney in March. In February, chief executive Guillaume Faury said the engine-maker's "failure to commit to the number of engines ordered by Airbus is negatively impacting this year's guidance."

Some relief may be coming. Pratt & Whitney's upgraded GTF Advantage engine received EASA certification in April 2026. But certification and meaningful production volume are separated by months of tooling and supplier qualification. Airbus has already pushed its target of 70–75 A320-family deliveries per month back to end-2027 — a delay of at least a year against earlier forecasts.

For 2026, Airbus has held its full-year guidance of approximately 870 commercial deliveries, alongside adjusted EBIT of around €7.5 billion and free cash flow of roughly €4.5 billion before customer financing. That guidance survived the Q1 miss intact. Whether it survives a second weak quarter depends heavily on whether Pratt & Whitney can accelerate engine shipments in the second half.

For investors, the news of fresh delays is unlikely to constitute a genuine shock. AIR.PA has been trading around €178–179 — well below the analyst consensus of roughly €210 — and the market has spent much of 2026 pricing in execution risk after a string of similar announcements. A guidance cut would be a materially different signal. The next hard data point is Airbus's Q2 delivery count, due in early July. If A320-family output does not recover meaningfully from Q1's 81 units, the 870-target will face serious scrutiny ahead of the summer earnings season.
