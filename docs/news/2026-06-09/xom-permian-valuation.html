Exxon's Permian economics are sound, but the premium price tag may keep buyers waiting

A pumpjack south of Midland, Texas — the heart of ExxonMobil's Permian Basin operations — Eric Kounce / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
XOM's Permian breakevens ($63–$69/bbl) are well below current WTI, but a 9.90x EV/EBITDA premium versus 6.44x for the sector leaves little margin for error.

West Texas Intermediate was trading around $93 a barrel on Tuesday, caught between ceasefire optimism and lingering Hormuz anxiety. For ExxonMobil shareholders, that band is comfortable territory: the company's Permian Basin operations break even at $69 per barrel in the Midland sub-basin and $63 in the Delaware — margins wide enough to absorb a meaningful correction in crude before the economics go negative. The question facing prospective buyers is not whether the business is good. It plainly is. The question is whether the market has already said so, loudly, in the share price.

The Permian case

Exxon's position in the Permian is a direct consequence of its $60 billion acquisition of Pioneer Natural Resources in 2024, which more than doubled its footprint across the basin. The company is on course to grow Permian output to 1.8 million oil-equivalent barrels per day this year, a figure that would make the basin alone a significant producing entity by global standards. On the operational side, the company has leaned into lightweight proppant technology that it says can lift individual well recoveries by up to 20% — a meaningful edge in a basin where incremental efficiency determines whether margins expand or merely hold.

The balance sheet reinforces the picture. Exxon carries a debt-to-capitalisation ratio of 15.4%, roughly half the 29.6% industry average, giving it room to sustain its buyback programme and a dividend record that stretches 43 consecutive years of annual increases at a 5.8% average rate.

All of this is real. None of it is secret.

Where the valuation sits

Exxon trades at 9.90 times trailing twelve-month EV/EBITDA. The broader integrated oil industry trades at 6.44 times. Chevron, the closest peer by scale and Permian exposure, sits at 9.72 times — a rough confirmation that quality operators command a premium, though Exxon's spread over its own sector is still notable. BP, by contrast, carries a 3.20 times multiple, a reflection of its messier balance sheet and restructuring overhang rather than a buying signal for XOM.

The premium is not irrational, but it is demanding. At 9.90 times, the market is already pricing in steady execution on the 1.8 million boe target, continued cost discipline, and no meaningful deterioration in crude. That last assumption carries its own weather risk: WTI's current elevated level reflects, in part, a geopolitical risk premium tied to Strait of Hormuz tensions. A durable ceasefire between Iran and Israel, or a resolution in the Gulf, could strip $10 to $15 from the barrel price — which would compress margins without affecting Exxon's fundamental cost position, but would almost certainly compress the multiple further.

Zacks rates XOM a Hold. The consensus twelve-month price target sits at $169.91, implying roughly 12% upside from Tuesday's close near $151. That return is adequate, not compelling, and assumes the premium holds.

The next hard data point comes with second-quarter production figures, which will either validate the 1.8 million boe trajectory or invite questions about whether Pioneer's integration is tracking to plan. Until then, the stock is not broken — it is expensive, and patience has a case.
