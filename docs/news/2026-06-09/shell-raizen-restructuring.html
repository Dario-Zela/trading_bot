Shell backs Raizen's $12.6bn restructuring in a strategic bet on Brazilian biofuels

Sugarcane plantation in São Paulo state, the heartland of Brazil's ethanol industry and home to Raízen's 35 production plants — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell injects R$3.5bn in fresh capital as Raízen closes Brazil's largest-ever corporate debt restructuring — a R$65bn out-of-court workout — signalling continued commitment to biofuels.

Shell's R$3.5 billion injection into Raízen — announced this week alongside a broader creditor deal to restructure 65 billion reais ($12.57 billion) of the Brazilian energy company's obligations — is the clearest public signal yet of how the oil major intends to deploy capital during the energy transition. It is also, depending on how one reads the numbers, either a vote of confidence in Brazil's biofuels industry or an expensive commitment to a bet that has already gone badly wrong.

A wager on cellulosic ethanol went sideways

Raízen was formed in 2011 as a joint venture between Shell and Brazilian conglomerate Cosan, consolidating their sugar, ethanol, and fuel-distribution assets into a single entity. The scale is not trivial: 35 production plants, crushing capacity of 105 million tonnes of sugarcane per year, and a network of more than 7,000 fuel-retail sites across Brazil and Argentina under the Shell brand. Revenue reached $45.5 billion in the 2023/24 crop year, making it the third-largest energy company in Brazil by turnover.

For most of the decade following its formation, the venture was profitable. Then Raízen made a large bet on second-generation, or cellulosic, ethanol — a technology that converts sugarcane bagasse, the fibrous waste left after juice extraction, into fuel. The economics were compelling on paper: higher yields from the same crop, a premium price for lower-carbon output, and proprietary technology developed in-house. In 2023, Raízen opened what it described as the world's largest E2G plant, at its Bonfirm Bioenergy Park in Guariba, São Paulo state, with a nameplate capacity of 82 million litres per year.

The market did not pay the premium at the pace the company needed. Corn-based ethanol, expanding rapidly in Brazil under government support, compressed margins across the sector. Then came the weather: the 2024/25 harvest yielded just 77.5 million tonnes of sugarcane, 6% below even Raízen's most pessimistic internal forecasts, as drought and crop fires cut both volume and quality. Rising interest rates — Brazil's Selic rate climbed sharply through 2024 — inflated financing costs on a balance sheet that had grown aggressive. Net debt ballooned from R$13.8 billion (1.3 times EBITDA) in 2021/22 to R$55.3 billion by the third quarter of crop year 2025/26. At 5.3 times EBITDA, leverage left almost no buffer. A R$15.6 billion net loss in that quarter, including an R$11 billion non-cash impairment provision triggered by credit-rating downgrades, confirmed what the debt markets had already priced in.

Shell writes the cheque — and defines the terms

The out-of-court restructuring announced this week covers substantially all of Raízen's unsecured obligations. Creditors holding more than 75% of affected debt signed on — the legal threshold required for a Brazilian extrajudicial workout to bind holdouts — in what constitutes the largest corporate debt restructuring ever recorded in the country. The terms offer creditors a choice: convert 45% of claims into Raízen equity at R$0.50 per unit, with the remaining 55% rolled into new instruments at extended maturities; or exchange the full balance for new debt. The structure is designed to reduce near-term cash obligations while diluting existing shareholders — the price Raízen's equity holders pay for avoiding a full judicial insolvency process.

Shell's R$3.5 billion contribution is fresh capital, not a refinancing of existing exposure. That distinction matters. The company is not merely restructuring what it already has at risk — it is writing a new cheque, roughly $577 million at current exchange rates, into a business that has just reported a record loss and seen its credit rating stripped of investment grade. The company framed the move in measured terms: "We will continue to work with Raízen's management team, its creditors and other stakeholders to support implementation of the plan." Rubens Ometto, Raízen's chairman, may contribute a further R$500 million through his vehicle Aguassanta Participações, though that commitment was described as conditional.

Notably, Shell's stated preference is to maintain Raízen's integrated structure — sugar, ethanol, fuel retail, and bioenergy under one roof — rather than break it apart for a sum-of-parts sale. The company stopped short of ruling out asset disposals once financial stability is achieved, leaving the door open for selective sales if the recovery falters.

What comes next

The deal converts a debt crisis into a restructuring process — a meaningful distinction but not a resolution. Raízen still carries more than R$55 billion in net debt even after the workout, and the extended maturities give management time rather than relief from underlying leverage. Three variables will determine whether the breathing room translates into genuine recovery.

First, the sugarcane harvest: a return toward the 90-million-tonne range that analysts consider normal for Raízen's planted area would materially improve cash generation and reduce the unit-cost penalty from underutilised crushing capacity. Second, interest rates: a sustained easing of the Selic rate, widely anticipated by Brazilian economists but not yet delivered at scale, would reduce financing costs on the new debt instruments. Third, the E2G price premium: whether cellulosic ethanol commands a durable margin over conventional first-generation output remains unresolved. If Brazilian fuel policy evolves toward lower-carbon mandates — the trajectory the government has signalled — the E2G plants could become the company's most valuable long-term assets. If corn ethanol remains the dominant substitute, the economics of those plants look far harder to justify.

Shell's capital commitment signals that the oil major has not abandoned the thesis. For investors watching the energy transition, that may be the most consequential data point in the deal — not the headline figure, but what it reveals about where a major oil company is placing its long-term bets when the cycle turns against it.
