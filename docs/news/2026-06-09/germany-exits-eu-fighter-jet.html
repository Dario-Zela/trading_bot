Germany pulls out of joint fighter jet programme with France in blow to European defence sovereignty

The full-scale FCAS Next Generation Fighter mockup at the 2019 Paris Air Show — the programme's most public moment, before nine years of deadlock ended it — Tiraden / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Germany and France have scrapped the €100bn FCAS stealth fighter, exposing Europe's structural failure to coordinate defence procurement just as rearmament spending hits record highs.

Germany has formally withdrawn from the Future Combat Air System, the joint stealth fighter programme it had been developing with France and Spain since 2017 — ending Europe's most ambitious defence project on the eve of the ILA Berlin air show. The decision, reached by Chancellor Friedrich Merz and President Emmanuel Macron on the sidelines of an EU-Western Balkans summit in Montenegro on June 6, kills the core manned-aircraft component of a programme that was supposed to equip European air forces from the 2040s onwards. Its price tag: €100 billion.

The project was conceived as both an industrial feat and a statement. Launched at the 2018 ILA Berlin air show by Merkel and Macron, and joined by Spain the following year, FCAS (or SCAF — Système de Combat Aérien du Futur — in French) was to replace France's Rafale jets and Germany and Spain's Eurofighter Typhoons with a sixth-generation aircraft: stealthy, networked, accompanied by unmanned wingmen. It was the kind of continental co-production that European leaders imagined when they talked about strategic autonomy — a proof that the continent could design and build its own most sophisticated weapons without American components or American sign-off.

What actually resulted was nine years of boardroom warfare.

The workshare that would not divide

The central dispute was industrial rather than political. France's Dassault Aviation, which has designed every French combat aircraft since the Second World War, insisted it should lead the fighter aircraft as prime contractor. Airbus Defence and Space, representing Germany and Spain, refused — arguing, with some justification, that such an arrangement had never been what was agreed and would reduce its companies to the role of subcontractors on a programme they were co-funding.

Dassault reportedly sought a workshare of up to 80%, citing its expertise in fighter design. Airbus held firm on parity. Dassault CEO Eric Trappier had already warned publicly that the project was "dead" if Airbus refused to accept the French company's terms. As late as February 2026, the programme had no confirmed timeline after missing a year-end milestone. A mediation process — launched over a Macron-Merz dinner in Brussels on March 18 — lasted exactly one month. On April 18, the German mediator concluded that a jointly built crewed fighter was no longer feasible.

Underneath the corporate manoeuvring lay a more fundamental problem. France's military requirements for the next-generation aircraft include the ability to carry nuclear weapons — reflecting France's independent nuclear deterrent, the force de frappe — and to operate from aircraft carriers, which the French Navy operates and Germany does not. Germany needed a platform for air superiority, not sea-launched nuclear strikes. Merz put the matter plainly in earlier discussions: if the two countries could not reconcile those differences, the project could not continue. They could not.

Spending more, coordinating less

The timing is painful. European defence spending is at its highest level since the Cold War. Germany expects its annual military budget to exceed €100 billion in 2026, a figure expected to approach €153 billion by 2029. NATO members have committed to a 5% of GDP target by 2035. Governments across the continent are scrambling to fill capability gaps exposed by Russia's war in Ukraine and — rather differently — by the Trump administration's intermittent questioning of American security commitments.

Yet the FCAS collapse illustrates the structural problem that more money alone does not solve. European NATO members currently operate twelve different main battle tank platforms; the United States operates one. A February 2026 McKinsey analysis found that equipment stocks in European NATO countries remain below their 2021 levels despite record budgets. The incentive structure — politically sensitive procurement, national industrial champions, home-bias spending — consistently pulls against consolidation, even when consolidation is the stated goal.

For defence contractors, the implications are direct. Fragmentation tends to produce smaller, costlier production runs, reducing economies of scale. It can also drive countries toward ready-made American alternatives: Germany has already purchased F-35s, and the F-35's deep integration with US logistics, software, and export controls means that each aircraft sold to Europe is also a long-term dependency on American political decisions.

What Germany does next

Not everything is cancelled. Macron and Merz agreed that the broader FCAS architecture — particularly the "combat cloud", a digital network designed to link manned aircraft, drones, and ground systems into an integrated network — will continue under the FCAS name. France has indicated it will pursue an enhanced Rafale F5 variant that can take the plane through to at least 2060, and is separately developing an unmanned combat air vehicle with Dassault.

For Germany, the options are less tidy. Berlin has had exploratory conversations about joining the Global Combat Air Programme, the rival sixth-generation effort led by Britain with Italy and Japan — a project that has moved faster partly because its industrial governance is cleaner. GCAP has consolidated its development under the Edgewing joint venture and is targeting a 2035 service date, a decade earlier than FCAS had planned. Germany is also examining deeper collaboration with Sweden's Saab, which produces the Gripen, or an expanded role for the Eurofighter.

The specific path Berlin chooses will shape European defence industrial politics for a generation. If Germany moves toward GCAP, it brings a large procurement and a large wallet into a programme that currently excludes continental Europe's biggest economy. If it buys more F-35s, it deepens the Atlantic dependency that FCAS was designed to reduce.

The next concrete signal will come at ILA Berlin itself, where industry responses to the FCAS cancellation will be closely watched — and where the German government may give clearer indication of which direction it intends to turn.
