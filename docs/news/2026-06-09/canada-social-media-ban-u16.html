Canada plans to ban under-16s from social media, sources say

Canada's Centre Block on Parliament Hill, Ottawa, where the online harms bill is expected to be introduced this week. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Canada's Carney government is set to table a social media ban for under-16s this week, but enforcement details remain unresolved and implementation challenges loom.

Canada's federal government is preparing to table legislation this week that would prohibit children under 16 from using social media platforms — a measure that has been widely anticipated since Prime Minister Mark Carney's Liberals signalled their intent earlier this year, but whose practical details remain thin.

The bill, set to be introduced on Wednesday, would fold the social media restriction into a broader online harms package. It would also establish a new Canadian digital regulator tasked with setting safety standards for platforms. Sources told The Globe and Mail and Global News that platforms meeting those standards could be exempt from the ban — a provision designed to give companies an incentive to comply rather than simply block Canadian teenagers and walk away.

Long on intent, short on mechanics

The obvious question — how exactly Ottawa plans to verify that a 15-year-old isn't signing up for Instagram — does not yet have a tidy answer. Based on Australia's model, options include requiring users to upload government-issued identification, using facial recognition software, or inferring age from behavioural signals such as friend networks and language patterns. Each method carries its own baggage.

Michael Geist, a Canada Research Chair in internet and e-commerce law at the University of Ottawa, has described age verification as "fraught with practical difficulties." Facial recognition, he notes, can be inaccurate and biased against racialized groups; mandatory ID uploads raise obvious privacy concerns for the broader population of adult users who would also be swept into the system.

Christopher Dietzel of Western University has pushed the critique further, arguing that an age gate "doesn't actually remove the harm. It does nothing to hold companies accountable" for the content and design choices that critics say damage young users in the first place.

Public opinion, at least, is clearly behind the government. An Angus Reid survey found that roughly 75 per cent of Canadians support restricting social media access for under-16s, a figure that spans political lines.

Not a first-mover

Canada would not be breaking new ground. Australia legislated a comparable ban in December 2025 — making it the first country to do so — and Malaysia followed shortly after. The UK, South Korea, and Spain are among the jurisdictions examining similar restrictions. The regulatory direction is clear; execution is where each government has stumbled.

Australia's experience is instructive and not entirely encouraging. Research published after the law took effect found widespread circumvention, with many teenagers bypassing age-verification systems through virtual private networks or simply lying about their birth year. A separate study noted that the restrictions had inadvertently cut young Australians off from online news.

On the domestic side, Manitoba had already become the first Canadian province to announce a youth social media ban, and Ontario has moved to limit device use in schools — pressure from the provinces that likely accelerated Ottawa's timeline.

Platforms in the crosshairs

The companies with the most to lose are familiar: Meta's Instagram and Facebook, TikTok, YouTube, and Snapchat together account for the bulk of teenage screen time. None of them has commented publicly on the Canadian bill. The market impact will depend entirely on what the final legislation requires and how aggressively the new regulator enforces it — details that will emerge only once the bill is published and debated.

The next concrete moment to watch is the bill's formal introduction on Wednesday. After that, the legislative calendar, any opposition amendments, and the regulator's eventual rule-making process will determine whether this becomes enforceable policy or an aspirational statement about childhood that proves as difficult to implement as it was popular to announce.
