Bumble divides users by scrapping its women-first chat rule

Whitney Wolfe Herd, Bumble's founder and CEO, at TechCrunch Disrupt San Francisco in 2018. — Steve Jennings / TechCrunch, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bumble scrapped its 12-year women-message-first rule after 66% of women surveyed said they'd rather men went first — splitting users as revenue and paid users slide.

Bumble spent twelve years telling women to make the first move. This week it told them they no longer have to.

On Tuesday the dating app confirmed that anyone — regardless of gender — can now send the opening message after a match, scrapping the rule that women had to message first or the match would vanish. Bumble also stretched the window to reply to a new match from 24 hours to 72. It is the clearest break yet from the feature the company built its entire identity on when it launched in 2014 as, in founder Whitney Wolfe Herd's phrase, the alternative to Tinder that put women in control of who messaged them.

In one sentence: Bumble has scrapped the women-message-first rule that defined it for twelve years, splitting its users just as the app's paying subscriber base and stock price keep shrinking.

The rule that built the brand

Wolfe Herd, who had previously worked at Tinder, founded Bumble on a simple pitch: in straight matches, only the woman could send the first message, and she had 24 hours to do it before the match expired. It was marketed as a corrective to the unsolicited-message problem on other apps, and it worked as a business story — Bumble went public in 2021 at close to $79 a share, largely on the strength of that pitch and Wolfe Herd's own story as the industry's most visible founder.

The company had already been sanding down the edges of the rule. In 2024 it introduced "Opening Moves," which let women set a pre-written prompt for a match to answer instead of writing something themselves — a soft admission that always messaging first was, for many women, more chore than empowerment.

What actually changed, and why

Bumble said the decision followed an internal trial and a survey of more than 2,200 US women in which 66% said they would rather men sent the first message, citing stress and the burden of always initiating. The company reported that opening messages became more evenly split between men and women during testing, and that chat activity rose overall.

Wolfe Herd framed the shift as expanding the brand's founding idea rather than abandoning it: "While women making the first move was a radical idea, being women-first was never about prescribing just one way to connect." She said users wanted "more flexibility, less pressure, and more opportunities to create real, meaningful connections."

The timing is hard to separate from Bumble's numbers. In its second-quarter results, published August 5, revenue fell 15.2% year-on-year to $210.5 million and paying users dropped 16.4% to 3.2 million, from 3.8 million a year earlier. Goldman Sachs and RBC both cut their price targets on the stock in the days after — Goldman to $3.50, RBC to $4 — and shares that once traded near $79 have been changing hands below $3. The messaging announcement itself gave the stock a rare lift, up roughly 5% on the day.

A divided user base

Reaction split along familiar lines in Bumble's own Reddit community, r/Bumble. Some users said the rule had outlived its usefulness and welcomed being spared the pressure to always write first. Others argued it was the one thing that made Bumble legible as a product: a woman-initiated message was a clear, low-noise signal of real interest, in a way that an inbox open to anyone from the first minute is not. "If you women want men to message first, use a different app," one commenter wrote, a line that captures the complaint succinctly — that Bumble is dissolving into the pool of apps it was created to stand apart from.

That is the harder problem underneath the messaging tweak. Dating apps across the market are fighting the same slump — user fatigue, subscription cancellations, a generation less willing to pay for swiping — and are converging on similar fixes: AI-assisted profiles, in-person events, group hangouts. Bumble itself has been testing a paid group-meetup feature called Plans. When the product features start to look interchangeable, the thing that used to differentiate Bumble was the founding rule itself. Removing it doesn't just change a feature; it removes the one story the brand told that competitors couldn't copy.

Bumble's next quarterly report, due in the autumn, will show whether the messaging change and the extended reply window move the paying-user number, or whether the company has simply traded its founding story for a feature set that now looks a lot like everyone else's.
