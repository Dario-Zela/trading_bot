EU begins enforcing AI Act, with disclosure duties and fines up to €15m

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which now enforces the AI Act's transparency rules through its AI Office. — EmDee / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
EU regulators began enforcing AI Act transparency rules on 2 August, with fines up to €15m for chatbots and deepfakes that fail to identify themselves as AI.

Brussels has spent three years telling the world how AI ought to behave. As of this month, it has started checking.

On 2 August, the European Commission's AI Office and national regulators across the bloc began enforcing a new batch of rules under the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act, the first real transparency mandate to hit AI companies operating in Europe. Chatbots must now tell users, plainly, that they are not human. Deepfakes — AI-edited or AI-generated images, audio and video — must be labelled as such. Content generated by systems like image and text generators needs a machine-readable marker so platforms and regulators can detect it later. Get it wrong, and the fine can run to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is larger.

That penalty ceiling puts the AI Act in the same weight class as the EU's data-protection regime, GDPR, which reshaped how the world's tech companies handle personal data after it took effect in 2018. The comparison is not accidental — Brussels built the AI Act's enforcement architecture, including the extraterritorial reach and the turnover-linked fines, directly on that template.

What actually changed on 2 August

The obligations that took effect fall under Article 50 of the Act, and they are narrower than "the AI Act is now enforced" headlines suggest. Four categories of AI system are covered: anything that talks directly to people (chatbots, voice assistants), anything that generates or alters images, audio, video or text, anything that reads emotion or categorises people from biometric data, and deepfakes or AI-written text on matters of public interest.

"As enforcement begins, we are taking an important step towards AI that people and businesses can understand and trust, and whose benefits are shared widely across our society," said Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, in the Commission's announcement.

One detail is tripping up companies that assumed their AI vendor had it covered: the disclosure duty sits with whoever deploys the system, not whoever built it. OpenAI cannot put a disclosure banner inside a customer's chatbot; the company running that chatbot has to do it, and cannot contract the obligation away. For firms that bolted a third-party AI model onto a customer-service or trading-support tool without touching the interface, that is now their liability, not their vendor's.

About 190 organisations — including Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic and Mistral — signed a voluntary Code of Practice ahead of the deadline, giving them a documented path to show compliance rather than waiting to be tested by a regulator's interpretation of the law. Firms that generate synthetic content and were already on the market before 2 August get a grace period to 2 December 2026 to add the machine-readable marking; the disclosure duties themselves do not wait.

What didn't happen

The more consequential half of the Act was quietly pushed back. Rules governing "high-risk" AI systems — those used in hiring, education, credit scoring, migration and border management — were due to apply alongside the transparency rules but have been postponed to 2 December 2027, sixteen months later, with Brussels citing the need for technical standards that are not yet finished.

Digital-rights groups were not placated. Stefi Richani of the Equinox Initiative, which campaigns on migrant rights, said the delay "will increase surveillance and discrimination" for people already navigating asylum and border systems, arguing it spares industry from scrutiny while leaving the people most exposed to algorithmic decisions without protection for well over a year. It is also the second major postponement in the Act's rollout, and campaigners worry it sets a pattern: transparency rules that are cheap for companies to bolt on go live on schedule, while the rules that would force changes to how high-stakes systems are actually built keep sliding.

The fines that took effect this month, in other words, are the smaller of the Act's two headline tiers. A separate ban on outright prohibited practices — social scoring, manipulative AI, unrestricted facial-recognition databases — has carried penalties of up to €35 million or 7% of turnover since August 2025, a full year before the transparency rules arrived.

Why it reaches beyond consumer apps

The AI Act does not brand algorithmic trading itself as "high-risk" — that territory still belongs to existing financial regulation like MiFID II, and the Act layers on top of it rather than replacing it. But the transparency duties are indifferent to sector. A robo-advisor that talks to retail clients, an AI research-summary tool a bank ships to customers, or a fintech chatbot answering account queries all fall inside Article 50 the moment they interact with a person or generate content — regardless of whether the underlying firm thinks of itself as a tech company or a bank.

That is the quieter story inside the AI Act's rollout: obligations that once sat with an AI lab now sit with whichever company put the interface in front of a customer. Any financial firm that has spent the past two years bolting generative features onto customer-facing products has, whether it registered the fact or not, just inherited a compliance duty it cannot hand back.

What's next

Two dates matter from here. On 2 December 2026, the grace period for machine-readable content marking ends, forcing generative-AI providers already in the EU market to retrofit detection tags rather than build them in from scratch. And on 2 December 2027, the postponed high-risk rules — covering hiring algorithms, credit scoring and biometric categorisation — are due to take effect, assuming Brussels does not push them back again. Whether the technical standards regulators promised actually arrive in time will say more about the Act's credibility than this month's chatbot disclosures ever will.
