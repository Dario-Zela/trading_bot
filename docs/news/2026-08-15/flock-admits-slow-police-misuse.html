Flock surveillance boss admits firm was too slow to act on police misuse

A Flock Safety license-plate reader camera and solar panel, part of the roughly 100,000-camera network used by US police departments. — Julian Focareta / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Flock Safety's CEO told the BBC the company took too long to stop police misusing its surveillance cameras, as cities cancel contracts and its $8.4bn valuation faces scrutiny.

Garrett Langley, the chief executive of surveillance company Flock Safety, was asked by the BBC a simple question: had it taken his firm too long to protect its cameras from misuse by the police officers who use them. His answer was just as simple. "Yeah... yes."

That admission lands after months of reporting — led by the Washington Post and picked up widely since — documenting officers around the US tapping into Flock's network of license-plate cameras, drones and gunfire sensors for reasons that had nothing to do with solving crimes. The Post identified at least 46 officers accused, charged or convicted of misusing the system, several of them for tracking former or current romantic partners. In one case cited by the BBC, cameras were used to help trace a woman who had had an abortion.

What Flock is changing

Flock's cameras now cover roughly 100,000 locations across the US, reading number plates and flagging vehicles for police departments that subscribe to the service. Access to that data was, until recently, governed mostly by trust: officers logged searches, but audits of why they searched were optional. Under the changes announced this week, that stops being optional. Every search will need to be tied to a specific case code and offense type before it runs, with an emergency override for genuine urgency. Default data retention drops from 30 days to seven, though police can extend it for active investigations. And by the end of the year, every law-enforcement customer will be required to switch on Flock's anomaly-detection tool, which flags unusual search patterns — a feature only about a third of agencies had bothered to activate voluntarily.

Langley described the situation with the kind of hindsight that sounds better in a press statement than it does to someone who was tracked by an ex-partner using a police login: "Of course, we could have done more earlier."

The bigger squeeze

The misuse cases have already cost Flock customers. More than 50 cities have cancelled their contracts this year, according to the company's own count, and separate tallies put the number of local governments rejecting or dropping automated license-plate readers even higher once county and township deals are included. The American Civil Liberties Union has run an active "Get the Flock Out" campaign, and it sued alongside the Electronic Frontier Foundation last November over San Jose's use of the cameras. A second front has opened around federal immigration enforcement: reporting has shown Flock data reaching ICE and Customs and Border Protection without the knowledge of the local police departments that installed the cameras, prompting some of the contract cancellations on its own.

None of this is happening to a small startup. Flock raised $275 million last year at a $7.5 billion valuation, backed by Andreessen Horowitz, Founders Fund and Kleiner Perkins among others, and a subsequent share sale this spring pushed its implied worth to roughly $8.4 billion. It has not filed to go public, but investors betting on an eventual IPO are watching a company whose core product — access to a nationwide surveillance network — depends on public trust that its own executives now concede was allowed to erode.

What to watch

The test for Flock's new safeguards is whether case-code requirements and mandatory anomaly detection actually stop officers from running personal searches, not just whether they log them more visibly. City councils weighing renewal decisions over the next few months — and any state legislature moving to regulate ALPR data sharing with federal agencies — will be the next checkpoints for a company whose valuation assumes the surveillance-as-a-service model keeps expanding, not retreating.
