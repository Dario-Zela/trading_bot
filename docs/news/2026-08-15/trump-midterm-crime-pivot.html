Trump pivots midterm messaging to crime as war and economy weigh on GOP

President Trump, whose midterm messaging has shifted from the Iran conflict toward crime statistics as gas prices and war fatigue weigh on GOP prospects. — Daniel Torok / White House (public domain)
In one sentence
As war-driven gas prices and inflation sour voters, Trump has pivoted his midterm pitch from Iran to crime statistics, a strategy retreat GOP strategists read as alarm.

President Trump spent Friday at the Nassau County Police Academy in Garden City, New York, standing beside Attorney General Todd Blanche and FBI Director Kash Patel to tout a national drop in violent crime. FBI figures released this week show violent crime fell 9.3% from 2024 to 2025, with murder down 18.1% and robbery down 18.5%. Trump put his own gloss on the numbers: over 3,100 fewer murders and 1.1 million fewer violent crimes than the year before he took office, he said, while Blanche noted the Justice Department has charged 170,000 people since Trump returned to the White House.

It was a pointed choice of subject. With eleven weeks until the midterms, Trump's two dominant storylines — a five-and-a-half-month air campaign against Iran and a cost-of-living squeeze that has followed it — are both liabilities. Crime, by contrast, is terrain where voters have traditionally trusted Republicans. The venue carried its own signal: Trump used the trip to endorse Nassau County Executive Bruce Blakeman for New York governor, a day after Elise Stefanik suspended her own campaign, calling Blakeman "MAGA all the way" for his record on migrant crime and border enforcement.

The war came home through the gas pump

The pivot follows a rough stretch tracing directly back to Operation Epic Fury, the US-Israeli air campaign against Iran that began February 28. Iran's retaliation — missile and drone strikes on energy infrastructure and a near-closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a corridor for roughly a fifth of the world's oil — pushed US gasoline above $4 a gallon this month, the highest in four years and about 50 cents above pre-war levels. The optics are unkind to Trump personally: in his February 27 State of the Union address, delivered the night before the campaign began, he boasted that gas was "below $2.30 a gallon in most states" and "$1.99 in some places." He has since waved off the increase as "a very small price to pay," while press secretary Karoline Leavitt has called it a "short-term disruption for long-term gain."

Voters are not convinced. Polling this month found 54% of Trump's own 2024 voters name cost of living as their top concern, with a plurality of non-MAGA Trump supporters citing rising prices as his central failure. An NBC News survey earlier this year put approval of his handling of inflation at just 36%, against 62% disapproval — his weakest marks on any issue, worse even than the war itself.

That gap between message and mood is the real story. A White House pivoting toward crime statistics eleven weeks before an election is, in effect, conceding that its preferred arguments on Iran and prices aren't landing. The next test comes with September's jobs and inflation data, along with any movement toward a ceasefire or reopening of the Strait — either of which would do more for Republican prospects than any speech at a police academy.
