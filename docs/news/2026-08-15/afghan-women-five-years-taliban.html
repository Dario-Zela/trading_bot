Afghan women describe unrecognisable lives five years into Taliban rule

A woman in a burqa on a street in Kabul; restrictions on movement, dress and speech have deepened steadily since 2021. — Ninara / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
A BBC investigation finds Afghan women five years into Taliban rule confined largely indoors, locked out of work and school, with the exclusion now costing the economy an estimated $920m.

Five years ago this month, as Taliban fighters entered Kabul and the Western-backed government collapsed in a matter of days, the group's spokesmen promised a version of Islamic rule that would respect women's rights "within the framework of Islamic law." A BBC investigation marking the anniversary finds what that framework has actually meant in practice: girls barred from school beyond the sixth grade, women locked out of almost every profession, and a woman's presence in public life reduced, in the words of one UN agency, to something closer to erasure than restriction.

The accounting is now detailed enough to be almost bureaucratic in its precision. Afghanistan's de facto authorities have issued more than 100 edicts, decrees and directives since 2021 touching nearly every aspect of women's and girls' lives — from where they can walk to whether their voices may be heard by men outside their families. It remains the only country in the world that formally bars girls and women from education beyond the primary level, according to UNESCO, which puts the number now shut out of secondary school at 2.4 million.

A life measured in what's forbidden

The restrictions read, cumulatively, like an inventory of subtraction. Women were told in November 2021 to stop attending civil service jobs; by January of this year the authorities had simply removed them from the government payroll, and some learned of their dismissal only when a salary payment failed to land. They have been barred from working for NGOs since December 2022 and for the United Nations since April 2023. Beauty salons — one of the last women-run commercial spaces — were ordered shut in 2023. Parks, gyms and public baths followed. Last December, the Taliban's supreme leader went further still, ordering that new residential buildings have no windows overlooking courtyards, kitchens or wells — anywhere, in short, that a woman might be seen going about her day, on the reasoning that the sight could lead to "obscene acts."

The BBC's reporters, visiting households across the country, describe what this adds up to day to day: a population effectively confined indoors. UN Women's own survey work backs that up — half of Afghan women now leave their homes only once or twice a month, against daily outings for the overwhelming majority of men. Ninety percent of women surveyed said they needed a male relative's permission simply to go outside.

Some of the coping mechanisms are quietly devastating. The BBC spoke to Lima, an 18-year-old from rural Afghanistan with a talent for singing she is no longer permitted to use in public. She fills her days reading novels and talking to an AI chatbot styled as a favourite celebrity — "to talk, ask questions, or simply treat as a friend," she said, adding that it lets her "give yourself a fake hope." Another woman, Freshta, told the broadcaster she has not left her house since the takeover; her last outing, she recalled, was "just a week before the Taliban government took over." A psychologist interviewed for the investigation, identified only as Zarina, described a population absorbing loss in layers — separation from loved ones, lost jobs, interrupted educations, futures quietly cancelled.

The economics of exclusion

What began as a human rights story has hardened into an economic one. Women's labour force participation in Afghanistan now stands at roughly 7%, against 84% for men — among the widest gender employment gaps anywhere in the world. The fall was sharp and early: female employment dropped by around a quarter in the eighteen months following the takeover, according to UN agency data, even as the number of women actively seeking work roughly quadrupled. Those still employed are concentrated in low-paid, insecure work, while 74% of women report spending significant time on unpaid domestic labour, compared with 3% of men.

The World Bank and UN agencies estimate the continued exclusion of women from the economy will cost Afghanistan roughly $920 million — about 5.8% of GDP — between 2024 and 2026 alone, on top of losses already absorbed. Nearly three-quarters of women-led organisations surveyed reported funding cuts in 2025, and more than half expect to suspend operations or close entirely within the year, as international donors — wary of legitimising the Taliban and squeezed by their own budget pressures — pull back. More than 10.7 million Afghan women and girls are assessed to need humanitarian assistance this year, a number that keeps climbing as aid keeps shrinking.

Fawzia Koofi, the exiled former deputy speaker of Afghanistan's parliament and one of the most prominent voices against the restrictions, has argued that the cumulative effect of the bans amounts to women being "deleted" from public life. The UN's human rights office has gone further, with its special rapporteur on Afghanistan concluding that the treatment of women and girls meets the legal threshold for gender persecution as a crime against humanity — the basis for the term now used increasingly in diplomatic circles: gender apartheid.

What the numbers signal beyond Afghanistan

None of this is happening in isolation. A country that has excluded half its working-age population from formal economic life is a country with a shrinking base of taxable activity, a growing reliance on informal and illicit markets, and a deepening dependence on remittances and humanitarian transfers to keep households afloat. That combination tends to produce exactly the kind of fragile-state dynamics — currency pressure, food insecurity, displacement — that spill across borders into Pakistan, Iran and Central Asia, and that eventually force the hand of donor governments and multilateral lenders who would rather not engage with Kabul at all.

The Taliban government has shown no sign of softening its position ahead of the anniversary; if anything, the pace of new edicts — the window ban came only in December — suggests the opposite. The next marker to watch is whether Western governments and the UN Security Council, which has periodically weighed sanctions relief and engagement track proposals, tie any further recognition or aid normalisation explicitly to a reversal on women's rights, or whether — as human rights lawyer Belquis Ahmadi has warned — the world simply learns to live with it.
