UK to soften EV sales mandate after carmaker lobbying

Nissan's Sunderland plant — the carmaker linked its future directly to the pace of the ZEV mandate — Wikimedia Commons / Geograph.org.uk
In one sentence
Ministers have opened a review that could cut the UK's 2030 EV sales target from 80% to as low as 50%, after sustained carmaker lobbying over fines and margins.

The government opened a ten-week consultation on Friday into whether to loosen the electric vehicle sales quotas carmakers must hit between 2027 and 2035 — the clearest sign yet that ministers are prepared to trade climate ambition for industrial peace with an automotive sector that has spent much of the year warning it cannot hit the numbers as written.

In one sentence: Ministers have opened a review that could cut the UK's 2030 electric car sales target from 80% to as low as 50%, after months of carmaker lobbying over fines and falling profits.

The Zero Emission Vehicle mandate, in force since 2024, requires an escalating share of each manufacturer's UK new-car sales to be battery electric: 33% this year, rising in steps to 80% by 2030 and 100% by 2035. Vans follow a parallel ladder topping out at 70% by 2030. Miss the number and a manufacturer owes the Treasury £12,000 for every non-compliant car sold — reduced from an original £15,000 after an earlier round of easing — or must buy spare compliance credits from a rival that has overshot.

A target running ahead of demand

On paper the industry is close to compliant: battery electric cars made up 27% of new registrations in July, up 49% on a year earlier, and total EVs on UK roads passed two million this summer. But 2025 told a more uncomfortable story — sales ended the year at 23.4% against a 28% goalpost — and the strong headline growth this year is widely attributed to manufacturers discounting EVs heavily to buy compliance, a strategy several have said is not sustainable at current margins.

The consultation, running to 23 October, sets out a menu of alternatives to the current 80%-by-2030 trajectory: hold the line but extend the flexibilities that let firms borrow against future years or bank credit from an under-shooting combustion fleet; or cut the 2030 target outright to 70%, 60% or 50%. Vans face a similar range, from the existing 70% down to 40%. The government insists the 2030 ban on new purely petrol and diesel car sales is untouched — only the annual EV quota that gets manufacturers there is up for negotiation.

Lobbying that named the stakes in jobs

The pressure campaign has been unusually specific for an industry dispute. Nissan tied the mandate directly to the future of its Sunderland plant, one of Europe's largest car factories and a totem of UK manufacturing employment; Jaguar Land Rover and Stellantis pressed for relief citing the cost of retooling production lines faster than consumer demand is moving; and Ford — Britain's biggest van seller — is the manufacturer most exposed to the non-compliance payments on the commercial-vehicle side, with electric van sales running at roughly half the level needed to hit this year's target. Toyota, BMW, Mazda and Subaru have also lobbied, mostly over the pace rather than the destination.

Mike Hawes, chief executive of the SMMT, the industry's trade body, has been the loudest single voice, arguing the mandate was "devised in very different market conditions" and now sits "too far ahead of underlying natural demand." He wants what he calls a "commercially sustainable transition" — his preferred framing for a target set closer to what manufacturers, rather than regulators, think the market will bear.

Transport Secretary Heidi Alexander, who has held the brief since 2024 and stayed in post when Andy Burnham replaced Keir Starmer as Prime Minister in July, framed the review as calibration rather than retreat: "It's right we keep targets under review to ensure they're practical and back British industry. The end goal hasn't changed — but we need to take business with us on the journey." Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds cast it as evidence-gathering: the consultation is about "listening to industry, examining the evidence and making sure the mandate continues supporting investment, innovation and competitiveness." The government's own framing cites "challenging and complex global economic conditions, including supply chain disruption and tariff and trade uncertainty" as the backdrop.

The charging industry's counter-case

Not everyone downstream of the policy wants it softened. Companies that have poured capital into charging infrastructure on the strength of the existing targets are warning that reopening them now undermines the investment case they made to lenders and shareholders. Vicky Read, chief executive of the trade body ChargeUK, said cutting the targets risks sending "the entire transition into a tailspin" and could leave Britain's reputation "as a market worth investing in" in tatters. Robin Heap of the charging operator Zest warned the review "risks undermining confidence at precisely the moment the market is gathering momentum," while Octopus EV's Gurjeet Grewal called a weaker mandate "exactly the wrong signal" and InstaVolt's Delvin Lane noted that "ultra-rapid charging investment doesn't happen on the back of uncertainty."

That split — automakers wanting relief, charge-point operators wanting certainty — is the crux of the credibility question the review raises. A government that built policy credibility on a fixed, escalating target now has to weigh the near-term cost of manufacturing job losses against the long-run cost of showing that sustained lobbying can move a statutory climate target. Other capitals are watching for the same reason: the EU faces comparable pressure from its own carmakers over 2035 combustion-engine rules, and a UK retreat would hand ammunition to those arguing Brussels should follow.

The consultation closes on 23 October, after which the government has said it will set out a revised trajectory before the current flexibility arrangements expire. The number to watch is less the headline 2030 figure than how far ministers move it — a shift to 70% would read as calibration, a drop to 50% as capitulation — and whether Nissan, JLR and Ford treat whatever emerges as sufficient to lock in UK production plans, or simply the opening bid in the next round of lobbying.
