Gilt yields ease on dovish BoE tone even as inflation forecast rises

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Gilt yields fell on a dovish BoE vote split, then partly reversed as the Bank's own forecast — CPI peaking near 3.2% — reasserted itself.

Traders in the gilt market spent the first half of August doing something that looks, on paper, like a contradiction: buying UK government bonds — pushing yields down — on the strength of a central bank that just told them inflation is going to get worse before it gets better.

The Bank of England held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, its fifth consecutive hold this year, in a 6–3 vote. That alone was unremarkable. What moved the market was the tone. Three members — chief economist Huw Pill, Catherine Mann and Megan Greene — pushed for an immediate quarter-point hike to 4%, arguing energy-driven price pressure from the Middle East conflict justified tightening now. The majority, led by Governor Andrew Bailey, disagreed, and Bailey's language afterward leaned dovish: "global conditions look to be more uncertain and inflationary, while domestic conditions are on balance more benign," he said, adding that the underlying disinflation process "remains on track."

Gilts rallied on the split. The 10-year yield, which had touched a three-month high near 4.99% ahead of the decision, drifted down toward 4.9% in the days that followed — its lowest level since 10 July — as traders read the 6–3 hold, and Bailey's framing of it, as confirmation that the Bank is in no hurry to tighten further and may be closer to resuming cuts than the hawkish dissent suggested.

The number that doesn't fit the mood

Here is the awkward part. The same Monetary Policy Report that accompanied the dovish-sounding hold contains a central projection showing CPI inflation climbing from June's 2.6% to a peak of roughly 3.2% in the fourth quarter of this year — well above the Bank's 2% target. Under that forecast, inflation doesn't fall back below target until the first quarter of 2028.

That is the tension the headline points at: a committee striking a patient, glass-half-full tone in its communication while its own numbers describe inflation getting materially worse for the rest of the year. The Bank's explanation is that the drivers are different in kind. Domestic price and wage pressure — the part monetary policy actually influences — has continued to cool, which is what let the majority resist the hawks' call for a hike. The upward push in the headline number is coming from energy prices, driven by the Strait of Hormuz standoff between the US and Iran, which the MPC treats as a supply shock rather than a signal to tighten.

Not a one-way trade

Whether that distinction holds is precisely what the gilt market has spent the following two weeks testing, and the answer so far is: not comfortably. After bottoming near 4.9% in early August, the 10-year yield climbed back to around 4.97% by 13 August and 5.04% the following day, as crude prices swung on shifting signals about a US-Iran deal and stronger UK GDP data — 0.4% quarter-on-quarter growth in Q2, plus a 0.3% monthly gain in June — complicated the case for near-term cuts. In other words, the dovish rally has already partly unwound.

That round trip is the story in miniature. A single dovish vote split does not settle the argument between a central bank confident that underlying inflation is beaten and a market unsure whether an oil-price shock will keep headline CPI uncomfortably high into next year. For anyone pricing rate-cut timing off central bank rhetoric alone, the past two weeks are a reminder that forward guidance and the inflation forecast sitting next to it don't always point the same way — and when they diverge, it's usually the forecast that eventually wins the argument.

The next test lands with August's CPI print, due mid-September, followed by the Bank's next rate decision on 17 September — the first chance for the MPC to show whether July's dovish framing survives contact with an inflation number that its own projections say should already be climbing.
