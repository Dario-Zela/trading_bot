Cadence raises 2026 outlook on AI-agent driven EDA demand

Cadence CEO Anirudh Devgan, who told investors agentic AI is a "demand accelerator" for the company's design software. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Cadence raised its 2026 forecast on AI-agent demand for chip-design software, but shares have fallen 13% in a month on fears the demand won't hold pricing power.

Cadence Design Systems spent Tuesday's earnings call telling investors that artificial intelligence is about to make its software more valuable than ever. Investors spent the following weeks selling the stock anyway.

The chip-design software maker — one of two companies whose tools sit behind nearly every processor built by Nvidia, AMD, Apple and Broadcom — raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to a range of $6.26 billion to $6.34 billion, up from its prior forecast, and lifted non-GAAP earnings guidance to $8.05–$8.15 a share. Second-quarter revenue came in at $1.584 billion, up 24.2% year-over-year, with non-GAAP earnings per share of $2.11, both ahead of consensus. Backlog hit a record $8.1 billion, up from $7.8 billion at the end of 2025.

And yet Cadence shares have fallen roughly 13% over the past month, sliding from an all-time closing high of $416.39 on June 2 to the low $330s in recent sessions. The gap between what management is saying and what the stock is doing is the story.

In one sentence: Cadence raised its 2026 forecast on AI-agent demand for chip-design tools, but the stock has dropped 13% in a month as investors weigh whether that demand will actually hold pricing power.

The pitch: AI agents need more, not less, EDA

Electronic design automation — EDA — is the unglamorous software layer that turns a chip architect's intentions into a manufacturable layout, running the simulations and verification cycles that catch errors before they get etched into silicon at a cost of tens of millions of dollars per mistake. Cadence and its larger rival Synopsys have controlled the vast majority of that market for decades; recent estimates put their combined share above 70%.

CEO Anirudh Devgan's argument, repeated on the earnings call, is that AI agents are a "demand accelerator" rather than a threat to that business. The logic: autonomous design agents don't replace EDA tools, they call them more often. An agent iterating through thousands of design variations still needs Cadence's simulation and verification engines to check each one, which should mean more usage, not less, as the work becomes automated. The company pointed to early traction — its ChipStack AI "super agent" has more than 20 customer engagements and is running in production on live chip designs; ViraStack, its agentic tool for analog design, has more than 25 engagements and has delivered two-to-tenfold productivity gains in customer testing, according to the company. Cadence also closed a $3.18 billion acquisition of Hexagon's design and engineering business in February, pushing further into multiphysics simulation for aerospace and automotive customers — a bet that AI-driven design work will spread well beyond semiconductors.

By segment, the growth was broad: IP revenue grew more than 40% year-over-year, System Design and Analysis rose 37%, and core EDA — the traditional business — still grew 18%. Devgan was careful to note the company is not baking a "sudden step function" from agentic AI into its own numbers, which if anything makes the guidance raise look conservative rather than promotional.

Why the market isn't buying it

The sell-off predates the earnings beat and has a specific trigger. In mid-July, Chinese AI lab Moonshot AI disclosed that its Kimi K3 model — a 2.8-trillion-parameter open-weight system — had autonomously run a complete chip design flow in 48 hours using only open-source tools, producing a functional design with 1.46 million standard cells and touching no licensed software from Cadence or Synopsys. Both stocks fell roughly 9-10% in a single session on the news.

The demo has real limits: it used a 45-nanometer process, several generations behind the 3-nanometer and 2-nanometer nodes where advanced AI accelerators are actually built, and where Cadence and Synopsys tools remain deeply entrenched. BNP Paribas called the reaction overblown and told clients to buy the dip; Mizuho argued Kimi K3 actually reinforces the agentic-AI demand thesis rather than undermining it. But the episode crystallized the market's real anxiety: if AI agents can eventually be pointed at open-source toolchains instead of proprietary ones, the pricing power that has let Cadence post non-GAAP operating margins above 45% could erode even as usage volumes climb.

That anxiety sits on top of a broader correction across semiconductor-linked software after a run that had significantly outpaced the S&P 500 through the first half of 2026, and on top of a valuation that leaves little room for doubt. Even after the pullback, Cadence trades near 66 times trailing earnings and roughly 36 times forward estimates — the low end of its own five-year range, but still well above the software sector's mid-20s multiple. Zacks rates the stock a Hold, citing strong growth and momentum scores undercut by a weak value grade.

The margin question

This is the tension trading desks are actually pricing: Cadence sits at a genuine choke point in the AI buildout, the place every chip — including the accelerators powering AI itself — has to pass through before it can be manufactured. That position should be one of the more durable monetization stories in the AI trade, immune to the commoditization hitting application-layer AI products. Management's own numbers this quarter — 24% revenue growth, record backlog, margin guidance raised alongside revenue — support that read.

But infrastructure position and pricing power are not the same thing, and the stock's reaction says investors aren't yet convinced Cadence can keep both as agentic tools proliferate. If AI agents genuinely expand the addressable market by running more simulation cycles, as Devgan argues, current multiples could look cheap in hindsight. If agents instead accelerate a shift toward cheaper or open alternatives at the margin — even only at legacy process nodes to start — today's premium starts to look like the vulnerability rather than the reward.

The next real test comes with Synopsys's own quarterly results, and with whatever Cadence reports on ChipStack and ViraStack customer conversion when it next updates guidance. Until then, the divergence between a raised forecast and a falling stock stands as a live read on how the market is pricing AI infrastructure businesses more broadly: rewarding demand growth is easy; underwriting that the margins survive the AI agents that are driving it is another matter.
