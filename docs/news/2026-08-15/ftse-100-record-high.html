FTSE 100 closes at fresh record high

FTSE 100 prices scroll on a screen outside the London Stock Exchange at Paternoster Square, with St Paul's Cathedral reflected in the glass. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The FTSE 100's record close this month was driven almost entirely by energy and bank stocks — and has since given back ground for five straight sessions.

London's blue-chip index has spent the summer doing something it rarely does two years running: setting records. The FTSE 100 closed at 10,921.68 points on 7 August, its latest peak in a rally that has added roughly 13% since the start of the year and pushed the index to the edge of 11,000 for the first time. The advance was not spread evenly. It was carried, disproportionately, by two corners of the market — energy and financials — that tend to do well for reasons that have little to do with the health of the wider British economy.

The energy leg came from geopolitics. Brent crude has traded above $85 a barrel for most of August, and briefly above $90 in late July, as fighting in the Middle East kept the Strait of Hormuz under pressure and stoked fears of a wider supply shock. Shell and BP rose in step. The financials leg came from earnings. NatWest reported first-half attributable profit of £3.0 billion, a return on tangible equity of 19.7%, and raised its full-year guidance — prompting a near 4% jump in its shares. Aviva added to the mood on 14 August with a 24% rise in first-half operating profit to £1.33 billion and a 7% increase to its interim dividend.

A rally with a narrow base

The trouble with a rally built on oil prices and bank margins is that neither says much about the state of the underlying economy — and the numbers back that up. In the week to 31 July, 96% of the FTSE 100's 131.82-point gain came from just two trading sessions, according to a weekly market wrap, a degree of concentration that looks more like a handful of catalysts landing at once than broad-based conviction. Miners and pharmaceutical stocks, meanwhile, have gone the other way: since the 7 August high, the index has fallen for five consecutive sessions, dropping to 10,750.11 on 14 August as Antofagasta, Glencore and Rio Tinto slid on weaker industrial metal prices and AstraZeneca and GSK each lost more than 2%.

That split matters because it is a reasonably clean test of what has actually been driving London higher. Energy holds up as long as the Strait of Hormuz stays tense; banks hold up as long as rates stay elevated and earnings keep beating low expectations. Neither of those conditions speaks to UK growth, wages or investment. China's economy is slowing — second-quarter GDP growth came in at 4.3% — and at home the Bank of England held rates at 3.75% for a fifth straight meeting on 30 July, with three policymakers pushing to raise them further on concern that energy-driven inflation could prove sticky.

The next test comes on two fronts. Corporate earnings season continues through late August, and any broadening of gains beyond oil and banking stocks would be the clearer signal that investors are betting on Britain rather than just on Brent and interest margins. The Bank of England's next rate decision, due 17 September, will show whether the MPC's more hawkish members are gaining ground — a shift that would keep financials well supported but do little to answer the question the past fortnight has posed: whether London's record run reflects the economy underneath it, or just the sectors sitting on top.
