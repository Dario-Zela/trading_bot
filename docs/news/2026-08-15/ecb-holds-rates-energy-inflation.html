ECB holds rates elevated as Middle East conflict keeps energy inflation high

ECB President Christine Lagarde has resisted pre-committing to a rate path as energy-driven inflation stays above target. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Euro-area inflation rose to 2.9% in July as energy costs re-accelerated, keeping the ECB's 2.25% rate on hold with a hike still in play for September.

The European Central Bank left its deposit rate at 2.25% on 23 July, and the data released since then explain why Frankfurt is in no hurry to move. Euro-area inflation ticked up to 2.9% in July, from 2.8% in June, according to Eurostat's flash estimate — a reminder that the region's inflation problem has not been core-driven wages or overheating demand for the best part of two years now, but energy, and energy is still being set in the Middle East.

Energy prices rose 10.0% year-on-year in July, up sharply from 8.5% in June, which itself had been a retreat from May's 10.8%. That June dip followed a memorandum of understanding between the US and Iran that briefly calmed oil markets; the July reacceleration followed renewed fighting. Core inflation, which strips out energy and food, also crept higher, to 2.5% from 2.4%. Services ran at 3.3%. None of it is dramatic. All of it points the same direction.

A hike that's still on the table

The ECB's 2.25% rate is not a legacy setting — it was raised there in June, the Governing Council's first hike in years, a direct response to the conflict-driven inflation shock. July's decision to hold was unanimous, but President Christine Lagarde made plain it was not a signal of comfort. "We are not pre-committing to a particular rate path," she told reporters, adding that some governors had asked themselves whether a hike, not a hold, was the right call in July. The Bank's June bulletin had already flagged gas prices running some 97% above pre-conflict levels, with crude around 30% higher — a gap that a ceasefire could close quickly, and a fresh escalation could just as easily widen.

What has kept the ECB from moving further is the absence, so far, of second-round effects. "We are not seeing it," Lagarde said of any pass-through into wage bargaining or corporate pricing surveys — the mechanism by which a temporary energy shock hardens into something a central bank has to break with higher rates for longer. That is the judgment call underlying the whole stance: hold the line on the headline number, watch the wage data closely, and reserve the hike for if the shock stops looking temporary.

The divergence trade

The contrast with Washington is doing real work in currency markets. The Federal Reserve has held its target range at 3.50%–3.75% since July, but soft retail sales have pushed traders toward pricing a larger cut at its 16 September meeting. An ECB that is debating hikes while the Fed debates the size of its next cut is a textbook setup for the rate differential to compress in the euro's favour — and for anyone holding growth-sensitive eurozone exposure to feel the other side of that trade.

The next test lands on 10 September, when the Governing Council meets again in Berlin with a fresh set of wage and inflation data in hand. Whether July's uptick was a blip or the start of a trend will likely decide whether Lagarde's next move is another hold, or the hike some of her colleagues already wanted.
