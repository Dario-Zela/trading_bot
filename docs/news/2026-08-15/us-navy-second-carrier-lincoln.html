US Navy sends second carrier to relieve USS Lincoln after reported issues

USS Abraham Lincoln (CVN-72), which has now spent more than 260 consecutive days at sea supporting operations against Iran. — U.S. Navy photo by Capt. Lee Apsley / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A carrier is racing from the Pacific to relieve the USS Lincoln after a record 260-day deployment, leaving the Pacific fleet without carrier cover.

The USS Abraham Lincoln left San Diego last November on what was billed as a routine six-month rotational deployment. Nine months on, the carrier is still at sea — more than 260 straight days without a single port call, the longest continuous stretch recorded for a modern US aircraft carrier — and the Navy is now scrambling to get it home.

The USS George Washington, based in Yokosuka, Japan, and the Navy's only forward-deployed carrier in the Pacific, has been diverted from a routine western Pacific rotation to relieve it. Tracking data show the ship passing through the Strait of Malacca this week, on a course that would put it in the Middle East around August 22.

An extension nobody planned for

The Lincoln's deployment was stretched by the US war against Iran, which pulled the carrier and its roughly 5,000 sailors and Marines into the Arabian Sea and Strait of Hormuz well past its original return date. Family members and a Military Times report this month described worsening conditions aboard: shortages of basic supplies, contaminated water, plumbing failures, mail disruptions, and strained mental health among the crew, with some relatives alleging attempted overboard incidents.

US Central Command has pushed back hard, calling the more extreme claims — including reports of seven deaths — "baseless," while confirming that one sailor did go overboard on August 3 and was recovered safely. Senator Richard Blumenthal has given the Navy until August 27 to answer a formal inquiry into conditions on the ship, and Senator Ruben Gallego, a Marine combat veteran, is pushing for a bipartisan Senate delegation to visit the carrier directly. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has insisted the Navy is providing crews with "everything we can provide them at every single moment."

A fleet stretched thin

Whatever the truth of individual claims aboard the Lincoln, the underlying arithmetic is not in dispute: pulling George Washington off its Pacific rotation leaves that theatre without a carrier for an unknown stretch, at a moment when Washington has been emphasising deterrence against China in the South China Sea and around Taiwan. Retired Vice Admiral Mike Franken warned that deployments stretched this long ripple through the wider fleet for years, since crews and ships need extended recovery and maintenance periods that then compete for scarce yard capacity and personnel. With eleven carriers nominally available but several tied up in multi-year maintenance overhauls at yards like Newport News, each unplanned extension forces the Navy to rob one theatre to cover another.

That dynamic is the one worth watching for defense investors: not this single relief operation, but whether it becomes the new normal. A Navy simultaneously sustaining Middle East operations and a Pacific deterrent posture, without adding carriers or expanding maintenance throughput, is a Navy that will keep making this trade — and each time it does, it adds pressure on shipbuilders and yard operators to close a capacity gap that budgets have not yet addressed. The Senate's August 27 deadline for answers from the Pentagon is the next concrete marker; whether the Lincoln finally makes port after that will show whether this was a one-off strain or a preview of how thin the carrier fleet has become.
