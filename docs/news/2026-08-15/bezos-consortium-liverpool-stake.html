Bezos-led consortium buys 30% stake in Liverpool FC at $7bn valuation

Anfield, Liverpool FC's home ground, where FSG has held ownership since rescuing the club from near-administration in 2010 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Bezos-backed consortium bought ~30% of Liverpool FC at a record $7bn valuation, with an option to take majority control within a year.

Liverpool Football Club, six-time champions of Europe and perpetually the most romanticised club in English football, now has Jeff Bezos as one of its financial backers. A consortium fronted by the British investor Amit Bhatia, and bankrolled in large part by the Amazon founder's K5 Sports fund and the family office of Facebook co-founder Eduardo Saverin, has bought roughly 30% of the club at a valuation just north of £5.75bn (about $7bn) — a record for an investment in a football club anywhere.

Fenway Sports Group, the Boston-based ownership group led by John Henry that has run Liverpool since 2010, keeps majority control and day-to-day operational say for now. But the deal, structured through a vehicle called 1892 Holdings — a nod to the year the club was founded — carries an option for the new investors to take majority ownership within twelve months, at a valuation reported to be around $8bn.

Who's actually buying in

The consortium's public face is Bhatia, a London-based investor and the son-in-law of steel billionaire Lakshmi Mittal, who previously co-owned Queens Park Rangers. He becomes Liverpool's vice chairman and takes a seat on an expanded board. "To be welcomed as a partner in a club of this stature is a huge privilege," he said after the deal closed.

The money, though, is mostly American tech wealth. Bezos is investing through K5 Sports, an offshoot of the K5 Global fund that has previously backed ventures spanning esports to entertainment; Bryan Baum of K5 takes a board seat, but Bezos himself does not. Saverin's involvement runs through EE Capital, the family office he manages with his wife, Elaine, who also joins the Liverpool board. None of the three principals has any prior operating experience in football — which is, in its way, the point. This is capital allocation, not a passion project with a spreadsheet attached.

From administration to $7bn

The scale of the number is easier to read against where Liverpool started under American ownership. FSG bought the club in 2010 for around $476m (roughly £300m at the time), rescuing it from a previous ownership pairing — Tom Hicks and George Gillett — that had loaded the club with debt and taken it to the edge of administration. Fifteen years, two Premier League titles and a Champions League trophy later, a 30% slice alone is worth close to fifteen times what FSG paid for the whole club. Forbes now ranks Liverpool the world's fourth most valuable football club, behind only Real Madrid, Barcelona and Manchester United.

That trajectory — distressed asset to blue-chip franchise — is precisely the shape tech money has learned to like. Sports clubs, unlike most of what venture and growth investors buy, come with an artificially fixed supply: there will only ever be one Liverpool, one Premier League. Broadcast rights and matchday revenue provide a cash-generative floor, while global fan bases outside the traditional buyer's home market offer upside that doesn't correlate neatly with equities or bonds.

Part of a bigger reallocation

Liverpool is not an isolated data point. Tech-sector money controlled around 6% of major U.S. sports franchise ownership in 2022; by 2025 that figure had risen to roughly 20%, and 2026 has already produced two deals bigger than this one. The Los Angeles Lakers changed hands for $12.5bn in a sale to Joshua Kushner and Bob Iger, and Vinod and Neeru Khosla bought the NFL's Seattle Seahawks for close to $9.6bn. Set against those, Liverpool's $7bn valuation for a minority stake looks less like an outlier and more like the latest instalment in a pattern: founders who made their fortunes in software, cloud infrastructure and venture capital are rotating a slice of that wealth into franchises they can't build faster with an AI model, and can't be diluted by a competitor's product launch.

For a Premier League that has spent a decade absorbing American ownership — Manchester United, Chelsea, Everton and others already answer to owners across the Atlantic — the Bezos-Bhatia-Saverin deal confirms rather than starts a trend. What it adds is the presence of the world's second-richest man as a name attached to the ledger, even without a seat at the boardroom table.

The deal still needs Premier League approval under the league's owners' and directors' test, a formality expected to clear within weeks. The more interesting date is roughly twelve months out, when the consortium's option to take majority control comes due — the point at which Liverpool's famous supporters will find out whether this was a passive stake or the opening move in another ownership change entirely.
