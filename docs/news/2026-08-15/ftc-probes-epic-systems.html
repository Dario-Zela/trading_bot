FTC opens antitrust probe into health-records giant Epic Systems

The FTC's Apex Building headquarters in Washington, D.C., where the antitrust investigation into Epic Systems originates. — Harrison Keely / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
The FTC has opened an early-stage antitrust probe into Epic Systems' control over hospital data access, adding to lawsuits from Texas and rival Particle Health.

The Federal Trade Commission has opened an antitrust investigation into Epic Systems Corp., the Verona, Wisconsin-based software company whose electronic health records now hold data on roughly 82% of Americans, according to two people familiar with the matter cited by Reuters. The probe is early-stage — no complaint has been filed, and it may go nowhere — but it adds a federal dimension to a company that until recently operated almost entirely outside the antitrust spotlight, despite becoming, in effect, the default operating system for American hospital care.

Epic is privately held, so there is no ticker to move on the news. But its footprint is the point: the company's systems sit inside hospitals that collectively treat more than 280 million people, and KLAS Research's 2026 market-share report puts Epic at 43.7% of the acute-care hospital market and 56.9% of hospital beds — a share that has climbed for five straight years, up from 31% in 2021. Annual revenue reached $5.7 billion in 2024, roughly 70% higher than in 2020. For a private company, that is about as close to systemic importance as software gets.

What investigators are asking

According to STAT News, which has spoken to people contacted by FTC investigators, the inquiry runs along two tracks. The first concerns data access: whether Epic uses its dominant position to delay or block rival technology firms from pulling patient records they need to operate, even when hospitals and patients want that data shared. The second concerns labor — non-compete agreements that bar former Epic employees from taking jobs at competing health-technology firms, a practice the FTC has scrutinized across industries since its now-stalled 2024 rule attempting to ban noncompetes nationally.

The FTC has sent civil investigative demands — the agency's version of a subpoena — to other companies in the health-technology sector, asking how Epic grants or withholds the data connections known in the industry as interoperability. State attorneys general have joined some of those calls or reached out separately, according to STAT, suggesting a level of coordination beyond a routine preliminary review. Epic has denied wrongdoing. A spokesperson said the company is "a leader in interoperability to support patient care" and does "not engage in anticompetitive behavior," adding that it is hospital customers, not Epic, who ultimately control access to their own patients' records.

A company already fighting on two fronts

The FTC did not arrive first. Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued Epic in December, alleging the company runs an illegal monopoly by charging penalty fees to hospitals that try to route data to competing applications and by restricting parents' default access to their children's MyChart accounts once a child turns 12. Epic's databases, according to that complaint, hold more than 325 million patient records — north of 90% of the US population, counting duplicates across providers.

Separately, Particle Health, a data-access startup, has an antitrust suit against Epic proceeding in federal court in New York after a judge in 2025 declined to dismiss the core monopolization claims. Particle alleges Epic cut off its access to patient records through the Carequality health-information exchange on a pretextual privacy complaint, then used the disruption to steer Particle's customers toward Epic's own data products. A separate 2025 suit from CureIS Healthcare accuses Epic of running an "Epic-first policy" that pressures hospitals to drop third-party software. Veeva Systems has separately challenged Epic's use of non-compete and stock-purchase agreements to box out competitors.

Epic has also seen churn at the top: Sumit Rana, seen as a potential successor to founder and CEO Judith Faulkner, left the company in early July, and Seth Hain, who led Epic's artificial-intelligence efforts, departed shortly after. Neither departure has been tied publicly to the legal pressure, but the timing has not gone unnoticed among people who track the company.

Why interoperability keeps coming back

The recurring theme across the FTC probe, the Texas suit and the Particle Health case is data portability — a policy priority written into federal law nearly a decade ago. The 21st Century Cures Act, passed in 2016, explicitly bars health IT vendors from "information blocking": practices that interfere with the access, exchange or use of electronic health information. The law gave the Department of Health and Human Services enforcement power, but critics have long argued that penalties have been too rare and too small to change vendor behavior in a market where switching an entire hospital system off Epic can cost tens of millions of dollars and take years.

An antitrust case is a different lever than an information-blocking case — it targets market structure rather than a specific data-sharing refusal — and a loss for Epic on that front could force more than a fine. Remedies in monopolization cases can include mandated licensing terms, restrictions on exclusivity contracts, or court-supervised interoperability standards, any of which would ripple through how hospitals negotiate with Epic and how much leverage smaller EHR and data-access vendors have when they try to compete for the same customers.

What comes next

The FTC investigation is still at the fact-finding stage, and agencies routinely decline to act after issuing civil investigative demands. The more concrete near-term catalyst is the Texas case, where discovery is underway and early motions will test how far the state's monopolization theory survives contact with Epic's defense that customers, not Epic, control their own data. The Particle Health case in New York, now past the motion-to-dismiss stage, is likely to produce internal Epic communications through discovery that could shape both the FTC's thinking and the Texas litigation. For a company that has spent three decades avoiding the kind of scrutiny applied to Big Tech platforms, all three tracks now run at once.
