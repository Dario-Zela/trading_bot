BBC faces claims it was warned of author's missing-clothes assertion in child murder case

BBC Broadcasting House in London, where the corporation's whistleblowing unit received the 2022 warning. — Briantist / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A Guardian probe says the BBC's whistleblowing unit was told in 2022 a true-crime author had a murdered child's missing clothes, and closed the case in 2025 with no action.

The BBC's own whistleblowing unit was told, in writing, that a bestselling true-crime author had been boasting for years about possessing the missing clothes of a nine-year-old murder victim — and closed the case three years later without further action, according to a new Guardian investigation.

The clothes belonged to Karen Hadaway, who was killed alongside her best friend Nicola Fellows, both aged nine, in Brighton's Wild Park in 1986 — a case that became known as the Babes in the Wood murders. Karen's mother handed the bloodstained clothing to the then-BBC journalist Martin Bashir in 1991, intending for it to be used for DNA testing. When detectives asked for it back in 2004, following changes to double jeopardy law that would eventually enable Russell Bishop's 2018 conviction for the killings, the BBC could not find it. The corporation apologised and settled with Karen's family in 2023.

What the whistleblowers said

The new claims concern what the BBC knew, and when. According to the Guardian, the freelance journalist Eileen Fairweather — who was present when Karen's mother first handed over the clothes, and who began reinvestigating the case in 2021 after a separate BBC-commissioned inquiry found Bashir had forged documents to secure his 1995 Diana interview — reported to the BBC's whistleblowing unit in 2022 that the true-crime author Christopher Berry-Dee was widely believed to be the last person known to have had Karen's clothes. Ian Heffron, Nicola Fellows's uncle, separately emailed the office of director-general Tim Davie with the same warning. The BBC closed its inquiry in 2025 with no further action taken.

Berry-Dee, Britain's biggest-selling true-crime author with close to a million books sold, told the Guardian it was "100% correct" that he had obtained the clothes, saying he later burned them. He has written that Karen's family gave them to him "with tears in their eyes" so he could arrange DNA testing, but multiple sources told the paper they recalled him displaying the clothing years afterwards, i.e. after they had supposedly already gone missing from the BBC. He did not reconcile the two accounts.

The clothes claim surfaces amid a wider Guardian investigation in which several women have accused Berry-Dee of sexual and violent offences spanning decades, allegations he denies in full.

Asked about the whistleblower reports, the BBC declined to address the specifics, saying only that it believed "this matter has been investigated as thoroughly as possible." That answer is unlikely to satisfy Karen's family or Fairweather, whose central question — how a national broadcaster came to lose forensic evidence in a live double-murder case, and then apparently sat for three years on a credible lead about where it went — remains unanswered. The Guardian's wider investigation into Berry-Dee, including the allegations from multiple women, continues, and any police response to the renewed claims about the clothes is the next thing worth watching.
