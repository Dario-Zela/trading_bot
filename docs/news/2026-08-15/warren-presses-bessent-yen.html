Warren presses Bessent to justify yen intervention

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose department confirmed the U.S. role in the July 31 yen intervention but has not disclosed its cost. — U.S. Department of the Treasury / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Elizabeth Warren has demanded Bessent detail costs and rationale behind the first joint U.S.-Japan yen intervention since 1998.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has spent two weeks explaining to markets why Washington helped buy yen for the first time since 1998. Now he has to explain it to Congress.

Senator Elizabeth Warren, the ranking Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee, sent Bessent a letter dated August 13 demanding a full accounting of the intervention: how much taxpayer-linked money was spent, what triggered the decision, and what conditions — if any — attach to further U.S. support for Japan's currency defense. "The administration has yet to provide a detailed justification for its intervention," Warren wrote, "nor has it officially disclosed how much taxpayer-linked funds were spent purchasing yen."

The episode she's asking about is unusual enough to merit the scrutiny. On July 31, after the yen sank to its weakest level against the dollar in nearly four decades, the U.S. and Japan carried out their first joint intervention to prop up the currency since the Clinton administration. Japan did most of the heavy lifting, spending in the tens of billions of dollars from its own reserves. The American contribution was smaller — estimates put it in the single-digit billions — but structurally odd: rather than selling dollars to buy yen, the New York Fed sold euros drawn from Treasury's Exchange Stabilization Fund. Bessent has since pushed the Warsh-led Federal Reserve to expand its FIMA repo backstop, giving foreign central banks, Japan included, more room to defend their currencies without dumping Treasuries.

Why the euro trade raised eyebrows

Currency economists have questioned the mechanics as much as the politics. Edwin Truman, a former Treasury assistant secretary, called the euro-funded approach "weird," arguing that a direct dollar sale would have been more effective and more legible to markets. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution put the underlying risk more bluntly: "FX intervention is a confidence game. The last thing you want is to give markets any kind of reason to ask questions." Warren's letter is, in effect, that question being asked in public, by the committee with jurisdiction over Treasury.

What Bessent has said, and hasn't

Bessent's public justification has been regional rather than bilateral: a weak yen, he has argued, risks dragging down other Asian currencies through competitive devaluation, and "a stable yen is not only important for the U.S., but very important for the entire region." What he has not done is publish a dollar figure for the U.S. share of the operation, or detail the terms of any further backstop for Tokyo — the two things Warren's letter presses hardest on.

Currency intervention has traditionally been one of the few corners of economic policy where Treasury acts with minimal congressional interference, on the theory that telegraphing intent undermines the tool itself. Warren's letter doesn't change that legal reality, but it puts down a marker: if the yen weakens again and Washington intervenes a second time, it will do so with a Senate Banking Committee record demanding it show its work. Bessent has not set a public deadline for responding.
