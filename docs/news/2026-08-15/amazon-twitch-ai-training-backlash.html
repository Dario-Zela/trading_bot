Amazon uses Twitch livestream content to train AI, sparking opt-out backlash

Twitch's San Francisco headquarters, in the city's Financial District. — 9yz / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Amazon trains AI on Twitch streamers' content by default, and its own product chief admitted an opt-in version would have found no takers.

Amazon has confirmed what many Twitch streamers only found out by accident this week: the company has been feeding their livestreams into its artificial intelligence training pipeline, and the only way to stop it is to go looking for a switch nobody told you existed.

The setting surfaced on August 12, when a streaming-industry reporter spotted a new toggle buried in Twitch's account settings, under Security and Privacy, labelled something close to "allow your channel content to train generative AI models at Amazon." There was no email, no pop-up, no banner on the homepage. The link to the settings page simply started circulating on social media, and it was already switched on for every account.

A toggle nobody asked for

The scope is broad. Twitch says eligible content includes livestreams, past broadcasts, clips, highlights, images, chat logs and text — and, notably, audio, which the company says may be used to refine speech-to-text systems that power automatic captions on Twitch and elsewhere in Amazon's product line. Practically every part of what a creator produces on the platform is in scope unless they actively pull it out.

Amazon, which bought Twitch for $970 million in 2014, had reportedly been training on this data before the toggle ever existed — the company referenced the practice at an industry event back in 2024. What changed on August 12 wasn't the policy so much as the fact that, for the first time, streamers were given a way to say no. Twitch framed the update as adding a control, not announcing a new use of data, which is precisely what infuriated people: the training had apparently been happening in the background all along.

The quiet part, said out loud

The backlash was immediate and loud enough that Twitch's chief product officer, Mike Minton, addressed it directly on a livestream that drew nearly 3,000 concurrent viewers, many of them posting objections in the chat in real time. Asked why the company hadn't simply made the program opt-in, Minton didn't dress it up.

"If this was opt-in, nobody would opt in. That's honestly the answer."

It is a rare admission from a technology executive that a policy exists specifically because asking permission would have produced the "wrong" result. Mary Kish, Twitch's head of community, followed up by acknowledging the new opt-out setting reflected "this community's voice" pushing back against AI training — an implicit concession that the default had never reflected that voice at all. Some streamers also reported that the toggle appeared to switch itself back on after they'd turned it off, compounding the sense that opting out was being made deliberately hard, whether by design or by bug.

Not the first, won't be the last

Twitch's approach — train by default, let users find the exit if they can — mirrors what Meta already does with public content posted to Facebook and Instagram, where opting out is likewise on the user rather than the company. What sets the Twitch case apart is the medium: a livestream is a person's face, voice and unscripted speech, running for hours, often for years — a far richer training substrate than a photo caption or a status update. And unlike a Facebook post, a stream is frequently a creator's livelihood, monetised through subscriptions and ads that Twitch itself takes a cut of. Nothing in Twitch's announcement mentions compensation for creators whose content trains models that could, eventually, replace some of what they do — generate synthetic voices, auto-clip highlights, or produce AI moderation and captioning tools Amazon can sell elsewhere.

That asymmetry — a platform that owns the pipes, sets the defaults and keeps the upside, versus millions of individual creators who must each notice, understand and act to protect their own content — is becoming the defining fight of the AI-training era, playing out simultaneously in publishing, music and now live video. Twitch says roughly 7 million channels go live in a typical month; only a fraction of them are likely to have found the toggle at all.

What's next

Regulators have started paying attention to exactly this kind of consent design elsewhere — the European Union's AI Act, now entering its enforcement phase, imposes disclosure duties on firms training models on personal data, and could become a template pressure point for U.S. and U.K. creators demanding the same opt-in standard Amazon avoided. For now, the practical test is narrower: whether Twitch fixes the reported bug that re-enables the setting, whether it sends a direct notice to streamers rather than relying on word of mouth, and whether Amazon says anything at all about compensating the creators whose years of footage already trained the models before anyone had a choice.
