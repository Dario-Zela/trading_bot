Why tech bosses keep sharing their AI manifestos

Mark Zuckerberg published his 6,500-word AI manifesto, "The Future is for Everyone," on 10 August 2026 — the latest entry in a crowded genre. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Tech bosses' sprawling AI essays double as lobbying documents, each pitch conveniently matching its author's commercial interest.

Silicon Valley has developed a new house style: the CEO manifesto. Not a press release, not a quarterly call, but a personal essay — thousands of words long, published under a founder's own name, laying out where AI is headed and how it should be governed. Four have landed in the space of a few months, and the timing is not incidental.

Mark Zuckerberg opened the latest chapter on 10 August with a 6,500-word piece titled "The Future is for Everyone," arguing that broad access to AI models — Meta's preferred open-weight approach — diffuses power better than the tightly-held systems built by rivals. It followed Dario Amodei's own entries: Anthropic's chief executive wrote "Machines of Loving Grace," a 13,000-word case for AI-driven abundance that Anthropic printed and bound as merchandise, then followed it with "The Adolescence of Technology," roughly 20,000 words warning of near-term risk and calling for mandatory safety testing and tighter export controls on the chips that let rivals train competing systems.

The policy pitch beneath the prose

Strip away the philosophy and each essay doubles as a lobbying document. Google DeepMind's Demis Hassabis has proposed an industry-funded, federally overseen "Frontier AI Standards Body" to test models pre-release — a framework that would let credentialed labs largely police themselves. Nvidia's Jensen Huang has taken the opposite tack, rallying Meta, Microsoft, Google and OpenAI to sign a joint letter defending open-weight models against restriction, on the grounds that openness helps American developers and defenders more than it helps attackers. Microsoft's Satya Nadella has used long-form essays to press his own vision of the AI economy, while Sam Altman, notably, has pulled back to shorter, more operational posts.

Each position lines up neatly with its author's commercial interest: the company selling access wants standards bodies; the company selling chips wants openness; the company most exposed to safety criticism wants mandatory testing written into law before someone else writes the rules first. Axios has dubbed the split the "open-weight paradox" — the same openness that spreads innovation and strengthens cyber defence also puts increasingly capable systems beyond anyone's ability to recall.

Who is actually reading these

What's less clear is whether anyone outside the industry is the intended audience. Fortune reported this week that the manifestos' real readers are competitors, venture capitalists, policymakers and technology journalists — not the public these essays claim to address. One communications adviser quoted by Fortune likened the genre to flag-planting; an organisational consultant noted that the longer and more unfiltered the document, the more likely it is to be misread. Unlike Warren Buffett's shareholder letters, earned through decades of results, these essays arrive before the product has proven much of anything.

The real test is whether the words translate into positions once regulators start drafting text. The EU's AI Act enforcement, which began this month with disclosure duties and fines of up to €15m, is the first live venue where these stated philosophies meet actual penalties — and where it will become clear which manifesto was a genuine policy proposal and which was branding.
