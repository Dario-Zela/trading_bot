BP returns to Venezuela to run Loran Phase 2 gas project

In one sentence
BP will operate Venezuela's Loran Phase 2 gas project with Gulf partners XRG and UCC, its biggest re-entry since Maduro's fall and Washington's sanctions rollback.

BP has been awarded operatorship of Phase 2 of the Loran gas field off Venezuela's coast, the clearest sign yet that a major Western oil company is willing to bet on the country's post-Maduro opening. The licence, signed in Caracas on August 14, gives BP a one-third working interest alongside two Gulf state-backed newcomers to Venezuela: Abu Dhabi's XRG, part of ADNOC, and Qatar's UCC Oil and Gas Holding, each also taking 33.3%. The field, in the Plataforma Deltana block off the eastern coast, holds more than 4 trillion cubic feet of recoverable gas — part of the larger Loran-Manatee accumulation that straddles the maritime boundary with Trinidad and Tobago, where Shell is developing the other side under the Manatee name.

The deal is not really about BP's appetite for risk. It is downstream of a much larger event: the removal of Nicolás Maduro from power in January, when he was transferred to the United States to face federal charges, and the handover of authority to Vice President Delcy Rodríguez, who has kept the existing institutional structure intact while courting foreign capital. Washington followed with a rewrite of the rules — a new Organic Hydrocarbons Law in January opened exploration, extraction and transport to private and foreign firms without PDVSA's old majority-stake requirement, and the Treasury's OFAC office reissued seven general licences covering oil exports, diluent sales and energy services.

A company that has been here before

BP's history in Venezuela is a study in how to survive a nationalization. When Hugo Chávez forced foreign operators to cede majority control to PDVSA in 2007, BP negotiated a minority stake and stayed, while ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips refused and had their assets seized outright — a decision that cost Caracas years of arbitration and roughly $2.5 billion in awards. BP's more recent Venezuelan foray was shorter-lived: a licence for the Cocuina field awarded in 2024 was pulled the following year when Washington tightened sanctions again. Thursday's award builds on a framework agreement BP signed with Caracas in April, the same month Chevron transferred its own Plataforma Deltana interests, including Loran, to PDVSA as part of an asset swap — clearing the way for the licence now going to BP's consortium.

"The award of the Loran Phase 2 licence is an important step forward," said BP chief executive Meg O'Neill, calling it a reflection of "the strong collaboration we have established with the Government of Venezuela and our partners." Rodríguez was blunter about her own priorities, telling reporters she has "a special interest in gas to promote national development" — a reminder that Venezuela's electricity grid, still recovering from June's earthquakes, needs gas-fired power more urgently than it needs another crude stream.

The licence is not yet a green light. BP itself notes the agreement remains subject to regulatory approvals and compliance with international sanctions rules, and lawyers tracking the file are withholding judgment until Washington's paperwork catches up with Caracas's. The next marker is Shell's Manatee project across the Trinidad line, which is targeting first gas next year — a timeline BP's side of the field is now trying to match.
