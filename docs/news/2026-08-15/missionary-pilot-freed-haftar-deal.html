US missionary pilot freed in Niger-Libya hostage deal brokered via Haftar

Khalifa Haftar, commander of Libya's eastern Libyan National Army, pictured in 2024 — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A US missionary pilot held nine months by an Islamic State affiliate in Niger was freed in a deal brokered through Libyan commander Khalifa Haftar.

The handover happened quietly, late on a Thursday in eastern Libya, with none of the choreography that usually accompanies a hostage release. An American missionary pilot, kidnapped from outside his own home in Niger ten months earlier, was delivered into US custody — and the man who made the delivery possible was not a diplomat but a warlord.

Kevin Rideout, a pilot for the North Carolina-based Christian charity Serving in Mission, was seized by three armed men in Niamey, Niger's capital, on October 21, 2025. He was driven west into Tillaberi, a stretch of scrubland along the Niger River that has become one of the most contested patches of ground in the Sahel, fought over by rival jihadist factions linked to al-Qaeda and Islamic State. He spent the following nine months in the custody of an Islamic State affiliate. On Thursday, according to Reuters, he was handed to authorities loyal to Khalifa Haftar in eastern Libya, then passed to US officials. SIM said in a statement that "Kevin is in good health in the care of US officials." President Trump posted that he was glad to have helped and looked forward to welcoming Rideout home.

A warlord as fixer

What made the release possible, according to Reuters sources, was not a military raid or a diplomatic breakthrough but a chain of intermediaries running through some of the Sahel's murkiest territory. The recent push to free Rideout was led by Cherif Ould Tahar, a Malian Arab businessman whose ties to regional armed groups and smuggling networks made him useful precisely because he operates outside formal state channels. His counterpart on delivery was Haftar, the 77-year-old commander of the self-styled Libyan National Army, who controls the country's east from Benghazi and has spent a decade fighting, and periodically negotiating with, everyone from Tripoli's UN-recognised government to Russian mercenaries.

One source told Reuters the Libyan side paid a ransom, though the details of what was exchanged, and with whom, remain unclear. The State Department and White House both declined to comment on the arrangement.

Why Washington needs Haftar

Haftar is not a natural partner for the United States. He has been accused for years of human rights abuses by rights groups, and his 2019 assault on Tripoli — abandoned after more than a year of fighting — left the question of who actually governs Libya unresolved. But he has spent that same period building something the Trump administration now finds useful: a security network that reaches south into the Sahel, where the US has lost most of its own footing. Niger's junta expelled American forces and shut down the drone base at Agadez in 2024 after Washington tried to condition cooperation on distancing the country from Russia. Mali and Burkina Faso, run by juntas of their own, have leaned further toward Moscow since. A senior State Department Africa official has made repeated trips to the region this year working toward intelligence-sharing arrangements with the same governments Washington once shunned — the broader recalibration this hostage deal fits inside.

Haftar's role in Rideout's release, brokered largely through back-channel figures rather than any government, is the clearest evidence yet that this recalibration extends to him personally. It gives him something he has wanted for years: standing as a security partner Washington cannot easily ignore, at a moment when he is also said to be courting American backing ahead of Libya's long-delayed elections.

The insurgency behind it

The deal does not touch the underlying problem. Islamic State's Sahel Province has expanded its reach across the Mali-Niger-Burkina Faso border zone even as it fights turf wars with the rival, al-Qaeda-linked JNIM for control of towns and smuggling routes. Tillaberi, where Rideout was taken, has recorded mass-casualty attacks on villages that refuse to pay the group's zakat tax or submit to its authority, and both insurgent networks have grown more capable, not less, since foreign militaries scaled back. Rideout's nine months in captivity is a data point in that trend line, not an exception to it.

For markets, the episode carries little direct weight — no sanctions shift, no announced US policy change on Libya or the Sahel. Its significance is what it signals: that Washington is willing to route sensitive operations through contested, sanctioned-adjacent figures when the alternative is having no lever at all in a region where it once had bases, drones and troops. The next marker to watch is whether that willingness converts into something more formal — a resumed US security relationship with Niger's junta, or explicit American backing for Haftar ahead of any Libyan vote.
