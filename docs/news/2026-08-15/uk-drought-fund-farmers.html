Ministers unveil £65m drought fund for farmers after backbench pressure

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who announced the £65m drought support package after weeks of pressure from Labour backbenchers. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Burnham's £65m drought fund answers backbench anger, but £15m for reservoirs and a 9% food-inflation forecast suggest the relief is smaller than the crisis.

Andy Burnham stood in front of cameras this week and did something his own MPs had been demanding for weeks: he called drought a national security issue. The prime minister announced £65m in emergency support for English farmers, most of it channelled through the Sustainable Farming Incentive, as almost three-quarters of England sits in official drought following the driest July since records began in 1836.

The money is real, and farmers need it. Whether it is enough is a different question — one the government's own advisers, and a fair number of Labour backbenchers, are not shy about raising.

The pressure that forced the moment

The package did not arrive out of nowhere. For weeks, climate-conscious Labour MPs had been warning that the government's response to a summer of wildfires and failed harvests lacked urgency. Andrew Pakes, the Labour MP for Peterborough, said he wanted to see more from ministers after the National Farmers' Union warned of the potential for food shortages. Fleur Anderson argued the UK was "fundamentally unprepared" for a hotter future and needed a proper plan for food resilience. Sky News reported that some backbenchers were prepared to describe the mood as bordering on revolt if the government pressed ahead with new North Sea oil and gas licensing while treating drought as a secondary concern.

That backdrop matters for reading the announcement correctly. This was not a package built from a Treasury spending review with agriculture identified as a priority; it was a political release valve, timed to a moment when 71.3% of England was officially in drought and Britain had separately declared a wildfire "tinderbox" and deployed the military to help fight fires. Dame Angela Eagle, the Defra secretary, framed it as recognition that farmers should not carry the risk of a changing climate alone. That is true. It is also the kind of language governments reach for when a fund is smaller than the crisis it is meant to address.

What's actually in the £65m

The headline figure breaks down into three pieces. £50m goes into the Sustainable Farming Incentive, lifting its 2026 budget to £290m, with extra money prioritised for the areas hit hardest by drought. £15m is earmarked for on-farm reservoirs — infrastructure that lets growers capture winter rainfall rather than relying on summer river abstraction that regulators increasingly restrict. The remainder of the package is regulatory rather than financial: farmers enrolled in environmental schemes will be allowed to graze or cut land where grass has failed without losing their stewardship payments, and new water-access flexibility is promised for food producers.

NFU president Tom Bradshaw called the announcement "welcome" and said it included "many of the NFU's asks," while cautioning that "more detail is needed to understand how these will work in practice." That is a diplomatic way of saying the union does not yet know whether the money will reach farms fast enough to matter this season.

Does it match the scale of the problem

This is where the political win starts to look thinner. Gavin Lane of the Country Land and Business Association described the reservoir fund as "little more than a temporary fix," noting £15m covers only a fraction of the infrastructure farmers actually need — reservoirs typically cost £1,000 to £3,000 per 1,000 cubic metres to build, and the UK has not completed a major new reservoir since 1991. AHDB figures show wheat yields already running around 13% below expectation for the week of 27 July, with some Suffolk growers reporting oats, wheat and vetch yields down 25–30%. Wholesale prices have moved further and faster: potatoes up around 40%, tomatoes up more than 60%, iceberg lettuce up roughly 90%. The Food and Drink Federation has lifted its full-year food inflation forecast to at least 9%, even as the Office for National Statistics' headline food inflation reading sat at a comparatively calm 1.7% in June — a gap that suggests the pressure is still working its way from farm gate to supermarket shelf rather than having already shown up in the data.

Set against that, £65m — with £50m of it simply topping up an existing incentive scheme rather than new crisis funding — reads less like a solution and more like a down payment. The government has not said which part of Defra's existing budget the money is being reprioritised from, which shadow Defra secretary Victoria Atkins seized on alongside her broader charge that Burnham had been "slow in realising that much of the country has been in drought for weeks."

What comes next

The more consequential move may land quietly. The government is due to publish updated planning guidance on August 17, folded into the National Planning Policy Framework, intended to strip away barriers that have made new reservoirs — on farms and at national scale — so hard to build for the past three and a half decades. Nine new reservoirs are already earmarked as part of a wider water-resilience push. Whether that guidance translates into shovels in the ground before next summer's dry spell, rather than the one after, is the test that will determine if this week's announcement was the start of a serious food-security strategy or a well-timed answer to an uncomfortable backbench meeting.
