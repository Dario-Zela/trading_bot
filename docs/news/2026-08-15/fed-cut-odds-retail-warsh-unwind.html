Soft retail sales and a collapsing Warsh rate-hike trade lift odds of a jumbo Fed cut

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
July retail sales fell 0.6% against a forecast 0.1% gain, adding to a collapsing bet on a Warsh-led rate hike and tilting markets toward a 50bp Fed cut in September.

Two Fed-watching stories collided this week, and together they moved the ceiling off market expectations for how far the Federal Reserve might cut interest rates next month. First, American shoppers pulled back harder than anyone forecast. Second, the crowded bet that new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh would preside over a rate hike has been unwinding since he declined to deliver one.

Neither data point alone would have been dramatic. Together, they pushed traders toward pricing a jumbo, half-point cut at the September 16 meeting — a scenario that looked like a fringe bet as recently as ten days ago.

Consumers hit the brakes

The Commerce Department's advance retail sales report, released August 14, showed spending fell 0.6% in July from June — the sharpest monthly drop since May 2025, and a clean miss against a consensus that had called for a 0.1% gain. Strip out autos and the picture barely improves: sales ex-autos fell 0.3% against expectations of a 0.2% rise. The Fed's preferred "control group" measure, which feeds directly into GDP calculations, dropped 0.4% versus a forecast 0.3% increase — the first negative control-group print since September 2025.

The damage was broad rather than concentrated in one volatile category, which is what caught economists' attention. Motor vehicle and parts dealers fell 1.8%, giving back June's incentive-driven gain. Non-store retailers — largely online sellers — dropped 2.2%, a hangover from Amazon's Prime Day promotion pulling spending forward into June. Electronics and appliances slipped 0.5%. On a year-on-year basis, nominal retail sales growth cooled to 5.0%, down from an upwardly revised 6.7% the prior month — a sharper deceleration than the headline monthly number alone suggests.

"American consumers are showing signs of fatigue," said Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union. "July retail sales were disappointing on all levels." Bernard Yaros of Oxford Economics was more measured: the numbers "warrant a downgrade to the spending forecast," he said, but it would be "premature to write off the consumer" on one report. The dollar didn't wait for the caveats — it slid to its lowest level since May within hours of the release.

The trade that bet the wrong way

The retail miss landed on top of an already-unwinding position that had defined the summer's rates trading: the so-called Warsh trade. Kevin Warsh took the Fed chair on May 22, replacing Jerome Powell after Senate confirmation, arriving with a reputation — earned during his 2006-2011 stint as a Fed governor — for hawkishness. Ahead of his second policy meeting as chair on July 29, traders built what Bloomberg described as a record short position in fed funds futures, topping a million contracts and roughly $5 trillion in notional value, effectively wagering that Warsh would move the committee toward a hike.

The meeting delivered a split decision instead. The FOMC voted 9-3 to hold the federal funds rate at 3.50%-3.75%, with three officials dissenting in favor of a hike — an unusually hawkish dissent count that kept the rate-hike trade alive for a few more weeks. It didn't survive the data that followed. July's CPI came in at 0.1% headline and 0.2% core, both in line with tame estimates; producer prices were flat at the headline level and up just 0.1% at the core. "Given recent data, Mr. Warsh is very unlikely to sound stridently hawkish," said Samuel Tombs, chief U.S. economist at Pantheon Macroeconomics, whose base case now has the Fed holding rates through year-end rather than raising them. September rate-hike odds on prediction markets fell from roughly 60% earlier in August to around 25% by the time the retail sales report landed — and the report finished the job.

Now the argument is about size

With a hike effectively off the table, the debate among rates traders has shifted entirely to how large the Fed's eventual cut will be — a 25-basis-point move that keeps the committee's options open, or a 50-basis-point cut that acknowledges the consumer is weakening faster than the data had shown through midsummer. Fed funds futures pricing swung toward the larger move in the hours after the retail sales release, putting a 50-basis-point cut at the September 16 meeting as the more likely outcome for the first time since the easing debate began.

That repricing puts Warsh in an uncomfortable spot. The inflation data that briefly justified patience — tame CPI, flat PPI — is also the data that removes his cover for staying hawkish. A weak consumer, meanwhile, argues for moving before conditions deteriorate further, but acting aggressively on a single soft retail print risks looking reactive rather than data-driven, precisely the charge critics leveled at his predecessor. The three dissenting hawks on the July committee haven't gone anywhere, and a 50-basis-point cut would be a larger reversal from their position than a 25-basis-point one.

What's next

The next hard data point arrives well before the September 16 decision: August retail sales, due September 16 — the same day as the meeting itself — meaning the Fed will vote before seeing it, leaving July's numbers as the most recent read policymakers have in hand. More immediately, Warsh delivers his first keynote address as Fed chair at the Jackson Hole symposium on August 28, themed this year around financial innovation in payments and policy. He has signaled the speech will step back from what he called "near-sighted debates" over the next rate decision — but with fed funds futures now leaning toward a jumbo cut, markets will be parsing every line for confirmation, or pushback, regardless of what he intended to talk about.
