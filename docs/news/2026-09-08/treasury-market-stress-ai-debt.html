Big Tech's AI debt binge, buyback 'repression' fears and weak data converge on Treasuries

Data center infrastructure of the kind Big Tech is financing with a record wave of bond issuance. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Big Tech's record $320bn AI bond binge, weak-data-defying yields and Bessent's bond buybacks are converging pressures on the Treasury market.

Three separate stories about the Treasury market landed this week, and they turn out to be the same story. Big Tech is borrowing at a pace that rivals the U.S. government's own bond calendar. Treasury yields are climbing even as economic data softens, which by the old playbook should be pulling them down. And the Treasury Department is now buying back its own long-dated debt to keep a lid on borrowing costs — a move at least one veteran commodities strategist reads as an admission that demand has cracked. Put together, the picture is of a government debt market that is no longer the default, friction-free place to park capital, and a set of buyers and issuers all adjusting to that fact in real time.

The debt machine feeding the AI boom

Amazon, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle have issued a combined $194 billion in bonds through early July this year, up 79% from all of 2025, according to figures cited by Bloomberg. Zoom out to the broader universe of AI-linked issuers — including the special-purpose vehicles increasingly used to finance data centers — and BNP Paribas puts the year-to-date total near $220 billion. Extrapolated through year-end, combined Big Tech bond issuance is on pace to hit a record $320 billion in 2026, a 60% jump from last year and roughly nine times where it stood as recently as 2024. That sum now equals close to 70% of total Treasury bond issuance for the year.

"We believe the AI debt boom is now driving Treasury yields higher," the Kobeissi Letter argued this week, framing Amazon and Meta as "serious competition to Uncle Sam" in the long-end of the bond market. The mechanism is simple: corporate bonds tied to AI buildouts currently pay roughly 200 basis points more than comparable Treasuries, and that spread is pulling fixed-income capital away from government debt at the margin. Not every analyst reads it as alarming — Jai Kedia at the Cato Institute called it "healthy competition to government bonds from increasingly valuable corporate bonds" — but the scale is no longer trivial next to Washington's own financing needs.

The timing compounds the pressure. The 30-year Treasury yield climbed to roughly 5.34% in mid-August, its highest since 2007, before settling back near 5.25%-5.27% in early September. The 10-year touched 4.80%, a level last seen in January 2025. Corporate issuers are not causing that move on their own, but they are now large enough to matter to it.

A demand problem hiding in plain sight

That rise in yields is unusual for another reason: it has coincided with a run of weaker economic data, not stronger. Historically, soft growth or labor numbers pull long-term yields down, as investors price in slower activity and eventual rate cuts. Robin Brooks, a senior fellow at the Brookings Institution, argues that the decoupling is the real story — more so than the deficit numbers everyone already knows about. "As far as I can tell, it's an all-hands-on-deck situation where long-term yields are concerned," Brooks wrote, describing demand for Treasury debt as "weaker than first meets the eye."

The fiscal backdrop he's reacting to is not new: the national debt stands near $40 trillion, the annual deficit is heading toward $2 trillion, and interest payments alone now run about $1.25 trillion a year. What Brooks is flagging is that buyers who would normally absorb that supply — and reward weak data with lower yields — are behaving differently this cycle, plausibly because they have somewhere else to put the money, including the wave of AI-linked corporate paper described above. Not everyone shares his urgency: economist Ed Yardeni has argued current levels reflect normalization of a 10-year yield in the 4%-5% range rather than a demand crisis.

Bessent's buyback, and the 'financial repression' read

The Treasury has responded with a tool it rarely deployed at this scale: buying back its own long-dated bonds to support prices and cap yields. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent doubled the size of those long-end buyback operations to at least $4 billion apiece, with the larger operations beginning September 9 — the day this piece was reported.

Jeff Currie, the former Goldman Sachs chief commodities strategist, has been the most pointed critic of the move. "A sovereign that has to buy its own bonds to set the price has already conceded that the market won't," Currie wrote, calling the buybacks a form of financial repression — a policy that holds borrowing costs artificially low at the expense of savers and bondholders. His conclusion is that the same forces distorting the Treasury market are bullish for gold: with real yields effectively capped by policy while physical scarcity pushes up commodity prices, he describes the current setup as "scarcity repricing the numerator" while "repression debases the denominator." Currie turned outright bullish on gold, silver and agricultural commodities in mid-August and has set a long-term price target of $10,000 an ounce for gold.

The common thread

Strip away the specifics and the three angles describe one mechanism from different vantage points. Big Tech's AI buildout needs financing on a scale that now shows up as a rival claim on the same investor base the Treasury depends on. That competition is one plausible explanation for why yields keep rising even as the economic data that would normally cool them off. And the government's main policy lever for pushing back — buying its own bonds — is being read by at least some market veterans not as reassurance but as confirmation that natural demand has thinned. None of this means a funding crisis is imminent; deficits, buybacks and volatile long-end yields have coexisted with functioning markets before. But the size of the numbers is new. A $320 billion corporate bond wave competing for the same buyers as a $2 trillion federal deficit is a different market than the one that priced Treasuries five years ago.

The next test comes quickly. Wednesday's enlarged buyback operation will show whether Treasury's checkbook can hold the 30-year under 5.3% without further help. Behind it sits the Treasury's next quarterly refunding announcement, which will reveal whether issuance plans shift in response to the corporate crowding-out story, and a fresh run of labor and inflation data that will tell whether Brooks's demand-side diagnosis or Yardeni's normalization view is closer to right.
