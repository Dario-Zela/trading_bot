Citigroup nears a China brokerage license as Beijing edges open to Western banks

Jane Fraser, Citigroup's chairman and CEO, whose restructuring drive now turns toward China as a growth lever. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Citigroup is close to a wholly owned China brokerage license — a small step for its own numbers, but a signal that Beijing still wants Wall Street inside its markets.

Citigroup expects to receive final approval within weeks for a wholly owned brokerage license in mainland China — the kind of regulatory nod that, four years ago, Western banks routinely waited on for so long they stopped mentioning it in earnings calls. The bank first applied in December 2021. Chinese securities regulators finished their review in May 2026. Now, according to Reuters sourcing, the final license could land as soon as this month, with timing that may coincide with Xi Jinping's scheduled visit to Washington in late September for talks with President Trump.

That overlap is probably not an accident. Citi CEO Jane Fraser was part of Trump's delegation on a state visit to Beijing back in May, the same week Chinese regulators signaled the securities license was cleared in principle. Financial-market access has become one of the few threads Washington and Beijing can pull on without it turning into a fight — useful for both governments when so much else in the relationship is not.

A four-year queue, nearly at the front

Once approved, Citigroup Securities (China) Co. Ltd. will be authorized to conduct A-share brokerage, underwriting, research and principal trading in China's onshore market — the full menu that Beijing withheld from foreign firms until 2020, when it lifted the ownership caps that had forced global banks into joint ventures with local partners, typically capped at minority stakes. Citi will be the seventh foreign institution to secure a wholly foreign-owned securities license under the new regime, following JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Standard Chartered, BNP Paribas, Mizuho and UBS.

The bank is already sizing up the buildout: roughly 50 employees today, a target near 100 by the end of 2026, filled through a mix of internal transfers from Hong Kong and other parts of Asia and external hires focused on technology, healthcare, consumer goods and financial institutions — plus, notably, AI and semiconductors, the sectors Beijing most wants foreign capital chasing. It is a modest headcount next to the scale of the ambition, but onshore securities licenses are won in years, not quarters, and Citi is playing catch-up against rivals already generating meaningful profit from theirs: Goldman's China unit posted about 1.46 billion yuan ($205 million) in 2025 profit, JPMorgan's roughly 984 million yuan, Morgan Stanley's a comparatively modest 138 million yuan.

Why Citi needs this more than the headline suggests

The context matters. Citigroup spent 2024 and 2025 cutting roughly 20,000 jobs worldwide, exiting retail banking in 14 international markets, and collapsing management layers from thirteen to eight — a restructuring severe enough that, until recently, the more interesting question about Citi was whether it could stop shrinking, not where it might grow. The turnaround has started to show: first-quarter 2026 revenue was the bank's highest in a decade, return on tangible common equity hit 13.1%, its best since 2021, and the stock is up roughly 83% since Fraser took over in 2021. But that recovery has largely been about cost discipline and reorganization, not new revenue engines — which is where the China license fits.

At Citi's 2026 investor day, management set a target of lifting investment-banking wallet share from 4.7% in 2025 to above 6% in the near term, part of what the bank calls its "OneCiti" push to cross-sell more products to the same institutional clients. That is a group-wide target, not a China-specific one, and a single onshore brokerage license — competing against three rivals with a head start and a fourth, Morgan Stanley, that moved earlier still — will not carry it there alone. What the license does is let Citi extend relationships it already has. The bank's existing China franchise in foreign exchange, cash management and trade finance gives it a client book to sell brokerage and underwriting into, rather than having to build one from scratch.

The signal in Beijing's yes

The more durable story here may not be about Citi's balance sheet at all. Financial decoupling has been a live theme for several years — sanctions risk, data-security rules, sovereign-fund reallocation away from US assets — and against that backdrop, a Chinese regulator approving a fourth major US bank for full securities ownership reads as a deliberate signal that Beijing wants to keep Wall Street's capital-markets expertise flowing into onshore trading and underwriting, even as it restricts foreign involvement elsewhere in its financial system. Access is not the same as welcome, and profitability for these units so far has been thin next to the scale of China's markets. But a government that wanted to freeze Western banks out had an easy lever to pull for four years and chose, in the end, not to pull it.

The next marker is the license itself — whether it clears before or alongside Xi's Washington visit, and whether Citi's China unit can move beyond a headcount build toward the kind of profit numbers Goldman and JPMorgan are already posting. Citi's own wallet-share target has no fixed deadline attached beyond "near term," which leaves the more precise test in Beijing's hands, not Citi's.
