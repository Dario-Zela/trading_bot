BEYOND THE TAPE · MONETARY DEBATE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-08 · 4 min read

Economist Steve Hanke tells JD Vance to 'stick to knitting' over Friedman

Vice President JD Vance, whose embrace of "Hamiltonian" economics over Milton Friedman's free-market doctrine drew a public rebuke from economist Steve Hanke. — Emily J. Higgins / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Economist Steve Hanke told VP JD Vance to "stick to knitting" for calling Milton Friedman's economics outdated in favor of a Hamiltonian, state-directed approach.

Milton Friedman has been dead for nearly two decades, but he still has people willing to go to the mat for him on social media — and this week it was Steve Hanke, the Johns Hopkins economist who counted Friedman as a friend and mentor, throwing the punch.

The target was Vice President JD Vance, who told Michael Knowles' podcast back in late June that "American economic policy on the right is now much more Alexander Hamilton than it is Milton Friedman," and predicted the Hamiltonian tradition — tariffs, industrial policy, an activist state — "will dominate American conservative economic thinking for the future." Vance argued Friedman's small-government, free-market instincts belonged to a 1980s America that still had "a very rich and powerful institutional Christianity" providing moral guardrails markets alone can't supply. Without that scaffolding, he suggested, laissez-faire just optimizes for GDP while hollowing out the things — stable families, manufacturing towns, a living wage — that conservatives are supposed to care about.

Hanke wasn't having it. "Instead of denouncing my friend, mentor and Nobelist Milton Friedman, VP JD Vance should stick to knitting," he wrote on X. "If he bothered to listen to Friedman, he might learn a thing or two." He then quoted Friedman from a 1979 interview: "The government is TOO BIG. It's TOO INTRUSIVE. It RESTRICTS what we can do. It's becoming our master instead of our servant. We've got to react against it and cut it down to size."

Two economists' worth of pedigree, deployed as an insult

"Stick to knitting" is a specific kind of putdown — the sort economists reserve for people they consider unqualified amateurs wandering into their field. Hanke has earned the right to be prickly about it: he built the currency board that stopped Bulgaria's hyperinflation in 1997, has advised half a dozen governments on dollarization, and served on Reagan's Council of Economic Advisers. Friedman, for his part, won the 1976 Nobel in economics and spent decades as the intellectual anchor of free-market conservatism — the man Vance is now trying to politely retire.

That's really what makes this more than a Twitter spat. Vance isn't a crank; he's a sitting vice president road-testing a rebrand of Republican economics. His term for it is "American developmentalism" — the idea that "the economy is a tool to service the dignity of the human person," and that government should actively shape markets toward family formation, manufacturing jobs and community, rather than simply staying out of the way. It's the same intellectual current that has powered the Trump administration's tariff push and its comfort with industrial policy, dressed up in Hamiltonian rather than populist language.

Ideology versus implementation

What the exchange doesn't tell you is whether any of this changes anything concrete. Vance made his Hamilton-versus-Friedman comment in June; Hanke's rebuttal only surfaced this week, months later, which says more about the durability of the underlying argument than about any fresh policy news. There's no bill, no nominee, no Fed pick attached to either man's remarks — just two people arguing about which dead economist better describes the moment.

That matters for anyone watching monetary policy specifically, since Hanke has also been a vocal critic of Federal Reserve independence and money-supply management under Jerome Powell. But the Vance-Hanke clash is about fiscal and industrial philosophy — tariffs, the proper size of government — not about who controls interest rates. Reading a Fed-independence threat into a podcast soundbite from three months ago would be getting ahead of the evidence.

The more interesting tell is that Vance felt the need to pick this fight in public at all — a vice president don't usually go out of their way to declare a Nobel laureate obsolete unless they think the argument is winnable inside their own coalition. Whether that translates into an actual doctrine — tariff schedules, subsidy programs, a Friedman-skeptic at Treasury — is the thing worth watching, not the knitting joke itself.

The next real test will be whether Vance's rhetoric shows up in the administration's next budget request or trade actions, rather than just its podcast circuit.
