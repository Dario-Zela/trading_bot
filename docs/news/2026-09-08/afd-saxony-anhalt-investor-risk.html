AfD's Saxony-Anhalt win keeps investors wary of Germany's policy drift

The Landtag of Saxony-Anhalt in Magdeburg, where the AfD now holds 39 of 83 seats after its September 6 election win. — Torsten Maue / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The AfD's historic Saxony-Anhalt win adds political uncertainty to Germany's fragile recovery, with markets watching for wider fallout as Berlin votes September 20.

The far-right Alternative for Germany won a state election in Saxony-Anhalt on September 6, taking 43.8% of the vote — more than double its 2021 result and the strongest showing by a party of the far right in a German regional poll since 1945. The AfD will hold 39 of 83 seats in the Magdeburg parliament, three short of an outright majority, leaving it without a clear path to govern but with an unambiguous mandate to try. For a bond market and a currency bloc already nursing doubts about Berlin's ability to reform, the result adds a political variable that is hard to price but impossible to ignore.

Saxony-Anhalt itself is not where the risk lives. The state accounts for less than 2% of German output, and the three eastern states voting this autumn — Saxony-Anhalt, Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania, which follow on September 20 — together control just 11 of 69 votes in the Bundesrat, the upper house that can block federal legislation. Chancellor Friedrich Merz had warned before the vote that he could not imagine international investors attending a groundbreaking ceremony hosted by an AfD state premier; the actual near-term economic exposure is smaller than that rhetoric implied.

Where the real pressure sits

The concern that has shown up in market commentary is reputational rather than fiscal. Bloomberg strategist Conor Cooper noted that Germany's bund market trades as a safe haven partly because of a presumption of political stability, and an AfD advance chips at that presumption even without changing a single budget line. The immediate market reaction bore this out: the DAX slipped 0.3% and the 10-year Bund yield ticked up just a single basis point, to roughly 3.35%. That yield's climb from 2.8% in June has been driven mainly by energy prices and the European Central Bank's policy path, not by domestic politics — evidence that traders are, for now, treating Saxony-Anhalt as noise rather than signal.

The more durable worry is what Oxford Economics has flagged: a widening political fracture in Berlin that makes the coalition's already difficult negotiations over pensions, the labour market and energy policy harder to close. Those are precisely the reforms Germany's federal government needs to push through to arrest a manufacturing slide that shows up starkly in the data — Volkswagen is cutting roughly 100,000 jobs, 15% of its workforce, by the end of the decade; German car production sits 16% below 2019 levels; and Intel scrapped a €30 billion semiconductor project in Saxony-Anhalt itself last year. A manufacturing PMI reading of 54.3 in August, a 51-month high, offers a counterweight, but not enough to settle the argument.

The EU dimension

Germany's coordinating role on EU industrial policy — subsidy design for the green transition, the bloc's response to Chinese overcapacity in autos and solar, the terms of any future carbon-border adjustments — depends on a federal government with the political room to negotiate and, when needed, to spend. A CDU-led coalition distracted by managing losses to its right flank in three regional legislatures has less of that room. Oxford Economics puts the stakes plainly: continued stalemate risks a downward revision to Germany's potential growth rate, which would ripple into the eurozone-wide calculus on fiscal coordination that Berlin has historically anchored.

The next test arrives quickly. Berlin and Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania vote on September 20, and a further AfD surge in the capital would be harder to wave off as an eastern regional anomaly. Traders watching Bund yields relative to swaps, and any drift in the spread between German and periphery eurozone debt, will have their next real data point then — alongside whatever the CDU-led coalition manages, or fails, to agree on pension and energy reform in the weeks before.
