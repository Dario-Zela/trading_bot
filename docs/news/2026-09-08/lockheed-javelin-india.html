Lockheed Martin advances Javelin co-production plans with India

U.S. Marines fire a Javelin anti-tank missile during a training exercise — the same system now headed for co-production in India. — Lance Cpl. Jonathan Willcox / U.S. Marine Corps (public domain, Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Lockheed Martin and RTX's Javelin venture signed an MoU with Tata Advanced Systems to assemble anti-tank missiles in India, deepening a defense-industrial bet on the Indo-Pacific.

Lockheed Martin's anti-tank missile business is about to get an Indian address. On August 30, the Javelin Joint Venture — a 50-50 partnership between Lockheed Martin and RTX — signed a memorandum of understanding with Tata Advanced Systems Limited to establish a final assembly and integration facility for the Javelin missile in India. It's the clearest signal yet that Washington's defense contractors are betting on India as a manufacturing partner, not just a customer.

The mechanics are straightforward. Sub-assembly kits will still be built at Lockheed's plant in Troy, Alabama, and guidance electronics units will still come from RTX's Tucson, Arizona facility — the missile's American manufacturing base stays intact. What's new is the last step: final assembly and integration, currently done entirely in the U.S., will move to a Tata Advanced Systems facility in India. Rich Liccion, the Javelin Joint Venture's vice president, called it "a pivotal step in ensuring a resilient supply chain" for the weapon system. Tata Advanced Systems CEO Sukaran Singh framed it as bringing "critical assembly expertise and knowledge transfer" into India's defense industrial base.

From emergency buy to production line

The co-production plan follows, and was arguably accelerated by, a much smaller and more urgent deal. Earlier this year India signed a roughly $45.7 million agreement to buy a batch of Javelins outright — an emergency purchase to plug a capability gap in its anti-tank arsenal, unrelated to any local manufacturing. That one-off buy was always understood as a placeholder while New Delhi and Washington negotiated the bigger prize: a standing production line inside India. The August MoU is the first concrete step toward that line actually getting built, though neither company has disclosed a facility location, opening timeline, or projected job count.

The missile itself needs little introduction to anyone who has followed the war in Ukraine, where more than 10,000 Javelins have been supplied to Ukrainian forces since Russia's invasion and the weapon has become something of a battlefield celebrity — a shoulder-fired, fire-and-forget system that lets a single soldier take out armor from a standoff distance. Since its introduction, the Javelin Joint Venture has produced more than 55,000 missiles and 12,000 reusable command launch units.

Why India, and why now

India has spent the past decade trying to reduce its reliance on Russian hardware, which historically supplied the bulk of its military equipment, while also building a domestic arms industry under its "Make in India" push. That effort isn't only about buying American: New Delhi's Defence Acquisition Council approved a roughly ₹52,000-crore ($6 billion) package in July that includes India's own indigenous man-portable anti-tank missile, the MPATGM, developed as a homegrown alternative to exactly the kind of weapon Lockheed is now offering to build locally. The Javelin deal has to compete with that program for the Indian Army's long-term procurement dollars, not just with Russian or Chinese suppliers.

For Lockheed, the calculus fits a broader pattern in its Indian dealings this year. The company opened a formal bid for India's roughly $10.5 billion medium transport aircraft contest in August, pitting its C-130J against Brazil's Embraer C-390, while keeping its more contentious F-35 discussions strictly government-to-government rather than pursuing a direct sales pitch — a sign that Washington is still tightening how far fifth-generation fighter technology can travel, even to a partner it's courting elsewhere. Localizing a decades-old, lower-tier system like the Javelin is a far easier proposition than localizing a stealth fighter, and it lets Lockheed build goodwill — and industrial relationships — without touching the sensitive end of its export controls.

The market read

Lockheed shares have traded near $526, up roughly 30% since the start of the year, a run driven more by broad defense-spending tailwinds than by any single India announcement. Zacks currently rates the stock a Hold. The Javelin co-production deal is unlikely to move that needle on its own — no financial terms were disclosed, and Javelin revenue is a rounding error next to Lockheed's F-35 program. Its significance is strategic rather than fiscal: a foothold in India's defense-manufacturing base ahead of larger competitions still to be decided, and a supply chain with a second geography if U.S. production capacity is ever strained.

The near-term catalyst to watch is less financial than administrative — whether the MoU converts into a signed production contract with a disclosed facility and timeline, which would be the point at which India's defense ministry and Tata put real capital behind the announcement rather than intent.
