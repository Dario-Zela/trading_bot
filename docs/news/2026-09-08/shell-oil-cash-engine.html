Shell rides $97 oil to a $21bn cash haul, though war-risk costs loom

In one sentence
Shell's $21bn cash haul reflects $97 oil, but war-risk insurance and Qatari LNG disruption from the Hormuz crisis are quietly eating into the same windfall.

Shell shares climbed 1.3% on Monday to $34.83, a modest move that nonetheless captured something larger: the London-listed major is now generating roughly $21 billion a year in operating cash flow, with Brent crude trading above $97 a barrel for the first time since late July. The stock's gain outpaced a softer broader market, as traders leaned into the simplest read of the moment — expensive oil means a fat cash machine for the companies that pump it.

That read is not wrong. It is, however, incomplete. The same conflict pushing crude toward $100 — a widening confrontation involving Iran and the Strait of Hormuz — is also driving up the cost of getting that oil to market, insuring it, and keeping Shell's own facilities running. The windfall is real. So is the bill.

The cash engine

Shell's second quarter, reported July 30th, delivered $9.8 billion in adjusted earnings and $21.4 billion in cash flow from operations, its best quarterly haul since the 2022 energy shock that followed Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Brent averaged $104 a barrel during the quarter, up from $81 in the first three months of the year. Net debt fell to about $42 billion, gearing sits at 19%, and management announced a further $3 billion share-buyback program — the 19th consecutive quarter Shell has bought back at least that much stock.

On paper, that is a company converting a hostile world into shareholder returns with practiced efficiency. Shell's integrated model — production, refining, trading and a large LNG book — gives it several ways to profit from dislocation, and the market has rewarded that flexibility: shares trade at roughly 14% above the $81.65 fair-value estimate that GuruFocus's valuation model puts on the stock, a premium investors are paying for exposure to exactly this kind of volatility.

What the oil price doesn't show

The same tensions lifting Brent are also inflating the cost side of Shell's ledger, and the scale of that inflation is not trivial. War-risk insurance premiums for tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz have surged from roughly 0.25% of hull value before the crisis to a range of 3% to 10% now, according to shipping-market data reported through the summer — turning a policy that once cost an operator around $250,000 on a $100 million vessel into one costing $3 million to $10 million. Traffic through the strait, which normally carries around a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, collapsed by more than 80% at the height of the fighting, from 120–140 vessels a day to as few as two.

Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex — the source of roughly a fifth of global LNG trade routed through Hormuz — has also sustained damage serious enough that analysts now expect flat-to-negative LNG trade growth in 2026, the first contraction in over a decade. Shell is one of the larger equity holders in Qatari LNG capacity, which means the same chokepoint squeezing tanker economics is also squeezing volumes on a business line the company has spent a decade building up.

Shell's own chief executive, Wael Sawan, has been blunt about the scale of the disruption. Speaking as the conflict widened earlier this year, he estimated the market had "dug ourselves a hole of close to a billion barrels of crude shortage," warning that the deficit grows "every day" the fighting continues, and cautioned governments against policy responses that could magnify supply shocks further — arguing there is no "national security without energy security." Those are not the words of an executive treating the crisis as pure upside.

The earnings math traders should watch

None of this shows up cleanly in a single stock-price move. A 1.3% gain on a $97 oil print is the market pricing commodity beta — the mechanical relationship between crude prices and an oil major's revenue. It is a much blunter instrument for pricing in higher insurance premiums on Shell's shipping and trading operations, reduced Qatari LNG throughput, or the operational friction of running assets in a region where the primary maritime artery has, at times, nearly shut down.

That gap matters because it is exactly where earnings surprises come from. Cash flow from operations is a top-line measure of how much money came in the door; it says nothing about what Shell had to spend to keep the door open. If insurance, freight and security costs continue climbing faster than the market's mental model assumes, Shell's net income and free cash flow could grow more slowly than the headline oil price would suggest — even as revenue and CFFO look robust.

The next real test comes with Shell's third-quarter results, expected in late October, which will show whether the elevated war-risk premiums and the Ras Laffan disruption are compressing margins on the trading and LNG divisions specifically, rather than just the oil-price line everyone already prices in. Until then, the $97 headline will keep doing most of the work in Shell's stock price — whether or not it is telling the whole story.
