BP names Ian Tyler permanent chair, closing the chapter on May's boardroom turmoil

BP's head office at St James's Square, London. — WhisperToMe / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
BP made interim chair Ian Tyler permanent four months after firing his predecessor over unspecified conduct and governance concerns.

BP has stopped auditioning for a permanent chair. On September 2, the London-based energy major named Ian Tyler, a 65-year-old former construction boss who has sat on its board for less than a year and a half, to hold the job on a lasting basis — closing out a vacancy that began in May when the company fired his predecessor with a single sentence about "governance standards, oversight and conduct" and no further explanation.

The appointment ends the most disruptive stretch in BP's boardroom since Bernard Looney was ousted as chief executive in 2023 for concealing personal relationships with colleagues. It does not, on its own, resolve the deeper question hanging over the company: whether a fifth change of chief executive and second change of chair inside four years reflects a business finally settling into a new strategy, or one still searching for stable footing.

What actually happened to the last chair

Albert Manifold, the former CRH chief executive who took over as BP chair in October 2025, lasted barely eight months. The board dismissed him with immediate effect on May 26, 2026, saying only that "serious concerns" had been raised about governance and conduct. BP has never detailed the underlying complaint in public, but reporting at the time — citing four people familiar with the matter — described a pattern of aggressive treatment of colleagues that surfaced through a whistleblower report. The BBC reported the specific allegations amounted to bullying and overbearing behaviour. Manifold rejected the characterisation, calling it a "false narrative," and took legal advice. BP's shares fell 9% on the news, a reaction that said as much about investor fatigue with the company's leadership churn as about the incident itself.

Tyler, who had joined BP's board as a non-executive director only in April 2025, was installed as interim chair the same day. The four months since then have functioned as a probation period — for him and, implicitly, for the board's ability to run a clean search after an ugly exit. Dame Amanda Blanc, the senior independent director who led that search, said Tyler "brings significant experience providing challenge and support to executive teams, while maintaining strong governance." Blanc herself will not seek re-election at next year's annual meeting, part of a broader refresh of BP's board rather than a single swap at the top.

Why the board reached for Tyler specifically

Tyler's résumé is heavier on engineering and construction than on oil. He ran Balfour Beatty from 2005 to 2013, chairs the building-materials group Grafton and sits on the board of miner Anglo American; he has also chaired Cairn Energy, giving him at least some upstream exposure. What he is not is an oil-and-gas lifer or an outside activist pick — he is an internal promotion from a board that had just been embarrassed by an external hire gone wrong, and the safest read of his selection is that BP wanted continuity and a known quantity over another high-profile outside appointment.

That caution makes sense given what BP has been through. The chair's chair has now changed hands three times in three years — Helge Lund, then Manifold, now Tyler — while the chief executive's office has turned over even faster. Murray Auchincloss, who succeeded Looney, announced his own departure in December 2025 after just two years, saying simply that "now is the right time to hand the reins to a new leader." His replacement, Meg O'Neill, arrived from Woodside Energy in April 2026 as BP's fifth chief executive since 2020 — the first woman to run a Western oil major — tasked with accelerating a pivot back toward core oil and gas that Auchincloss had already begun.

The pressure Tyler inherits

None of this leadership turnover happened in a vacuum. Elliott Management built a roughly 5% stake in BP in early 2025, valued at the time near $3.8 billion, and pushed publicly for the company to abandon much of its renewables spending, cut costs and lift annual free cash flow toward $20 billion by 2027. BP's February 2025 strategic reset — raising oil and gas investment to around $10 billion a year while cutting transition spending by more than $5 billion — was announced under that pressure and after 2024 profit collapsed to $381 million. The reset has since hardened: BP suspended its $750 million-a-quarter share buyback in February 2026 and dropped its guidance on returning 30–40% of operating cash flow to shareholders, choosing instead to prioritise cutting net debt from roughly $22–23 billion toward a $14–18 billion target by the end of 2027. Whether buybacks resume is now explicitly O'Neill's call, not a board commitment.

That is the real substance of what Tyler is being asked to steward: not a governance fix in isolation, but a board stable enough to let O'Neill execute a contested strategic pivot without another chair-level distraction. The stock, up roughly 25% year-to-date, suggests investors have been rewarding the direction of the reset regardless of the boardroom noise — which raises the possibility that Tyler's appointment is less a resolution of a crisis than a formality confirming a decision the market had already priced in.

What to watch next

BP reports third-quarter results in late October, the first under Tyler as permanent chair and only the second full quarter under O'Neill — a natural checkpoint for whether the debt-reduction targets are on track and whether the board signals anything on restarting buybacks. Beyond that, Blanc's eventual successor as senior independent director will be the next test of whether this board can complete a leadership change without incident, something it has now failed to do twice in three years.
