Poland's regulator widens shrinkflation scrutiny of food and consumer giants

Packaged food products on supermarket shelves — the kind of goods now under UOKiK's shrinkflation review. — Frankie Fouganthin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UOKiK is reviewing whether Danone, Mondelez, Nestlé and Unilever disclosed shrinking pack sizes clearly enough to Polish shoppers.

Poland's competition and consumer protection authority, UOKiK, has opened a review into whether four of the world's largest consumer-goods companies — Danone, Mondelez International, Nestlé and Unilever — misled shoppers by quietly shrinking package sizes while holding prices steady. The inquiry covers food, cleaning and personal care products sold in the country, and marks one of the most direct enforcement moves yet against a practice regulators across Europe have watched with growing irritation since the inflation surge of 2022–23.

"Reducing the weight without changing the price effectively means that consumers pay more for the product," UOKiK president Tomasz Chróstny said, adding that the practice makes it harder for shoppers to compare prices across brands and over time. The regulator's specific question is narrower than the practice itself: not whether shrinkflation happened, but whether companies disclosed it clearly when packaging stayed the same size while the contents inside got smaller.

Mondelez, maker of Oreo and Milka in the region, confirmed it is cooperating. "We confirm that Mondelez Polska is cooperating with the Office of Competition and Consumer Protection and is providing all necessary clarifications," a spokesperson for the company's Polish unit said. Danone, Nestlé and Unilever have not issued detailed public responses.

A court ruling opened the door

The legal grounding for UOKiK's move is a Court of Justice of the European Union decision in April, in Case C-301/25, which found that a product complying with food-labelling law does not shield a company from a separate finding that its packaging practices misled consumers under EU unfair commercial practices rules. That distinction matters: shrinkflation itself is not illegal in the EU, since manufacturers are generally free to set pack sizes as they choose. What regulators are now testing is the adequacy of disclosure — whether a shopper glancing at a familiar box would reasonably know less product is inside for the same price.

Poland is not the first mover. France introduced a rule in 2024 requiring large and medium-sized retailers to post in-store notices flagging when a food or drink product has shrunk without a price cut, shifting the disclosure burden partly onto supermarkets rather than manufacturers alone. UOKiK's review, by contrast, targets the producers directly, and Poland already has a track record of pursuing companies over misleading price-reduction claims following a wave of first-time fines earlier this year for unrelated pricing practices.

If UOKiK concludes shoppers were deceived, it can escalate to formal proceedings, which under Polish law can carry penalties of up to 10% of a company's annual turnover — a ceiling steep enough to focus the attention of finance teams well beyond Warsaw. No proceedings have been opened yet; the current review is a preliminary fact-finding exercise.

The stakes extend past Poland's borders. Consumer-goods manufacturers leaned on pack-size reductions as a quieter alternative to sticker-price increases during the post-pandemic inflation run, a tactic that helped protect gross margins as input costs rose. A finding against any of the four companies would hand regulators elsewhere in Central and Eastern Europe — and potentially Brussels — a template for their own inquiries, at a moment when CPG margins are already under investor scrutiny after several quarters of volume softness. Watch for whether UOKiK's preliminary review converts into formal charges, and whether any EU member state cites the C-301/25 ruling to open a parallel case of its own.
