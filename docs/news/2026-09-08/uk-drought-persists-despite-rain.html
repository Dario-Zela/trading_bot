Drought and hosepipe bans persist despite England's wettest week since February

Grimwith Reservoir in North Yorkshire, its shoreline drawn back by months of low rainfall. — Peter Bond / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
England's heaviest week of rain since February has failed to shift reservoir, groundwater or drought status — leaving hosepipe bans and cost pressures intact into autumn.

It rained harder across England this past week than at any point since early February — up to 48mm in central parts of the country, enough to lift river flows and dampen soil that has been cracking since spring. And yet the drought has not moved. Ten of the Environment Agency's operational areas remain formally in drought, four more are in "prolonged dry weather" status, and roughly seven in ten hosepipes in England are still, technically, illegal to use.

That gap between a genuinely wet week and an unchanged drought map is the story. Ten water companies — among them Thames Water, Anglian Water, Southern Water and, since 18 August, Wessex Water for the first time in fifty years — have Temporary Use Bans in force, covering tens of millions of customers from London and the Home Counties down through Hampshire, Dorset, Somerset and the South West. Bristol Water joined the list on 27 August.

Why one wet week doesn't undo a dry summer

National reservoir storage stood at 58% of capacity at the start of September — 19.2 percentage points below where it should be for the time of year — and fell further, not further recovered, even as the rain fell, because storage figures lag behind a single week's inflow. Eleven reservoirs are below half full; nine, including Grafham Water, Rutland Water and Haweswater, are classed as exceptionally low. Groundwater tells a similar story: levels across chalk aquifers in the Wessex Downs and Yorkshire Wolds remain depressed after a dry spring left little to recharge before summer heat set in. Philip Duffy, the Environment Agency's chief executive, put it plainly when he chaired the National Drought Group last month: "This current rainfall isn't enough to change the overall drought picture, and it is possible to have simultaneous drought and flood as heavy rain falls on compacted soils."

The deficit has a price tag. Analysts estimate the dry summer could cost UK farmers up to £390m in lost yield, with wholesale prices already climbing — tomatoes up more than 60%, iceberg lettuce up 90%, potatoes up over 40% — pressure that filters into food inflation over the coming months. The government has responded with a £65m support package for farmers and a new round of its Water Management Grant due to open this autumn, while water companies face scrutiny over whether bill increases tied to drought resilience investment are next.

The Environment Agency puts the threshold for meaningful recovery at 100% of average rainfall — 482mm — between now and the end of March. Anything short of that, and the current bans, along with the cost pressures they signal for agriculture and utilities, are likely to persist into next spring. The next Environment Agency weekly bulletin and the National Drought Group's next meeting will show whether September's rain was the start of a turnaround or a single wet interruption in a longer dry stretch.
