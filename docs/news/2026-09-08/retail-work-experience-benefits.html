Retailers to offer 100,000 young benefit claimants work experience

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who has made tackling youth unemployment a signature policy since taking office in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / Crown Copyright (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Forty retailers have pledged 100,000 work-experience placements for young benefit claimants under a Burnham-government scheme starting in October.

Forty-odd retailers filed onto the same page of a government announcement this week, all agreeing to a version of the same offer: a few weeks of shop-floor experience for young people who have never had a job, in exchange for the government's help clearing what officials call Britain's worst youth unemployment problem in over a decade.

The scheme, badged "Opening Shift" by the Department for Work and Pensions and the British Retail Consortium, aims for 100,000 placements for 16-to-24-year-olds by the end of this Parliament. Tesco, Asda, Sainsbury's, Greggs, M&S, John Lewis, Currys, Primark, Boots, Ann Summers and Amazon are among the names attached so far, with 11,305 placements already pledged. It was unveiled at a Retail Jobs Summit in Parliament, flanked by Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden and Alan Milburn, who is chairing a government review into youth worklessness.

The numbers behind the push

Nearly a million 16-to-24-year-olds in Britain are classified as NEET — not in education, employment or training — the highest rate since 2013, at roughly one in seven of the age group. Officials warn that without intervention the figure could climb past 1.25 million within five years. The retail sector, meanwhile, has shed some 125,000 jobs since Labour took power in 2024, a detail that has not escaped critics of the plan.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who framed the initiative as a personal priority after moving from Manchester's mayoralty to Downing Street in July, described the trap it is meant to spring: "Too many young people are trapped in a vicious cycle where they need experience to get a job and need a job to get experience." Marc Bolland, the former M&S chief executive now advising the DWP, called it a way of "creating thousands more opportunities for young people to build a future."

The mechanics are straightforward. Placements run two to four weeks, 16 to 35 hours a week, arranged through jobcentres, with a workplace "buddy" assigned to each participant and an interview guaranteed at the end. Universal Credit continues throughout, meaning the placements are unpaid in the conventional sense — participants draw benefits rather than a wage from the retailer.

Not everyone is applauding

That detail has drawn the sharpest criticism. Sainsbury's, which pledged 10,000 of the placements in August, cut 3,000 jobs last year while reporting over £1 billion in operating profit — a contrast opposition commentators have been quick to point out. Usdaw, the shopworkers' union that helped kill a similar unpaid-placement scheme under the coalition government in 2012 by threatening retailers with reputational damage, has so far stayed quiet, a reversal some observers read as a sign of how much political room a Labour government has to run schemes a Conservative one could not.

The DWP maintains that sanctions — the withdrawal of benefits for refusal to participate — remain part of the wider Youth Guarantee architecture the scheme sits within, though ministers have stressed Opening Shift itself is being offered, not imposed.

What it does and doesn't tell markets

For all the scale of the ambition, this is a domestic labour-market policy story rather than a macroeconomic one. It says nothing about the direction of UK payrolls, wage growth or the Bank of England's rate path — the placements sit outside the standard employment count, and any effect on hiring or productivity data would take years, not months, to show up. Retailers are absorbing the placements as a cost of doing business and public relations rather than a shift in staffing strategy.

Placements begin in October. The number to watch is not the headline 100,000 but how many placements convert into paid jobs once the interview guarantee comes due — that figure, not the pledge total, will tell whether Opening Shift dents the NEET count or simply relabels the queue for it.
