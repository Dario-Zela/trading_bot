Oil holds near $97 as Iran tensions and German politics keep markets on edge

An oil tanker at sea — Brent crude rose to nearly $98 a barrel after US strikes on Iranian tankers. — Shipspotter Stahl / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Europe closed mixed as the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt landslide and $97 Brent lifted bond yields, while Wall Street sat out Labor Day.

European markets closed in a muddle on Monday, and the muddle was the story. Bond yields rose. Oil rose further. Equities barely moved, drifting in different directions on different exchanges rather than committing to a direction at all. With Wall Street shut for Labor Day, the session belonged to two threads that don't usually share a headline — a German state election and a weekend of strikes on Iranian oil tankers — and both pointed the same way: up for yields, up for crude, and nowhere in particular for stocks.

In one sentence: Europe closed mixed as the AfD's Saxony-Anhalt landslide and $97 Brent lifted bond yields, while Wall Street sat out Labor Day.

A landslide with no government to show for it

The Alternative for Germany won 43.8% of the vote in Sunday's Saxony-Anhalt state election, more than double its result five years ago and enough for 39 of the 83 seats in the new state parliament — three short of the outright majority its lead candidate, Ulrich Siegmund, had been chasing. It is the strongest result the party has posted anywhere in Germany, and it lands in a state that already sends a disproportionate share of hard-right sentiment to Berlin.

What happens next is genuinely unresolved. Every other party that cleared the threshold — the CDU chief among them — has ruled out governing with the AfD, the informal "firewall" that has kept the party out of power nationally and in every state so far. Elected representatives have 30 days to cobble together a coalition before the new Landtag has to convene on October 6. Markets read that uncertainty as a reason to demand more yield for holding German paper: the 10-year Bund ticked up on the day, part of a broader rise across European government debt, with investors also positioning ahead of Thursday's European Central Bank meeting, where a rate move is widely expected.

Oil's other flashpoint

The second thread had nothing to do with Germany and everything to do with the Gulf. The US struck three Iranian oil tankers over the weekend, destroying one, after US Central Command said Tehran had targeted American warships with ballistic missiles. Iranian security officials responded by threatening to declare "a new restricted zone" outside the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil.

Brent crude jumped to roughly $97.89 a barrel, up from $95.18 on Friday and a six-week high, with US Energy Secretary Chris Wright telling reporters Sunday that a nuclear deal with Tehran looked unlikely any time soon. The rally lifted the oil majors even as everything around them wobbled: Shell gained 1.3% and BP 1.2% in London, a reminder that higher crude is a straightforward win for producers and a straightforward headache for anyone reading it as an inflation signal.

Stocks stayed put

That headache showed up in the equity numbers, such as they were. The FTSE 100 slipped 8.96 points, or 0.1%, to 10,822.13, and the FTSE 250 fell 0.3% to 24,506.68 — both dragged by the prospect of pricier energy feeding through to inflation just as UK Chancellor John Healey used a speech in Coventry to promise fiscal restraint ahead of the October 28 Budget. Healey said the Prime Minister and he were "in lockstep" on "meeting the fiscal rules at the upcoming Budget, to balancing the books with a buffer to protect against uncertainty" — a message aimed squarely at a bond market already jumpy about the government's borrowing room. Elsewhere in Europe the picture was patchier still: some indices firmer, others softer, none moving with much conviction. Wall Street, closed for Labor Day, was absent from the picture entirely, leaving European desks to set the tone without the usual US afternoon follow-through.

Reading the split

What makes Monday's session worth pausing on is not any single number but the shape of the whole board. Bond yields and oil moved together and moved for reasons that compound each other — a fragmented Germany raises the political risk premium on European debt just as a tighter Strait of Hormuz raises the inflation premium on everything that runs on diesel. Equities, by contrast, went almost nowhere. That is not a market shrugging off the news; a genuine shrug would look like gains. It is closer to a market unwilling to place a bet either way until it knows whether the AfD result curdles into a governing crisis, whether Iran actually restricts Hormuz traffic, and what Healey's fiscal rules look like once they are written down on October 28. The next real test comes Thursday, when the ECB meets against a backdrop of rising yields it did not choose and an oil price it cannot control.
