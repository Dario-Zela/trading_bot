June JOLTS revisions show layoffs up, hiring down, adding to Fed rate-cut case

A "now hiring" sign in a storefront window — job openings fell sharply in the latest JOLTS revisions. — Nathan Sack / Unsplash
In one sentence
Revised June JOLTS data show layoffs up 19,000 and hires down 16,000, cementing a 95.6% market-priced chance of a Fed rate cut at the September 15–16 FOMC meeting.

The revisions arrived quietly, buried in a routine data release, but they told a story markets had been waiting to hear all summer: the US labor market is softer than the government first reported, and it is getting softer at exactly the moment the Federal Reserve is deciding whether to cut interest rates.

When the Bureau of Labor Statistics published July's Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey on September 1, it came bundled with revisions to June's figures. Layoffs and discharges that month were revised up by 19,000, to roughly 1.8 million. Hires were revised down by 16,000, to 5.3 million. Job openings — the number of positions employers say they are actively trying to fill — were cut by 177,000, to about 7.2 million. Quits fell too, down 19,000 to 3.2 million, as workers grew warier of testing the market for something better.

None of these individual revisions is dramatic on its own. Together, they move in one direction: fewer companies hiring, more companies cutting staff, and fewer workers confident enough to quit. That is the textbook signature of a labor market losing momentum, not collapsing but cooling in a way that shows up first in the data nobody watches closely — the backward-looking corrections — before it shows up in headline payrolls.

A pattern, not a blip

July's own numbers, reported alongside the June revisions, extended the trend rather than reversing it. Layoffs climbed to roughly 1.8 million, above the roughly 1.6 million economists had penciled in. Job openings slipped to about 7.18 million, missing forecasts near 7.38 million. The quits rate — the share of workers voluntarily leaving their jobs, and a proxy for how confident they feel about finding another one — sits at 1.9%, matching the floor last seen in late 2024 and again in late 2025. Workers, in other words, are gripping the jobs they already have.

This is the second straight month the JOLTS report has undershot expectations, following a similarly weak reading in early August that first rattled rate-cut calculus. Taken with July's disappointing payrolls print, the pattern is now two data releases deep, which is the point at which economists stop calling a soft number noise and start calling it a trend.

Not every read on the data is alarmed. Joe Seydl, senior markets economist at J.P. Morgan Private Bank, described the broader picture as moderation rather than danger: "The labor market is moderating, not collapsing, with hiring trends stable and wage growth contained." That is a fair characterization of the openings and quits data specifically, which still sit well above pre-pandemic norms. It is the layoffs line — the one moving in the wrong direction two reports running — that has done the most to shift the conversation.

What it means for the Fed's September meeting

The timing is what turns a data footnote into a market event. The Federal Open Market Committee meets September 15–16, with a rate decision due at 2:00pm ET on the 16th, and the current target range has sat at 3.50%–3.75% since December. Going into the JOLTS release, traders already leaned toward a cut. Afterward, CME's FedWatch tool put the probability of a September cut at 95.6%, effectively pricing the move as settled barring a surprise between now and the meeting.

Fed officials have been signaling openness to easing for weeks. Governor Christopher Waller has repeatedly argued for cutting in September specifically to front-run labor-market weakness rather than wait for it to show up unambiguously in the unemployment rate — a preemptive posture that this batch of JOLTS revisions gives some retrospective support to, since the softening was already underway in June and simply hadn't been fully counted yet.

For rate-sensitive positioning, the practical effect is that a cut is now closer to a formality than a forecast, which shifts the live debate from whether the Fed moves to how it frames the move — and how many more cuts the updated Summary of Economic Projections pencils in for the rest of 2026. A dot plot that signals a longer easing cycle would matter more to Treasury yields and equity valuations than the cut itself, which markets have already largely absorbed into pricing.

What comes next

The next hard data point is the August employment report, due before the FOMC meeting, followed by the Fed's own updated projections on September 16. Between now and then, the question for markets is less "will they cut" — that looks decided — and more whether the committee's language and dot plot describe one adjustment or the start of a series. Given a labor market where layoffs are rising and hiring is slowing in the same set of revisions, the case for the latter is not hard to make.
