AI data-centre boom in Australia raises questions over water and power costs

NEXTDC's P2 data centre in Perth, part of the company's growing Australian facility network. — Samuel Wiki / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Australia's AI data-centre pipeline has grown to four Eraring coal plants' worth of capacity, and new rules will make operators fund their own power and water.

Australia is in the middle of a data-centre building spree that even the companies driving it admit has outrun the electricity grid's ability to keep up. Across New South Wales and Victoria, developers have more than 44 projects in the pipeline — a combined 11.4 gigawatts of proposed capacity, or roughly four times the output of the Eraring coal plant, the country's largest. Most of that is being built to run AI workloads: model training, inference, and the cloud services layered on top.

In one sentence: Australia's AI data-centre pipeline now totals more gigawatts than four Eraring coal plants, and regulators are moving to make operators cover the power and water costs themselves.

The numbers involved are large enough that the federal government has stepped in. Data centres currently draw a bit over 2% of the electricity on Australia's National Electricity Market. The Australian Energy Market Operator expects that to triple to around 6% by 2030 — from 3.9 terawatt-hours a year to about 12. AEMO says the grid isn't at risk yet, but only if new generation capacity arrives "on time" to replace retiring coal plants and absorb the extra load. That's a big if: Australia's coal fleet has a long history of missing its own retirement and replacement schedules.

Who's building, and where

The capital committed is real money, not vapourware. Microsoft has pledged AUD $25 billion to expand its Australian footprint. NEXTDC is mid-construction on a 350-megawatt facility in Horsley Park, Western Sydney — its first project to connect directly into the NSW transmission grid — with around 1,800 workers on site at peak and completion due next year. Amazon Web Services has signed deals for nine renewable-energy projects adding 430MW of generation specifically to power its Australian data centres, an approach that tries to solve the supply problem by bringing new electrons rather than just drawing on the existing pool.

Not every operator is doing that. Transgrid, the NSW transmission operator, told a state parliamentary inquiry that the scale, pace and geographic concentration of data-centre development amounted to a "material step-change" for planning purposes — utility-speak for: we haven't seen demand pile up like this in one place before.

The price and water math

The Climate Council and modelling cited by AEMO estimate that if new data-centre demand isn't matched by new renewable generation and storage, wholesale electricity prices across the main grid could rise more than 20% on average by 2035 — with NSW facing increases up to 26% and Victoria up to 23%. That's a cost that ultimately lands on households and businesses sharing the same grid, not just the tech tenants.

Water is the quieter half of the story. A single 250-megawatt hyperscale facility can use between 0.88 and 5.5 gigalitres of water a year for cooling, according to Infrastructure Australia's 2026 analysis — a wide range that itself reflects how differently facilities are designed. By 2030, data centres are projected to consume about 2% of Sydney's water supply and just under 1% of Melbourne's. That sounds marginal until drought conditions return, at which point a fixed industrial draw competing with household restrictions becomes a much harder political conversation.

Canberra tries to write the rules

At a National Cabinet meeting in Sydney on 26 August, the Albanese government pushed a plan requiring new data centres to source the equivalent of 100% renewable power, backed by battery or gas firming, and to become net electricity generators over time — producing more than they draw from the grid. Operators would also pay the full cost of their own grid connections, curtail usage during system stress, and fund any water infrastructure their projects require.

The clean version of that plan didn't survive contact with the states. Queensland and the Northern Territory objected to being locked into a renewables-only mandate, and the agreed framework now leaves room for state-owned fossil generators to make a case-by-case argument for powering data centres with coal or gas. Energy minister Chris Bowen insists there are no formal "carve-outs," but critics, including the Climate Council, argue the exemption pathway amounts to one in practice. Legislation is due in early 2027.

What it means for the capex case

None of this kills the investment thesis outright, but it does change its shape. Projects that pencil out on the assumption of cheap, available grid power look different once operators must fund their own generation, pay full connection costs, and absorb curtailment risk during peak demand. That favours large, well-capitalised players like Microsoft and AWS who can bundle in dedicated renewable supply, and makes life harder for smaller developers betting on grid access alone. The next marker to watch is the 2027 legislation itself — whether the net-generator and renewables requirements survive state pushback intact, or get diluted further before they're law.
