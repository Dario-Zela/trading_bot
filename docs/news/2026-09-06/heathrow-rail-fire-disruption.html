Grass fire near Paddington severs Heathrow rail links, strands Elizabeth line passengers

Elizabeth line trains at Paddington station, the terminus for the airport's suspended rail links on Saturday. — Geograph.org.uk (Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
A grass fire near Paddington cut power to the Elizabeth line, GWR and Heathrow Express simultaneously, evacuating 380 stranded passengers and exposing Heathrow's single-corridor rail dependency.

A patch of dry grass caught fire on Saturday afternoon next to the railway at Scrubs Lane, in North Kensington, and by early evening it had done something no timetable disruption usually manages: it cut Heathrow off from central London by rail almost entirely.

London Fire Brigade sent six engines and around 40 firefighters to the blaze, which broke out just after 4pm and burned close enough to trackside electrical equipment to knock out the power supply. The result cascaded fast. Elizabeth line services between Paddington and Heathrow Terminals 4 and 5 stopped, as did the branch to Reading. Great Western Railway cancelled its Reading services. Heathrow Express, the airport's premium 15-minute link to Paddington, ran no trains in either direction. National Rail warned of "major disruption" lasting the rest of the day.

The most striking image of the afternoon wasn't the fire itself but its human residue: roughly 380 passengers stuck on a stalled Elizabeth line train had to be evacuated, with fire crews assisting Transport for London staff in getting people off safely. Social media filled with photos of crowds banked up at Paddington concourse, phones out, departure boards blank where Heathrow services should have been.

One corridor, one airport

What made Saturday notable wasn't the fire's size — grass fires near railway embankments are a routine feature of a dry British summer — but how much of Heathrow's rail capacity collapsed from a single point of failure. The Elizabeth line, GWR and Heathrow Express all share broadly the same electrified approach out of Paddington, so a power failure on that stretch doesn't inconvenience one operator, it takes out the lot simultaneously. For an airport that handles around 200,000 passengers a day, and which has spent a decade selling itself on rail connectivity as an alternative to the M4 and M25, that is a narrower margin than the marketing suggests.

Heathrow has no shortage of contingency road links, taxis and buses, and the disruption cleared by Sunday morning, with National Rail confirming Elizabeth line, Heathrow Express and GWR services had all returned to normal. But the outage landed on a Saturday, historically one of the airport's busiest days for leisure travel, and it followed a now-familiar pattern: a small, localised fault outside anyone's control taking out infrastructure that millions of passengers implicitly treat as redundant.

That pattern is worth watching rather than shrugging off. Heathrow's own resilience planning has already been tested this year by power outages and weather-related disruption elsewhere in the network; investors in airport operators and rail concessions increasingly price in the assumption that single-corridor dependencies are a tail risk worth naming, not an act of God to write off after the fact. The next hot, dry spell — and the next grass verge near a live line — will be the test of whether anything about Saturday's near-miss changes.
