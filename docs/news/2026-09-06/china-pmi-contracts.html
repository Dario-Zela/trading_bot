China factory activity contracts for a second month as services offer a lone bright spot

An electronics factory in Shenzhen, the export-manufacturing hub at the centre of China's PMI slowdown. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
China's factories contracted for a second straight month in August even as services surveys rebounded, leaving Beijing's 2026 growth target increasingly out of reach.

China's factories shrank for a second straight month in August, even as the survey gauging the much larger services sector jumped by the most in over a year — a split that leaves Beijing facing an uncomfortable choice as it tries to hit a growth target it is already behind on.

The official manufacturing purchasing managers' index, compiled by the National Bureau of Statistics, rose to 49.8 in August from 49.2 in July, according to data released on 31 August. Any reading below 50 signals contraction, and this was the second month running the index sat under that line, ending a four-month run of expansion that had lasted into June. New orders bounced back to 50.6 from 48.5, and new export orders returned to growth at 50.1 from 49.6 — evidence that the improvement was not merely a statistical artefact. NBS statistician Huo Lihui noted that "both production and demand expanded in tandem," with the gains concentrated in high-tech manufacturing and larger firms; equipment and high-tech PMI sub-indices both cleared 51, while consumer goods and energy-intensive industries stayed stuck below 50.

Two surveys, two stories

The picture gets murkier once the non-manufacturing PMI and the private-sector survey are laid alongside the official manufacturing number. The NBS non-manufacturing PMI, which blends services and construction, was unchanged at 49.0 in August — the weakest reading since December 2022 — as construction slumped to 46.9 amid heavy rain and typhoon disruption in several provinces. Read on its own, official data would suggest services were dragging, not lifting.

The rebound the triage points to instead shows up in the RatingDog China General Services PMI — the S&P Global-compiled survey that businesses and traders still refer to by its old name, Caixin, before a Shenzhen credit-research firm bought the naming rights from S&P Global last year. That gauge jumped to 51.4 in August from 50.4 in July, beating the median economist forecast of 50.6 and marking a recovery from a near two-year low. The equivalent private manufacturing gauge told a similarly rosier story, climbing to 51.5 from 50.9, its fastest expansion in three months, helped by the strongest rise in export orders in six months.

The divergence between official and private surveys is not new — the two draw from different, only partially overlapping panels of firms, with the private gauge tilted toward smaller, export-facing and coastal businesses, and the official one weighted more heavily toward large and state-linked companies. But the gap widened noticeably in August, and it matters for how the month's data gets read: private-survey optimism about a services and export pickup sits awkwardly next to an official record showing construction in retreat and the broader non-manufacturing sector flat at a three-year low.

A target already slipping

Whichever survey is favoured, the trend leaves Beijing short of where it wants to be. The government set a 2026 growth target of "around 4.5% to 5%" at the March National People's Congress, the lowest and, unusually, the first explicit range rather than a single figure in over a decade — itself a tacit admission that hitting a precise number was getting harder. Second-quarter GDP grew 4.3%, below even the lower bound of that range, with the property downturn and soft household spending doing most of the damage.

Zhiwei Zhang, chief economist at Pinpoint Asset Management, cautioned it was too early to call August's improvement a turning point, noting that any lift in commodity-linked manufacturers likely reflected supply constraints pushing up prices rather than a genuine rebound in demand. Commerzbank's China economists went further, arguing that two months of broad PMI contraction leave full-year growth tracking below target and that, combined with a fiscal spending rollout running behind schedule, the case for the People's Bank of China to cut rates or reserve requirements before year-end has strengthened — even if the stronger services print gives the central bank a reason to hold off in the near term.

Official messaging has leaned the other way. An article in the People's Daily this month argued China is "not excessively reliant" on large-scale stimulus and remains capable of hitting its growth goal without it — a signal that Beijing sees the current softness as manageable rather than an emergency requiring another 2015- or 2020-style flood of credit.

The choice ahead

The underlying tension is one of allocation, not just magnitude. Export-facing manufacturers have benefited from front-loaded shipments and a pickup in AI-linked hardware demand this year, but that channel is exposed to US tariff policy and a global trade environment that could turn again with little warning. Services and consumption, by contrast, respond more directly to domestic policy — social spending, property stabilisation, labour-market support — but have proven stickier to move, as the flat non-manufacturing PMI shows. Leaning harder into export-oriented manufacturing risks reinforcing the very overcapacity and trade friction that drew retaliatory tariffs in the first place; leaning into consumption requires transfers and structural changes to household income that Beijing has so far resisted at scale.

The next test comes with September's PMI readings, due at the start of October, and with any fresh signal from the PBoC on rates ahead of the Fed's own decision this month. A third straight month of manufacturing contraction, on the official measure, would make the argument for stimulus considerably harder for Beijing to wave away.
