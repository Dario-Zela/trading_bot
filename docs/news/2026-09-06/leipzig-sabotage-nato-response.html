NATO and EU respond after Germany blames Russia for Leipzig airport sabotage plot

Leipzig/Halle Airport, base of NATO's Strategic Airlift International Solution and the site of an alleged Russian sabotage plot against cargo aircraft. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Berlin blamed Russia for an explosive-drone plot against cargo planes at Leipzig airport, prompting a Brussels meeting between von der Leyen and Rutte — but no suspect has yet been named.

The drone that mattered most never went off. On the evening of August 4, a first-person-view quadcopter carrying roughly 800 grams of Semtex struck the wing of a Ukrainian Antonov An-124 cargo aircraft on the apron at Leipzig/Halle Airport and fell to the tarmac without detonating. A second drone clipped a Boeing 757 freighter the following night, forcing it to divert to Hannover with minor nose damage. A third, carrying about 50 grams of a substance suspected to be hexogen — a military-grade explosive — turned up in a field on the airport's western edge ten days later.

Four weeks on, Berlin has put a name to who it believes sent them. On September 1, the German government formally attributed the plot to Russia, and the following day European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen met NATO Secretary General Mark Rutte in Brussels to coordinate a response. The venue mattered as much as the message: Leipzig/Halle is not an ordinary freight hub. It hosts NATO's Strategic Airlift International Solution, the pooled-fleet arrangement that keeps An-124 heavy-lift aircraft flying almost daily to alliance battlegroups from Finland to Romania, and it has served as Antonov's operating base since the airline's fleet was evacuated from Hostomel airport near Kyiv in the opening days of Russia's 2022 invasion. An attack there does not just threaten a civilian airport — it threatens the logistics spine of NATO's eastern reinforcement.

What Germany says it found

German federal prosecutors, who took over the case from Dresden authorities on August 6 citing its significance, have described the first drone as fitted with professional-grade explosives, a detonator and a SIM card that would have allowed it to be flown remotely from anywhere in the world. Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt said investigators identified components, explosives and a detonator configuration "known to us from other Russian hybrid operations and its war against Ukraine," and that a relay antenna found near the village of Kursdorf pointed to a supporting communications network rather than a single improvised attack. DNA traces recovered from the wreckage were, according to Euronews reporting, still being matched to individuals rather than an organisation when the September 1 announcement came — meaning the public attribution rests more on technical signature than on named suspects, though German media have since reported that investigators are looking at two men travelling on Russian passports issued in Belarus and Lithuania.

Washington weighed in alongside Berlin: a U.S. intelligence assessment, conducted jointly with German services, concluded the drone's construction bore the hallmarks of Russia's military intelligence directorate, the GRU. Moscow has rejected all of it. Ambassador Sergey Nechayev called the accusations "unsubstantiated, absurd and irrational," and Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov threatened to close Germany's Goethe-Institut cultural centres in Moscow, St Petersburg and Yekaterinburg in retaliation for Berlin's decision to shut the Russian consulate in Bonn and the Russian House cultural centre in Berlin.

Brussels closes ranks

"These incidents involving Russian drones have become the new normal in Europe."

Von der Leyen's phrase, delivered after the Rutte meeting, was less a description of Leipzig alone than of a pattern German and EU officials say has been building since January — airport officials have logged some eighteen separate drone incursions disrupting German air traffic this year, of which Leipzig is simply the most serious. Rutte, for his part, said Berlin had presented NATO allies with evidence he considered clear, and welcomed the raft of German countermeasures: the threat level was raised from "general" to "high," a new interior-ministry drone-defence unit is being staffed with 150 additional personnel across four regional bases, and a dedicated counter-drone research centre opened on August 18 at the German Aerospace Center's Cochstedt airfield, roughly 100 kilometres from Leipzig. Von der Leyen said EU foreign ministers would follow with a package of additional sanctions targeting Russia, though the scope of those measures had not been finalised as of this week. France, Poland, Italy, Britain, Sweden, Czechia and Estonia have each issued statements of solidarity with Berlin.

An accusation without a smoking gun — yet

What is notable about the Leipzig case, set against a now-familiar run of European sabotage allegations — undersea cable cuts in the Baltic, parcel bombs routed through European logistics networks, GPS jamming that forced von der Leyen's own aircraft to navigate by paper map over Bulgaria a year earlier — is how much of the technical case Germany has chosen to make public and how much it has not. Prosecutors have detailed the explosive payload and the drone's electronics in unusual depth for an active investigation, a signal officials say is intended to make the attribution harder for Moscow to dismiss. But no suspect has been named in court, no arrest has been announced, and the DNA evidence that would tie the device to a specific operative rather than a general Russian modus operandi remains, by Berlin's own account, incomplete. That gap is precisely why this case reads differently from a routine accusation cycle: the alliance is treating a still-unproven attribution as sufficiently credible to raise a national threat level and convene NATO's secretary general, well ahead of the courtroom evidence that would normally justify it.

The test of that judgment will not come from another statement in Brussels. It will come from whether Germany's federal prosecutors can produce a named suspect and a forensic chain linking the Kursdorf antenna and the Semtex-laden drone to a Russian state actor — and from whether the sanctions package EU foreign ministers are due to finalise this month goes beyond symbolic consulate closures. Until then, Leipzig/Halle's runways keep moving Antonovs east, under an interior-ministry threat level that has not been this high since the war in Ukraine began.
