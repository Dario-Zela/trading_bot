Prince William to represent King Charles at funeral of Norway's King Harald V

The Prince of Wales at a D-Day commemorative service in Normandy, June 2024 — a similar representative role to the one he takes on in Oslo. — European Union / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Prince William will attend King Harald V's Oslo funeral on 9 September in King Charles's place, per long-standing royal protocol.

Buckingham Palace has confirmed that the Prince of Wales will travel to Oslo on 9 September to represent King Charles III at the state funeral of King Harald V, who died on 28 August at the age of 89 after a 35-year reign. The service takes place at Oslo Cathedral at 1pm local time, followed by a procession to Akershus Fortress, where Harald will be buried privately in the Royal Mausoleum alongside his wife, Queen Sonja, and his father, King Olav V.

The arrangement is not a snub, nor a comment on the King's health. It is protocol, plain and simple: reigning British monarchs do not attend the funerals of other heads of state, a convention that held even for the death of Pope Francis last year, when William stood in for his father in Rome. Charles, 77 and continuing treatment for an undisclosed form of cancer, released a statement last week mourning the loss of his "dear cousin" — the two men were related through Queen Victoria's descendants, a thread that runs through most of Europe's remaining monarchies.

A well-worn role

William will not travel alone. His aunt, the Princess Royal, is also attending Oslo, though in what the Palace has described as a private and personal capacity — she is godmother to Norway's new monarch, Haakon VIII, who acceded to the throne immediately on his father's death under Norway's constitution. There is no coronation to follow: Norway's parliament scrapped the requirement in 1908 as an anachronism, so Haakon and his wife, now Queen Mette-Marit, assume their titles without further ceremony.

Representing the Crown at state funerals abroad has become one of William's more regular duties, following the same pattern set by Charles when he was Prince of Wales — turning up at the solemn, camera-heavy occasions so the monarch does not have to leave domestic duties or, as now, ongoing medical treatment. It is a role heavy on symbolism and light on substance: no bilateral meetings are scheduled, and Downing Street has not attached any diplomatic agenda to the visit.

Harald's long reign

Harald V came to the throne in January 1991 as the first Norwegian-born monarch to inherit the crown directly in over 600 years, and built a reputation as an unusually approachable king — an Olympic sailor who competed at three Games, married a commoner against the establishment's early misgivings, and later became the first reigning monarch to visit Antarctica. He was widely credited with steadying the country after the 2011 Utøya and Oslo attacks that killed 77 people, appearing at memorial services within days.

None of that history changes the calculus for markets or Whitehall. Norway's constitutional monarchy carries no executive power, its oil-and-gas-funded economy runs on a fiscal framework untouched by the change of head of state, and the succession itself was never in doubt — Haakon has been crown prince and heir since birth. The funeral is a moment of genuine national mourning for Norwegians, and a diplomatic courtesy for Britain, but not, in any meaningful sense, an event with downstream consequence.

The next marker is procedural rather than political: attention shifts to how Haakon VIII settles into the role over the coming months, and whether Norway's palace makes any changes to the largely ceremonial duties the monarchy performs. For now, Oslo Cathedral on Wednesday is where the story ends.
