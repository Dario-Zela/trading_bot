UK travellers face renewed EU border queues as check flexibility expires

Vehicle check-in booths at the Port of Dover, one of the crossings still waiting on working biometric kiosks. — Chris Morgan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The EU's temporary licence to pause biometric border checks expired on 6 September, leaving UK travellers exposed to longer queues at busy airports, even as Channel crossings stay in manual mode due to unrelated software delays.

The cushion that has kept Europe's new border-check regime from clogging airports and ports all summer ran out on Saturday — and the busiest routes for British travellers are about to find out what the system looks like at full strength.

Since the EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) began rolling out in October, member states have leaned on an informal safety valve: the European Commission allowed border posts to pause the biometric fingerprint and facial-scan checks the system requires whenever queues built up dangerously. Greece, France, Portugal and Spain all used it repeatedly through the peak season. Frontex, the EU's border agency, confirmed the flexibility would not be extended past 6 September, despite a joint push from Airlines for Europe, IATA and airport body ACI Europe to keep it running through the winter schedule.

"Extending the possibility of partial suspension beyond 6 September is absolutely necessary until the end of the winter season," Thomas Reynaert, IATA's senior vice-president for Europe, said ahead of the deadline.

Why the queues built up in the first place

EES replaces the old passport stamp with a database entry — fingerprints, a photo and a digital record of entry and exit — for every non-EU visitor, including Britons, on their first crossing. IATA puts the processing time at roughly 90 seconds per traveller under the new system, against 20 to 25 seconds for a manual stamp. Multiplied across a full flight or ferry-load, that difference is what turned routine queues into research subjects: a Financial Times analysis with Qsensor found average waits at Frankfurt airport reaching two hours in July, up from around an hour a year earlier, with Amsterdam's peak waits stretching to two hours and Munich's roughly doubling. ACI Europe told Politico that within days of April's full rollout, queues at airports in 15 countries were running two to three hours or more at peak times.

The suspension power was the industry's pressure valve for exactly those moments — a border post could simply wave people through on the old paper-check basis rather than let a terminal back up. From this week, that option is gone.

What it means for Channel crossings

For travellers using Dover, Eurotunnel or Eurostar, the picture is messier — and, for now, less severe. The biometric kiosks at all three crossing points have been running behind schedule for months, held up not by the July heat but by software: French border police still lack a working connection between the kiosks supplied at Dover and Eurotunnel and the systems needed to operate them, and Eurostar says it remains "waiting for a software update from the interior ministry." In the meantime, car passengers get a manual workaround — French officers open a digital EES file at the booth without capturing fingerprints or a photo, while everyone stays in the vehicle — and coach kiosks are still not live. The Port of Dover received £20 million in UK government funding in July to add kiosks and vehicle-holding capacity in Kent ahead of full rollout, but no date has been set for the Channel crossings to go fully biometric.

Airports bound for the Mediterranean are a different story. Airline sources have told reporters they expect only patchy enforcement at first, and that Greek border officials in particular have been informally waving UK passengers through unchecked to avoid backing up queues — a practice one senior airline source expects to continue regardless of what the rulebook now says.

What's actually at stake here

That gap between the rule and the practice is the real story: Brussels has withdrawn the formal safety valve, but enforcement on the ground looks set to remain uneven, shaped by whichever border post is most desperate to keep a terminal moving. For the aviation and tourism lobby, the complaint is less about principle than infrastructure — kiosk numbers and staffing that haven't caught up with a system rolled out faster than airports could resource it. None of this yet comes with a passenger-number impact attached; no carrier or destination has reported bookings pulled because of border delays, and the routes hit hardest by real queues in July, Frankfurt and Munich, are transit hubs rather primarily British holiday gateways.

The next test comes with the autumn half-term getaway in late October, when volumes rise again and the reprieve some airports have quietly kept alive will be harder to justify. Watch for whether the Commission revisits the flexibility question before then, and whether Dover's software fix finally arrives — the one crossing where British travellers have, so far, avoided the worst of it entirely by accident.
