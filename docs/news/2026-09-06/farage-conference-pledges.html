Farage closes Reform conference with pledge to raise tax-free allowance to £15,000

Nigel Farage, who closed Reform UK's Birmingham conference warning of Britain's 'terminal decline'. — Laurie Noble / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Farage closed Reform UK's conference pledging a £15,000 tax-free allowance and £80bn of savings — bold arithmetic, but no path to power yet.

There is a version of this speech Nigel Farage has given before, in tents and on beaches and outside referendum counts, and the closing address to Reform UK's conference in Birmingham on Saturday hit the same notes: Britain in decline, the political class at fault, a leader offering deliverance. What was new this time was the arithmetic. Reform used its two days at the NEC to publish, in more detail than before, what it would actually do with the first hundred days of a government it does not yet have and, on current polling, might.

"Britain is in terminal decline, and this may be the last chance to change direction," Farage told delegates, casting the next general election as an existential vote rather than a routine one.

In one sentence: Farage closed Reform's conference with a £15,000 tax-free allowance pledge and a raft of 100-day promises that are eye-catching but, without a parliamentary majority, not yet a market event.

The headline number

The centrepiece, announced by shadow chancellor-in-waiting Robert Jenrick a day before Farage's closing speech, is a promise to raise the income tax personal allowance from £12,570 to £15,000 in Reform's first Budget — the largest single increase in the allowance's history. Reform says it would take 2.9 million people out of income tax altogether, benefit around 40 million people, and save a typical taxpayer roughly £500 a year. It is billed as a first step toward £20,000 by the end of a five-year parliament.

None of it is free. Reform prices the allowance rise at £17.7 billion in year one, rising to £21 billion by year five, and says it would be funded from £80 billion of savings it has already identified: £52 billion from welfare reform, £7 billion a year from cutting foreign aid, £10 billion from scrapping net zero subsidies, £7 billion from shrinking the civil service and quangos, and £10 billion in new revenue — chiefly a higher immigration health surcharge and a levy on employers of foreign labour.

The rest of the hundred days

Tax was one plank of a wider pitch. Reform also pledged to end Channel crossings within 100 days, cut £250 a year from household energy bills by scrapping green levies, and cut £50 billion from the benefits bill. Farage said any minister who failed to deliver would be sacked within the same window — "replaced with someone with more courage," as he put it — a line aimed as much at Reform's own discipline as at Westminster's.

Why the market shrugs, for now

Economists were quicker to respond than markets. Simon French of Panmure Liberum warned that Reform's broader tax agenda — including the eventual £20,000 allowance, corporation tax cuts and zeroing NHS waiting lists — implied a £70–80 billion annual fiscal hole and carried "the high probability of an immediate and violent sterling crisis" if enacted without matching savings materialising. Reform's deputy leader, Richard Tice, dismissed the analysis as "juvenile claptrap," insisting the savings come first and the tax cuts follow only once they land.

Both things can be true without moving gilts or sterling this week. Reform has no MPs in a position to legislate any of this, no date for an election, and — as the IFS has noted of the party's wider allowance ambitions — a plan that leans heavily on savings not yet delivered by any government, of any colour, at that scale. Sterling traded above $1.32 on Saturday, nowhere near the $1.03 low struck during the 2022 mini-Budget crisis that such warnings implicitly invoke.

What would change that

The distance between a conference pledge and a Budget box is currently the whole of a general election, which need not happen before 2029 though Farage himself has suggested Labour's troubles could force one sooner. Until Reform's poll lead — currently narrowing, according to recent surveys — looks like a plausible path to Downing Street, gilt and currency markets have little reason to price in £80 billion of assumed savings and £21 billion of annual tax cuts. What would change that calculus: a snap election announcement, a fiscal watchdog costing of Reform's numbers, or a poll lead wide enough that investors stop treating the party's manifesto as rhetoric.
