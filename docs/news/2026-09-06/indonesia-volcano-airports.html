Indonesia shuts Jakarta and Lampung airports after Anak Krakatau eruption

Mount Anak Krakatau, the volcanic island that rose from the caldera of the 1883 Krakatoa eruption, in the Sunda Strait between Java and Sumatra. — Lord Mountbatten / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Ash from Mount Anak Krakatau grounded roughly 200 flights and shut Jakarta's main airport and Lampung's Radin Inten II, exposing fragility at a key Southeast Asian air and cargo hub.

Indonesia halted operations at its busiest airport and at a regional hub in southern Sumatra on Sunday after Mount Anak Krakatau, the volcano that rose from the collapsed caldera of the original Krakatoa, sent an ash column as high as 15 kilometres into the atmosphere over the Sunda Strait.

Soekarno-Hatta International Airport, which serves greater Jakarta and is Indonesia's largest by traffic, suspended flights from 1:30 a.m. local time. A follow-up ash test between 12:35 and 1:05 a.m. confirmed particulate contamination over the runways, and authorities pushed the closure back twice, first to 5:30 a.m. and then to 9:30 a.m., as ash kept drifting into flight paths. Roughly 200 flights and, by one airport count, more than 22,000 passengers were affected. Radin Inten II International Airport in Lampung, on the Sumatran side of the strait and closer to the volcano, shut separately until 8 a.m.

"Safety remains the top priority in every operational decision."

That was the line from Lukman F. Laisa, an Indonesian civil aviation official, and it captures the trade-off at the heart of every ash-related shutdown: airlines and regulators would rather cancel a day of flights than risk an engine ingesting glass-sharp volcanic particulate mid-air, a hazard that has brought down aircraft before. Garuda Indonesia said it was adjusting numerous Jakarta services; AirAsia Indonesia reported cancellations and rescheduling on its own network. Singapore Airlines cancelled two Jakarta flights and delayed two more into the following day.

A volcano that has done this before

Anak Krakatau — literally "child of Krakatoa" — emerged from the sea in the 1920s on the site of the 1883 eruption that killed more than 36,000 people and altered global climate for a year. It has never fully settled since. A partial flank collapse in December 2018 triggered a tsunami that killed more than 400 people along the coasts of Java and Sumatra without any warning, because the wave was generated by landslide rather than earthquake. That history is why Indonesia's geological agency moved quickly this week to say the current activity poses no comparable tsunami risk: officials assess that the section of the volcano rebuilt since 2018 remains too small to produce a collapse capable of generating one. A three-kilometre exclusion zone is in force around the crater regardless, and coastal residents in Banten and Lampung provinces have been told to prepare for ashfall.

The eruption itself has been building for months. Indonesia's Geological Agency raised Anak Krakatau's alert status from Level II to Level III in early July after a dense ash plume, and Strombolian explosions — the intermittent, fountaining kind — have continued since. What changed this weekend was scale: six eruptions overnight into Saturday produced lava fountains and detonations audible up to 700 kilometres away, according to Indonesia's meteorological agency, BMKG, and pushed ash to an altitude that put it squarely in commercial flight corridors.

Why a brief ash cloud matters beyond Jakarta

Soekarno-Hatta is not just Indonesia's gateway; it is one of the connective hubs of Southeast Asian aviation, routing passenger and cargo traffic between the archipelago's 17,000 islands and onward to Singapore, Malaysia and beyond. A shutdown measured in hours rather than days did not, on its own, threaten the kind of prolonged paralysis Europe saw when Iceland's Eyjafjallajökull grounded transcontinental air traffic for a week in 2010. But the episode is a reminder of how thin the margin is at Southeast Asia's logistics chokepoints: a single volcanic system straddling one of the world's busiest shipping and flight corridors can, within a matter of hours, cancel hundreds of flights, strand tens of thousands of passengers and interrupt air-cargo schedules that regional manufacturers and exporters increasingly treat as just-in-time infrastructure rather than backup capacity.

Anak Krakatau sits almost exactly between Sumatra and Java, the two islands that carry the bulk of Indonesia's population and industrial output. Any disruption at Jakarta's main airport radiates outward into the country's air-freight network, and by extension into the tighter regional supply chains — electronics, garments, agricultural exports — that route through Indonesian hubs on the way to Singapore or onward to global markets.

What to watch

BMKG and Indonesia's geological agency are continuing ash-dispersion and seismic monitoring, and further closures are possible if the volcano produces another high-level eruption; Anak Krakatau has remained at an elevated alert level since July and shows no sign of quieting. Airlines including Garuda and AirAsia are still working through rebooking backlogs from the weekend's cancellations, and the coming days will show whether the disruption stays a one-off or becomes a recurring feature of the strait's aviation calendar, as it has been intermittently since the volcano's 2018 collapse.
