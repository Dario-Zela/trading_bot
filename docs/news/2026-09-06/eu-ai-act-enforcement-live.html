EU AI Act enforcement goes live with fines of up to 3% of global turnover

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, whose AI Office began active enforcement of the AI Act's transparency rules this month. — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The EU began enforcing AI Act labelling rules on 2 August with fines up to 3% of turnover, even as it pushed the tougher high-risk-AI rules back to December 2027.

Since 2 August, an AI chatbot operating in the European Union has been legally required to tell you it isn't human. A deepfake video has to carry a label — visible or embedded in the file's metadata — saying it was made or altered by a machine. And the European Commission's AI Office, working with regulators in each of the 27 member states, now has the power to fine a company up to €15 million or 3% of its global annual turnover for getting this wrong, whichever figure is bigger.

That is the headline. The more interesting story is what enforcement is not doing yet, and what that choice reveals about how Brussels intends to regulate artificial intelligence over the next several years.

What actually kicked in

The rules that became enforceable this month sit in Article 50 of the AI Act, the EU's sprawling 2024 regulation. They are transparency rules, not capability limits. Nobody at the AI Office is telling a lab what its model is allowed to learn or do. The obligations are narrower and, on paper, simpler: say what you are.

Interactive systems — chatbots, voice agents, automated customer-service lines — must make clear to users they're talking to a machine, not a person, and that disclosure can't be buried in a terms-of-service page nobody reads. Synthetic or manipulated audio, video and images that resemble real people or events need a machine-readable mark, so that platforms and detection tools can identify them automatically, alongside any label a viewer might notice. A related deadline, 2 December 2026, gives providers of generative systems already on the market before August time to retrofit older content with the same watermarking.

Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, framed it in aspirational terms: enforcement, she said, is "an important step towards AI that people and businesses can understand and trust, and whose benefits are shared widely across our society." The Commission's own announcement was more utilitarian, describing the goal as reducing "deception and manipulation" while giving business "clearer obligations and a practical way to show compliance."

The AI Office also holds a second, separate lever over general-purpose AI models — the foundation models from labs like OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic that underpin most commercial AI products. It can demand technical documentation, run its own evaluations, and for the most powerful "systemic risk" models — trained using more than 10^25 floating-point operations — assess risks directly. More than 180 organisations have signed the voluntary Code of Practice meant to demonstrate compliance with these GPAI obligations; OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Microsoft, Amazon and IBM are among the signatories. Meta declined, citing what it called legal uncertainty in the text.

The rules that didn't arrive

What makes this month's enforcement start notable is the contrast with what was originally supposed to happen alongside it. The AI Act's harder edge — its restrictions on "high-risk" systems used in hiring, credit scoring, education and law enforcement — was meant to bind from August 2026 too. It doesn't.

In a package known as the Digital Omnibus, adopted by the Council on 29 June after Parliament approval in mid-June, the Commission pushed that deadline back to December 2027, with a further category of obligations delayed to August 2028. The Commission's stated reason is technical: the harmonised standards companies need in order to know how to comply with the high-risk rules won't be ready until late 2026 at the earliest, so enforcing the underlying obligations before the standards exist would have punished companies for failing a test nobody had written yet. Critics, including several MEPs who voted for the original AI Act, read it differently — as a rollback dressed up as simplification, granted under pressure from US tech firms and European industry groups worried about compliance cost. The Omnibus was also criticised for leaving the compute threshold that defines a "systemic risk" model unchanged, even as models have grown well past it.

So the sequencing that has actually happened is the reverse of what the 2024 law's boosters advertised. The rules now live are the ones that touch disclosure and labelling — procedural, relatively cheap to implement, and hard for a company to argue against in public. The rules that would have constrained where and how AI systems get deployed against real people's job, loan and school applications are the ones sitting on ice for at least another year.

What it signals for the industry

For AI companies and their investors, that ordering matters more than the fine print of any single label requirement. A disclosure regime is a compliance-engineering problem: watermarking pipelines, UI copy, documentation processes. It raises costs at the margin but doesn't touch what a model can be trained to do or where it can be sold. A high-risk regime, by contrast, would have forced real product and deployment decisions — which is exactly the part now deferred.

That doesn't mean the EU has gone soft. The AI Office is reportedly adding roughly 40 staff to a base of around 165, and the fines on the books are real: up to 7% of global turnover for the most serious violations elsewhere in the Act, once those provisions do land. But the practical message to the industry this month is that Brussels is enforcing what it can enforce cleanly now, and buying time on the parts that are harder to define and easier to fight in court or in Council negotiations.

The next marker is 2 December, when the watermarking deadline for pre-existing generative systems falls due — a real-world test of whether "machine-readable mark" turns out to mean the same thing to a regulator in Warsaw as it does to one in Dublin. The one after that is December 2027, when high-risk obligations are due to bind for real, assuming the harmonised standards actually arrive and the Omnibus's critics don't win another round of delay first.
