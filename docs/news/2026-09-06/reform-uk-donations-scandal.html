Reform UK donations scandal deepens as aides resign and party is reported to police

Nigel Farage, whose party is under investigation after an undercover documentary alleged aides sought to circumvent the UK's foreign-donation ban. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Two senior Reform UK aides quit and the party was reported to police after an undercover sting alleged they schemed to route foreign money into party polling via a fake US donor.

It began, as these things often do, with wine and oysters. Over lunch in his Clacton constituency in July, Nigel Farage sat down with a man calling himself the UK-based son of a wealthy American financier, thanked him for his generosity, and pronounced the arrangement between them "all above board." The son's name was invented. So was his father's fortune. Both men were undercover reporters, and the meal was the closing scene of a months-long sting that has since cost Reform UK two of its most senior aides and landed the party a police referral.

Dan Jukes, a long-time communications adviser and one of Farage's closest confidants, and James Orr, the party's head of policy, resigned on 3 September after Channel 4 News broadcast footage gathered by the investigative outfit Verbatim. In it, both men are recorded discussing how to route foreign money into Reform's coffers while keeping its origin hidden — conduct that, if proven, would breach the core protection in British election law against outside interference in domestic politics.

The sting

Verbatim's operatives spent months posing as "William Lockwood," a UK-based son of a fictitious American businessman eager to bankroll Reform's rise. The undercover pair told Orr and Jukes they wanted to fund polling that would generate favourable headlines without anyone knowing where the money came from. According to the broadcast recordings, Orr told them: "I wouldn't breathe a word of this to anybody," adding that "we could easily get away with it," and suggesting a structure in which the son, not the father, would appear as the formal donor — "your father has got deniability." Jukes, in a separate recording, called the scheme one of the party's "little clandestine projects" that was "so valuable," and introduced the undercover reporter to a colleague as a "key donor for [an] off the book project."

The scheme concerned three opinion polls commissioned from JL Partners at a combined cost of £32,500 — a £18,000 survey in January that generated headlines about Labour, a £4,500 poll for the Telegraph in April, and a £10,000 poll that made the front page of the Times in June. None disclosed that Reform had commissioned them, still less that an undisclosed backer had paid for them. Two of the three polls landed shortly before May's local elections, in which Reform made its strongest showing yet. The operation culminated with the Clacton lunch, where the fake donor's father discussed a prospective £500,000 gift with Farage present.

Why the money trail matters

UK law bans political parties from accepting donations from foreign sources outright. Anything over £500 must come from a "permissible donor" — a UK-registered voter, company or organisation — and the recipient party is legally obliged to take "all reasonable steps" to verify the source before banking it. Doing anything to disguise the true origin of a donation, or to help an impermissible donor route money through a UK intermediary, is itself a separate criminal offence under the Political Parties, Elections and Referendums Act 2000, regardless of whether the disguised money is ultimately accepted.

That distinction is central to the dispute now playing out in public. Farage insists no money actually changed hands — "no illegal money. In fact, no money was taken at all," he told reporters at Reform's Birmingham conference — and has cast the recordings as "boasts" and "loose pub talk" by aides showing off rather than admissions of a functioning scheme. But the offence of conspiring or arranging to evade the rules does not require a completed transaction, only an agreement or an attempt. The Liberal Democrats have written to the Metropolitan Police and the Electoral Commission alleging exactly that — "acceptance and concealment of a donation from an impermissible donor" — and the governing Labour Party has said it will also report the matter. The Met has said it will "assess" the complaint; the Electoral Commission, whose maximum civil fine for breaches is capped at £500,000 or 5% of a party's turnover, whichever is lower, has limited practical power to compel testimony and no capacity to bring criminal charges itself.

A party already under scrutiny

The polling affair does not stand alone. Farage has separately faced questions since April over an undisclosed £5 million gift from Thailand-based crypto entrepreneur Christopher Harborne, which he has described as a personal gift for security rather than a party donation, and deputy leader Richard Tice faces his own questions over undeclared gifts. Both deny wrongdoing, and Farage and Tice have gone on the attack, suing the National Crime Agency over what they allege were leaked details of financial inquiries into the party. Reform's official line on the Verbatim documentary has been blunter still, dismissing it as "a hoax perpetrated by foreign-funded, climate change activists posing as would-be party donors" and insisting the party runs "a robust vetting process for prospective donors approved by the Electoral Commission."

The cumulative effect has been visible in the polls. Reform, which spent much of the past year leading national surveys, has slipped behind Labour in several recent polls taken since the scandal broke, a reversal that lands just as the party used its Birmingham conference to present itself as a government-in-waiting, promising to raise the tax-free personal allowance to £15,000 and to end small-boat Channel crossings within its first fortnight in office.

What comes next

For a party built on distrust of Westminster's rules, the test now is whether Britain's electoral finance regulator has enough teeth to enforce them. The Electoral Commission's initial assessment of the complaint is likely to take weeks rather than days, and any Metropolitan Police decision on whether to open a formal criminal investigation will hinge on evidence beyond the broadcast recordings — internal Reform records, bank transfers, or testimony from Jukes and Orr themselves, both of whom have stepped back only from their roles, not from the party. Farage has told broadcasters he expects further scrutiny before this settles, calling the funding row "only the opening round." Whether that scrutiny produces charges, a Commission fine, or nothing more than a damaging news cycle will say as much about the strength of Britain's foreign-donation firewall as it does about Reform UK.
