Aon nears $17bn deal to buy USI Insurance from KKR

The Aon Center in Chicago, one of the insurance broker's landmark offices. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Aon is buying USI Insurance from KKR for $17 billion, one of 2026's largest deals and a sign PE is still finding exits in insurance consolidation.

Aon plc has agreed to buy USI Insurance Services from KKR for $17 billion in cash, one of the largest deals struck anywhere this year and the clearest sign yet that private equity is willing to cash out of its insurance-brokerage bets even as broader M&A activity cools.

The agreement, signed August 30 and announced the following day, will see Aon absorb the tenth-largest U.S. insurance broker — a Valhalla, New York-based firm with roughly $3 billion in annual revenue, more than 10,500 employees and close to 200 offices serving mid-sized businesses with property and casualty, employee benefits, personal risk and retirement services. The all-cash transaction is expected to close in the fourth quarter of 2026, pending regulatory approval.

In one sentence: Aon is buying USI Insurance from KKR for $17 billion, one of 2026's largest deals and a signal that private equity is still finding exits in insurance consolidation.

KKR's payday

For KKR, the sale closes out a nine-year position that began in 2017, when the firm first backed USI at a valuation of about $4.3 billion. Three follow-on investments later, the exit delivers a return of roughly six times KKR's original 2017 capital and 3.4 times its overall invested capital across the life of the holding. KKR, as USI's largest shareholder, expects to book approximately $3.3 billion in after-tax proceeds and around $2 billion of adjusted net income from the sale — a number large enough that KKR shares rose about 2% on the announcement, even as Aon's fell.

That gap in market reaction is the more interesting data point. Aon stock dropped roughly 6-7% in the days following the announcement, wiping out close to $5 billion in market value from a pre-deal capitalization of about $75 billion. Investors are not questioning the strategic logic so much as the price and the balance sheet required to pay it: Aon is funding the acquisition entirely with new debt, valuing USI at about 14.5 times synergized trailing adjusted EBITDA, and has suspended share buybacks for the near term to prioritize deleveraging. Moody's affirmed Aon's Baa2 rating but shifted its outlook to stable from positive, estimating that pro forma leverage will jump from 2.8x to as high as 4.8x at closing before Aon works it back toward a 2.8-3.0x target over roughly 24 months. S&P moved its outlook to negative on similar grounds. Cantor Fitzgerald and Mizuho both trimmed price targets on Aon while keeping constructive ratings, reflecting a market that likes the destination but is wary of the route.

The middle-market bet

Aon's rationale centers on scale in the middle market, a segment the company sizes at more than $40 billion in the U.S. alone. This is Aon's second major push into that territory in three years, following its $13 billion acquisition of NFP in 2024 — a deal that, taken together with USI, signals a deliberate strategy rather than an opportunistic one-off. Aon expects the combined middle-market platform to generate $395 million in annual run-rate adjusted EBITDA benefits from revenue and cost synergies, along with expanded access to the fast-growing excess-and-surplus insurance market and a larger proprietary data set for underwriting and analytics.

Aon chief executive Greg Case called the combination the establishment of "the premier U.S. middle-market platform," and in comments to CNBC framed the shareholder value potential as "maybe the greatest I've seen in my 20-year career as CEO." USI's own chief executive, Mike Sicard, is set to become president of Aon and global CEO of its middle-market business once the deal closes — a leadership arrangement that suggests Aon is buying management continuity along with the book of business, not just folding USI into an existing structure. The deal is expected to dilute Aon's earnings per share in 2027 before turning accretive from 2028.

A consolidating industry

The USI transaction lands inside the most concentrated run of insurance-brokerage dealmaking on record. Between late 2023 and mid-2025, four mega-deals — Gallagher's purchase of AssuredPartners, Aon-NFP, Brown & Brown's acquisition of Accession, and Marsh McLennan's purchase of McGriff — totaled more than $44 billion combined. Industry data suggests the sector's "top four" strategic acquirers, Marsh McLennan, Aon, Arthur J. Gallagher and Brown & Brown, together with a Truist-led consortium, absorbed roughly $46 billion of specialty and middle-market broker value in the fourteen months to January 2026. The pattern reinforces a thesis taking hold among bankers who cover the space: for private equity firms sitting on $5 billion-plus brokerage platforms, the strategic buyers are increasingly the only exit large enough to matter.

That matters beyond insurance. KKR's willingness to sell a marquee asset at scale — rather than hold for a public listing or seek another sponsor-to-sponsor trade — reads as a vote of confidence in current pricing and in strategic buyers' appetite for debt-funded deals, at a moment when overall M&A volume has been soft. Whether that confidence is rewarded depends largely on execution risk that now sits with Aon: integrating a 10,500-person organization while working leverage down from near 5x, without the cover of buybacks or acquisitions elsewhere, is a multi-year test rather than a closing-day headline.

What comes next

Regulatory clearance is the immediate gate, with closing targeted for the fourth quarter. After that, the numbers to watch are Aon's deleveraging path against its 24-month target and whether the promised $395 million in synergies materializes on schedule — the two metrics that will determine whether Wall Street's initial skepticism about the price tag fades or hardens into a longer-term overhang on the stock.
