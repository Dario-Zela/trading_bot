US strikes Iranian tanker as UK petrol prices hit war-era high

An oil tanker at sea — Iran's 'shadow fleet' vessels, like the destroyed M/T Kylo, use flags of convenience and disabled transponders to move sanctioned crude. — Shipspotter Stahl / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
CENTCOM sank the Iranian tanker M/T Kylo and disabled two others after Iran missiled US warships, as UK petrol hit its highest price since the war began.

The pump price a British driver sees at the forecourt and the smoke rising off a Comoros-flagged tanker in the Gulf of Oman are, at this point in the 2026 Iran war, the same story told from opposite ends of the supply chain. On September 5, US Central Command confirmed it had struck three Iranian crude carriers after the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at two US Navy ships. Within a day, the RAC reported that UK petrol had climbed to its highest price since the war began on February 28 — a coincidence of timing that is not really a coincidence at all, but the two ends of the same chokepoint tightening at once.

Three tankers for two missiles

The sequence, as CENTCOM tells it, ran like this: an IRGC unit fired ballistic missiles at a US aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer operating in the region. Both ships evaded the attack; no American personnel were hurt. Washington's response was to hit back not against a military target but a commercial one — three vessels CENTCOM describes as part of Iran's oil "shadow fleet," the aging, frequently re-flagged tankers Tehran uses to move sanctioned crude, often with transponders switched off and ownership obscured through flags of convenience.

The tanker M/T Kylo — sailing under a Comoros flag and also known as the Noxen — was struck and destroyed in the Gulf of Oman. It was unladen at the time, and CENTCOM says its crew was warned to abandon ship beforehand, so no crude reached the water. Two more vessels, the M/T Downy off Iran's Kharg Island export terminal and the M/T Stark 1 near the port of Jask, were disabled but stayed afloat.

CENTCOM commander Adm. Brad Cooper put the arithmetic in blunt terms: "If you fire on two of our ships, we will impose a far greater economic cost on you by destroying three of your ships. We will not hesitate to defend US forces and, if necessary, destroy Iran's limited and exposed oil fleet." That is a doctrine stated out loud — the tanker fleet, not just naval assets, is now the pressure point, and Washington has told Tehran as much.

Weaponising the shadow fleet

Iran did not let the framing stand unanswered. Within hours, the IRGC said it had targeted three tankers transiting the Strait of Hormuz along with three US-linked vessels, warning ships against using "unauthorised waterways": "Do not be deceived by the 'terrorist' US military and refrain from any suspicious movement... Otherwise, you will be targeted." Two tankers were also reported to have struck naval mines in the strait days earlier. What began as a dispute over warship safety has become, in the space of a week, a live threat to any vessel transiting one of the world's three or four indispensable oil arteries.

Markets have registered it, if unevenly. Brent crude, which sat near $70 a barrel before the war began, spiked above $120 during the initial fighting in the spring, fell back toward $70 after a June framework deal briefly cooled things, and has climbed steadily since that deal collapsed — trading near $96 on September 4, up close to 9% on the week, its strongest weekly gain since mid-July. Vessel transits through the strait fell to roughly five commodity ships a day over the preceding weekend, well below normal flow, and the going rate to ship a 270,000-tonne crude cargo from the Gulf to China has risen to roughly $78 a tonne from about $74 — insurers and charterers pricing in a war-risk premium that has become a permanent fixture of the route rather than a spike that fades.

What is notable is what the market has not done, which is panic. Oil is elevated, not spiking in the way it did after the February outbreak or the missile exchanges in the spring. That restraint may not survive a genuine closure of the strait, but for now traders appear to be treating tanker-for-tanker strikes as a contained, if ugly, exchange rather than a prelude to blocking the waterway outright.

The bill lands at the pump

For UK households, the transmission mechanism is unglamorous but direct: wholesale crude feeds refined product feeds forecourt prices. The RAC put average UK petrol at more than 163p a litre this week, the highest since the war's outbreak in February, tracking the renewed climb in Brent since the June ceasefire framework fell apart. Diesel remains below its own April 15 peak of 191.54p a litre, but is rising on the same trend. The RAC Foundation calculated earlier in the conflict that the war had already added more than £300m collectively to motorists' fuel bills — a figure that will only have grown through the summer's on-off diplomacy and the past week's tanker exchanges.

That household cost is the plainest evidence of how this war reaches beyond the Gulf. It also explains why Cooper's "economic cost" framing cuts both ways: the US can sink Iranian tankers to squeeze the IRGC's export revenue, but every barrel pulled from the market or rerouted around a war-risk zone shows up, eventually, at a Tesco Extra forecourt in Norfolk.

What to watch

The question for markets is not the tanker strikes themselves but whether they become routine. A tanker-for-tanker exchange that stays confined to disabling ships — as this one largely has, with no spill from the Kylo and no reported casualties from the missile exchange — is a manageable risk premium. A move toward mining the strait itself, or a serious attempt by Tehran to close it to traffic, is a different order of event entirely. Near-term signals worth tracking: the RAC's weekly fuel price update, Brent's reaction to any further Iranian statements on Hormuz transit rules, and whether shipping insurers push war-risk premiums high enough to start deterring charterers outright — at which point the chokepoint closes itself, without either side firing another shot.
