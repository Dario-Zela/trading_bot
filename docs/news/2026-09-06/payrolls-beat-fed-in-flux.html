August payrolls beat clouds knife-edge Fed meeting as Trump renews rate-cut demands

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., ahead of the Fed's September 15–16 policy meeting. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
August payrolls beat forecasts by triple, pushing Fed hike odds above two-thirds even as Trump threatens trade cutoffs over rates.

In one sentence: August payrolls smashed forecasts and pushed Fed rate-hike odds above two-thirds, setting up a September 15–16 showdown between a hawkish Kevin Warsh and a president demanding cuts.

The US economy added 162,000 jobs in August, more than triple the 53,000 that economists had penciled in, and the number landed in Washington like a live wire. Unemployment held at 4.1%. June and July payrolls were revised up. Wages rose 3.1% from a year earlier, hotter than forecast. By any conventional reading, this is a labor market that does not need rescuing — and that is precisely the problem for a Federal Reserve that, one week out from its next meeting, had been leaning toward raising rates to choke off stubborn inflation.

Traders reacted the way they do when a hike becomes more likely rather than less: they sold. The S&P 500 closed lower on Friday, the two-year Treasury yield jumped to roughly 4.37% — its highest since January 2025 — and the ten-year drifted up near 4.78%. Gold and the euro fell as the dollar firmed. According to the CME FedWatch tool, the market-implied probability of a quarter-point hike at the Fed's September 15–16 meeting climbed to somewhere in the high 60s, having stood at just 35% before Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech ten days earlier.

A hawk in the chair, and a deadline closing in

That shift traces directly back to Warsh, who was confirmed in May as the Fed's 17th chair after Trump nominated him to succeed Jerome Powell. At Jackson Hole on August 28, Warsh gave what several outlets described as his most hawkish reading yet of the inflation picture, noting that core PCE inflation had held at 3.7% year-over-year in July — a level he called "concerning" — and recommitting the Fed to its 2% target. The speech did what Fed communications are designed to do: it moved the odds. Futures pricing for a September hike roughly doubled in the days that followed, then climbed further once the payrolls number landed.

The mechanics of the decision are straightforward enough: the federal funds rate currently sits at 3.5%–3.75%, and a quarter-point hike would take it to 3.75%–4%. What makes the meeting genuinely live is that a Fed governed by a chair Trump himself installed now appears more likely than not to move in the opposite direction from what the president wants — a dynamic almost without precedent in recent Fed history, where a president's own appointee is publicly signaling the case for tighter, not looser, policy.

Trump escalates, with Cabinet backup

Trump did not take the payrolls beat as reassurance. He took it as an argument. In a Truth Social post Friday, he wrote that the Fed should "LOWER THE RATE OR I'LL STOP TRADING WITH COUNTRIES WITH WHICH WE HAVE A DEFICIT," reviving a threat to invoke emergency trade powers — the same International Emergency Economic Powers Act that has underpinned his tariff actions — against countries running surpluses with the US. Speaking to reporters in the Oval Office later that day, he said he was under "no obligation" to keep trading with such partners and repeatedly floated cutting off trade with Canada entirely.

The pressure was not Trump's alone. Vice President JD Vance told reporters the administration believes "the Fed should be lowering interest rates," adding that it would be "nice to have some help from the Federal Reserve" on top of the administration's own efforts to hold borrowing costs down. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has separately said he expects a cut by September. Taken together, it is an unusually coordinated public campaign — president, vice president and Treasury secretary all pushing the same message in the same week — aimed at a Fed chair who now looks inclined to do the opposite just as the strongest jobs report in months has handed him the data to justify it.

The data underneath the headline

The case for a hike gets murkier the closer one looks. Three days before the official payrolls release, ADP reported private employers added just 38,000 jobs in August, the weakest reading since January and short of the roughly 47,000 expected — a report dragged down by a 17,000-job loss in manufacturing and a 16,000 decline in professional and business services. Initial jobless claims ticked up to 206,000 from 203,000, also slightly above forecast. Neither report matched the strength the Bureau of Labor Statistics later found. Even the composition of that BLS number was uneven: bars and restaurants supplied much of the headline gain, while information-sector jobs shrank, a pattern some economists tie to companies substituting AI tools for headcount in tech and media roles.

That gap between the official print and the private-sector and claims data is what gives Standard Chartered's contrarian call its logic. The bank has argued the Fed should still deliver a 50-basis-point "catch-up" cut in September — doubling its earlier 25bp forecast — framing it not as a reaction to any single weak report but as an adjustment for a labor market whose underlying breadth, in its reading, has been deteriorating for weeks beneath a headline number that will itself likely be revised. It is a minority view; most desks are pricing a hike or a hold, not a cut of that size. But it is a reminder that August's 162,000 is one data point sitting atop a stack of others — ADP, claims, sector-level churn — that tell a less tidy story.

What the split leaves unresolved

The Fed does not typically move on a single report, and Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks predate Friday's number by more than a week — meaning the payrolls beat reinforces a hawkish tilt that was already forming rather than creating it from scratch. What it does is remove any excuse to wait. Between now and September 16, the committee has one more full week to weigh a jobs report that argues for tightening against private-sector and claims data that argue for caution, all while the White House applies more direct pressure on a sitting Fed chair than has been seen in decades. The August CPI print, due before the meeting, will be the last major data point either side gets to make its case. After that, it is Warsh's call — and whichever way he goes, someone with real leverage over trade policy will be unhappy with the answer.
