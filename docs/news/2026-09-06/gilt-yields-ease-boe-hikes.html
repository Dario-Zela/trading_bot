UK gilt yields ease ahead of US jobs data, but BoE hike bets hold firm

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose Monetary Policy Committee next meets on 17 September. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Gilt yields dipped to 5.15% ahead of US jobs data, but markets still price a BoE hike by year-end and another by March 2027.

Gilt yields eased into the weekend as traders squared positions ahead of the closely watched US employment report, but the pullback looks like a pause for breath rather than a change of heart. The 10-year UK gilt yield slipped to 5.15% on Friday, down marginally from levels not seen since 2008, as a retreat in oil prices took some of the sting out of an inflation scare that has dogged the market since the summer. Underneath that move, the wager that the Bank of England will raise interest rates again this year — and once more in early 2027 — has barely budged.

A retreat from multi-decade highs

It has been a bruising few months for gilt holders. The 10-year yield touched roughly 5.21% at the start of September, extending a climb that took the 30-year gilt as high as 5.78% in May, its worst level since 1998. Two forces have been doing the damage: a fresh bout of concern over Britain's fiscal position, and an energy shock triggered by the conflict between Israel, the US and Iran, which sent oil through $100 a barrel earlier this year as Tehran moved to disrupt tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. The RAC Foundation puts the extra cost to UK motorists at more than £300m in fuel bills alone since the fighting began.

That energy squeeze pushed UK consumer price inflation up to 2.9% in July from a 15-month low of 2.6% in June, and the Bank's own forecasts see it peaking near 3.2% in the fourth quarter. Friday's dip in yields tracked a softer session for crude rather than any real shift in the inflation outlook — traders trimming exposure before a data point big enough to move every major bond market at once, not a verdict on the UK story.

The hawks aren't backing down

What makes gilts unusual next to Treasuries or bunds right now is the direction of travel. Markets are pricing roughly 32 basis points of Bank of England tightening by the end of 2026 — effectively a full quarter-point hike — with a second move priced in by early 2027, a striking reversal for a central bank that cut rates five times through 2025 to bring Bank Rate down to 3.75%, where it has now sat through five straight meetings.

The September 17 decision itself is not where traders expect the move to land — swaps imply under 4 basis points of tightening for that meeting, putting the odds of a September hike at roughly 15%. The action is expected instead at the November 5 meeting, which comes with a full Monetary Policy Report, or in December.

Governor Andrew Bailey has so far resisted being pinned to that timetable. Speaking ahead of the meeting, he said the Bank was seeing "quite subdued second-round effects" from the energy shock and pointed to a labour market that has been "softening... for some time now," arguing the MPC remains a meeting-by-meeting committee rather than one following a pre-set path. Chief economist Huw Pill has struck a more hawkish note, arguing that raising rates now could reduce the risk of the Bank having to tighten more aggressively later if the inflation overshoot proves stickier than expected. That split — a governor urging patience, a chief economist urging pre-emption — is a fair summary of where the whole rates market currently sits: pricing in hikes it isn't certain the data will ultimately demand.

Across the Atlantic

The US data that gilts were bracing for landed hot. Nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000 in August, more than treble the 53,000 consensus estimate, while unemployment held at 4.1%. Treasury yields jumped on the release — the 2-year note touched its highest level since January 2025 — as markets pushed the odds of a Federal Reserve rate hike this month to close to 60%, a sharp turn for a Fed that had spent much of the year fending off pressure from President Trump to cut. Sterling, for its part, held above $1.35 into the release, a reminder that a hawkish BoE and a potentially hawkish Fed are not mutually exclusive outcomes — both central banks are now grappling with inflation that has proved more durable than their own forecasts assumed a year ago.

For gilt investors, that leaves a market pricing tightening from a central bank that says it hasn't decided anything, against a backdrop where the American data that was meant to provide clarity has instead handed the Fed the same dilemma. The next test comes quickly: the Bank of England's September 17 decision will show whether Bailey's caution or Pill's pre-emption is winning the argument inside the MPC, while the November 5 meeting — flagged by the market as the more likely point for a first hike — will be shaped by wherever oil prices and the Iran ceasefire talks have settled by then.
