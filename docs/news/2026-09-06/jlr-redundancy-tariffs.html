Jaguar Land Rover opens redundancy programme as tariffs squeeze margins

The Solihull plant, where Land Rover Discovery and Range Rover models are built. — Land Rover MENA / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
JLR opened a voluntary redundancy scheme targeting up to 4,000 jobs to save £1.7bn, as US tariffs, a cyberattack and falling Chinese sales converge.

Jaguar Land Rover has opened a voluntary redundancy programme for salaried and management staff, the company confirmed on Friday, as Britain's largest carmaker tries to claw back £1.7bn in costs over the next two years. Reports in The Times put the number of jobs at risk as high as 4,000 — close to a tenth of JLR's roughly 40,000-strong workforce, three-quarters of which is based in the UK. JLR itself has declined to confirm a figure.

The company's own language was studiously bland. It needed to adapt to "evolving global market conditions," it told staff and union representatives, a phrase doing a great deal of work to cover a year in which JLR has been hit by a ransomware attack, US tariffs, and a sharp fall in Chinese demand more or less simultaneously.

A year of compounding shocks

It is worth separating the causes, because they arrived in a pile-up rather than a single blow. A cyberattack in late August 2025 — claimed by a group calling itself Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters — forced JLR to halt production entirely for roughly six weeks, at an estimated cost of £70m a day. The disruption was severe enough that the UK government stepped in with a £1.5bn loan guarantee at the end of September to keep suppliers afloat, backed by UK Export Finance. The company's own accounts told the story bluntly: pre-tax profit for the year to March 2026 collapsed to £14m, down from £2.5bn the year before.

On top of that came trade policy. Washington's tariffs on imported vehicles, introduced in 2025, were later softened for UK-built cars under a bilateral deal agreed that May — cutting the rate to 10% but only on the first 100,000 vehicles shipped annually, with anything above that quota facing 27.5%. For a company that sells heavily into the US and builds nothing there, even the discounted rate is a permanent tax on its most important export market, and the quota caps how much of that market it can serve without paying more.

China has offered no relief. JLR's wholesale volumes there fell by more than a quarter in the most recent quarter, part of a broader retreat by premium importers as domestic electric brands take share and the wider economy cools. Group-wide retail sales were down 15% in the same period. Chief executive PB Balaji has talked up a shift toward a smaller, higher-margin lineup in China and greater reliance on the US and UK — but that pivot takes years to pay off, and the redundancy programme is a much more immediate instrument.

The union response

Unite general secretary Sharon Graham framed the cuts as the predictable result of long-standing pressures rather than a one-off shock, pointing to what she called a "perfect storm" of underinvestment, tight zero-emission vehicle mandates and high industrial energy costs across the UK auto sector. "It cannot be acceptable that workers again are made to pay the price," she said. Unite national officer Des Quinn described it as "an incredibly worrying and stressful time for JLR workers," adding that the union was "working round the clock to deliver the best possible outcome."

Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds was due to meet JLR chief executive PB Balaji this week, a sign of how closely Whitehall is tracking a company it has already backstopped once this year with public money. That the state's largest recent auto intervention and this redundancy programme sit only months apart says something about how thin the cushion has become.

What the timing signals

The more interesting question for anyone watching the sector is not whether JLR is under pressure — the numbers settle that — but why it is acting now, before the underlying trade picture has fully settled. The US tariff quota is fixed for the moment, China's slowdown looks structural rather than cyclical, and the cyberattack's costs are largely booked. A redundancy programme announced against that backdrop reads less like panic and more like a company choosing to take the pain on its own schedule, cutting fixed costs before a possible further slide in volumes forces harsher action later.

That calculus will be closely watched elsewhere in the premium segment. JLR's exposure to US tariffs and Chinese demand is not unique — German luxury manufacturers face a similar combination, if in different proportions — and a large, visible cost-cutting move at one of Europe's most prominent premium brands tends to give cover, or pressure, for others to follow. The next marker will be whether the voluntary scheme meets its target headcount without compulsory cuts, and whether JLR's half-year results, due later this year, show the £1.7bn savings plan translating into an actual recovery in margin rather than just a smaller cost base absorbing the same shocks.
