Von der Leyen to press €10tn savings mobilisation plan in State of the Union

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, who addresses EU lawmakers on September 16. — Etienne Ansotte / European Union
In one sentence
Von der Leyen will use her September 16 State of the Union to push a plan mobilising €10tn in EU household savings to close Europe's investment gap.

Ursula von der Leyen will stand before the European Parliament in Strasbourg on September 16 and ask a question that sounds simple but has taken Brussels the better part of two years to frame: why does Europe sit on roughly €10 trillion in household savings while its companies scramble for capital?

The European Commission president previewed the pitch on August 27 at the La REF business conference in Paris, telling French executives that European bank deposits are "sluggish" — earning little, financing less, and mostly channelled abroad when they are invested at all. A significant share of EU savings that does reach capital markets ends up in the United States rather than funding firms at home. Her State of the Union address will make the case for the Savings and Investments Union (SIU), the Commission initiative launched in March 2025, with new urgency.

The numbers behind the pitch

The scale is the story. Mario Draghi's 2024 competitiveness report put Europe's annual investment shortfall at roughly €800 billion — about 4-5% of GDP — concentrated in energy transition, digitalisation and defence, areas where the bloc's spending trails both the US and China and the gap is widening rather than closing. Public budgets, squeezed by defence commitments and debt-service costs, cannot close that gap alone. The Commission argues the €10 trillion sitting in low-yield deposits is the obvious alternative source, and estimates that its package of measures could unlock up to €470 billion in additional investment if implemented in full — a meaningful dent, but well short of Draghi's number on its own.

What's actually on the table

The SIU is not a single law but a bundle of technical fixes the Commission has been assembling since March 2025: a revamp of the EU's securitisation rules to make it cheaper for banks to offload loans and free up balance-sheet capacity for new lending (the Council reached a negotiating position on this in December); a review of pension frameworks, including the Pan-European Personal Pension Product and occupational pension rules, aimed at nudging more retirement saving into markets via auto-enrolment; and tax-advantaged savings and investment accounts modelled loosely on ISAs, which the Commission published a blueprint for last year. None of it forces anyone to move their deposits — the plan works only if it makes market investment more attractive than a savings account, at a moment when EU savers have shown limited appetite for equity risk.

The politics of getting there

Von der Leyen wants agreement among all 27 member states by the end of the year, though she has signalled a willingness to proceed with a smaller coalition of willing states if unanimity proves elusive — a familiar EU workaround, and an implicit admission that some capitals remain unconvinced. Pension and savings policy sits close to national sovereignty, and finance ministries in countries with strong domestic banking sectors have been wary of rules that make it easier for capital to flow across borders rather than stay put.

What it means for markets

For investors, the interesting part is less the €10 trillion headline than the mechanics: securitisation reform would matter to European bank balance sheets and credit issuance well before any household reallocates a euro of savings, while pension and tax-account changes are multi-year plays on flows into European equity and bond funds. None of it is likely to move yields in September. The address on the 16th will set the Commission's rhetorical framing for the year; the substance will be tested in the securitisation trilogues and pension-framework negotiations that follow, and in whether the promised €470 billion shows any sign of materialising by the time Brussels next takes stock.
