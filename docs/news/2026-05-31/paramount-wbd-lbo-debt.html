Paramount is pulling every lever to sell LBO debt for its $110bn Warner Bros. bid

Warner Bros. Studios in Burbank, California — the flagship lot of the company at the centre of the $110bn deal. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Paramount Skydance's $110B Warner Bros. bid hinges on placing $50B in debt across investment-grade bonds, junk notes, and loans — the largest such package any non-sponsored borrower has attempted.

Paramount Skydance Corp. has spent the past three months building one of the most elaborate debt structures in corporate finance history — and it still isn't done. The company's plan to buy Warner Bros. Discovery Inc. for $110 billion in cash requires placing roughly $50 billion in bonds and loans, a financing exercise so vast that even in a market hungry for yield, the architects of the deal have had to work every available corner of the credit universe to make it work.

The definitive merger agreement was struck on February 27, after a bidding contest that at one point included Netflix. Since then, the focus has shifted from who wins to how the winner pays. The answer, still taking shape as of late May, involves a financing mix that would be unusual for most borrowers: $30 billion in investment-grade bonds, $12 billion in high-yield notes, and another $7.5 billion in leveraged loans, split across U.S. dollars and euros, stretched across maturities from three to ten years.

Layering the Stack

The sheer breadth of the capital structure tells its own story. Investment-grade buyers — insurers, pension funds, sovereign wealth managers — will absorb the bulk of the paper, but only if the pricing is right. Early discussions on the 10-year investment-grade tranche have centered on spreads of 2.5 to 2.75 percentage points above the benchmark rate, a level that reflects both the scale of the issuance and the post-deal leverage that comes with it. Loans, intended for leveraged-finance investors who prefer floating rates, are being discussed at roughly 3 percentage points over benchmark, at around 99 cents on the dollar.

The decision to carve the high-yield portion into both dollar and euro tranches — with maturities at five, eight, and ten years — reflects a deliberate effort to broaden the investor base. A single $12 billion slug of junk bonds in one currency or one maturity would require a far deeper discount to clear the market. By disaggregating the issuance, Paramount's advisers — Bank of America and Citigroup are leading — can target distinct pools of demand without overwhelming any one corner of the market.

The financing was originally backstopped at $57.5 billion by Bank of America, Citigroup, and Apollo Global Management, before being trimmed to $54 billion and then $49 billion as the banks sold exposure into a broader syndicate of 18 financial institutions. The step-downs indicate normal market-making mechanics — the underwriters reducing balance sheet risk ahead of a formal launch — but they also trace the contours of a deal that has been in continuous negotiation with credit markets since February.

A Record-Setting Warmup

Before the main event, Warner Bros. Discovery itself completed a financing that functions as the deal's market rehearsal. The company priced a $15 billion cross-border term loan B in May — the largest leveraged loan since the financial crisis, second-largest in history behind the 2007 TXU transaction — to refinance its existing bridge facility ahead of the merger close. Initial guidance was for spreads of 2.75 to 3.0 percentage points; by the time the book closed, pricing had tightened to 2.5 percentage points, with an original-issue discount of 99.75 cents rather than the 99 cents guided at launch. Investor orders topped $30 billion, more than double the available paper.

That $15 billion term loan, administered by JPMorgan with a bookrunner group including Barclays, BNP Paribas, Deutsche Bank, Goldman Sachs, UBS, and Wells Fargo, consumed 73 percent of May's total institutional loan supply — and still tightened at pricing. The message from credit markets was clear: at current spreads, demand is there, even at unprecedented scale.

The Weight of the Numbers

The question investors are pricing, beneath the spread negotiations, is how quickly this leverage comes down. Post-merger, the combined entity is expected to carry net debt approaching $80 billion — roughly 7 times pre-synergy EBITDA, a figure that deal skeptics have described as frightening. Paramount's own projections model net leverage at close of 4.3 times on a fully synergized basis, with a target of investment-grade credit metrics within three years.

That target depends on more than $6 billion in annual synergies materializing, derived from combining two of the largest media operations in the world. It also depends on the Ellison family backstop. David Ellison, who serves as chairman and chief executive of Paramount Skydance, is backed by his father Larry, whose fortune is estimated at $247 billion. The family has pledged to inject additional capital into the combined company if needed to defend an investment-grade rating — a commitment that gives rating agencies something to hold against a leverage profile they would normally penalize.

The broader credit backdrop has been cooperative. May 2026 is tracking as the busiest month for leveraged loans since January, and U.S. junk-bond issuance is running at a five-year high, up more than 35 percent year-on-year. Corporate M&A loan volume reached $15.1 billion in May, the highest monthly figure since January 2020. That doesn't mean the market absorbs anything — lenders remain selective, and no single borrower has attempted a $50 billion bond-and-loan package in peacetime credit markets. But the window to launch is open.

What Comes Next

The main Paramount bond-and-loan package had not formally launched as of late May, with bankers describing the timeline as within the next couple of weeks. How the investment-grade tranches price against guidance will be the first concrete read on whether credit markets are endorsing the deal's leverage math or merely tolerating it. The WBD term loan tightened by 25 basis points from guidance and cleared with oversubscription; replicating that outcome across a $30 billion investment-grade book would be a different order of magnitude.

Beyond the financing, the transaction still requires regulatory clearances and a shareholder vote from Warner Bros. Discovery investors, with the deal expected to close in the third quarter. For debt traders, the immediate catalyst is the bond launch itself. If the order book builds cleanly and spreads tighten, it validates Paramount's structural engineering. If the deal is forced to widen or restructure tranches mid-flight, it frames a harder question — about where the ceiling sits for mega-cap M&A in a credit market that has reopened, but not unconditionally.
