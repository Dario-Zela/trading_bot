Erewhon sued over unpaid rent at its Culver City store

In one sentence
Hackman Capital Partners is suing Erewhon for $275K in unpaid rent at its Culver City store, where the grocer pays over $1M a year to anchor a property now listed for sale.

The Erewhon grocery chain, which sells $22 smoothies and charges $86,500 a month in base rent for its Culver City outpost, has stopped paying for the space. So says Hackman Capital Partners, the real estate firm that owns Culver Steps, which filed suit in Los Angeles Superior Court last week alleging Erewhon owes more than $275,000 in unpaid rent, fees, and damages.

The complaint, dated May 21, is spare: "Landlord has demanded payment from tenant and tenant has failed to pay." Neither party responded to requests for comment.

The rent maths

The lease—signed in June 2021, commenced June 2023, and running through May 2038—commits Erewhon to roughly $86,500 a month for the first five years, stepping up to $95,000 and then $105,000 over two successive five-year intervals. The per-square-foot rate of $6 to $7.30 a month is roughly double the Los Angeles commercial retail average. Annual occupancy costs clear $1 million before maintenance, taxes, and common-area fees are added.

David Greensfelder, a Bay Area retail real estate consultant quoted in coverage of the suit, put the threshold plainly: the Culver City store would need to clear more than $1 million in weekly sales to support those lease costs. "Erewhon could be doing fabulously," he said, "and still not be doing $1 million in sales a week." Erewhon's most recently disclosed annual revenue is roughly $253 million across all its locations combined — meaning each store carries a heavy individual load.

This is not the chain's first rent dispute. In late 2022, Erewhon faced similar accusations at its Studio City location, a case that generated countersuits over alleged improper rent increases and lease violations before fading from public view.

The landlord's own finances add texture to the timing. Hackman Capital Partners — the world's largest independent studio property owner — has disclosed a $1.1 billion mortgage default at its Radford Studio Center and faces foreclosure proceedings on a separate $100 million Culver City loan on the former Sony Pictures Animation campus. Culver Steps itself has been listed for sale at roughly $150 million. Hackman's need for consistent rent rolls is not obscure.

Erewhon, for its part, is mid-expansion. Chief executive Tony Antoci has described 2025 and 2026 as the beginning of "Erewhon 2.0" — a push toward 20 U.S. cities, including New York and Miami, backed by a 65,000-square-foot commissary kitchen in Vernon, California, that quintuples prepared-food production capacity. New California locations in Glendale, Manhattan Beach, and West Hollywood opened or are opening this year; Thousand Oaks follows in 2026.

The strain between expansion ambition and unit-level economics is precisely what the Culver City dispute makes visible. A retail model that requires more than $1 million in weekly turnover per store just to cover occupancy leaves narrow tolerance for demand softening — and consumer-spending surveys in 2026 show even higher-income households gravitating toward value in food categories once considered insulated from trade-down pressure.

The case before Los Angeles Superior Court will turn on whether Erewhon cures the default or contests the amount. Any counterclaim — as emerged in the Studio City dispute — could surface more detail about the chain's unit-level economics than the privately held company would otherwise volunteer. Erewhon has approximately 30 days to file a response.
