STANDING WATCH · IRAN · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-29 · 4 min read

Iran: US reopens a channel to Tehran as sanctions pressure continues

In one sentence
The WSJ reports a US push to revive Iran talks via a hard-liner, while Axios reports nuclear demands and possible post-midterm strikes, as Treasury tightens sanctions.

The United States is reported to be reopening a line to Tehran at the same time as it tightens the financial squeeze on it. The two tracks, one diplomatic and one coercive, are moving together. That makes the next few weeks a test of which one Washington is really counting on.

The Wall Street Journal reports that the Trump administration has made an effort to revive peace talks with Iran, with Vice President JD Vance reaching out to an Iranian hard-liner. Axios reports separately that Tehran is being pressed on nuclear concessions, and that large US strikes are being discussed for after the midterm elections. Neither report could be independently confirmed here, and the identity of the hard-liner and the terms on offer are not established in what is public. The reports are best read as signals of intent, not as evidence that negotiations have resumed.

A channel, not yet a negotiation

Vance has been the administration's front man on Iran before. Earlier this year he led US delegations in talks in Pakistan and Switzerland on Iran's nuclear programme. Those rounds produced an interim framework that requires, at a minimum, that Iran's stockpile of highly enriched uranium be diluted under international supervision. Parts of the president's own party criticised that deal, and Trump has threatened to hit Iran "very hard again" if Tehran does not deliver.

Against that history, an approach to a hard-liner is notable. Hard-liners in Tehran have long opposed direct dealings with Washington, and any settlement needs their acquiescence to survive. The outreach would make sense as an attempt to widen the circle of people who own a deal. It would also make sense as a way to show allies and domestic critics that diplomacy was tried before force. The reporting does not say which of these is closer to the truth.

The economic squeeze

The pressure side is better documented. Treasury launched what it calls Operation Economic Outcast on 24 August, aiming to sever the financial channels Iran uses to sell oil and evade sanctions. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been the campaign's loudest advocate. He told a House hearing on 15 September that "we have seen the currency collapse". In late September Treasury published a graphic putting the rial at about 2.44 million to the dollar, a record low, with a caption looking ahead to 3 million. Bessent has since said that Iran is running short of foreign currency and that oil output is starting to falter, and in an Iran International interview on 26 October he said the next eight weeks would be "even more intense".

These are the Treasury's own claims, and the sources for the exchange-rate figures are largely Iran-focused and regional outlets, not independent market data. Reports on the exact rate differ by date. The reported CNBC remarks, in which Bessent called the campaign "very successful", were not located, so that framing should be treated as unverified. The direction is consistent across accounts, though: a weak currency and a shortage of hard cash give Washington leverage, and Bessent's public timetable of eight weeks overlaps closely with the post-midterm window in the Axios report.

What the overlap suggests

The pairing is the point. Sanctions that bite hardest just as talks reopen are a standard way to improve a bargaining position. A strike threat timed for after 3 November is another. Read together, the reports are consistent with a strategy of offering a deal from a position of maximum pressure, with force as the fallback. They are also consistent with a quite different reading, in which the outreach is a box-ticking exercise ahead of a decision already taken. The evidence available does not separate the two.

The stakes reach beyond Iran. Fitch this week flagged Hormuz risk among the threats to the 2027 outlook, and any move from pressure to strikes would feed straight into the oil price and the risk premium traders attach to the Gulf.

What to watch

The first marker is the midterms themselves, after which the administration's room for manoeuvre changes. The second is whether Tehran publicly acknowledges any contact, or whether a date and venue for talks emerge. The third is the rial: Treasury's own 3 million target, and its eight-week timetable running into December, give a measurable yardstick. A deal, an escalation, or a drift between them will probably show first in those three places.
