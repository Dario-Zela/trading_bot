Flávio Bolsonaro denies fleeing Brazil after landing in Miami

In one sentence
Flávio Bolsonaro says he left Brazil "for the sake of Brazil", not to flee, but he landed in Miami after a court moved to seize his passports amid two Supreme Court inquiries.

Flávio Bolsonaro, the Brazilian senator who lost this month's presidential run-off to Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, has said he did not flee Brazil. He was seen stepping off a plane in Miami on Wednesday, after the country's courts had moved to take his passports. Brazilian officials and his critics describe the trip as flight from criminal investigation. He says he left "for the sake of Brazil".

What is known

According to the BBC, Bolsonaro was seen getting off a plane at Miami-Opa Locka Executive Airport on Wednesday morning. Flight data suggests the aircraft left Puerto Iguazú, on the Argentine side of the tri-border area, and stopped in Guayaquil, Ecuador, on the way. Argentina's own accounts of his movements were at first contradictory. Brazilian outlets reported on Tuesday that he had left the country overnight, while his campaign coordinator, Senator Rogério Marinho, initially said he was still in Brasília.

The departure followed a Supreme Federal Court order. Justice Alexandre de Moraes ordered his passports seized, citing a risk of flight, according to Gazeta do Povo. Federal Police could not find him to serve it. After he missed a 24-hour deadline to hand the documents over, Brazil cancelled them and ordered checks at border posts. The US, however, had issued visas to him and his family.

His statement, his first since leaving, was categorical:

"I did not flee Brazil; I left for the sake of Brazil."

He added that it had "never" been his intention to leave his homeland, but that he knew "exactly where this is heading". He described himself as a victim of a coup and of political persecution, and repeated claims of electoral fraud that have not been substantiated. Lula won the run-off by more than four million votes, and the result was confirmed by the head of Brazil's top electoral court, who was appointed by Jair Bolsonaro.

The legal exposure

Two matters are before the Supreme Federal Court, and he denies wrongdoing in both. One concerns possible irregularities in the transfer of nearly $70m (£53m) from Banco Master to Dark Horse, a biopic about his father. The other is an alleged money-laundering inquiry. Gazeta do Povo, citing the court record, links the inquiries to the former banker Daniel Vorcaro, whose Banco Master has been liquidated. No charges are described in the reporting reviewed here.

Time matters to the senator. His eight-year Senate term ends on 31 January, and with it the parliamentary immunity he now holds. Whether a trip abroad reads as flight depends heavily on that date. His critics see an exit timed to precede it. His supporters see a politician who expects an unfair process once the protection lapses.

Reaction, and a family precedent

Lula criticised the secretive exit: "those who have nothing to hide have nothing to fear." Donald Trump, asked about it, said: "We gave him a pass to come into the country." He declined to address the fraud claims, saying only that the election was "very strange" and that the Bolsonaros had been treated "very unfairly".

There is a precedent. Jair Bolsonaro left for Florida in December 2022, before Lula's inauguration. Gazeta do Povo also quotes Eduardo Bolsonaro, who lives in the US, from an earlier interview: if his brother did not win, he was in favour of him leaving the country. The same outlet reports an unverified claim, from a lawyer said to be close to Trump, that Secretary of State Marco Rubio arranged the exit. Treat that as unconfirmed.

Markets

None of the reporting reviewed here records a market reaction. The investor angle is a reasonable question, since a contested transition and an opposition leader abroad bear on political risk, but it is not yet demonstrated. Lula's win was decisive and formally certified, which limits the room for the disputed-result scenario that investors would price most heavily.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The first is whether the Supreme Federal Court escalates beyond cancelling the passports, for instance with an arrest warrant or a request for extradition, which would turn a political dispute into a diplomatic one with Washington. The second is whether Bolsonaro returns before 31 January, when his immunity ends. The third is whether the US, which has granted visas, treats him as a visitor or entertains an asylum claim, as some Brazilian reports suggest he may make.
