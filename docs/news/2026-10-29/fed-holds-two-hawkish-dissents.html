Fed holds at 3.75-4.00% with two dissents for a hike as Warsh leaves December open

In one sentence
The FOMC held at 3.75%-4.00% on a 10-2 vote, with Cook and Hammack dissenting for a 25bp hike, and Warsh left December open while one prediction market priced an 82% chance of a hike.

The Federal Reserve left its benchmark rate unchanged on Wednesday, but the vote was not unanimous in the usual way. The Federal Open Market Committee voted 10-2 to hold the federal funds target range at 3.75%-4.00%. Both dissenters wanted to raise it. Governor Lisa Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack each preferred a 25 basis-point increase, which would have taken the range to 4.00%-4.25%. The statement said the Committee needs more information before setting the policy stance.

A hold with dissents on one side only is a different signal from a hold with a split vote. Two members are not asking the Fed to wait longer. They are asking it to reverse direction.

A split about direction, not timing

Dissents at a hold usually argue about speed: cut now or cut later, hike now or hike later. Here the argument is about the cycle itself. Nobody dissented in favour of a cut, so the committee's disagreement runs only in one direction. Ten members were content to sit at 3.75%-4.00%, and two judged that the current setting is too loose given where inflation is.

The statement's own language is cautious. Saying the Committee needs more information before setting the policy stance tells readers that the next move has not been chosen. It does not confirm a tightening bias, and it does not rule one out. For a committee with two open hawks, that wording keeps the majority from committing in either direction.

The committee has also changed leadership, and that colours how the vote reads. Kevin Warsh is chairing his first meetings, and a first-time chair has an interest in showing a committee that argues openly. Whether that is a deliberate style or an accident of timing is not something the vote can settle.

Warsh: inflation too high, but 'monetary medicine' is working

At the press conference, Warsh held two positions at once. According to the discovery summary of the remarks, the chair said inflation remains too high, and also that credit conditions have tightened since September, which Warsh counted as part of the "monetary medicine" already at work. Warsh also said third-quarter GDP growth is likely above 3%. These characterisations come from a secondary summary, and the exact wording should be checked against the full transcript and the Bureau of Economic Analysis release.

The two claims fit together as an argument for patience. If tighter credit is already restraining demand, the Fed can hold its rate and let financial conditions do some of the work. But strong growth cuts the other way, and it is the hawks' best evidence. An economy running above 3% with inflation still too high is the case Cook and Hammack are implicitly making for a higher rate.

Warsh left December open. That is consistent with the statement and with the dissents: a chair who wanted to close the door on a hike would have said so, and one who wanted to promise it would have had to explain why the committee did not do it on Wednesday.

Markets price a hike the Fed has not promised

Traders have moved further than the Fed. Polymarket, the prediction market, was pricing an 82% probability of a rate hike at the December meeting on the day of the hold. That figure comes from a single, thinly regulated venue and should not be read as the consensus of professional rate markets. Even so, the gap is the point. The Fed's statement did not confirm a tightening bias, yet a market is pricing a hike as the likely outcome.

Two readings are possible. One is that traders treat the two dissents as the leading edge of a majority, since committees that start with two hawks often end with more. The other is that the pricing reflects the strong growth data and the lack of any dovish signal, and the dissents are incidental. The evidence available does not separate the two. What can be said is that the committee's wording and the market's price are not aligned, and one of them will have to move before December.

The consequences run through the usual channels. A higher expected policy rate would push up Treasury yields at the short end, support the dollar and raise borrowing costs for households and companies that price off short-term rates. A hold that the market reads as a delayed hike has already begun to do some of that work.

Oil and rates stop moving together

One further remark from the chair complicates the picture. Warsh said the usual relationship between oil prices and market-set interest rates has recently broken down, according to a Benzinga report. The same report describes oil futures rising while market rates fell over the period. Benzinga is the only source for this, and neither the date nor the figures could be checked against a transcript or wire report.

If accurate, it matters because oil is a common shortcut for inflation expectations. Higher crude usually lifts expected inflation and pushes rates up. A period in which crude rises and rates fall removes that shortcut and leaves the Fed with less guidance from energy markets about whether price pressure is building.

Separately, a Benzinga item citing Reuters says the Fed plans to move to eight meetings a year from autumn 2027, with one meeting holding no rate vote and one devoted to strategy, and to cut policy-setting meetings to six a year in 2028. This could not be confirmed and is not an announced decision. If it holds, it changes how often the Fed can surprise markets, but not the stance it takes at any one of them.

What to watch

The next decision is the December meeting, and the committee will have the intervening data on inflation and the labour market to work with. The relevant tests are specific: whether third-quarter GDP is confirmed above 3%, whether inflation readings move toward the Fed's target, and whether any other voter joins Cook and Hammack. A third dissent for a hike at the next meeting, or a chair who stops describing December as open, would show that the committee had settled the question it left open on Wednesday.
