Japan's haunted homes attract bargain-hunting buyers

In one sentence
As Japan's population shrinks and empty homes pile up, properties with a death in their past sell at steep discounts that bargain hunters are now pricing in.

In Japan, a death in a house can cut its price. Buyers have noticed, and a growing number are treating the stigma as a bargain.

The trade is in what the market calls jiko bukken, or "incident properties": homes and flats where someone has died by murder, suicide or an unattended death, often a lonely one. Disclosure law generally obliges agents to say so. That obligation, together with a reluctance to live in a place with a dark history, pushes prices below those of comparable homes.

How big is the discount?

There is no official national statistic, and the figures that circulate are indicative rather than systematic. A property expert quoted by Reuters in 2025 described one house as priced about 20% below the market because of its history. Village House, a rental agency, puts suicide-related discounts at 10% to 30%, depending on whether the death was inside a unit or in a common area. A July 2026 Business Today report cites roughly 30% in big cities and up to 50% or more in smaller towns. Brokers have said the worst cases, such as murder sites, can fall much further or fail to sell at all.

The spread is wide, which is itself informative. The discount appears to depend on how the person died, where the property is and how many alternatives buyers have.

Why now

The backdrop is demographic. Japan's population has fallen since its 2008 peak, and the 2023 housing survey counted roughly 9 million vacant homes, about 13.8% of the national stock. When supply of empty houses is that abundant, sellers have less room to hold out for a clean-slate price, and a stigma that once made a property unsellable becomes something to negotiate over.

The Economist, as quoted on an online forum, notes that a death need not be disclosed after three years, so a discounted property can later be resold at full price. Several firms now do business around the stigma: specialists who spend nights in a home to show it holds no lingering spirits, and others who offer ceremonies for lost souls. That would turn the taboo into a service line as well as a price signal.

What to watch

The limits are plain. This is a niche corner of a market where plenty of vacant homes are simply unwanted, haunted or not, and some akiya are on offer for next to nothing regardless of their history. The test of the "stigma trade" is whether buyers who pay 20% to 30% less can resell without the discount, as the three-year rule implies. Without systematic transaction data, which does not yet exist, the evidence is anecdotal. The next housing survey, and any move by regulators to collect figures on incident properties, would show whether this is a durable pricing category or a curiosity.
