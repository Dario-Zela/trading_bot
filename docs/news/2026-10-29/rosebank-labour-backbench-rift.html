Labour MPs urge No 10 not to overrule the energy secretary on Rosebank

In one sentence
Labour backbenchers are urging No 10 not to overrule energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh, reportedly minded to refuse Rosebank, as the Treasury is said to favour approval.

Labour backbenchers are lobbying Downing Street not to overrule the energy secretary if she refuses permission for Rosebank, the large undeveloped oil field west of Shetland. The dispute is partly about one field. It is also a test of who holds the final say on North Sea licensing, and of how far the government will bend its climate commitments under pressure from the Treasury.

Who is deciding

The energy secretary is Miatta Fahnbulleh, not Ed Miliband, who held the post when the consent problem first arose. According to sources cited by the Guardian on 21 October, she is "minded" to refuse environmental permission for Rosebank. No decision has been announced. Her aides say she is the sole decision-maker.

The decision is back with ministers because the Court of Session quashed Rosebank's original consent in January 2025. The court found that the developers had not assessed scope 3 emissions, meaning those released when the oil is burned. A fresh assessment has been under way since.

The government approved the smaller Jackdaw gasfield last week. Prime Minister Andy Burnham described it as "a bridge to the future". Fahnbulleh has argued that Jackdaw is a gasfield serving the domestic market, while Rosebank is mainly an export oilfield, so the same reasoning does not carry over.

The backbench case

The Guardian reports that Labour MPs have been pressing both the prime minister and the chancellor to leave her ruling alone. Burnham says the decision is Fahnbulleh's. But his Commons remarks on Jackdaw led some ministers to read his promise of a "pragmatic" approach to oil and gas as a sign he would support Rosebank. Chancellor John Healey is reportedly in favour of the scheme, according to sources close to him.

Catherine Fookes, a Labour member of the energy select committee, said: "Approving Rosebank will blow a hole in our green credentials." Clive Lewis questioned whether the Treasury's expected tax receipts can be trusted, since they rest on operator estimates. One MP warned of a major rebellion if Healey pushed the project through.

MPs were willing to accept Jackdaw because it was framed around domestic gas supply. Their objection is that Rosebank is a different case.

The Treasury's numbers

The Treasury expects about £500m in tax revenue from Rosebank by the end of this parliament, a figure that has appeared in Office for Budget Responsibility forecasts since 2019. The OBR says oil and gas output would fall by roughly 14% a year without new fields.

The campaign group Uplift argues the tax take may be limited, and could be eroded by tax allowances and decommissioning costs. Areeba Hamid of Greenpeace UK says future revenues would leave public services exposed to oil prices, and taxpayers liable for decommissioning bills.

Some in government argue she can approve the field without breaching carbon commitments. Their reasoning is that most of the oil would be exported, and emissions from burning it abroad do not count against UK targets. That is the very question the court ruling put at the centre of the case, and critics are likely to say it sidesteps the scope 3 finding.

Aberdeen South MP Douglas Lumsden, who backs the field, has written to the energy secretary calling a refusal "cruel, unnecessary and uneconomic". Scottish Labour leader Michael Marra has also urged approval.

What to watch

The immediate question is whether Fahnbulleh issues a ruling, and whether Downing Street or the Treasury intervenes if she refuses. Any ruling would set a precedent for other North Sea applications that depend on the same end-use emissions assessment. An overruling would also test the line that the decision belongs to the energy secretary alone. Much of the reporting rests on private briefings, so the reported leanings may change before a formal announcement.
