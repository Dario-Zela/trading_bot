Netanyahu's bloc leads Israel's election but falls short of a majority

In one sentence
With most votes counted, Netanyahu's bloc has 53 of 120 seats, Eisenkot's 48 and Arab parties 19, leaving no clear path to 61 and a real risk of deadlock.

Israel has voted, and the arithmetic has not produced a government. With about 86% of votes counted, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc leads on 53 seats in the 120-seat Knesset, eight short of the 61 needed for a majority. The main opposition bloc, led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, has 48. The 19 seats won by predominantly Arab parties sit outside both camps. Neither side can govern without something it has so far refused to offer.

These are partial figures. The count reported by Ynet on Wednesday morning covered 93% of polling stations and 85.6% of votes, and it did not yet include the "double-envelope" ballots. Those are cast by soldiers, diplomats, prisoners, hospital patients and others voting outside their home precinct. More than 500,000 are expected, and counting runs until Thursday evening. Because they can move a seat or two between parties near the electoral threshold, the final distribution may differ. The Central Elections Committee has said results will stay "nearly final" rather than fully certified, with further checks next week.

The seat breakdown

Likud is the largest single party on 26 seats. Its prospective partners are the ultra-Orthodox and hard-right parties:

  • Shas: 7
  • United Torah Judaism: 7
  • Otzma Yehudit: 7
  • Religious Zionism-Zehut: 6

That adds up to 53.

The opposition bloc is more fragmented but its parties are close in total. Eisenkot's Yashar! has 21, Naftali Bennett's Beyachad 12, the Democrats 8 and Yisrael Beytenu 7, which makes 48. The Joint List has 13 and Ra'am 6, for 19.

The figures have moved during the count. Earlier tallies put Netanyahu's bloc as high as 57 and then at 54 and 55 as more ballots came in. The trend has been downward, which is why the early talk of a clear win has faded.

Three ways it could break

A Netanyahu-led government. He would need eight more seats, and none of the obvious sources is easy. Ynet reports that Washington regards a role for Itamar Ben-Gvir, who leads Otzma Yehudit, as unacceptable. It also reports that President Trump may press Netanyahu towards a broader unity government. No such arrangement has been agreed. A unity government would mean luring in some of Eisenkot's partners, such as Bennett or Yisrael Beytenu, whose campaigns were built on removing Netanyahu. In his overnight speech at the Jerusalem convention centre Netanyahu said that "the Zionist majority must come to its senses and unite". That reads as an appeal to those parties and to Eisenkot's voters.

An opposition government that relies on Arab parties. The sums work on paper. Eisenkot's 48 plus the Joint List and Ra'am would be 67. Joint List chairman Yousef Jabareen has said his party is ready to "pave the way for removing the Netanyahu, Ben-Gvir and Smotrich government". But a majority on paper does not make a coalition. Relying on Arab parties, even from outside the cabinet, has long been taboo for most Jewish parties, and some of Eisenkot's own allies are unlikely to accept it. Yair Golan, the Democrats' leader, wrote on X that "Netanyahu lost. I know how to count to 120." Eisenkot said overnight that "the public chose change". Neither statement says how a government would be assembled.

Deadlock and a caretaker government. If neither side reaches 61, the current government stays in office with its formal powers, though it is expected to show restraint on major decisions. Israel has been here before. The March 2020 election, which also left Netanyahu short, was followed by a long stalemate and more elections. A repeat is the outcome many of the country's political analysts regard as the default.

What it means

A prolonged stalemate would leave policy on regional security and foreign relations in the hands of a caretaker administration. It could not take big decisions but would still be in charge. Allies and adversaries would then be negotiating with a government whose future is unknown. The question of Ben-Gvir's role makes this a matter for Washington as well as Jerusalem.

The next fixed points are the end of the double-envelope count on Thursday evening and the further checks next week. After the results are certified, the president consults party leaders and gives one of them the first chance to form a government. Which candidate gets that mandate will show who is regarded as closer to 61. The final count will determine whether Netanyahu's eight-seat gap is a negotiating position or a lasting weakness.
