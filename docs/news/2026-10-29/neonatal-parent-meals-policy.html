Mother of critically ill baby denied hospital food because she wasn't breastfeeding

In one sentence
A Welsh health board's policy of feeding breastfeeding mothers on children's wards left a mother of a critically ill baby unfed for 28 hours, prompting calls for meals for all parents.

A mother whose newborn daughter was critically ill in a Welsh hospital says she was refused a meal because she was not breastfeeding. She has called the rule outdated. The health board says its policy is to feed breastfeeding mothers, and that ward teams can give other parents basic food case by case.

The account comes from BBC Wales, published on 29 October. It has not been independently verified, and the health board's written policy has not been published. What follows rests on the BBC report and the board's statement to it.

What happened

Sophie Price, 22, from Newport, gave birth at Grange University Hospital in Cwmbran, Torfaen. About five days later her daughter, Florence, was on a paediatric ward. Ms Price says she was told she was no longer a patient and would not be given meals, because she was not breastfeeding. She had by then gone about 28 hours without eating.

The report describes a difficult recovery. She was bleeding and in significant pain, had had her hips put back in place after dislocating them during the birth, and had fluid build-up (oedema) in her feet that made walking hard.

Florence was diagnosed with bronchiolitis, rhinovirus and a blood infection. Her lungs could not work unaided, and she was put on a machine that pushed air into them through a mask. Her parents were told she might need to be put into an induced coma at any point. The family spent about three weeks in hospital. Ms Price left for 24 hours, then returned when Florence stopped breathing properly.

Relatives took turns to bring food around their working hours, and the family says it spent more than £1,000 on canteen meals and takeaways. Her husband, Ryli, had paternity leave that was running out during the stay.

The rule

According to the BBC's account of the health board's position, the distinction turns on who counts as the patient. On a maternity ward, mother and baby both do, so meals are provided whether or not the mother breastfeeds. On a paediatric ward only the child is the patient. The board says its policy is to provide meals to breastfeeding mothers there, because this supports the baby's feeding.

That rationale has a logic to it, but it leaves a group uncovered. A mother who is formula feeding, or whose baby is too ill to feed at all, falls outside it, though she may be as tied to the bedside as anyone. Ms Price calls the policy "outdated" and says it discriminates against mothers who formula-feed. She wants hospitals to provide meals to all mothers regardless of how they feed.

Formula was a second point of dispute. The board says it is "provided as needed". Ms Price says staff told her she would have to bring her own. After her mother contacted the hospital, it continued to supply formula for Florence.

The health board's response

Aneurin Bevan University Health Board said it was "sorry to hear about Sophie's experience" and is committed to supporting families. It said ward teams can provide basic food and refreshments where parents cannot reach the canteen, have limited support or cannot leave the bedside. It also pointed to a ward voucher scheme for discounted meals, said financial hardship can be raised with ward staff, and said requests are considered case by case.

The gap between the stated policy and Ms Price's experience is the crux. If the discretion exists, it was not offered here, or not offered clearly. A rule that relies on parents knowing to ask, while they are frightened and exhausted, will reach some families and miss others.

What remains unclear

The BBC report names one health board. The wording of the policy, how often discretion is used and whether other trusts apply similar rules are not established. Earlier complaints of a similar kind have surfaced in patient-feedback posts and local reports in Wales and Scotland, but those are anecdotal and cannot be assumed to reflect current practice.

The next test is whether Aneurin Bevan publishes its catering criteria or revises them, and whether other boards are asked to say how they treat non-breastfeeding parents on children's and neonatal wards.
