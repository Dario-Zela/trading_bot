Ofcom sets out how social media can verify users' ages

In one sentence
Ofcom has published the age-checking methods social media firms must meet under UK online safety rules, naming approaches such as photo ID, facial age estimation and open banking.

Ofcom, the UK communications regulator, has published how social media companies can check that their users are old enough. The document turns a general legal duty under the Online Safety regime into a set of named methods that platforms will be measured against. The first reporting came from the BBC. It gave few specifics, and this article has not seen the full Ofcom text, so the detail below comes from Ofcom's earlier guidance and reporting on its July 2026 report.

What counts as a proper check

Ofcom's test, set out in its existing guidance, is "highly effective age assurance". A method must be technically accurate, robust, reliable and fair. The regulator's non-exhaustive list of methods that can meet that bar includes:

  • photo ID matching, where a user uploads a passport or driving licence and it is compared with an image of their face;
  • facial age estimation, where software estimates age from a face scan;
  • open banking checks, where a bank confirms to the site that the user is over 18 without sharing a full date of birth;
  • credit card checks, mobile network operator checks and digital identity services.

Self-declaration, meaning typing in a birth date, does not count. Neither do payments that do not require the payer to be an adult. Ofcom has also recommended liveness detection, which stops a child holding up a still photo of an adult to fool a camera.

Why the bar is moving

In July, Ofcom's first report on age assurance found that social media firms had not consistently enforced their own minimum ages. Some leaned on age inference, which guesses age from behaviour and is not on Ofcom's list of methods capable of being highly effective. Facial age estimation and photo ID matching were the most common checks in use, and open banking was among the least used. Ofcom concluded that current inference systems are unlikely to support a meaningful ban on under-16s at the point of entry.

The government said in June that it intends to require social media services to use highly effective age assurance to keep under-16s out. Ofcom had promised Parliament, by the end of October, a rapid assessment of what such checks look like in practice for deciding whether a user is over 16. The BBC report appears to be tied to that work, though this could not be confirmed against Ofcom's own page.

What it means for platforms and users

For platforms, the practical effect is that a published list of acceptable methods replaces room for argument. A firm that keeps relying on self-declared ages or inference will struggle to say it has met the standard.

For users, the cost is data. Face scans, ID documents and bank links all mean handing something over at sign-up. Open banking is the least revealing of these, since the bank passes on only an over-18 result. Privacy questions are likely to follow, and the Information Commissioner's Office has already issued a joint statement with Ofcom on age assurance.

The next thing to watch is the detail: whether the assessment sets an accuracy threshold for the over-16 question, and how soon any under-16 restriction applies. Reporting suggests restrictions could take effect in 2027.
