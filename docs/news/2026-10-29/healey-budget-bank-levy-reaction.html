Healey's Budget: £100 energy payment funded by banks and landlords, with a cautious OBR verdict

In one sentence
Healey's Budget doubles the bank surcharge to 6% for 15 months to part-fund a £100 energy payment; banks rallied, gilts barely moved, and the OBR warned of stagnant incomes.

John Healey's first Budget, delivered on 28 October, spreads the cost of a one-off £100 winter energy payment across banks and landlords. The market's verdict was muted. Gilts barely moved, sterling slipped by a tenth of a cent on the dollar, and the three biggest domestic lenders rose. The bank tax went up by less than had been feared, and that, rather than the sum raised, is what traders priced.

The bank surcharge: smaller than feared

The surcharge on bank profits above a set threshold doubles from 3% to 6%, on top of the 25% corporation tax rate. It lasts 15 months. It partly reverses the cut from 8% made three years ago under Rishi Sunak. One report put the yield at about £305m this year and nearly £1bn next year. Those figures come from a market-wire summary and have not been checked against the Treasury's costings.

The pre-Budget fear was larger. Bank shares fell on 1 October as 30-year gilt yields touched their highest since 1998. NatWest dropped 5.2%, Lloyds 4.4% and Barclays nearly 4%, according to Reuters. Mid-month, KBW analysts said a bigger hike could raise £2bn a year and cut about 6% from lenders' earnings. Bloomberg reported on Budget day that the increase would be less than expected. Some in the market had pencilled in 8%.

So the relief was real, though its size depends on the report. Reuters had Lloyds, Barclays and NatWest up between 1.3% and 1.6% in early trade. Later accounts put the gains at roughly 2.5% to 3.6% in the afternoon. Closing levels were not available to this desk. Gary Greenwood of Shore Capital called the measure a "modest and temporary earnings headwind". The word "temporary" matters most for capital returns. A 15-month surcharge dents buyback capacity for a year or so. A permanent one would reset the valuation.

Who pays, and for what

The energy payment is £100 for about 20 million households. One report limits it to council tax bands A to D, at a cost of £2.6bn. The bank surcharge covers only a fraction of that cost. The remainder falls on other measures. The main one is a 2p increase in tax rates on property and savings income. The OBR warned that this "risks leading to a steady rise in rents", by deterring landlords and passing costs to tenants.

The reported 1% squeeze on departmental budgets, meant to help pay for defence, could not be confirmed in the coverage this desk could reach. Nor could the size of the defence increase. Reports say major defence decisions were deferred to next year's spending review, and Kemi Badenoch criticised Healey over the delay. Until the Treasury's figures are public, the 1% squeeze is best read as a stated intention rather than a costed one.

On fuel, Healey cancelled the duty rises Reeves had planned. That is a freeze, not a cut. It is the item low earners are complaining about, since the support went into energy bills instead.

Gilts, sterling and the OBR

The 10-year gilt yield fell about 2 basis points to roughly 5.32%. The 30-year fell about 3.5 basis points to roughly 5.71%. Sterling slipped 0.1% to $1.3295 and 0.2% against the euro, at 83.73p. The FTSE 100 rose 0.1% and the FTSE 250 fell 0.2%. Kathleen Brooks of XTB said the bond market was "calling it a very boring budget". The Debt Management Office's gilt remit was not covered in the reports consulted, so any shift in issuance or maturity mix is unconfirmed.

The forecaster was less calm. The OBR cut next year's growth forecast to 1%, about half a point below March. It sees inflation rising to almost 4%. It expects real household disposable incomes by 2030-31 to be only 2% above pre-Covid levels, describing a "pretty grim picture" with "virtually no growth". Higher rates and inflation add £14bn to this year's debt interest bill.

Headroom against the fiscal rules is £15bn by 2029-30, in line with a Reuters poll but below the £23.6bn–£24bn left by Rachel Reeves in the spring. Healey pledged to rebuild it to £22bn by the end of the forecast. A cushion of £15bn is thin against a £14bn swing in one year's interest costs. That is why the quiet gilt reaction is conditional.

What to watch

The Bank of England is expected to raise rates by 0.25 points to 4% at its meeting next week. With CPI at 3.5% last month and European gas near €100 per MWh, that decision will test the Budget's arithmetic more than the Budget did. The surcharge's 15-month life and the spending review will set the terms for bank payouts and for defence.
