Hack leaks details of thousands of US immigration agents

In one sentence
Records on more than 41,000 DHS staff, including thousands of ICE and CBP personnel, were published online by a group calling itself the Department of Peace, per the BBC.

Records on more than 41,000 staff at the US Department of Homeland Security have been published online, including thousands who work in immigration enforcement. The BBC reported the leak on Wednesday. It turns a data breach into a safety question for officers and their families.

The material includes names, work addresses, phone numbers and descriptions of each person's work. By the BBC's count, more than 21,000 of those listed work for Customs and Border Protection (CBP) and nearly 6,600 for Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE). Other records cover Homeland Security Investigations, the Federal Protective Service and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. The data also names dozens of previously unknown operations, the agents who led or worked on them, and the tools they used, including products from Cellebrite and Palantir.

This account rests on the BBC's reporting as syndicated by Yahoo. The database itself was not examined, and the agency has confirmed only a potential breach.

Who published it, and why

A group calling itself the Department of Peace says it took the information from DHS. The journalist and security researcher maia crimew and the transparency group Distributed Denial of Secrets (DDoSecrets) published it in a searchable database called "Icepick". DDoSecrets previously hosted BlueLeaks, the 2020 release of US police data.

The publishers say it is not a full directory of DHS agents. DDoSecrets co-founder Emma Best said the aim was to replace "anonymity with accountability". Best said the data identifies officials overseeing covert operations, the government's hunt for leakers and personnel in deportation programmes.

The Department of Peace's spokesperson pointed to ICE's conduct and to DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin, who took the job in March. The spokesperson cited his July statement that he was "turning up the heat".

The group said it reached the agent profiles through Okta, the single sign-on service that controls staff logins to internal systems. An Okta spokeswoman said the company knows of the claims but that "Okta is secure". She said it has no evidence its data or systems were compromised. How the intruders actually got in is therefore unresolved.

The safety question

DHS said it is aware of a potential breach and is working to address it. A spokesperson said the department takes threats against employees seriously and will update the public when more information is available. The agency has not said whether it can verify the records.

The exposure matters because of how enforcement work is now done. Agents often work masked and rarely identify themselves, though the law requires it, and politicians have pressed DHS to enforce that rule. ICE agents have shot more than two dozen people this year and killed at least nine, according to the BBC, including Renée Good and Alex Pretti in Minnesota, neither of whom was an arrest target. Anonymity is a safety measure for officers. It is also a point of contention for critics who want those officers identifiable and answerable. The leak lands in the middle of that dispute.

It is also not the first. In January, details of about 4,500 ICE and Border Patrol employees reached the activist site ICE List, which said a DHS whistleblower supplied them. The BBC describes this one as the third such government leak or hack in recent weeks. It cites the ShinyHunters group's breach of the FBI last month, which took personal information on thousands of agents, and a suspected hacking attempt that took the Federal Election Commission offline.

What to watch

Two questions will decide how serious this becomes: whether DHS confirms the records are authentic, and whether it explains the access route, given Okta's denial. The department has promised an update. How far the data spreads beyond the Icepick site, and whether any individuals are targeted as a result, will show whether the safety risks officers fear materialise.
