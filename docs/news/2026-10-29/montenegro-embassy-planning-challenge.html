Neighbour of new Montenegro embassy in London sues council over mental-health impact

In one sentence
A Pimlico resident is taking Westminster council to the High Court, saying it ignored the mental-health impact of the Montenegro embassy next door; the council seeks summary judgment.

A woman who lives next door to Montenegro's new embassy in Pimlico, central London, is taking Westminster City Council to the High Court. She argues it failed to weigh what the embassy's arrival would do to her mental health when it dealt with the building's planning.

Diana Simpson, 35, a Stanford graduate, lives on Cambridge Street with her parents. Her father is a former US diplomat who has lived on the street for more than 30 years. She has applied for judicial review and potential damages, according to the Guardian's report, which was syndicated by AOL. Mr Justice Mould is hearing the case.

A family home, then an embassy

The embassy has moved into a five-bedroom family home on the street. The change of use, and the flagpole that came with it, were in place before planning permission was sought. Residents objected to the flag and a plaque. The Montenegrin chargé d'affaires responded by hosting drinks for neighbours, which, by the account of the case, did not settle every concern.

Simpson receives disability benefits for clinical anxiety, long Covid and a functional neurological disorder. She says her sleep is regularly disrupted and that she sometimes moves to a different room. She describes waking with a fear of intruders from the embassy, which she calls "mostly irrational". She has considered whether she can stay in the house, which she had expected to be a long-term home.

The legal grounds

Her claim rests on several points. She says the council breached human rights and equality law. She also says it acted irrationally and ran a flawed consultation, and that it approached objections with a closed mind once the Foreign Office had given its permission.

Westminster denies the allegations. It says there is "no plausible interference" with her home or her private and family life, and it is seeking summary judgment, which would end the case without a full hearing. A council spokesperson said councils must decide in line with planning policy and declined further comment at this stage.

The claim tests an unusual question for planning law: how far a decision-maker must consider an individual neighbour's health, as opposed to the usual planning concerns of noise, traffic and visual impact. The summary-judgment application suggests the council thinks the answer is "not here".

Not the first embassy row

London's embassies often sit in converted houses, and neighbours do not always welcome them. Turkmenistan's embassy in an adapted family home on Victoria Road, Kensington, drew complaints about consultation and courtesy. North Korea has run an embassy for about two decades in a seven-bedroom 1920s house in Ealing, where neighbours report no problems.

I could not see the court filings or the planning file. The date of any hearing on the summary-judgment application is not given in the reporting. If the court grants that application, the claim ends early. If it refuses, the council will have to defend its handling of the application in full.
