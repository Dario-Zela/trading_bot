Russell Brand tells trial it was wrong to use fame to 'facilitate' sex

In one sentence
Russell Brand, who denies all charges, reportedly told his trial it was wrong to use fame to 'facilitate' sex, though the wording awaits checking against the court record.

Russell Brand, the comedian and former broadcaster, has told the jury at his criminal trial that it was wrong to use his fame to "facilitate" sex, according to the Guardian's report of his evidence. He denies every charge against him.

The remark is a notable one in a courtroom. Defendants rarely volunteer moral concessions while contesting allegations of rape and sexual assault. The line between conduct a witness regards as regrettable and conduct the law treats as criminal is the one a jury will be asked to draw.

This article rests on the Guardian's account of the testimony. The exact wording, and the question that prompted it, have not been checked against a court transcript. The headline's phrasing is the newspaper's, not a verified quotation.

The case

Brand was charged in April 2025 with rape and sexual assault offences involving four women. The alleged offences date from roughly 1999 to 2005, the years when he was building a career across stand-up, radio and television. He has consistently denied the allegations, and he is presumed innocent.

The trial was listed at Southwark Crown Court for 2026, with a start expected in June. Whether proceedings are at the stage of his own evidence, as the Guardian's report implies, is for the court record to establish. Nothing here should be read as a finding on any count.

Fame and consent

What gives the testimony its wider interest is the subject it touches: whether celebrity, as a form of power, shapes encounters that are later disputed. That question has run through the entertainment industry's reckoning of recent years, in courts, in newsroom investigations and in workplace policy.

A defendant describing fame as something he used, and saying that was wrong, does not settle that debate. In law, conduct that is morally questionable is not the same as an offence. The jury will be directed on that distinction, and on the standard of proof, when the evidence closes. Admissions of poor behaviour can be used by either side: a prosecution may argue they show a pattern, while a defence may argue they show candour.

The case changes no law and moves no market. Its reach is to readers of culture and legal news, and to a long-running argument about influence in show business.

What to watch

The next markers are the remainder of the cross-examination, the closing speeches and the judge's summing-up. The jury's verdicts on each count, delivered separately, will decide the matter. Reporting restrictions and the court's own timetable govern what can be published until then.
