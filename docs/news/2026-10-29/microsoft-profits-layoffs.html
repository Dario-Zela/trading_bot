Microsoft profits soar days after announcing thousands of layoffs

In one sentence
Microsoft reported a $36.6bn quarterly profit days after offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 staff, as it balances heavy AI spending against costs.

Microsoft told roughly 60,000 employees on Monday that they could take a voluntary buyout. Two days later it reported a quarterly profit of more than $36.6bn (£27.5bn). The two announcements are separate events, but together they show a company that is booking record earnings while shrinking its payroll to pay for artificial intelligence.

The headline calls the departures "layoffs". The detail matters, because what Microsoft has described so far is an offer, not a dismissal notice. Per the BBC's report, which is the main source for the figures here, the company said it would offer voluntary buyouts to eligible staff in teams across Canada, India, the UK and the US. It framed the move as a way to adapt its workforce to "changing needs" and to create "greater flexibility". The BBC says the plan could cut more than a quarter of the workforce. Microsoft employs more than 220,000 people worldwide, so 60,000 is about 27%. How many will accept, and whether compulsory cuts follow, has not been reported.

The quarter

The results cover the three months to the end of September. Revenue was $92.3bn, up 19% on a year earlier, and profit exceeded $36.6bn. Microsoft Cloud revenue rose 30%. Shares rose almost 2.9% in after-hours trading in New York on Wednesday.

Chief executive Satya Nadella said the development of AI had increased adoption and was delivering "real-world impact across every industry". The reported figures do not include a breakdown of how much of the growth came from AI products and how much from the cloud business that carried it before.

The reports I could find also give no capital-spending figure for the quarter. Earlier in the year, Microsoft's guidance for capital expenditure was about $50bn for the September quarter and $175bn for calendar 2026. That guidance was lowered after the company changed how it accounts for long-term data-centre leases. The actual spend for the quarter was not in the coverage reviewed, and it is the number that would test the argument below.

Why the timing invites a conclusion

The tempting reading is that Microsoft is cutting staff to fund AI. The evidence for it is circumstantial, and it is worth separating what is known from what is inferred.

What is known: the company has pointed to costs and to heavy AI investment as the context for the buyouts. The BBC reports that Microsoft is offering them "to cut costs as it pours billions into artificial intelligence". The BBC also notes that Amazon, Meta and Alphabet are expected to spend more than $700bn between them this year on AI chips and infrastructure such as data centres.

What is inferred: that the savings from fewer staff are sized to offset that spending. Microsoft has not said so, and a profit of this size means it is not cutting out of necessity. A company earning $36.6bn in a quarter can fund data centres without trimming payroll. If the cuts are a choice, the more likely logic is margin protection. Investors have to be shown that AI capital spending will not erode profitability, and a smaller wage bill is one way to show it.

The contrast with Alphabet, which reported in the same week, is useful. Its shares fell 9.75% after quarterly net profit dropped 25% year on year, and it said it would keep spending heavily on AI. Investors rewarded Microsoft for profit growth and punished Alphabet for profit decline, though both are spending on the same buildout. That suggests the market is less worried about the size of AI budgets than about whether earnings hold up beside them.

What it means for workers and the sector

For employees, a buyout is a different proposition from a layoff. It lets staff choose to leave with financial support and career-planning help, in the company's description, but a buyout offered to a quarter of the workforce tends to signal that headcount is expected to fall either way. Staff who decline may not be insulated from later, compulsory rounds.

For the wider tech labour market, the signal is that strong results no longer protect jobs. Earlier Microsoft rounds, such as the roughly 9,000 cuts announced in July 2025, also came while the company was profitable. This one is far larger if take-up is high.

Three things to watch next. First, Microsoft's filing for the quarter should show capital expenditure and any restructuring charge, which would put a cost on the buyout programme. Second, the company will need to disclose how many accept. Third, the next set of results, due in late January, should show whether operating margins improve as headcount falls while AI spending keeps climbing.
