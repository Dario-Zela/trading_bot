Boeing, Airbus and airlines press Congress for $20bn air traffic control upgrade

The FAA control tower at Denver International Airport, with a United Airlines jet on the tarmac below. — Bmurphy380 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A Boeing-Airbus-airline coalition is asking Congress for $20 billion more to finish rebuilding an air traffic control system now costing aviation an estimated $34 billion a year in delays.

Boeing and Airbus do not agree on much. As the world's two dominant planemakers, they spend most of their energy trying to take market share from one another. But on July 15, both companies signed the same letter to Congress, alongside American Airlines, United Airlines, dozens of airports, pilot unions and general-aviation groups, asking for $20 billion more to finish rebuilding the country's air traffic control system.

"Our nation's aviation system is a key national asset, but it is under increasing strain."

That is the kind of coalition that does not assemble over a routine ask. Airlines for America, the trade group representing American and United among others, organized the letter alongside Boeing, Airbus, the U.S. Travel Association, the Air Line Pilots Association and more than 50 other organizations — a rare alignment of manufacturers, carriers, labor and airports that usually spend their lobbying dollars pointing in different directions.

In one sentence: A Boeing-Airbus-airline coalition is asking Congress for $20 billion more to finish rebuilding an air traffic control system that is now costing aviation an estimated $34 billion a year in delays.

What the money buys

The FAA's current modernization plan, unveiled by Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy last year, calls for replacing or upgrading hundreds of aging air traffic control facilities and deploying new hardware for controllers who, in some towers, are still working from equipment installed in the 1980s. By the end of 2028 the agency wants 5,000 new high-speed network connections — fiber, satellite and wireless — installed nationwide, along with 27,000 new radios and 612 new radar systems.

Congress has already put money behind the effort once. Lawmakers approved $12.5 billion last year as a down payment, and the bill signed into law on February 4 formally authorized the modernization program while also barring any move toward privatizing air traffic control — a long-running fight in Washington that flared again as the program took shape. Duffy has said the Department of Transportation's full ask was closer to $19 billion, and in April he told Reuters he was seeking $10 billion for the program's next phase, with the remainder to be requested from Congress later. The coalition's $20 billion letter effectively asks lawmakers to close that gap in one appropriation rather than string it out.

Why the industry is suddenly aligned

Two events in the past 15 months turned modernization from a budget line item into a political imperative. In the spring of 2025, a series of telecommunications failures at Newark Liberty International Airport knocked out radar and radio contact between controllers and aircraft for stretches of several seconds — enough to trigger ground stops, mass cancellations and a public reckoning over how much of the system still runs on copper wiring. Then, on March 22 this year, an Air Canada Express regional jet landing at LaGuardia collided with an airport fire truck that was not carrying a transponder — a basic tracking device the FAA's ground-radar system needed to see it. Both pilots were killed, and the airport shut down for about 14 hours. The National Transportation Safety Board's investigation is ongoing, but the incident put a fatal, concrete face on what had been an abstract infrastructure argument.

Underneath both incidents sits a staffing problem the money alone will not fix. The FAA had roughly 10,800 fully certified controllers as of June, against a staffing target of 12,563 — itself a figure the agency has already trimmed down from an earlier 14,633 estimate. The shortage is not evenly distributed: it is acute enough at Chicago O'Hare that the FAA capped scheduled flights there at 2,708 a day, a cut of nearly 15% from what airlines had planned, running from mid-May through late October. Duffy says the department has hired 2,400 controllers since March, 20% more than the equivalent stretch of 2025, but new hires take two to three years to reach full certification.

The margin case

For an equity desk, the interesting number is not $20 billion — it is the estimated $34 billion a year that flight delays and cancellations are now draining from the aviation sector and the wider economy, according to industry-cited figures circulating alongside the coalition letter. That drag shows up in airline income statements as crew overtime, aircraft repositioning costs and idle jets sitting at outstations instead of flying revenue-generating hours — the kind of utilization erosion that shows up in cost-per-available-seat-mile long before it shows up in a headline delay statistic. For Boeing and Airbus, a gridlocked system caps how many flights the existing fleet can fly, which indirectly caps how urgently airlines need to take new aircraft deliveries.

What the letter does not settle is whether Congress will actually write the check. The $12.5 billion approved last year came attached to a broader legislative vehicle, and there is no equivalent bill currently moving that could carry $20 billion without a dedicated appropriations fight. The next marker to watch is whether the fiscal year 2027 transportation appropriations process, which typically takes shape in the fall, includes a specific FAA modernization line — and whether Duffy's department formalizes the $10 billion "next phase" request he floated in April into an actual budget submission. Until one of those materializes, the industry's unusual show of unity is a lobbying position, not a funding commitment.
