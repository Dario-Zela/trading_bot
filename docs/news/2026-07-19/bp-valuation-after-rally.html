BP shares questioned as 'too expensive' after 133% rally

BP's head office — the company suspended share buybacks in February 2026 to prioritise balance sheet repair over shareholder distributions. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP's shares have rallied roughly 133% over five years, but a rich P/E, a suspended buyback and a lawsuit are testing whether the re-rating is earned.

BP shares have gone from a byword for value-trap to one of the better trades in European energy — a five-year total return, dividends included, of roughly 133%. The rally has been driven by a post-pandemic oil and gas price recovery, a 2025 cost-cutting reset under pressure from activist investor Elliott Management, and a run of upstream project start-ups in the Gulf of America, Brazil and the UAE. The question now circulating on Simply Wall St and among sell-side desks is whether the re-rating has outrun the fundamentals.

On a trailing basis, BP trades on a price-to-earnings ratio of around 31.6x — against an industry average near 13.6x, a peer average of 11.8x, and Simply Wall St's own modelled "fair" multiple of 23.3x. By that screen, BP "now screens as overvalued on earnings, with the current P/E sitting well above both industry and peer averages as well as its own modelled fair ratio," the site's analysis concluded, adding that the premium "leaves less margin for error if the gas growth projects or cost discipline do not progress as hoped."

Some of that gap is optical. Recent earnings have been distorted by impairments and one-off charges, so the trailing multiple looks richer than the forward one — analyst consensus puts BP's forward P/E closer to 10-11x, in line with Shell's roughly 7-8x once next year's expected earnings recovery is factored in. That gap between trailing and forward multiples is itself part of the argument: bulls say the market is pricing a normalised earnings run-rate; skeptics say it is pricing a recovery that still has to show up in the numbers.

The dividend question

The more concrete test is capital returns. In February, BP's board suspended its share buyback programme — which had already been trimmed from $1.75bn to $750m a quarter under former chief executive Murray Auchincloss's strategy reset — and retired its guidance of returning 30-40% of operating cash flow to shareholders, diverting cash instead to cutting net debt toward a $14bn-$18bn target by 2027. The quarterly dividend itself was left untouched, with management reiterating plans to grow it roughly 4% a year, and at a 2026 forecast yield near 5.5% BP still pays more than Shell's projected 4.2%. Dividend cover from free cash flow remains comfortable, even if cover from reported earnings looks thin — a distortion common to oil majors carrying heavy non-cash charges, not evidence of an imminent cut.

A separate legal overhang is worth flagging rather than dismissing: BP is among several retailers, alongside Marathon Petroleum, Walmart and 7-Eleven, named in a California federal lawsuit filed in June alleging algorithmic fuel-pricing software let stations coordinate prices — a novel test of the state's new algorithmic price-fixing statute, with limited earnings impact but real reputational risk.

The next hard data point arrives on August 4, when BP reports second-quarter results. Management has already flagged a $2.1bn-$2.4bn earnings boost from stronger oil, gas and refining margins, offset by roughly $1bn of lower-carbon impairments and a seasonal dip in upstream output. That mix — real cash flow against continued write-downs — is likely to be the next skirmish in the valuation argument.
