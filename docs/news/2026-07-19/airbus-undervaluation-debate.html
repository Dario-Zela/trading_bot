Airbus undervaluation debate revived by 'quantum' ground lease

An Airbus A350, part of the widebody family whose delivery ramp underpins the backlog side of the valuation debate. — AlexanderCraker / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Simply Wall St's model flags Airbus as 30-40% undervalued, but a 52% EBIT drop and engine-supply strain complicate the bargain case ahead of July 29 earnings.

Run Airbus through a discounted cash flow model and the answer looks almost too easy: buy. Simply Wall St's valuation tool puts the European planemaker's fair value near €281 a share against a market price hovering around €193-194 — a gap of roughly 30 to 40 percent, depending on which day's inputs you catch. On a price-to-earnings basis the story rhymes: Airbus trades at 30.3 times earnings versus a model-derived "fair" multiple of 35.06 times. Both screens say cheap. Neither says why the market disagrees.

That disagreement is the actual story. Airbus enters its next earnings report, due July 29, with a balance sheet and delivery record that read more like a company mid-stumble than a bargain sitting in plain sight. First-quarter 2026 revenue fell 7% to €12.7 billion and adjusted EBIT was cut in half, to €300 million, as Pratt & Whitney's inability to commit to promised engine volumes squeezed the ramp-up of narrowbody output. Net cash fell 19% in three months, to €9.8 billion from €12.2 billion at the end of 2025. Bears cite tighter climate regulation and a fragile, consolidating supplier base as reasons margin recovery could stall even as volumes climb.

The backlog says something different

Set against that is a demand picture that has barely wobbled. Airbus closed the first quarter with a commercial backlog of 9,031 aircraft — north of ten years of production at the 870-jet delivery target management has set for 2026 — and second-quarter deliveries jumped 39% year-on-year to 237. Full-year 2025 profit margin actually improved, to 7.1% from 6.1%, on net income up 23% to €5.22 billion. Seventeen of 25 analysts covering the stock rate it Buy or equivalent; consensus 12-month targets cluster near €210, itself a premium to today's price. None of this resolves the valuation-model gap so much as explains it: the market is pricing in execution risk on engines and margins that a backward-looking DCF doesn't fully capture, while the model is pricing in a demand cycle the bears aren't disputing.

The 'quantum' distraction

Into that debate landed a headline about Airbus signing a "quantum" ground lease — language that reads like marketing gloss but describes something concrete. On July 15, Airbus Netherlands and satellite operator SES signed a ground lease with the municipality of Noordwijk, next to the European Space Agency's ESTEC campus, to build an optical ground station for SES's EAGLE-1 satellite — a genuine quantum key distribution demonstrator, not a branding exercise. It has no bearing on Airbus's aircraft margins or backlog, and its satellite component has already slipped to late 2027. Investors chasing the word "quantum" into the undervaluation debate are chasing a coincidence of timing, not a new data point.

The real test arrives with second-quarter results on July 29, when Airbus updates guidance on engine supply and margin trajectory. Until then, the valuation gap is a bet on whether the backlog outruns the supply chain — not a verdict.
