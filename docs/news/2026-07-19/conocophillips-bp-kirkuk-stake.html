ConocoPhillips buys 42% stake in BP's Kirkuk oil fields in Iraq

Oil field infrastructure at Kirkuk, northern Iraq, where ConocoPhillips has bought into BP's redevelopment contract. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
ConocoPhillips is paying to co-own 42% of BP's redevelopment contract at Iraq's century-old, historically contested Kirkuk oil field.

ConocoPhillips is buying its way back into Iraq, and it is doing so at one of the country's oldest and most politically fraught oil fields. The Houston-based producer has agreed to acquire a 42% stake in BP Energy Company of Kirkuk Ltd, the vehicle BP created last year to redevelop the Kirkuk complex in northern Iraq, according to statements from both companies. Financial terms were not disclosed, but the fields involved hold more than 3 billion barrels of oil equivalent in initial recoverable resources, with BP putting the field's total potential closer to 9 billion barrels.

The agreement was struck to coincide with Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi's visit to Washington this week, where he has been pitching American companies on Iraq's oil, gas and power sectors. For ConocoPhillips, it marks a return to a country the company exited more than a decade ago. For BP, it is the latest step in a broader retreat from projects it can no longer fund alone.

What ConocoPhillips is actually buying

The deal covers the Baba and Avanah domes of the Kirkuk field itself, plus the nearby Bai Hassan, Jambur and Khabbaz fields — collectively one of the largest redevelopment contracts BP has signed in the Middle East. BP's original agreement with Iraq's state-owned North Oil Company and North Gas Company took effect in October 2025, with an initial baseline production target of 328,000 barrels a day. A senior Iraqi oil official told Reuters earlier this year that BP could spend up to $25 billion over the life of the project.

Importantly, Iraq's state companies remain the formal operators — the redevelopment is structured as a contract, not a concession, with operatorship eventually passing to an entity staffed mainly by Iraqi personnel. What ConocoPhillips has bought is a substantial economic interest and a seat alongside BP in steering that redevelopment, not outright control of the asset. BP's own account of the arrangement notes the joint venture is not expected to require significant capital contributions from ConocoPhillips up front, which suggests the entry price was structured to reward BP for work already done rather than fund new spending.

Ryan Lance, ConocoPhillips' chief executive, framed the move in the company's usual language of capital discipline: "This unique redevelopment opportunity is well aligned with our disciplined investment framework, providing access to a material, high-quality and long-life resource base, comfortably meeting our cost of supply threshold." BP chief executive Meg O'Neill, who took over from ExxonMobil in April 2025, called Kirkuk a "world-class resource base." Investors read the announcement as a plus for ConocoPhillips: its shares rose roughly 2% on the day, against a declining S&P 500.

A field that has changed hands by force, not just by contract

Kirkuk is not a neutral piece of geology. Discovered at Baba Gurgur in 1927 and brought into production in 1934 by the Iraq Petroleum Company, it has been producing for almost a century and still accounts for a significant share of northern Iraq's output. It is also one of the country's most contested pieces of territory. Kurdish forces seized the Kirkuk and Bai Hassan fields in July 2014 amid the chaos of the Islamic State's advance, and held them for three years before Iraqi government forces retook the area in October 2017, in the aftermath of the Kurdistan Regional Government's independence referendum. Kirkuk's status was left unresolved by Iraq's 2005 constitution, which promised a referendum on the province under Article 140 that has never been held.

That history is precisely why the scale of this bet matters. BP's original contract, and now ConocoPhillips' entry into it, presumes that Baghdad's hold on Kirkuk — reasserted only in 2017 — will hold for the twenty- or thirty-year horizon that oilfield redevelopment requires. It is a wager on Iraqi state capacity as much as it is a wager on geology.

Why now, and why BP is the seller

The timing points to two separate forces converging. The first is American strategy: Washington has been encouraging investment in Iraqi energy infrastructure to reduce Baghdad's dependence on Iranian gas and electricity imports, a push that has taken on new urgency after hostilities between the United States and Iran earlier this year raised fears about disruption to the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of global oil shipments pass. Al-Zaidi's Washington trip, and the deal's timing around it, reads as Baghdad demonstrating it can attract American capital rather than Chinese.

The second is BP's own balance sheet. O'Neill has spent her first year simplifying BP's portfolio, tightening capital discipline and recycling cash through asset sales and selective partnerships rather than shouldering full project costs alone — a marked change from the expansive ambitions BP signaled when it first won the Kirkuk contract. Selling down 42% of a $25 billion, multi-decade commitment fits that pattern: BP keeps its foothold and its operating role, while offloading a large share of the capital exposure to a partner with its own Middle East ambitions.

What to watch

The deal is expected to close by the end of 2026, subject to regulatory approvals. The two companies have not disclosed a purchase price, which will likely surface in ConocoPhillips' next quarterly filings. Beyond the transaction itself, the number worth tracking is production: BP's baseline target of 328,000 barrels a day at Kirkuk is a modest fraction of the field's estimated 9-billion-barrel potential, and how quickly that ramps up will say more about Iraq's political durability than any press release could.
