ARK Innovation ETF keeps bleeding assets as investor patience wears thin

Cathie Wood, founder and CEO of ARK Invest, whose flagship ARKK fund has shrunk to roughly a quarter of its 2021 peak assets. — Caroline Wood / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
ARKK is seeing sustained outflows amid waning investor confidence in Cathie Wood's flagship fund.

Cathie Wood's ARK Innovation ETF has spent 2026 doing what it has done in most years since the 2021 peak: losing money for shareholders while losing shareholders' money outright. The fund, ticker ARKK, has shed roughly $313 million in net outflows so far this year, according to fund-flow data reported by Benzinga, pushing assets under management down to about $7.2 billion. At its high-water mark in early 2021, ARKK held close to $29 billion.

In one sentence: ARKK is seeing sustained outflows amid waning investor confidence in Cathie Wood's flagship fund.

The outflows are happening despite a year in which most of Wall Street has had little to complain about. U.S.-listed ETFs have pulled in more than $1 trillion combined in 2026. ARKK investors, by contrast, are heading for the exits — and the reason is not hard to find.

The gap that won't close

Year to date, ARKK is up roughly 2%. The Nasdaq 100 is up about 20%. The S&P 500 has gained around 9%. That is not a bad year turned into a mediocre one by a rounding error — it is a fund built to outperform growth benchmarks trailing them by high single digits to high teens, in a year when growth stocks have broadly worked.

Zoom out and the picture gets starker. Over five years, ARKK is down roughly 31%, versus a gain of about 116% for the Nasdaq 100 and 80% for the S&P 500. An investor who put money into ARKK at the depths of the pandemic-era enthusiasm and simply held has lost money in nominal terms while the indexes most comparable in spirit — heavy on technology, oriented toward growth — nearly doubled or better.

The fee structure compounds the problem. ARKK charges a 0.75% expense ratio for active stock-picking; Vanguard's S&P 500 fund charges 0.03%, and Invesco's Nasdaq-100 vehicle charges 0.015%. Investors have been paying twenty-five to fifty times more for a strategy that has, for half a decade running, delivered materially less.

Wood keeps buying anyway

None of this has visibly shaken Wood's own conviction. ARK's trading disclosures show the firm has been adding aggressively to Tesla through the second and third quarters — purchases including roughly $27.8 million in early April, further tranches of $14 million and $11.4 million days later, and another $41.2 million tranche of nearly 97,000 shares on July 2. ARK's published valuation model still targets $2,600 a share for Tesla by 2029, built around a bet that the company's robotaxi ambitions and its Full Self-Driving data trove — more than 1.3 billion cumulative miles, which ARK pegs at roughly 110 times the data-accumulation rate of Waymo — will eventually be repriced by the market. Wood has also directed the firm into SpaceX ahead of its IPO, buying more than $300 million worth of shares even as the private valuation has pulled back about 18% from its highs, and has added to positions in Palantir, Cerebras and Alphabet while trimming names like Roku and Alibaba.

The strategy is consistent with the one that made ARK famous: concentrated, high-conviction bets on a handful of disruptive themes, held through volatility on the premise that the market is mispricing long-duration technology. It is the same approach that produced ARKK's blockbuster 2020, when the fund returned over 150%. The trouble is that conviction investing only works as a business model if enough investors share the conviction long enough to ride out the drawdowns — and ARKK's flow data suggests that patience is thinning.

What it signals beyond one fund

The outflows matter past ARKK's balance sheet. Active management has spent years losing ground to passive index funds on the argument that stock-pickers can't reliably beat the market after fees; ARKK was supposed to be the counter-example, a fund whose manager's public profile and thematic focus could draw and hold assets even through rough patches. Instead it has become something closer to a cautionary tale — a reminder that a five-year record of underperformance is a hard thing for a story, however compelling, to outrun.

The next real test lands with Tesla's and SpaceX-adjacent earnings later this year, along with whatever comes of the SpaceX IPO process ARK has been positioning around. If Wood's autonomy and space bets start showing up in ARKK's returns rather than just its trading disclosures, the outflow trend could stabilize. If not, the fund's asset base — already less than a quarter of its 2021 peak — has further room to shrink.
