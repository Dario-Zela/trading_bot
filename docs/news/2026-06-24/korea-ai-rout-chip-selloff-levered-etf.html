Korea's AI rout sends chip stocks into freefall and puts the leveraged-ETF boom on trial

DDR4 memory modules of the type manufactured by SK Hynix and Samsung — the products at the centre of Tuesday's global semiconductor sell-off. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Korea's KOSPI fell 10% from its 2026 peak, dragging Nasdaq chip names down as much as 11% on Tuesday while the Dow stayed green on defensive rotation.

Tuesday's closing numbers on Wall Street offered a deceptive calm. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 0.2%, gaining roughly 118 points to end at 51,831. The Nasdaq 100 lost 2.4% — nearly 739 points — closing at 29,608. The S&P 500 fell 0.9% to 7,403. Those are very different stories wearing the same headline. Between them lies the shape of what the market is actually pricing.

The divergence traced back twelve time zones.

The Korea Catalyst

South Korea's KOSPI index dropped sharply on Monday, circuit breakers halting trading as the index breached thresholds set precisely for this kind of concentrated selling. It was the fourth circuit-breaker trigger of 2026, against none in all of 2025. By the session's end, the benchmark sat roughly 10% below its 2026 record high — with most of the damage concentrated in just two names.

Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix — which together account for nearly half the KOSPI's total market capitalisation and an estimated 70% of its 2026 gains — fell more than 12% each. Japan's Nikkei dropped 3.6%. Hong Kong's Hang Seng fell 1.8%. Kioxia, the Japanese memory-chip maker, tumbled more than 15%. When US markets opened Tuesday, the memo had arrived.

The proximate trigger was a report suggesting SK Hynix is shifting a portion of its DRAM capacity away from high-bandwidth memory — the AI data-centre variant that Nvidia's GPU clusters demand in enormous quantities — toward conventional general-purpose DRAM. If confirmed, that pivot would undercut one of the foundational assumptions of the chip-sector bull case: that AI infrastructure investment, with its insatiable appetite for specialised memory, would sustain demand through any macroeconomic headwind.

The rumour has not been confirmed by the company. That it moved markets this sharply is itself data.

In the US, the selling was direct and fast. Micron fell 9.5%, SanDisk 11.5%, ON Semiconductor and Corning each shed 8.9%, and Lam Research dropped 8.8%. Alphabet declined roughly 5%. Nvidia — which has spent most of 2026 trading as a proxy for AI capital spending — retreated 3.2%. The DRAM-focused ETF lost 12.6% on the session.

The Leveraged-ETF Amplifier

To understand the speed and severity of the losses, it helps to know what is sitting underneath chip-stock positions.

Global leveraged ETF assets stand at roughly $290 billion, of which $220 billion is in US-domiciled products. Over the ten sessions through Tuesday, US leveraged ETFs averaged about $20 billion per day in rebalancing activity — four times their one-year average. Nomura strategist Charlie McElligott estimates that for every 1% move in the underlying market, leveraged products generate approximately $9 billion in mechanical rebalancing demand. That is a structural amplifier, pushing prices further in whichever direction they are already moving.

South Korea has its own version of the same problem. A chipmaker-focused leveraged ETF launched there in May grew from $3 billion to $9 billion in assets in under two months. The country's top financial regulator this week said publicly it regrets having approved single-stock leveraged products. That statement, delivered during an active sell-off, did not calm sellers.

In the US, Direxion's Semiconductor Bull 3× ETF, ticker SOXL, was indicated down 19.7% in pre-market Tuesday. The product had risen roughly 291% year-to-date through late May, making it a retail favourite. It had also fallen 30.5% on June 5 when Broadcom's AI chip guidance came in roughly 7% below analyst estimates — a data point that many investors treated as a buying opportunity rather than a warning.

"Levered ETFs remain the biggest technical risk to this market, regardless of your fundamental assessment."
— Alexander Altmann, Barclays Equities Tactical Strategies

The arithmetic is not complicated: a 3× leveraged product that loses 30% in a session needs its underlying to gain more than 10% just to recover the ground. Volatility compounds the math against the holder even when the underlying ends flat — a structural decay that is well-documented but repeatedly surprises investors who buy in on momentum.

Where the Money Went

The Dow's muted gain tells you where some of it landed. Cloudflare rose 5%. ServiceNow gained 4%. Cybersecurity firm Rubrik advanced 6%. None of these companies are AI-absent, but none depend on semiconductor throughput as a direct revenue variable. The Russell 2000 fell 0.7%, indicating the rotation was selective: toward revenue-diversified software and security names, not a blanket defensive embrace.

A secondary thread complicates the chip sector's longer-term demand picture. Analysts have begun tracking a corporate shift from what they describe as "token maximisation" — deploying as much AI compute as budget permits — toward "token minimisation," substituting cheaper open-source or Chinese-developed models where performance gaps allow. Early cost comparisons peg top US model usage at roughly eight times the cost of the cheapest Chinese alternatives. If that calculus spreads through procurement decisions at scale, the upstream demand assumptions embedded in chip-stock valuations need revisiting.

Oracle's announcement this week of 21,000 layoffs, and cautionary comments from SoftBank's chief executive on AI infrastructure returns, added texture to the concern. Neither is decisive. Together they amount to more questions about the AI spending thesis than were circulating six months ago.

What to Watch

The most direct near-term test is Micron's third-quarter earnings, due later this month. The stock entered Tuesday near multi-year highs, and at least one sell-side analyst had already recommended trimming ahead of the print. Guidance on memory mix — specifically the proportion of high-bandwidth memory versus standard DRAM in forward orders — will tell the market whether Tuesday's South Korea sell-off was a false signal or a genuine inflection read.

SK Hynix has not confirmed the capacity-shift report that ignited the session. When it speaks — in a regulatory filing, an investor call, or a management statement — that will be the data point that resolves the ambiguity.

Until then, watch leveraged-ETF rebalancing volumes. At four times their historical average, the mechanical amplification of both upward and downward moves is running near its highest level of the year. A sector that corrects under those conditions corrects faster and deeper than the fundamentals alone would suggest. The bifurcation between the Dow and the Nasdaq on Tuesday was a symptom of that dynamic. Whether it marks a tactical air-pocket or the opening of a real cycle turn is the question the next few earnings prints will answer.
