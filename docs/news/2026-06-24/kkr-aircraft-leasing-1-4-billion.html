KKR commits $1.4bn to aircraft leasing

An Airbus A330-300 on approach — the widebody type at the centre of KKR and Altavair's passenger-to-freighter conversion strategy. — Masakatsu Ukon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
KKR's third aircraft-leasing portfolio with Altavair adds $1.4bn in equity, pushing the duo's eight-year aviation commitment past $8bn as tight jet supply lifts lessor returns.

Private equity giant KKR has committed another $1.4 billion in equity to its commercial aircraft leasing operation, the latest move in a near-decade partnership with Altavair that has quietly turned the New York-based alternative asset manager into one of aviation finance's larger players.

The announcement, disclosed June 17, marks the third dedicated aircraft-leasing portfolio the two firms have constructed together. Capital is drawn primarily from KKR's Infrastructure and Asset-Based Finance strategies — two sleeves that have grown steadily more central to the firm's private credit buildout and reflect a wider PE industry preference for hard, income-generating assets over equity exposure in an uncertain macro environment.

Eight billion and counting

The headline number is large; the cumulative figure is larger. Since KKR and Altavair struck their strategic partnership in 2018, KKR-managed funds have committed more than $8 billion to aircraft leasing and lending. The joint operation has acquired 188 aircraft and engine assets, placing them with 67 passenger and cargo operators worldwide. KKR's total aviation exposure — which includes AV AirFinance, Atlantic Aviation, KKR DVB Aviation Capital, and K2 Aviation — now exceeds $12 billion since 2015.

Brandon Freiman, KKR's head of North American infrastructure, framed the scale as confirmation rather than ambition: "Nearly a decade of strategic partnership with Altavair has deepened our conviction in the attractiveness of aircraft leasing, which we believe is poised to grow even further." Daniel Pietrzak, who heads KKR's global private credit operation, credited the structural fit: "The success of our strategic partnership is a testament to the power of combining our patient, long-term capital with Altavair's deep industry expertise."

The investment thesis is durable, if not glamorous. Airlines — particularly in emerging and mid-tier markets — increasingly prefer not to own aircraft outright. Balance-sheet pressure, hedging costs, and the operational flexibility that leasing provides all push demand toward lessors. Persistent production backlogs at Airbus and Boeing have kept used-aircraft values elevated, thinned supply, and broadly improved returns for well-managed platforms. That congestion in the new-build pipeline may ease, eventually, but not imminently.

Altavair, founded in 2003 with offices in Seattle, London, Dublin, and Singapore, brings transaction depth across the aviation cycle. The firm has completed more than $14.5 billion in leasing deals and managed 33 passenger-to-freighter conversions, specialising in the Airbus A330P2F platform. The most recent high-profile cargo transaction — a fleet of 10 A330-300P2F aircraft placed with Amazon Air — was acquired by IAT Leasing in March 2026, illustrating what exit liquidity looks like when the structure matures.

The preceding portfolio, the second the two firms assembled, supported a $582.9 million securitisation in February 2025, backed by 24 passenger aircraft. That deal validated the collateral quality for capital markets and sketched the recycling path KKR intends to run through the new vehicle.

The primary near-term watch point: whether airline demand for lease capacity — and the premium pricing that supply tightness currently commands — holds as new-build delivery schedules at the two manufacturers eventually lengthen. Any meaningful acceleration in Airbus or Boeing output would shift the balance. Until then, the economics favour lessors.
