US Senate votes to strip Trump of unilateral authority to strike Iran

The United States Capitol, where the Senate voted 50-48 to direct the withdrawal of American forces from the Iran conflict. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Senate voted 50-48 to order Trump out of Iran — a historic rebuke that collapses on contact with veto math and a 1983 Supreme Court ruling.

In short: The Senate voted 50-48 to order Trump out of the Iran conflict — but without a two-thirds veto-proof majority, the directive has no legal teeth.

The United States Senate voted 50-48 on Tuesday to direct President Donald Trump to withdraw American forces from hostilities against Iran without specific congressional authorisation — the first time in the current conflict that both chambers have put such a directive on the record. The measure landed with the force of symbolism and the grip of a suggestion.

For seventeen weeks, the United States has been at war with Iran. American and Israeli aircraft launched strikes on February 28, a campaign the White House has prosecuted without a formal declaration of war. The Pentagon is now seeking $80 billion in supplemental funding to cover costs analysts put at roughly $100 billion overall — $11.3 billion in the first week alone. Iran's Strait of Hormuz blockade that followed pushed oil, gas, and fertiliser prices sharply higher. Against that backdrop, Tuesday's vote was striking, even if its practical consequences remain uncertain.

A rare bipartisan rebuke

The resolution — S.J.Res.59, originally drafted by Representative Gregory W. Meeks of New York — had already cleared the House 215-208 on June 3. Tuesday marked the tenth Senate floor vote on a war powers measure since fighting began in late February; the previous nine all fell short. What changed was a small but significant band of Republican defections.

Four GOP senators crossed the aisle: Rand Paul of Kentucky, Susan Collins of Maine, Lisa Murkowski of Alaska, and Bill Cassidy of Louisiana. The margin was helped, incidentally, by two Republican absences — Mitch McConnell, who is hospitalised with an undisclosed condition, and Dave McCormick, who was travelling with the president for a rally in Pennsylvania. On the other side, Senator John Fetterman of Pennsylvania was the sole Democrat to vote against the measure.

Senator Tim Kaine of Virginia, the resolution's Senate champion, framed the vote in constitutional terms: "The most solemn power for Congress is Congress has the power to declare war, not the president." He went further in remarks to reporters: "I want to just make sure everybody understands what a big deal it is when, in the middle of this conflict, Congress — of two Republican houses — has looked the president in the face and said, this war is illegal."

Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer called the conflict "Trump's historic blunder" and predicted it would be recorded as one of the worst foreign-policy failures in American history. Senator Cory Booker pointed to the accumulating political pressure: "Every week we've been chipping away and seeing the truth that most of America doesn't approve of this."

The legal trap

There is a structural problem at the heart of Tuesday's victory, and it predates the current conflict by more than four decades.

S.J.Res.59 is a concurrent resolution — the mechanism established in Section 5(c) of the 1973 War Powers Resolution to direct presidential withdrawal of forces without requiring a presidential signature. But in 1983, the Supreme Court's ruling in INS v. Chadha cast serious constitutional doubt on exactly this device, holding that legislative actions with the force of law must pass both chambers and be presented to the president. The executive branch has argued ever since that the concurrent resolution mechanism is therefore unenforceable — a position no court has definitively overturned, because courts have consistently declined to adjudicate war powers disputes.

The White House has already indicated the resolution "has no significance" and will not alter the president's conduct. Trump has been even blunter about the underlying premise, declaring there are "no limits" on executive authority.

The instrument with actual legal weight would be a joint resolution — legislation that must be presented to the president, who would almost certainly veto it. Overriding that veto requires two-thirds majorities in both chambers. At current vote counts — 50-48 in the Senate, 215-208 in the House — a two-thirds override is arithmetically out of reach. Kaine is pursuing the joint resolution path anyway, partly to create a cleaner constitutional record: forcing a public veto builds the legislative history that could eventually support a court challenge, even if no near-term enforcement is possible.

The administration has also argued, on a separate ground, that the 60-day authorisation clock in the War Powers Act "reset" when a ceasefire was announced in April. Most legal scholars reject this interpretation, but the argument has not been tested in court.

What to watch

The political arithmetic is fixed for now, but the surrounding context continues to shift. Last week Trump signed a memorandum of understanding with Iran on a broader nuclear agreement, starting a 60-day negotiating clock. Peace talks are under way in Switzerland. Israel is still conducting strikes in Lebanon and the conflict has not formally concluded.

On the congressional side, Kaine's joint resolution will test how durable Republican crossover support actually is — voting for a nonbinding concurrent resolution is a different political calculation from voting for a measure the president will publicly veto. Kaine has acknowledged the work is unfinished: "I would like to do a bill that would update the war powers resolution from the 1974 version to make it more applicable to war circa 2026."

For markets, Tuesday's vote is unlikely, by itself, to move oil futures or defence-sector pricing. The concurrent resolution's lack of enforcement mechanism means the operational risk posture in the Gulf has not changed. The meaningful catalysts in coming weeks are whether the 60-day MOU negotiations produce a framework agreement, whether Israeli operations in Lebanon escalate or wind down, and whether Kaine's joint resolution reaches the Senate floor with any realistic prospect of surviving a veto. None of those conditions presently apply.
