Lutnick signals a possible move on Chinese robots — once Commerce finishes its review

A Unitree G1 humanoid robot on display at UAV Expo 2024 — Chinese robotics firms have moved rapidly toward mass production of units like this one. — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Lutnick told a private industry roundtable that Commerce is reviewing Chinese robotics imports and 'could act' — but no restrictions, tariffs, or timeline have been announced.

Howard Lutnick, the former Cantor Fitzgerald chief who now runs the Commerce Department, gathered executives from SpaceX, Boston Dynamics, JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, Siemens, and Rockwell Automation behind closed doors on Monday. The subject: what to do about Chinese robots.

What emerged from the room, according to three attendees cited by Politico, was not a policy announcement. It was a signal — the kind of pre-policy temperature-taking that Washington specialises in before the hard decisions start. The Commerce Department is reviewing Chinese state-subsidised robotics imports, Lutnick told the group, and action is possible once that review concludes.

"We don't want state subsidised robotics attacking us in America. This is the arms race that is coming — robotic arms are coming. We need to make sure they're produced in America."

For traders looking for something to price, there is not much to chew on yet.

The gap is real; the policy is not

The concern behind the rhetoric has a solid empirical base. China deployed roughly 1.8 million industrial robots in 2023 — approximately four times the US figure — and is now on course to control close to 80 per cent of the global humanoid robot market by the end of this year. A Guangdong factory that opened this spring is capable of producing 10,000 humanoid units annually; a Beijing facility is targeting half a million per year by 2030. The scale of that build-out has been visible for some time. It is not a new discovery.

The formal review Lutnick referenced traces to April 30, when Commerce announced a Section 232-style study into the national security implications of Chinese drones and robots. That study is ongoing, with no public timeline for its conclusions. Monday's roundtable appears to be part of a stakeholder-engagement process, not a sign that a decision is imminent. The language throughout — signals, possible, after the review — does the work of managing expectations without setting them.

Adding texture to the timing: on the same day as the roundtable, China's Ministry of Commerce expanded its dual-use export ban to cover ten US firms, including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth. Rare earth components feed directly into the robotics supply chains that Lutnick's room was ostensibly trying to protect. Commerce has not publicly addressed that particular wrinkle.

Congressional sentiment has been building for months — GOP lawmakers sent a formal letter in October 2025 requesting federal investigations into Chinese robotics — which means that when policy does arrive, the political infrastructure will be in place. The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital is separately reviewing loan facilities for domestic start-ups Foundation Robotics and Standard Bots, a sign that other parts of the administration are moving faster than Commerce's study timeline implies.

The concrete trigger to watch is the conclusion of that Section 232-style review. Until then, the roster of attendees — SpaceX, Goldman, Boston Dynamics — tells you who is in the room. It does not tell you what the room will decide.
