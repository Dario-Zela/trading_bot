Israel weighs US listing for multi-billion-dollar defence companies

An Iron Dome battery launches an interceptor, 2021 — Rafael's signature system is at the centre of the US listing discussions. — Israel Defense Forces / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Israel is exploring US listings for IAI ($33.7bn) and Rafael ($20bn), preferring American disclosure rules for classified defence programmes over stricter Israeli requirements.

The Israeli government is weighing whether to bring two of its largest state-owned defence contractors — Israel Aerospace Industries and Rafael Advanced Defense Systems — to American capital markets, according to Bloomberg. A delegation of government officials and senior executives is set to travel to New York in mid-July for a five-day exploratory visit, meeting with investors, underwriters, lawyers and regulators to assess the feasibility of a US initial public offering for one or both companies.

The numbers involved are substantial. IAI, maker of the Arrow anti-ballistic-missile system and a range of surveillance platforms, carries a working valuation of up to NIS 100 billion (roughly $33.7 billion). Rafael, best known internationally as the developer of Iron Dome and the Iron Beam laser-based intercept system, is valued at around NIS 60 billion ($20 billion). The Israeli government is considering selling stakes of up to 30 per cent in each, with the operation targeted for completion before the end of 2026. Together, the two companies reported 2025 revenues running well above $10 billion and hold combined order backlogs exceeding $50 billion — a measure of forward demand that has ballooned since the regional security environment deteriorated sharply in late 2023.

A disclosure problem dressed as a listing question

The pivot toward a US venue is not purely about investor appetite. At the centre of the discussions is a structural tension: both IAI and Rafael manage deeply classified programmes, and Israeli disclosure requirements are, by the government's own reckoning, a harder constraint to navigate than their American equivalents. US regulators are viewed as more accommodating on national security carve-outs — a calculus that makes Nasdaq or NYSE a more tractable venue than the Tel Aviv Stock Exchange for companies whose financials are threaded through with state secrets.

The July delegation, led by Maayan Harel, deputy head of Israel's Government Companies Authority, and accompanied by Ministry of Defense representatives, will specifically examine how Nasdaq handles classified information and whether a primary listing, a dual listing alongside the TASE, or a subsidiary-level vehicle makes sense. Each company operates around 40 subsidiaries, some of which may be better suited to a discrete listing than the parent.

Fiscal pressures add a further dimension. Both companies need capital to expand capacity — Rafael is already ramping Iron Dome and Iron Beam production — and the government, carrying a war-inflated deficit, would welcome the proceeds from a partial stake sale.

What to watch

The mid-July New York visit is the first concrete checkpoint. If the government and advisers emerge from those meetings convinced that US listing mechanics can accommodate classified-programme carve-outs, process timelines for both companies will sharpen considerably. IAI is considered closer to ready — it already publishes financials and has tradeable bonds — while Rafael's flotation is described by people familiar with the matter as further away but being actively accelerated.

A year-end completion target is ambitious given the regulatory and political sensitivities involved. Any transaction affecting US ownership of Israeli defence assets of this scale would attract scrutiny well beyond standard exchange rules. The July conversations will test whether a workable structure exists — and how fast one can be built.
