Radioligand therapies steal the show at ASCO 2026 — but manufacturing and cost risks are underpriced

PSMA expression and radiotracer uptake in prostate cancer — the biological mechanism that radioligand therapies exploit to deliver targeted radiation to tumour cells. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
RLTs dominated ASCO 2026 clinically, but a $135k-per-course price tag, isotope half-lives of days, and capacity gaps until 2028–29 cap near-term access.

At the American Society of Clinical Oncology's annual meeting in Chicago this month, radioligand therapies attracted an intensity of attention that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. In 2016, ASCO hosted a single radiopharmaceutical abstract. By 2026, the programme had expanded to multiple dedicated RLT sessions — a shift in scientific consensus that coincided with a 3.5-fold surge in radiopharmaceutical deal values in Q1 2026 compared with the same period last year.

The clinical case is building. Phase I data from Novartis's AcTION trial on 225Ac-PSMA-617 — an alpha-emitting therapy for metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer — were described as "quite encouraging" by analysts attending the meeting. Eli Lilly, AstraZeneca, and Bristol Myers Squibb have all entered the space through targeted acquisitions, signalling that big pharma has reached its own verdict on the modality. Andrew Tsai, a senior analyst at Jefferies, summarised the competitive picture with unusual directness: "Multiple players can win; that's my big picture view."

But the gap between clinical excitement and commercial delivery is wider than investor enthusiasm has priced in.

The infrastructure constraint that approval cannot solve

The physics of radioligand therapy imposes a manufacturing logic with no pharmaceutical precedent. Lutetium-177, the isotope in Novartis's approved Pluvicto, has a half-life of 6.6 days. Actinium-225, the isotope behind the next generation of alpha therapies, has a half-life of eight days. Conventional pharmaceutical batch release takes two to three weeks — a timeline that would leave either isotope substantially decayed before reaching a patient. The result is a "right-first-time" manufacturing imperative that is, per ISPE guidance published earlier this year, "a nonnegotiable expectation": rework is not feasible because the time required to address quality exceptions will exceed the product's useful life.

Each dose is, in effect, a batch of one — manufactured to a specific patient and administration date, then tracked through a serialised cold chain to a treatment centre equipped with radiation shielding and a trained nuclear medicine workforce. "Approval doesn't equal access," said Harpreet Singh, a former FDA oncology director and now CMO at Precision for Medicine. Expanding reimbursement coverage and isotope supply chains simultaneously is the access problem the approval milestone does not solve.

The cost dimension compounds the access gap. Pluvicto lists at roughly $27,000 per dose; a standard five-dose course runs to approximately $135,000. Novartis's co-pay assistance programme explicitly excludes Medicare and federal programme beneficiaries — the demographic most likely to present with late-stage prostate cancer.

Supply-side investment is accelerating but lagging demand. Novartis's fifth U.S. RLT manufacturing facility, a 46,000-square-foot site in Denton, Texas, broke ground this year and is not expected to be operational until 2028. TerraPower Isotopes, targeting a 20-fold increase in Ac-225 capacity via a $450 million Philadelphia facility, has set 2029 as its production start date. The pipeline of alpha therapies now moving through clinical trials will likely outpace isotope supply for at least three years.

The next material data point is the AcTION trial's Phase II readout, expected in late 2026 or early 2027. If alpha-emitter tolerability data hold — the salivary gland toxicity that produces irreversible dry mouth in Lu-177 patients is a known liability that early Ac-225 data must address — the supply constraint will become the binding limit on how fast the market can grow.
