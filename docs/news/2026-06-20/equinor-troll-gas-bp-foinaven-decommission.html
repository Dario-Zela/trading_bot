Equinor commits $412m to Troll gas growth while BP recycles Foinaven subsea infrastructure

The Troll A platform in the Norwegian North Sea, which currently supplies roughly 10% of Europe's total gas needs. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Equinor and partners commit $412m to a new Troll gas project targeting 2028 startup and 11 bcm of output, while BP closes out Foinaven via a recycling contract with DeepOcean.

Equinor and four partners have formally committed just over $412 million to a new subsea gas development at the Troll field in the Norwegian North Sea — a capital allocation decision that underscores how little the energy transition has altered the long-horizon investment calculus for one of Europe's largest producers.

The project, named TWIN (Troll West Increased Gas Recovery North), represents the third step of Troll's Phase 3 development. It consists of two new wells tied back through a subsea template to existing infrastructure, with production targeted to begin as early as 2028 and expected to add roughly 11 billion standard cubic metres of gas over its life. The ownership split runs: Petoro 55.93%, Equinor Energy 30.55%, Shell 8.19%, TotalEnergies 3.69%, and ConocoPhillips 1.64%.

Equinor's senior vice president for projects and subsea Norway, Gunnar Nakken, framed the rationale in supply-security terms: "The project helps sustain jobs, value creation and secure gas exports to Europe." The choice of words is deliberate — Troll already meets roughly 10% of Europe's total gas requirements, and holds approximately 40% of Norway's continental shelf gas reserves. A second tranche of Phase 3 is due on stream during 2026, meaning Troll is threading new production through the middle of the decade before TWIN extends the plateau toward 2030 and beyond. Equinor also notes that both the Troll A platform and the onshore Kollsnes processing plant run on shore-based electricity, giving the gas an unusually low-emission production profile by industry standards.

The NOK 4 billion commitment lands at a moment when European policymakers are accelerating grid investment and renewables mandates — Britain alone faces up to £240 billion in grid upgrades to hit clean-power targets — yet demand forecasts for flexible gas supply through the transition period remain stubbornly high.

A different North Sea calculus

While Equinor builds, BP is winding down. The British major has contracted DeepOcean to remove and recycle the remaining subsea infrastructure at the Foinaven field, located roughly 190 kilometres west of the Shetland Islands in water depths between 330 and 530 metres. The scope covers ten flexible process risers, three dynamic subsea umbilicals, one static umbilical, and associated buoyancy modules and clamps — the remnants left behind after BP removed the Foinaven FPSO in 2021.

Foinaven produced around 440 million barrels over its lifetime — roughly double what planners originally anticipated when the field came on stream in 1997. The decommissioning contract marks its formal close.

The commercial structure is notable. BP and DeepOcean are again using a sale-and-purchase arrangement — in which DeepOcean effectively acquires the recovered materials as part of the deal — rather than a conventional day-rate contract. The pair first applied the model at BP's Miller and Don fields. DeepOcean CEO Øyvind Mikaelsen described the approach as "a progressive and commercially innovative" model for subsea decommissioning, one that aligns incentive structures around recovery value rather than hours worked. Engineering and project management will run out of DeepOcean's Aberdeen office; offshore execution will use one of the firm's subsea construction vessels.

Taken together, the two North Sea moves land in opposite directions: one producer locking in bcm-scale gas output into the early 2030s, another methodically recovering capital from a field that closed its active chapter five years ago. For traders watching energy-transition risk, the signal from Equinor is the more consequential one — that management confidence in European gas demand through 2030 remains high enough to justify a nine-figure commitment with a 2028 first gas date. The next test of that confidence will come when Phase 3 step two comes on stream later this year, and the production trajectory through to TWIN's startup becomes clearer.
