Schiff calls the $40tn US debt pile a 'Ponzi' with no painless exit — his structural case holds, his timing rarely does

Peter Schiff, founder of Euro Pacific Capital, at a public event. — Gage Skidmore / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Schiff's 'giant Ponzi' label for the US Treasury rests on real fiscal math — $3bn/day in interest, $1tn annual costs — but his timing calls have a 15-year losing streak.

Peter Schiff has been predicting the collapse of the dollar since before most current market participants started their first real job. That consistency is, depending on your vantage point, either his greatest credential or his most obvious liability.

This week, the Euro Pacific Capital founder called the US Treasury "a giant Ponzi" — the latest iteration of a warning he has been delivering for close to two decades. The underlying arithmetic, for once, is hard to dismiss out of hand.

US national debt stands at roughly $40 trillion. The Congressional Budget Office projects net interest payments will breach $1 trillion in fiscal year 2026 — already the second-largest spending line in the federal budget, behind only Social Security. In the first seven months of the current fiscal year, Treasury averaged close to $3 billion a day in interest payments, outpacing Medicare and Medicaid combined. CBO Director Phillip Swagel said plainly in February: "Our budget projections continue to indicate that the fiscal trajectory is not sustainable."

Schiff's specific claims go further. He projects debt will top $50 trillion before the end of Donald Trump's second term, with annualised gross interest costs already running at $1.6 trillion. His argument is structural: as maturing debt rolls over at market rates rather than the suppressed levels of the 2010s, the cost of servicing the pile compounds faster than the economy can grow. He told listeners this week the crisis "is going to be driven by our creditors" and that "no one in Washington is even pretending to have a serious plan anymore. That's the day of reckoning." His prediction is that the Federal Reserve will ultimately choose inflation over genuine fiscal correction — the exit, when forced, will run through devaluation rather than austerity.

Worth separating from his timing

The structural argument is reasonably coherent. The timing record is not.

Schiff made his name calling the 2008 subprime crisis accurately, years before it broke. Since then, he has called a dollar collapse, a Treasury market rout, and hyperinflation on a rolling basis — most of it undelivered. When pressed on the gap between forecast and outcome, he has said he was "too optimistic on the world's ability to figure out the truth." That is a position almost impossible to falsify, and markets price it accordingly, treating his warnings as informative background rather than actionable signals.

None of which makes the debt trajectory benign. CBO's own 10-year projection has interest consuming 6.9 percent of GDP by 2056, up from 3.3 percent today — and that assumes no shock and orderly rollover at every maturity. The acute stress test is a Treasury auction that fails to clear, or a dollar move sharp enough to accelerate the dynamic Schiff describes. Neither condition exists today.

The next moments of reckoning are more prosaic: the autumn budget negotiations and the ongoing question of whether the debt ceiling deal reached earlier this year holds. Washington's most likely response to both — a deferral dressed as a deal — would be entirely consistent with how it has handled the last twenty years of the same conversation.
