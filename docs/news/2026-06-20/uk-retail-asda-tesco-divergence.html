Asda's £1bn loss and Tesco's 1.8% growth tell two very different stories about UK grocers

An Asda supermarket in Stanley, County Durham — the chain's pre-tax losses nearly doubled to £989m in the year ending December 2025 — Graham Robson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Asda's £989m loss from a botched Walmart IT separation and Tesco's below-forecast 1.8% UK LfL growth expose the execution risk embedded in UK grocery's transformation race.

The numbers tell opposite stories. Asda, Britain's third-largest grocer, posted a pre-tax loss of £989m for the year ending December 2025 — nearly double the prior year's £599m shortfall. Several hundred miles and a few market-share points away, Tesco reported £16.82bn in group sales for its first quarter of 2026, UK same-store sales up 1.8%, full-year guidance intact. Two UK grocers, two very different trajectories.

The divergence matters beyond its obvious face value. Both companies face the same structural pressure: a grocery market squeezed by persistent discounting, stubbornly elevated living costs, and the continued encroachment of Aldi and Lidl. The question is who can invest their way through it without losing the thread. Asda's 2025 results suggest that execution risk, more than intent, is the decisive variable.

Project Future: a £284m lesson in what not to do

Asda's losses were always going to be elevated. Its private equity owners, TDR Capital, acquired the business from Walmart in 2021 and inherited a grocer still operationally tethered to its former parent's infrastructure. The solution was Project Future — a programme to decouple more than 2,500 legacy Walmart systems and stand up entirely independent platforms — originally budgeted at £189m.

The final cutover happened in August 2025. It did not go well.

"Poor integration, insufficient testing, and a lack of capacity planning" was how executive chairman Allan Leighton — who returned to the business he ran in the 1990s when TDR brought him back in November 2024 — described what followed. The disruption struck depot-to-store stock flow first, then propagated into online availability. Leighton called it "totally self-inflicted." The one-off cost of that disruption: £284m. Total one-off charges for the year, which also included a £344m non-cash property impairment, came to £656m — more than three times the original programme budget, and a figure that has pushed the cumulative cost of the Walmart separation toward £1bn.

Strip out those charges and the picture is less catastrophic, but still troubled. Adjusted EBITDA fell 33.3% to £761m. Like-for-like sales declined 3.1% excluding fuel. Market share slipped to 11.5% by May, down from 12.3% at the start of the year. Net debt stands at £3.1bn, partially offset by £1.3bn in cash and £2.1bn in total liquidity — the balance sheet has been managed partly through store sale-and-leaseback transactions.

An Asda spokesman said the reported loss "does not reflect the underlying financial strength of the business, and continued powerful cash generation." Leighton's strategy centres on aggressive pricing — the Rollback and Asda Price Promise campaigns — a play he has acknowledged will take up to five years to show results. The early evidence is limited: in the first quarter of the current financial year, the sales decline had narrowed to 0.8%. Recovery to Q2 2025 trading levels is not expected until Q2 2026 at the earliest.

Tesco's Q1: solid, but the growth mix is changing


Tesco's UK like-for-like sales grew 1.8% in the first quarter, but missed analyst forecasts of 2.3%. Photo: Brian Robert Marshall / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)

Against that backdrop, Tesco's first-quarter update reads as reassuring rather than exciting. The £16.82bn in group sales for the 13 weeks to 30 May 2026 missed no dramatic marks on the downside. UK like-for-like sales grew 1.8%, led by fresh food at 3.6% and the Finest own-label range at 9%. Online sales rose 8.9%.

The share price did not celebrate. Tesco fell more than 2% on the day, the market having priced in closer to 2.3% UK LfL growth. The shortfall was partly attributable to Booker, the wholesale arm, where like-for-like sales fell 3.2% — worse than the 2.4% decline analysts expected — hit by the loss of a significant national customer account, adverse weather, and Easter timing effects. CEO Ken Murphy said he was "confident" in Booker's fundamentals, pointing to a two-year LfL of 3.2% in core retail. Full-year guidance of £3–3.3bn in adjusted operating profit was maintained.

The more instructive figure is the gap between LfL and total reported growth. UK and Ireland same-store sales rose 1.8%; UK total revenue including fuel rose 3.7%. That differential reflects fuel volume gains — fuel sales surged 19.5% — and the rising contribution of new store openings. As comp-store momentum decelerates from prior periods' pace — the two-year UK LfL is still a healthy 7.0% but clearly slowing — Tesco is increasingly dependent on network expansion and currency effects to sustain headline growth. That shift is manageable for a retailer of Tesco's scale and capital position. It is considerably less forgiving for a competitor still attempting to stabilise its cost base after a failed systems migration.

The trap that is hard to escape

What Asda and Tesco illustrate in tandem is a dynamic common to capital-intensive businesses trying to modernise while simultaneously competing on price. Investment in technology is not optional — Asda was always going to decouple from Walmart's systems. But execution failure means the upfront cost arrives on the income statement before any of the efficiency gains do. The business deteriorates before it improves, and in grocery, where net margins run to low single digits and customer switching costs are minimal, that window of vulnerability is expensive.

Asda's next significant inflection point is its Q2 trading update, which will show whether the narrowing sales decline has continued and whether the comparator effect from the August 2025 IT disruption is providing relief. For Tesco, attention shifts to whether Booker recovers in the current quarter and whether comp-store growth can re-accelerate as weather normalises. In a sector where standing still already costs money, both retailers are spending to maintain position — the difference, for now, is in who controls the timing of that exposure.
