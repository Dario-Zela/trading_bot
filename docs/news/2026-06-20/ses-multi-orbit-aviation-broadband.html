SES bets on multi-orbit connectivity to carve out space in crowded aviation broadband market

A Viva Aerobus Airbus A320 — the type of aircraft now carrying SES's multi-orbit satellite connectivity system — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
SES's multi-orbit inflight WiFi — combining GEO and LEO satellites via a 7cm antenna — went live on Viva Aerobus's 100-plane fleet on 1 June, adding a challenger to a market Starlink is already rewriting.

Satellite operator SES turned on its multi-orbit in-flight connectivity service on a Mexican low-cost carrier on 1 June — a commercial signal that legacy satellite companies are not ceding the aviation broadband market to SpaceX without a fight.

The carrier is Viva Aerobus, which runs an all-Airbus A320/A321 fleet and bills itself as Mexico's ultra low-cost airline. Eleven aircraft are already live on the SES system; the full deployment covers 100 planes. Viva becomes the first Mexico-based airline to fly with SES's new electronically steered array (ESA) antenna — a flat unit under seven centimetres tall that sits on the fuselage and simultaneously connects to satellites in two different orbital shells.

What multi-orbit actually means

The antenna links to SES's geostationary satellites (GEO, roughly 35,700 km up) and to Eutelsat OneWeb's low-earth-orbit constellation (LEO, roughly 1,200 km up), blending both feeds intelligently. GEO provides the wide-area blanket coverage that keeps the connection alive over oceans and remote corridors; LEO shaves latency and handles the throughput bursts that passengers notice when loading a page or joining a video call. The current system can deliver up to 195 Mbps download and 32 Mbps upload per aircraft — fast enough for multi-user streaming across a full cabin. SES groups this under its Open Orbits platform, positioned as the industry's first open-architecture, multi-orbit global network.

The commercial logic is clear enough. SES's Mobility segment — aviation, maritime, and land-mobile — posted 207.8% revenue growth on a reported basis in Q1 2026. That figure is heavily inflated by the Intelsat consolidation completed last year; the like-for-like number is a more modest 3.1%. Aviation is where SES is betting it can convert that scale into organic expansion.

The real test is competitive. Starlink Aviation is now the default choice for carriers wanting fast retrofits: 100–350 Mbps per aircraft, latency under 40ms, with Southwest, Air France, and Emirates all mid-rollout. Viasat, which had entrenched positions with major carriers, has watched some defect to SpaceX while it waits for its own multi-orbit Amara service to come together. SES is trying to sit between: more capable than ageing GEO-only hardware, less capital-intensive than building a bespoke LEO constellation from scratch.

A Boeing partnership gives the strategy a medium-term runway. Boeing is beginning factory-installation of SES's in-cabin hardware on select aircraft now, with a fully line-fit system expected around 2028 — meaning new-build planes could arrive at airlines already wired for multi-orbit connectivity.

The 100-aircraft Viva deal is modest in scale but meaningful as proof of concept. The milestones to watch: how quickly SES signs further airline customers in the Americas, whether the ESA antenna performance holds as Eutelsat OneWeb's constellation matures, and whether the Boeing line-fit timeline stays on track. The 2028 date is the clearest near-term indicator of whether SES's multi-orbit bet translates from marketing into metal.
