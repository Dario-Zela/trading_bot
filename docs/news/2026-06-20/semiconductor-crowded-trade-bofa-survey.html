Eight in ten fund managers are long semiconductors — and that's the problem

A 300mm silicon wafer — the substrate from which the AI trade's most crowded bet is literally fabricated. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
80% of fund managers call long semiconductors the most crowded trade in history — a BofA indicator configuration historically linked to near-term drawdowns of 2–20%.

An 80% consensus reading is rare in financial markets. Normally, professional investors disagree — that is what makes prices. But the June 2026 Bank of America Global Fund Manager Survey found that four in five respondents, polled across more than 200 institutional money managers, named one position as the most crowded trade in market history: long global semiconductors.

The word "history" is doing considerable work in that sentence. BofA has run its monthly survey for decades, and no previous crowded-trade designation has drawn this kind of unanimity. The nearest recent comparison — "long Magnificent 7" at its peak — never breached the low-60s. "Long gold" topped out near 58%. The semiconductor trade, at 80%, is in its own category. For context, second place in June sits at a distant 12%, also "long Magnificent 7." The chasm between first and second — 68 percentage points — is itself a structural anomaly.

From fringe to frenzy in sixty days

The velocity of the concentration is as notable as its level. In April, just 24% of managers pointed to semiconductors as the crowded trade. By May that had jumped to 73% — a 49-point single-month move that BofA described as the steepest one-month increase in the survey's history. June added another seven points. In two months, a minority view became an overwhelming consensus.

Equity allocations tracked the same trajectory. Aggregate fund positioning swung from net 13% overweight equities in April to net 50% overweight in May — the largest single-month shift on record. Cash levels fell from 4.3% to 3.9%. The iShares Semiconductor ETF gained more than 60% year-to-date through May, during which stretch it recorded 18 consecutive daily gains.

The proximate logic is legible enough. Semiconductors are the physical infrastructure of the AI buildout: Nvidia's processors power the data centers; TSMC and Samsung fabricate the chips; the capex cycle driving all of it has continued to expand. When a sector moves like that, the instinct to participate overwhelms the instinct to question. That, structurally, is the problem.

Hartnett's technical case

BofA chief investment strategist Michael Hartnett has spent the spring cataloguing what he characterises as the mechanical symptoms of a late-cycle crowded trade. The central data point: the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index (SOX) is currently trading 62% above its 200-day moving average.

Only two prior markets in recorded financial history have reached comparable deviations from that smoothing line. The Nasdaq Composite in March 2000 was trading 55% above its 200-day moving average before falling 78% over the following two and a half years. Shares of the Compagnie des Indes during the 1720 Mississippi Bubble — when Scottish financier John Law's scheme drove a 1,900% gain in under twelve months — peaked at a 73% deviation before the enterprise collapsed 80% by year's end. The current SOX reading of 62% sits between those two episodes.

"SOX semiconductor index remarkably trading 62% above 200dma, comparable only to French market at peak Mississippi bubble." — Michael Hartnett, BofA chief investment strategist

Hartnett is careful to separate mechanism from prophecy. He frames the measure explicitly as indicating fragility, not timing — the Nasdaq stayed elevated for months before its eventual collapse. The accompanying symptoms he identifies are familiar from late-cycle episodes: exponential price movement, extreme market concentration, collapsing volatility, and an unusual pattern in which equity inflows have begun pulling bond yields higher rather than the reverse. Crucially, Hartnett does not question AI's technological legitimacy. His concern is pricing mechanics, not the underlying technology.

The paradox of knowing

Crowd awareness does not dissolve crowds. That is one of the more uncomfortable features of the current configuration. In the June survey, 56% of managers described AI stocks as being in a "boom" phase — characterised by momentum and fear of missing out — while only 21% said the sector had entered genuine "euphoria." The implication: a large majority of investors who acknowledge the crowding remain in the trade, presumably on the theory that they will identify the exit before others do.

History offers limited comfort on that point. BofA's Bull & Bear Indicator — a composite of fund flows, positioning, and sentiment data — has risen to 8.5, above the 8.0 threshold the bank defines as a contrarian sell signal. Since 2002, the indicator has generated 17 such signals. In the two to three months following each, global equities declined an average of 2% to 3%, with maximum drawdowns in individual instances reaching 8.5% to 20%. The signals do not reliably identify tops, but they cluster around periods of elevated near-term volatility.

Diversified crowding — where several trades compete for the title — typically absorbs sentiment shocks better than monolithic concentration. When the exit routes are multiple, the liquidity crunch during forced selling is diffuse. When the exit is through one door, the rush is more consequential. A fund that needs to reduce risk in a hurry and holds semiconductor positions does not have many hedges to lean on; the "long Magnificent 7" trade at 12%, though correlated, is thin by comparison.

What comes next

The June survey flags two catalysts fund managers believe could force a reversal. Higher-for-longer interest rates are the more immediate concern. A net 34% of respondents now expect short rates to rise over the next twelve months — the highest reading since September 2022 — and 40% forecast at least one rate hike, up sharply from 16% in May. A rate regime that reasserts itself compresses growth multiples, and semiconductors currently trade at growth multiples.

The second potential catalyst is AI capital expenditure itself. At 56%, most managers still expect AI investment to expand. If any of the major hyperscalers reports a meaningful downward revision to data-center spending — or if chip demand shows early signs of digestion — the fundamental narrative supporting the trade would shift faster than positioning can follow.

For systematic and algorithmic approaches, the configuration presents a specific structure: crowded long positioning with low short interest creates conditions for forced selling if sentiment cracks, but also for sustained momentum if it does not. The SOX's distance from its 200-day moving average describes fragility; the BofA sell-signal history describes expected magnitude. Neither describes timing.

The next BofA Global Fund Manager Survey publishes in mid-July. The more immediate test may arrive before then: Nvidia's next earnings release, any hyperscaler capex guidance revision, or a Federal Reserve rate decision could all serve as the inflection point. At 80% consensus, the trade has very few remaining converts to recruit. The question is not whether the crowd will eventually thin — it will — but whether it does so in an orderly rotation or a rush.
