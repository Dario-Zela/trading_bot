AstraZeneca advances oral GLP-1 elecoglipron to Phase III, entering the crowded obesity drug race

AstraZeneca's Discovery Centre on the Cambridge Biomedical Campus, the company's global headquarters and primary R&D facility — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's elecoglipron showed ~11.8% weight loss at 36 weeks in Phase IIb, but enters Phase III two approved oral GLP-1 rivals already on the market.

AstraZeneca is moving its oral GLP-1 drug candidate elecoglipron into Phase III clinical trials for obesity and type 2 diabetes — an announcement timed to coincide with the American Diabetes Association's 2026 Scientific Sessions in New Orleans and the simultaneous publication of Phase IIb results in The Lancet. The decision cements the British-Swedish pharma giant's ambition to carve a position in a market that has, until now, been largely a two-company show.

The trouble is that by the time elecoglipron could conceivably reach patients, Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly will have had years to entrench their respective oral franchises. Novo's oral version of Wegovy received FDA approval in late 2025; Lilly's orforglipron (branded Foundayo) followed in April 2026. AstraZeneca is not fashionably late — it is structurally late — and the quality of its Phase IIb data will determine whether that matters.

What the phase IIb data actually show

The Phase IIb programme comprised two trials. VISTA enrolled adults with obesity or overweight, and SOLSTICE enrolled adults with type 2 diabetes. Both met their primary endpoints.

In VISTA, participants taking elecoglipron 75 mg once daily lost an average of 10.5% of body weight at 26 weeks, against 0.6% on placebo — a gap of nearly ten percentage points. The effect appeared to be still building: by 36 weeks, the treatment arm had reached 11.8% mean weight loss versus 0.3% for placebo, and the investigators noted that weight loss had not plateaued.¹ Some 88.8% of patients on elecoglipron achieved the clinical threshold of at least 5% weight loss at 26 weeks. In SOLSTICE, HbA1c fell by 1.9 percentage points versus 0.2 for placebo, and 90% of the treatment arm reached the guideline target of HbA1c below 7%.²

Safety signals were predominantly gastrointestinal — nausea, constipation, diarrhoea, vomiting — and characterised as mild to moderate in severity. Discontinuation rates were low, and importantly, no liver safety concerns were identified. That last point is not trivial: Pfizer's small-molecule GLP-1 candidate danuglipron was discontinued after a hepatotoxicity signal emerged, removing one rival from the field but also heightening scrutiny of the entire class.

Jefferies analysts described the results as achieving "competitive efficacy with standard tolerability" and said the data came in ahead of expectations. That is a notable endorsement, though analysts said "competitive" rather than "superior" — a distinction that will matter when pivotal Phase III data land.³

A crowded field with structural advantages and disadvantages

Orforglipron, in its pivotal obesity trial, produced 11.2% mean weight loss at 72 weeks on the 36 mg dose. Elecoglipron showed 11.8% at just 36 weeks in Phase IIb — numerically encouraging, but direct comparisons across trial populations, doses, and durations are unreliable without head-to-head data, which does not yet exist. The honest read is that elecoglipron looks like it belongs in the same general efficacy tier as orforglipron, without clear evidence of superiority.

What AstraZeneca is betting on is differentiation through breadth of indication and combination potential. The EMBOLD Phase III programme will study elecoglipron in obesity and overweight populations with and without diabetes. The ELUMINATE programme will test it as a monotherapy and in combination with dapagliflozin — AstraZeneca's established SGLT2 inhibitor, marketed as Farxiga — for type 2 diabetes. There are also planned cardiovascular and kidney outcome trials. The dapagliflozin combination angle is strategically coherent: AstraZeneca already owns the commercial and scientific infrastructure for that drug, and layering a GLP-1 on top of an SGLT2 inhibitor addresses a real clinical gap in cardiometabolic treatment.

The origin of the asset matters too. Elecoglipron was licensed from Shanghai-based Eccogene in November 2023 for $185 million upfront, with up to $1.8 billion in potential milestones — relatively modest terms for a potential blockbuster, reflecting the early stage at which AstraZeneca moved.⁴ Eccogene retains co-development and co-commercialisation rights in Greater China, which gives the drug a potential pathway into one of the world's largest metabolic disease markets independently of the Western Phase III timeline.

The share price and the valuation question

AstraZeneca stock (LSE: AZN; Nasdaq: AZN) has declined roughly 5% over the past month, pulling back from an all-time closing high of $208.45 reached on 27 February 2026 to around $174.93 as of 18 June. The retreat does not appear to be driven by any single news event — it tracks a broader reassessment of pharma valuations and, in AstraZeneca's case, investor uncertainty about pipeline execution across a crowded late-stage portfolio.

Discounted cash flow analysis from Simply Wall St suggests the stock may be trading at approximately 20.2% below its estimated fair value, though the methodology behind that figure is not fully disclosed and DCF estimates for pharma companies are acutely sensitive to pipeline success assumptions.⁵ Analyst consensus is more straightforwardly bullish: nine buy ratings against one hold, with an average price target of $224.49, implying roughly 28% upside from current levels.

The elecoglipron advance is not itself a near-term earnings catalyst — Phase III readouts are years away. But it does incrementally de-risk AstraZeneca's cardiometabolic ambitions, which management has previously said could generate more than $10 billion in peak risk-adjusted revenues across the portfolio.

What to watch

The most important near-term data point will be when AstraZeneca discloses its Phase III trial protocols in full — specifically, the primary endpoints, trial durations, and comparator arms for EMBOLD and ELUMINATE. Long-term outcomes trials (cardiovascular and kidney events) will not read out for several years, but their initiation dates will signal how seriously regulators and AstraZeneca itself are treating elecoglipron as a future cardiovascular therapy rather than a weight-loss-only product.

The combination data with dapagliflozin will be particularly closely watched. If that combination shows additive or synergistic effects on HbA1c, weight, or renal outcomes, AstraZeneca's case for differentiation strengthens considerably. If it does not, the company will have a GLP-1 pill competing on roughly equal terms with two already-approved products that will have had several years of commercial head start.

For now, the Phase IIb data give AstraZeneca a credible ticket to the race. Whether it can finish in the medals is a question for Phase III.
