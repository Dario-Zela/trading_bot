ConocoPhillips versus Viper Energy: global scale against Permian royalties in 2026

ConocoPhillips' Houston headquarters in the Energy Corridor — the company's $61.6bn revenue base spans five continents and five operating segments. — Public domain / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
COP offers global diversification at 10.6x forward P/E and 2.8% yield; Viper's Permian royalty model commands 21x but pays 5%, with all upside tied to Diamondback's drill bit.

Two energy companies, two very different bets. ConocoPhillips — a sprawling independent with operations across Alaska, Canada, Norway, Qatar, and the Asia Pacific — sits at a forward price-to-earnings of 10.6 times and yields 2.8% on a $3.30 annual dividend. Viper Energy, which owns roughly 86,000 net royalty acres in the Permian Basin but drills none of them itself, trades at 21.2 times forward earnings and yields around 5%. The valuation gap is not a pricing error. It is the market's verdict on two fundamentally different exposure profiles in a year when oil price direction remains genuinely contested.

The structural contrast is sharp. ConocoPhillips reported $61.6 billion in full-year 2025 revenue, up 8% year-on-year, with free cash flow of approximately $7.2 billion and a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.4 times. Its Q1 2026 adjusted EPS of $1.89 beat consensus by nearly 14%, and management raised full-year capital expenditure guidance to $12.0–$12.5 billion, partly reflecting expanded Permian activity. The portfolio runs across five operating segments and includes LNG exposure through its QatarEnergy and Shell partnerships — though the company trimmed its Q2 Qatar production outlook citing Middle East uncertainty. That uncertainty is precisely the kind of drag that a global footprint can generate.

Viper's model is deliberately narrow. It collects royalties on production — Diamondback Energy operates roughly 35% of its net royalty acreage, with ExxonMobil and others covering the rest. Viper spends nothing on drilling or completions. When operators drill more, Viper benefits; when they pull back, Viper stalls, with no offsetting global assets to absorb the shock. The company posted a net loss of $68 million in full-year 2025 — almost entirely non-cash — while generating $1.05 billion in operating cash flow, enough to cover its $328 million base dividend three times over. Management raised the base dividend 15% to $1.52 per share and pledged to return at least 75% of distributable cash through dividends and buybacks. Net debt fell by $600 million in Q1 2026 alone, to $1.59 billion, with a stated target of $1.5 billion — approximately 1.0 times leverage at $50 WTI.

The choice

For investors managing energy exposure, the two stocks solve different problems. ConocoPhillips offers diversification across geographies and commodity types, a relatively cheap valuation, and a balance sheet that can absorb price downturns without operational cuts. The complexity is real, but so is the cushion. Viper offers concentrated Permian upside with zero drilling-cost risk, a higher current yield, and a growth profile that is entirely a function of how aggressively Diamondback and its peers keep developing the Delaware and Midland sub-basins.

With 2026 production guidance of 120,000 to 132,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day, Viper's near-term output is already largely set by operators' existing drilling programs. ConocoPhillips' trajectory, by contrast, turns partly on geopolitical stability in Qatar and on whether Permian efficiencies keep pace with the company's expanded capital budget.

The next clear data point for COP is Q2 2026 earnings, when the Qatar production exclusion will either prove conservative or confirm a more persistent drag. For Viper, the watch item is whether net debt crosses below $1.5 billion — the threshold at which management has signalled further expansion of variable dividends.
