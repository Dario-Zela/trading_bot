Rio Tinto, Teck, and Glencore all chase the AI copper trade — with unequal execution risk

Kennecott's Bingham Canyon open-pit copper mine in Utah, the largest human excavation on Earth — a wholly owned Rio Tinto operation. — Doc Searls / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto's Nuton-AWS deal and Los Azules stake give it the most specific AI-copper growth story; Anglo-Teck awaits China clearance while Glencore trims its 2026 guidance.

The copper market's supply problem is well-documented. The International Energy Agency estimates that existing and planned mines can meet only about 70% of projected 2035 demand. Wood Mackenzie put the refined-copper deficit at 304,000 tonnes in 2025, with a wider gap expected this year. What is less settled is which mining companies are best placed to close it — and on what timeline.

Among the three largest publicly traded copper names chasing the AI and electrification trade, Rio Tinto has the freshest specific story right now. Teck Resources is working through a landmark merger that still needs China's sign-off. Glencore has the immediate scale but just trimmed its 2026 output guidance. The timelines — and the execution risks — are very different.

Rio Tinto: Nuton, Los Azules, and an AWS contract

The most concrete element of Rio Tinto's copper growth story is Nuton, a proprietary bioleaching technology that uses naturally occurring microorganisms to extract copper from primary sulphide ores without conventional concentrators, smelters, or refineries. Deployed at industrial scale for the first time at the Johnson Camp mine in Arizona, Nuton signed its first commercial customer in January 2026: Amazon Web Services, under a two-year agreement to supply low-carbon Nuton copper for US data center components. AWS also provides cloud infrastructure to accelerate Nuton's optimisation. It is a modest volume deal, but it anchors Rio Tinto's copper-for-AI narrative to something more tangible than a marketing slide.

The larger ambition is Los Azules, an undeveloped deposit in Argentina's San Juan Province containing an estimated 29.3 billion pounds of copper across indicated and inferred resources — enough to rank it among the largest deposits anywhere on the planet. Rio Tinto currently holds 17.2% of McEwen Copper, the project's operator, through the Nuton subsidiary, at an investment of roughly $100 million. It is reportedly in talks to increase that stake. McEwen Copper secured a $2.4 billion loan package in May 2026 and is targeting board approval for construction by the end of this year, with first production projected for 2029 to 2030.

The risks are real. Argentina's permitting environment has improved — the government's Large Investment Incentive Regime (RIGI) approved Los Azules in September 2024 — but executing a major mine build in Argentina carries political and logistical risk that a government stamp does not fully dissolve. Rio Tinto is a minority holder, not the operator; its ability to dictate the project's pace is limited by that structural fact.

The other runners

Anglo American and Teck Resources are pursuing a different form of copper concentration. Their all-stock combination, valued at roughly $53 billion, would create a top-five global copper producer with more than 70% of the combined portfolio in copper and combined annual volumes approaching 1.2 million tonnes. Regulators in Canada and the EU have cleared the deal. China and South Korea are expected to rule between September 2026 and March 2027 — the remaining gate before the merger can close. Anglo American has also simplified its portfolio ahead of the transaction, selling Australian steelmaking coal assets for up to $3.88 billion.

Glencore, by contrast, is coping with a near-term production setback. The company cut its 2026 copper guidance midpoint to 840,000 tonnes from 930,000, citing difficulties at its Collahuasi mine in Chile — even as Q1 2026 showed a 19% year-on-year jump in own-sourced output to 199,600 tonnes. The long-term targets are more ambitious: above 1 million tonnes by end-2028 and 1.6 million tonnes by 2035. Reuters Breakingviews noted in late May that Rio Tinto could have strategic reasons to revisit merger talks with Glencore, given complementary copper assets — a thesis that carries little urgency while Los Azules itself is still in development.

For context on the demand side: copper consumption by AI data centers is forecast to grow from 1.1 million metric tons in 2025 to 2.5 million metric tons by 2040, according to industry estimates. That number alone exceeds Glencore's entire current output target.

What to watch. The Los Azules board decision — expected in late 2026 — is the clearest near-term signal on Rio Tinto's commitment: whether it lifts its McEwen Copper stake will indicate how seriously it treats this path versus other capital priorities. On Anglo-Teck, the Chinese antitrust ruling is the pending gate, with a September 2026 start for the review window. And across all three names, a copper price that has already tracked toward $12,000 per tonne will do more to accelerate final investment decisions than any amount of strategic framing.
