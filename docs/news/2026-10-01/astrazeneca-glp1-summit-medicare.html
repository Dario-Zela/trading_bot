AstraZeneca advances its GLP-1 pill and backs Summit with $2bn as Medicare uptake hits 700,000

In one sentence
AstraZeneca moved its GLP-1 pill into Phase 3 and put $2bn into Summit, but the Summit deal is an oncology bet, not an obesity one, and the stock rose nearly 20% on it.

AstraZeneca had two things to say about obesity this week, and only one of them was about obesity. The FTSE 100 drugmaker moved its once-daily GLP-1 pill, elecoglipron, into Phase 3. It also agreed to put $2bn into Summit Therapeutics, a US biotech that is not a GLP-1 company. Summit's lead drug, ivonescimab, is a cancer antibody. The two announcements arrived while Medicare's obesity-drug rollout passed 700,000 new patients. Read together, they show a large pharma group betting on an obesity market that is growing fast and is already dominated by two rivals.

The framing that Summit is a "GLP-1 player" being undervalued does not survive a read of the filings. The valuation question at Summit is about oncology. It is worth separating from the obesity story, even though the news arrived together.

The pill: a late entrant with data held back

Elecoglipron is an oral small-molecule GLP-1 receptor agonist. AstraZeneca says it met its primary endpoints in two Phase 2b studies. Vista tracked weight loss over 36 weeks in 310 patients. Solstice looked at blood-sugar control in 406 people with type 2 diabetes, against placebo and semaglutide. The Phase 3 programme includes the EMBOLD trials in obesity and overweight, and the ELUMINATE studies in type 2 diabetes. Those will test the drug alone and alongside dapagliflozin.

What the company has not published is the size of the weight loss. When the Phase 2 results were first announced, chief executive Pascal Soriot would say only: "we have a very competitive profile." For a drug entering a market where efficacy is the main selling point, that is a notable omission. The company is going ahead to Phase 3 without having told investors the headline number.

It is also late. Novo Nordisk launched an oral GLP-1 in December, and Eli Lilly has its own pill, Foundayo, which Lilly says is favoured by patients who want convenience and moderate weight loss. AstraZeneca is running other obesity candidates, including AZD6234 and combinations. This looks like an effort to build a portfolio rather than win a single race.

Medicare supplies the demand

The demand side is clearer. Lilly chief executive David Ricks said roughly 700,000 seniors have started GLP-1 therapy since Medicare's temporary Bridge programme opened on 1 July, and that about 70% of them chose a Lilly drug. Enrolees pay $50 a month. Manufacturers supply the drugs at a net price of $245 a month, which leaves about $195 a month per patient for Medicare to cover. The programme is set to run to the end of 2027.

The figures cut both ways. Volume is arriving in a demographic that was under-served when patients paid for these drugs themselves. But the net price of $245 a month is well below earlier list prices, and the Lilly share of 70% leaves the rest of the market to Novo and any newcomers. Nothing in the available reporting says what role AstraZeneca would have. It has no obesity drug on the market, so for now the 700,000 is a measure of the market it hopes to enter, not of its own revenue.

The $2bn cheque: oncology, not obesity

The Summit deal is a straight equity purchase. AstraZeneca is buying roughly 109,000 convertible preferred shares, each convertible into 1,000 common shares, at $18.36 per common-equivalent share. That is a 10% premium to Summit's five-day weighted average price, and it gives AstraZeneca about 12% of the company. There is no licensing agreement. Summit keeps full commercial rights to ivonescimab, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody.

The money solves a specific problem. Summit reported under $690m in liquid assets in July, and the market had been pricing in a financing overhang. The investment brings its cash to about $2.5bn, enough to run Phase 3 trials. The companies will also test ivonescimab alongside AstraZeneca's CLDN18.2-targeting antibody-drug conjugate, sonesitatug vedotin, in gastrointestinal cancers. BMO Capital Markets read the deal as a sign of pharma's interest in the PD-1/VEGF class before it secures direct commercial rights.

Summit's shares rose nearly 20% on the news, according to Benzinga. That sits awkwardly with the idea that investors are ignoring AstraZeneca's vote of confidence. The stock had been marked down because of the funding risk, and the cheque removes it. Whether the shares still trade below analysts' price targets is the triage claim and could not be confirmed from the sources reviewed. Even if it holds, a discount to targets after a 10% premium paid by a strategic buyer is not obviously a mispricing. Targets often assume the drug works.

What ties the threads together

AstraZeneca is spending on two different bets. The first is a cardiometabolic pill with undisclosed efficacy, entering a market that Medicare has made larger and Lilly has largely claimed. The second is a minority stake in a cancer-drug developer, which buys exposure without paying for control. Neither is a call on GLP-1 valuations as such.

The next data points are specific. The first is the Phase 3 start for EMBOLD and ELUMINATE, and whether AstraZeneca finally publishes the weight-loss figures. The second is the closing of the Summit investment, which was expected within about a week of announcement. The third is Summit's Phase 3 ivonescimab readouts, which will settle whether $18.36 a share was cheap.
