Floating scarecrows help Cornish fishers stop seabirds drowning in nets

In one sentence
A two-year Cornish trial found a floating 'scarecrow' buoy and a bird-of-prey kite cut guillemot and razorbill net deaths by 59% and 71%, pointing to cheap compliance options.

Seabirds dive for fish, and gillnets hang in the water where the fish are. Every year an estimated 95,000 diving seabirds, mostly guillemots and razorbills, are caught in gillnets around Europe's coasts and drown. Off Cornwall, a two-year trial has tested two cheap ways to scare them off: a floating scarecrow with a staring, spinning eye, and a kite shaped like a bird of prey.

Both cut the risk markedly. Birds were 59% less likely to be caught near the scarecrow and 71% less likely near the kite, according to results published in Conservation Letters and released by the University of Exeter.

Eyes on the water

The scarecrow is called a Looming Eye Buoy. It is a pole-mounted float whose spinning head produces an animated eye effect. The RSPB and Devon-based Fishtek Marine developed it together. The alternative is the "scarybird", a commercially available kite in the shape of a raptor, flown over the nets.

Ten Cornish fishers took part. Cameras fitted to their boats recorded what came up in the nets with and without a deterrent, which gave the researchers a controlled comparison rather than anecdote. Most of the catches happened in winter.

That seasonality is the practical point. Tom Hooper of Fishtek Marine said deterrent efforts can be "directed and efficient", aimed at "key locations at specific times" rather than applied across a whole fleet all year. Tom Horton of Exeter said "simple, practical deterrents can substantially reduce seabird bycatch in gillnet fisheries."

Yann Rouxel of the RSPB put the difficulty plainly: gillnets "have proved a real challenge to develop mitigation methods for." Trawl and longline fisheries have had more options. The work was funded by Defra's Fishing Industry Science Partnership and Schmidt Marine Technology Partners.

The compliance question

The commercial interest goes beyond the birds. Tightening rules on bycatch put small operators in a bind. Acoustic alarms and modified nets cost money, and a small boat has thin margins to absorb it. A buoy or a kite sits at the cheap end of the range. The sources reviewed for this piece do not give a price per unit, so the cost advantage is still a claim to test, not a figure.

The wider test is whether a commodity producer can meet environmental rules with a simple device instead of rebuilding its fleet. Two-thirds reductions are meaningful, but they are not elimination. Regulators will decide whether a cut of that size, in one county's winter fishery, counts as compliance.

What to watch

Two things will decide how far this travels. The first is whether the effect holds in other fisheries and with other species. The second is whether regulators write deterrents into formal bycatch standards, and at what level of reduction. The Cornish data answers the first question for one place and one pair of species. The second is still open.
