Duffy presses FAA to speed certification of new aircraft

In one sentence
Transportation Secretary Duffy urged the FAA to certify aircraft faster, but with the 737 MAX 10 held up over software, no new deadline backs the call.

The US Transportation Secretary wants aircraft certified faster, and he said so on the same week the regulator held back a Boeing jet over a software question. Sean Duffy told an audience in Washington on 30 September that long approval timelines discourage investment and leave American manufacturers exposed to Chinese competition. "We need to make sure we have safe aviation," he said, "but can we move faster?"

The remark is a prompt, not a policy. No new deadline, staffing commitment or rule accompanied it. For investors in Boeing, Airbus and their suppliers, the useful question is whether a mechanism exists behind the rhetoric.

The slow part of the pipeline

Certifying a new airliner can take five years or more, with extensive testing and documentation. The recent record is long even by that standard. Boeing needed more than eight years to win approval for the smaller 737 MAX 7, which was finally cleared in August. Certification of the 777X is more than five years behind Boeing's original schedule.

The larger 737 MAX 10 is the live case. On 28 September, according to AeroTime's account, the FAA held off approving it after finding software that could raise pilot workload during go-around manoeuvres. Duffy indicated he saw no actual safety issue. That puts the secretary's public position in some tension with the technical judgment of the agency he oversees, which is the tension the story turns on.

What the FAA has actually proposed

The concrete step predates Duffy's remarks. In June the FAA proposed modernising commercial certification by aligning more of its requirements with those of the European Union Aviation Safety Agency. The aim is to cut the exemptions, special conditions and equivalent-level-of-safety findings that manufacturers currently negotiate case by case.

According to reports, the changes would codify common exemptions in areas such as cabin interiors, emergency systems, fuel-tank assessments and thrust-reverser protections. Consistent requirements across the two largest regulators should lower the cost, time and complexity of approvals. The reported beneficiaries are Boeing, Airbus, Embraer and Bombardier. The coverage reviewed did not specify a comment deadline or quantify expected time savings.

Deputy FAA Administrator Chris Rocheleau said in June that the agency was "in the final stages of certifying the smaller MAX 7 and larger MAX 10." The MAX 7 has since cleared. The MAX 10 has not.

Reading the signal

The market-relevant point is that harmonisation helps future programmes more than present ones. Boeing's near-term delivery numbers depend on the MAX 10 and 777X, both already deep in review, and neither is likely to be accelerated by a change to the rules for new applications. Engine makers and suppliers are tied to the same schedules.

So a credible signal would look like one of three things: a final rule with a date, a revised MAX 10 approval timeline from the FAA, or a stated target for 777X certification. Political pressure alone has a mixed record. The FAA's caution since the 737 MAX accidents is institutional, and a public push to move faster raises the cost to the agency of any later finding that it moved too fast.

What to watch

The next data points are the FAA's resolution of the MAX 10 software question and any publication of the harmonisation rule's final text. If the former slips well past year-end, Duffy's remarks will read as posturing. If the agency clears the software issue quickly and cites the new framework, they will read as direction.
