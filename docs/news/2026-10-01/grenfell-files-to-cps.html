Grenfell police pass files on 20 organisations and 54 people to CPS

In one sentence
Met police have sent prosecutors files on 20 organisations and 54 people over the Grenfell fire; the CPS is expected to decide on charges around June 2027.

Police investigating the Grenfell Tower fire have handed prosecutors the last of their charging files, naming 20 companies and organisations and 54 individuals as suspects. The Metropolitan Police submission to the Crown Prosecution Service, reported on 1 October, moves the case from investigation to prosecutorial review. It comes nine years after the June 2017 fire in west London that killed 72 people.

What the files contain

The Met says the offences it has put to the CPS range from corporate manslaughter and gross negligence manslaughter to fraud and fraud-related offences, and misconduct in public office. Corporate manslaughter can only be brought against organisations. Gross negligence manslaughter and misconduct in public office apply to individuals. The mix suggests investigators are looking at both the firms that made, marketed and installed the cladding and insulation, and at the people and public bodies responsible for oversight.

The scale of the inquiry is unusual. Police say they examined the roles of more than 15,000 individuals and 700 organisations. The summary reports alone run to more than four million words across 12,600 pages, and reference more than 45,000 documents, images and exhibits. The 54 and 20 are therefore a narrow group drawn from a very large field.

A long road to a decision

Sending files is not the same as charging anyone. The CPS must decide, suspect by suspect and offence by offence, whether there is enough evidence for a realistic prospect of conviction and whether prosecution is in the public interest. Reporting on the submission says the CPS expects to announce charging decisions in June 2027, shortly before the tenth anniversary of the fire. Trials would follow well after that. Bereaved families have described the wait as unbearable.

The public inquiry has already given prosecutors a detailed account of what went wrong, including the materials used in the refurbishment and the conduct of the firms involved. Criminal proceedings face a different test, however. Prosecutors must prove individual knowledge and fault to a criminal standard, and a long chain of contractors, suppliers, certifiers and public bodies makes that hard.

Why it matters beyond the courtroom

For the construction and building-products industries, the submission turns reputational and civil exposure into a possible criminal one. A conviction for corporate manslaughter can bring unlimited fines. Any listed company among the 20 organisations has not been publicly identified in the sources reviewed here, and naming them would be speculation. Investors in building-materials and contracting groups with UK legacy exposure have a reason to watch the CPS timetable. Parent companies of subsidiaries involved in the refurbishment or cladding supply chain are the likeliest to be affected.

The case also tests the post-Grenfell regulatory framework. Building safety reforms have tightened rules on high-rise residential buildings, but prosecutions are the clearest signal to the industry of where legal responsibility sits.

What to watch

The next fixed point is the CPS decision, expected in June 2027. Before then, the Met and CPS may say more about how the review will be staffed and phased, and whether any decisions will be announced in stages. Companies named by media outlets as suspects may also respond or update investors.
