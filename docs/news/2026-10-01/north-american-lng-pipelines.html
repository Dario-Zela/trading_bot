North American LNG: Trump announces $54bn Alaska project as TC Energy advances Coastal GasLink Phase 2

In one sentence
Trump's $54bn Alaska LNG pledge from South Korea lacks financing, schedule and permits, while TC Energy's Coastal GasLink Phase 2 rests on a final investment decision and a 2027 start.

President Donald Trump on Wednesday announced a plan for South Korea to invest $54bn in an Alaska natural gas pipeline and export project, while in British Columbia TC Energy said the second phase of its Coastal GasLink pipeline will go ahead after its LNG Canada partners took a final investment decision on expanding the export plant. The two North American LNG stories landed within days of each other. They differ sharply in how much of the plan is committed.

The Alaska plan

According to the White House, the Alaska line would run more than 800 miles from the North Slope to a liquefaction terminal on the state's southern coast. It would carry roughly 3.9 billion cubic feet of gas a day and produce as much as 20 million tonnes of LNG a year. The $54bn is a slice of the $200bn South Korea pledged under the US-Korea trade and investment agreement announced last year. The same package covers eight large nuclear plants and a power facility in Texas.

What the announcement did not carry is the usual apparatus of an investment decision. The accounts reviewed do not disclose a binding financing agreement, a construction schedule or a regulatory path. A pledge drawn from a broader trade-deal envelope is a statement of intent, not a contract. Alaska gas pipelines have been proposed for roughly half a century, and a project of this size has to secure offtake buyers, permits and lenders before it counts as capacity.

The politics are not hidden. Republican Sen. Dan Sullivan, a vocal supporter of the project, faces a competitive November race against Democrat Mary Peltola. It was the second time this week Trump used foreign-investment pledges to showcase a state with a tight Republican Senate contest.

British Columbia: a firmer footing

TC Energy's announcement is less grand and more bankable. The existing Coastal GasLink line carries about 2.1 billion cubic feet a day over 670 kilometres from Dawson Creek to the LNG Canada plant at Kitimat. Phase 2 is expected to nearly double that through new compressor stations and upgrades along the existing route, rather than new right-of-way. Construction is slated to begin in early 2027, with service in the early 2030s.

The structure limits TC Energy's exposure. LNG Canada acts as Phase 2 Execution Manager, while Coastal GasLink remains owner, operator and permit holder, an arrangement designed to cap its capital commitments and its exposure to cost and schedule overruns. Peak construction is expected to employ up to 2,100 people across five sites. Phase 2 follows the positive final investment decision on the LNG Canada expansion, which is the trigger that Alaska lacks.

What the comparison shows

On the stated numbers, Alaska's 3.9 bcf a day is roughly double Coastal GasLink's current flow, yet it has a headline price and no schedule. Coastal GasLink Phase 2 has an FID, a start date and a risk-sharing structure, yet no published cost figure in the coverage reviewed. Neither project adds a molecule of LNG to the market before the 2030s, so neither bears on current gas prices. Markets, which already price in an administration favourable to energy exports, have little reason to move on the Alaska announcement alone.

The next data points are concrete. For Alaska, the test is whether Korean funding is converted into signed financing, offtake agreements and permit filings, and whether any of that survives the November election. For British Columbia, it is the early-2027 construction start and any cost disclosure from TC Energy.
