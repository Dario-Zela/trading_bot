SEC proposes widening retail access to private markets

In one sentence
The SEC has proposed letting CPAs, CFA charterholders and others qualify as accredited investors, plus performance fees and monthly repurchases, to open private markets to more retail investors.

The Securities and Exchange Commission has taken a step toward opening private markets to a much larger group of ordinary investors. On 30 September it voted to propose three changes: a wider route to "accredited investor" status, looser rules for funds that offer periodic liquidity, and a broader pool of clients who can be charged performance fees. Together they would change who gets to buy private-market assets and how those assets are packaged.

The common thread is a change in what the law uses to define a sophisticated investor. Today the test is mostly financial: $200,000 in annual income ($300,000 jointly) or $1m in net worth excluding a primary residence. The proposals would add a second test based on what a person knows, or at least on what they have been certified to know.

From wealth to credentials

The SEC has opened a 60-day comment period on whether individuals could qualify as accredited investors by holding certain credentials. The list under consideration includes the CPA licence, the CFA charter, the Certified Financial Planner designation, and the FINRA Series 79, 86 and 87 licences. The agency is also asking about a FINRA-developed exam that anyone could take, regardless of income or assets.

Chairman Paul Atkins framed it as a matter of principle: "Accredited investor access to private offerings should not be limited solely to individuals satisfying financial thresholds."

The reasoning is easy to follow. A junior CFA charterholder on a modest salary may understand a private-credit fund better than a retiree who sold a house. But whether a credential tracks sophistication in the specific risks of private assets is an empirical question, and the proposal reads as a request for evidence rather than a settled answer. Passing an exam shows that someone has studied valuation, disclosure and risk. It does not show that they can absorb losses, or that they will sit still when a fund gates redemptions. Wealth, crude as it is, doubles as a loss-absorption test.

Fees and liquidity

The second and third proposals deal with the structure of funds. On fees, the SEC would let advisers to registered funds earn performance fees, capped at 20% of net gains, subject to approval by a fund board with a majority of independent directors. It would also redefine "qualified client", the status that currently permits performance fees. The new definition would follow the Regulation D accredited-investor standard and drop the separate net-worth ($2.7m) and assets-under-management ($1.4m) tests, according to AltsWire's summary of the release. A client who qualifies as accredited, including through a credential, would then also be fee-eligible.

This matters for incentives. A performance fee is the standard way hedge funds and private-equity managers are paid, and the proposal is meant to attract managers who have avoided retail-facing vehicles because they could not charge it. The cost is that a fee tied to gains rewards risk-taking, and the board's independence is the main safeguard.

On liquidity, the SEC would amend Rule 23c-3, which governs interval funds. Those funds must offer to repurchase shares at set intervals, currently quarterly, semi-annually or annually. The proposal would add monthly intervals, allow a longer deferral before the first repurchase offer, permit more frequent discretionary repurchases, and allow deferred sales loads to be deducted from repurchase proceeds. It would also allow multiple share classes for closed-end funds and move toward a principles-based approach to liquidity, in place of fixed requirements.

Monthly repurchases at net asset value make an illiquid strategy look more like a mutual fund. That lowers the barrier to entry, but it also sharpens a mismatch. A fund holding loans or private stakes that take years to sell is promising investors frequent access to cash. Net asset values for such holdings are estimates, not market prices, so a monthly exit window could be priced off marks that lag reality.

What is at stake

The commercial winners would be wealth managers and platforms that already sell alternatives to accredited clients and would gain a larger customer base. The risk is contagion. If sentiment turns, more retail money in less-liquid, less-transparent vehicles could produce a rush of repurchase requests, forced asset sales or gated funds, which would transmit stress into markets that currently have few retail participants. The SEC's own words about the scale of retail demand are not in the release summaries reviewed here, and estimates of the capital that could move are the industry's, not the agency's.

The next data points are procedural. The 60-day comment period on the accredited-investor question runs from Federal Register publication, and the fund-rule proposals will draw their own comments. Whether the credential test survives will depend on what commenters, including investor-protection groups, say about whether a CFA charter is a fair proxy for the ability to hold an illiquid asset through a downturn.
