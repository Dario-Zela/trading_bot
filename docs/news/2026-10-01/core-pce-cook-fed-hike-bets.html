Cooler core PCE trims Fed hike bets; Cook says inflation is 'too high for too long'

In one sentence
A core PCE undershoot lowered bets on further Fed tightening under Warsh, while Cook said inflation is too high for too long; Micron's results are seen as a test of the AI trade.

A softer-than-expected reading of core inflation has taken some of the heat out of the argument over further Fed rate rises. The data left investors asking a second question: if borrowing costs stop climbing, does the heavy spending on artificial intelligence still look as secure as the market has been pricing it?

The core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) index, the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge, came in below consensus. Markets responded by trimming the probability of additional tightening under Fed chair Kevin Warsh. The Nasdaq 100 rallied, the usual response when the expected path of rates shifts lower and long-duration growth stocks benefit from a lower discount rate.

What the data changed

Core PCE strips out food and energy and is the number policymakers watch when judging underlying price pressure. A miss against forecasts matters less for the level it reports than for what it does to the distribution of outcomes. Traders had been allowing for the chance that the Fed would raise rates again. A cooler print makes that less likely, and it lowers the financing costs that matter most to companies whose valuations rest on earnings years away.

One soft reading does not make a trend, and the data do not say the Fed is finished. They say the case for another increase is weaker than it was a day earlier. The distinction matters, because Fed officials are speaking as though the debate is open.

Cook: 'too high for too long'

Governor Lisa Cook offered the most pointed illustration of that debate. She reaffirmed the Fed's 2% inflation target and said inflation has been too high for too long. She also stressed that the labour market cannot be sacrificed to get there.

The second half of that statement is the notable one. Cook framed labour-market strength as a constraint on policy, to be weighed alongside price stability rather than traded away for it. Her remarks fit a wider concern at the central bank that further tightening risks significant job losses. Read together with the PCE miss, they suggest the hurdle for another hike is rising: the inflation data are less alarming, and the officials who might otherwise argue for more tightening are openly counting its employment cost.

That is an interpretation, not a signal of imminent cuts. Cook did not abandon the inflation target, and nothing in her remarks points to easing. The shift is in the balance of risks she described.

Micron as the test

The rate relief arrives just before Micron reports earnings, which investors are treating as a read on whether AI spending forecasts still hold. The memory maker supplies chips central to AI data-centre buildouts, so its results are an unusually direct view of whether customers are still spending at the pace the sector's valuations assume.

The logic runs both ways. Looser financial conditions flatter growth stocks regardless of what happens to demand, so a rally on a cooler PCE print says little about the strength of the AI trade itself. If Micron shows capital spending is still robust, even with rate relief in the background, that supports the sector thesis. If the results disappoint, they would suggest part of the recent strength rested on assumptions that falling rate fears were only temporarily masking.

The issue connects to a broader wariness about the AI boom. The Bank of England's Andrew Bailey has warned that it could trigger market shocks, though he said regulating AI is not the right place to start.

What to watch

The immediate catalyst is Micron's report and, in particular, what management says about customer spending plans. After that, the next inflation and labour-market releases will show whether the PCE miss was a one-off or the start of a pattern, and whether Cook's balancing language becomes the Fed's consensus view.
