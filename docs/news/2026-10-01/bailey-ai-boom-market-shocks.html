Bailey warns AI boom could trigger market shocks, but says regulating AI is 'not the right place to start'

In one sentence
Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey says AI-driven valuations could see a market correction, but argues testing and intervention capacity should come before AI-specific regulation.

The Governor of the Bank of England has warned that the money pouring into artificial intelligence could end in a market shock, and in the same breath has argued against writing AI-specific rules. Andrew Bailey said the Bank is watching AI investment "very carefully" and that "you could see some correction of asset prices at some point." Regulating AI itself, he said in a first-ever Substack article published on 30 September, "is not the right place to start."

The two messages are consistent. Bailey treats AI as a source of financial-stability risk to be monitored and stress-tested. He does not treat it as a category of activity needing its own rulebook.

The valuation warning

Bailey's market concern rests on scale. Nvidia is valued at about $5.5tn (£4.14tn). Alphabet, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon are each spending hundreds of billions of dollars on AI infrastructure. Anthropic and OpenAI are preparing stock-market debuts that are expected to draw hundreds of billions more from investors, according to the BBC's account of his remarks.

He did not forecast a crash or say that prices are wrong. His claim was narrower: AI may well lift economic growth, but "not everybody always wins," and a repricing is a plausible path. He reached for the dotcom-era precedent. "Google was not the first market leader in internet search," he said. "It was Netscape. Nobody can remember Netscape today."

The point is about dispersion. In a technology boom the sector can be right about the technology and still wrong about which firms capture the returns. Valuations that assume every large spender wins are exposed when some do not.

He named two further risks beyond asset prices. One is cyber vulnerability, with AI tools used to find and exploit weaknesses. The other is deepfakes. Bailey said he was himself the subject of faked images in June that depicted him with Nigel Farage.

Why regulation is 'not the right place to start'

The regulatory half of the message is the more unusual one. Bailey acknowledged that AI risks are "real and increasingly significant." He rejected halting or prohibiting development: "on the contrary, the benefits are immense." He argued that policymakers should begin with "rigorous" testing to find vulnerabilities, and with safeguards to contain them. A system of intervention and clear boundaries for AI behaviour should come before any formal framework.

He did not rule out rules later. A more formal framework "may well emerge over time," he wrote, and testing "should not be seen as an alternative to future regulation." The sequencing is the substance: "understanding, testing and establishing credible points of intervention must come first."

His sharpest concern was about frontier systems that learn and improve on their own. Without intervention points, he warned, these could become "a closed loop in which the model progressively governs itself." The UK's AI Security Institute is the body that would support this testing-first approach.

The source reporting does not say how the Bank would apply this to trading models or valuation tools, or whether it would use existing macroprudential, conduct or capital rules. That reading is an inference from the stance. A governor who says regulation is not the starting point, and who frames the task as testing and intervention capacity, is most plausibly relying on existing supervisory machinery for now. The Bank has not said so in the material reported.

The contrast with Washington and the labs

The BBC placed Bailey's position against a divided international backdrop. Leaders at AI companies including Anthropic and OpenAI have called for development to slow. President Trump has rejected slowdown proposals, citing America's competitive lead. After meeting technology executives he said they had signed a "morally binding" document on AI risks, which is a pledge and not a rule.

Bailey sits between these poles. He is neither asking for a slowdown nor endorsing a hands-off approach. He wants the capacity to test and to intervene built first.

He also described AI as a tool for the Bank itself. It could speed up work supporting the Monetary Policy Committee's rate decisions, he said, as an aid and not as an autonomous decision-maker.

What it means for market participants

For firms deploying AI in trading, risk or valuation, the practical reading is cautious. No new AI-specific guardrail was announced, and none was previewed with any date. But the Governor has put the Bank's attention on two things: how concentrated and richly priced AI-linked assets are, and how robust AI systems are under stress. Firms should expect supervisors' questions to focus on those areas, even without new rules.

The stability risk he describes is also a pricing question. A correction in AI-linked equities would hit portfolios and the banks that lend against them. It would also bear on how readily the pending Anthropic and OpenAI listings could be absorbed.

Several things bear on what comes next. One is the timing and reception of those flotations, which will be the first large market test of the valuations Bailey is watching. Another is whether the Bank turns his call for testing into concrete exercises, for instance in its Financial Policy Committee work, and whether the AI Security Institute's testing role expands. A third is whether Bailey's "over time" caveat on formal regulation acquires a timetable. None has been set.
