Christa Pike survives two lethal injections, lawyer says

In one sentence
Tennessee gave Christa Pike two doses of pentobarbital, her lawyers say, but she survived and was hospitalised; the governor has cancelled the state's remaining 2026 execution pending a review.

Tennessee tried to execute Christa Pike on Wednesday night and did not succeed. The state gave the 50-year-old two full doses of pentobarbital, according to her lawyers, and her heart did not stop. She was taken from the prison by ambulance and is being treated at a nearby hospital. Her legal team said in a statement: "The State of Tennessee once again failed to carry out a lawful execution."

The word "again" matters. The Tennessee Department of Correction (TDOC) had already failed once this year, in May, when the execution of Tony Carruthers was halted after a doctor could not establish a central line. Pike's case is different in kind. By the account of media witnesses, the drugs were delivered and the condemned person survived them.

A day of reversals

Pike was convicted of the 1995 murder of Colleen Slemmer, 19, a fellow student at a job-training programme in Knoxville. Pike was 18 at the time. She and her boyfriend, Tarayl Shipp, who was 17 and received a life sentence, attacked Slemmer. A third participant, Shadolla Peterson, turned state's witness. Pike was due to become the first woman executed in Tennessee in more than 200 years. Only 18 women have been put to death in the US since 1976.

Wednesday's proceedings were chaotic before the injections began. At about 9am the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit stayed the execution, 2-1, to consider whether Pike's allegations of childhood sexual abuse had been fully weighed at sentencing. At a special master hearing in August a state lawyer had said: "The state does not dispute the terrible things that Miss Pike suffered." At 5:50pm the US Supreme Court vacated the stay, 6-3. The three liberal justices dissented, arguing the order "unnecessarily prevents" lower courts from considering her appeals.

What the witnesses saw

The Nashville Banner's timeline, drawn from media witnesses at Riverbend Maximum Security Institution, shows how the evening unfolded. Pike spoke at length and sang with her spiritual advisor. She said she would leave the world "with love" and was "at peace." She also complained of pain in her arm and asked, "Does it happen like that?"

At 7:46pm the curtain to the witness room closed after her breathing became rhythmic. Three minutes later it reopened to admit her spiritual advisor, which suggested to observers that the department meant to try again. It closed a second time at 8:05pm, when Pike began snoring. At 8:53pm the chamber microphone went dead and the warden asked witnesses to leave. Pike was still breathing. An ambulance and a fire truck arrived at 9:15pm and the ambulance left at 9:28pm.

"It appears this did not go how this was supposed to go," said John North of WBIR, one of the witnesses. "We have a lot of questions. And the Department has a lot of answers they need to provide."

Tennessee's protocol calls for two 50-millilitre syringes of pentobarbital. Pike's lawyers say she received both. The Banner reports that the protocol has no procedure for what happens if both doses are given and the inmate survives. TDOC said after 11pm that it "followed every step of the State's lawful, established execution protocol" and that Pike had been moved to an off-site medical facility.

Pike's attorneys allege difficult vein access, blown veins, degraded pentobarbital and a lack of emergency medical care. These are allegations from one side, and the state has not accepted them. If the drug was compromised, that points to supply and storage. If the venous access failed, that points to training and personnel. Either would be a defect in the system and not a one-off.

Why it reaches beyond one prisoner

Botched executions are not new, and courts have tolerated them. Reporting on the Pike case notes that seven other people have survived medical problems after execution teams failed to find a vein. None is reported to have stayed alive after receiving the full drugs.

That distinction bears on the legal test. Since Baze v. Rees (2008) and Glossip v. Gross (2015), US courts have required prisoners to show a substantial risk of severe pain and to propose a feasible alternative method. A pattern of failure within a single state in a single year, one that fails at the point of access and now at the point of effect, is the kind of evidence challengers have lacked. The two Tennessee failures come under the same protocol and the same department. Lawyers will argue they show a protocol that does not work as written.

There is a practical question too. Pentobarbital is the drug most US states use, and states have often kept secret where it comes from and how it is stored. If the drug's potency is in doubt, the issue extends beyond Tennessee.

The state's response so far has been cautious. Governor Bill Lee has called off Tennessee's remaining execution for 2026 and asked for a third-party review of the process. The review's scope, its leadership and whether its findings will be published have not been announced.

What to watch

Three things will determine what follows. The first is Pike's medical condition, and with it the question of whether Tennessee could seek to carry out the sentence again. Her lawyers will almost certainly argue that a second attempt after two failed ones is unconstitutional. The second is the terms of the independent review, and whether it can obtain the drug's testing records and the execution team's training files. The third is the pending Sixth Circuit appeal on Pike's sentencing, which the Supreme Court's ruling did not resolve. Courts in other states with pending executions will also be asked to weigh Tennessee's record in their own proceedings.
