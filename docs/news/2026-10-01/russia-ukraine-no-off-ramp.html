Putin shows no sign of stopping as Russia doubles down on Ukraine

In one sentence
Russia is building up for a longer war, not a settlement, leaving Europe with higher defence budgets and a lasting energy-cost burden.

Seven months into what Western officials once hoped might be a negotiating year, there is little sign in Moscow of a leader looking for a way out. Russia's war in Ukraine is now in its fifth year, and the most recent signals from both sides point to a longer conflict, not a settlement. For Europe, that means paying for it on two fronts at once: in the budgets of its armed forces and in the price of its energy.

Preparing for escalation

In August, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said Russia was readying itself for a harder phase of the war, not for talks. He listed rising ballistic-missile production, North Korean equipment arriving in Russia and preparations for mobilisation.

"Every step Russia takes — increasing ballistic missile production, bringing in North Korean equipment, preparing for mobilization — all of this shows that Moscow is preparing not for peace, but for escalation."

That account comes from Kyiv, which has an interest in presenting Russian intentions at their darkest, and it should be read that way. The evidence for it is circumstantial: a state that is expanding missile output and importing foreign materiel is building for a long campaign. Nothing in the public record points to a Kremlin that is conserving resources for a deal. This piece could not independently confirm the latest battlefield and economic details beyond those reports.

Europe's bill

The clearest measurable consequence so far is military spending. NATO figures released in July, ahead of the alliance's summit in Ankara, show European members' spending in 2026 running more than 90% higher in nominal terms than in 2022. Total spending by the alliance's European members is expected to reach about $639bn this year, with well over a third going to procurement and research and development. European allies and Canada raised core defence spending by roughly 20% in real terms in 2025 alone, which NATO describes as the sharpest annual rise among European members since 1953.

Those are commitments that outlast any single ceasefire. Procurement contracts for munitions, air defence and armour run for years. Governments that have signed them have little incentive to unwind them even if the fighting stops, which is why defence contractors have been treated by investors as a structural holding and not a trade tied to headlines.

The energy side is harder to pin down. Europe cut most of its dependence on Russian pipeline gas after 2022 and has leaned on liquefied natural gas instead, which exposes it to global competition for cargoes. Higher costs are already visible in household bills: UK energy bills face their biggest rise in four years, as covered elsewhere in this edition. A war with no end in sight keeps a risk premium in European gas pricing and keeps the region's industrial users exposed to supply shocks, though this reporting did not turn up a current price figure to size that premium.

What the duration assumption means

For anyone pricing commodity hedges or sector positions, the working question is how long the war lasts, not whether it ends this quarter. The evidence above argues for the long case. Russia shows no reduced commitment, Kyiv is warning of escalation and European governments are budgeting as if the confrontation will persist for years.

The next markers are concrete. Winter heating demand will test Europe's gas storage and its LNG supply. Any Russian mobilisation order would confirm Kyiv's warning and mark a real change in Moscow's posture. And budget announcements from European capitals this autumn will show whether this year's defence surge is the new baseline or a peak.
