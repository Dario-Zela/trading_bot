Tuchel: impossible for England to find another Kane

In one sentence
Tuchel says England will not find a copy of the 33-year-old Harry Kane and must look for different solutions, with Bellingham up front one idea he has floated.

England's manager has been asked, again, who comes after Harry Kane. His answer was that nobody does.

"You will not get another Harry Kane, first of all," Thomas Tuchel said this week. "We look for different options and solutions. There will be someone else, but it will not be Harry or a copy of Harry."

The remark is candid, but it is not news. It restates a constraint English football has lived with for years. Kane is 33, captains the side, and plays his club football at Bayern Munich, where Tuchel coached him. By Tuchel's account he is a "complete package", an elite finisher who also does a great deal of unglamorous running. Reports differ on his club tally last season, with figures of 61 and 73 goals across all competitions in circulation. Either way, he is in the form of his career.

The gap behind him

The problem is less that England lack strikers than that none of them has yet shown Kane's range. A centre-forward who scores, drops deep to link play and presses from the front is a rare thing. Few players of any nationality do all three at the top level. England have had one for a decade, and the habit of relying on him has made the question of what follows him easy to postpone.

Tuchel's reply suggests he is not looking for a like-for-like successor. He is thinking about the shape of the team instead. One idea he has tried is moving Real Madrid's Jude Bellingham into the central attacking role, on the view that the midfielder has qualities that would let him play as an unconventional No. 9. That would mean redesigning the attack rather than casting a new lead. It is also a quiet admission that the usual route, finding the next out-and-out striker, is not obviously open.

What it means

There is no urgency here. Kane is fit, in form and nowhere near retirement, and Tuchel's comment lacks a catalyst beyond the routine press-conference question. Its value is as a signal. The manager is planning for a post-Kane England that plays differently, not one that simply swaps in a younger version of the same player.

That plan will be tested in the matches ahead, when Tuchel has the chance to try variations around Kane while he is still there to anchor them. The more useful evidence will be how any alternative forward, or a Bellingham experiment, performs when Kane is rested or absent. Until one of them produces numbers, the answer to Tuchel's own question stays the same: someone else, but not a copy.
