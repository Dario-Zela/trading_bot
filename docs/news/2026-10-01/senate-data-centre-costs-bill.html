US Senate set to block data-centre power-costs bill

In one sentence
The Senate voted 57-43 against advancing the Ratepayer Protection Act, short of the 60 needed, leaving data-centre power-cost allocation to the states and the next Congress.

The Senate has already voted down a bill that would have made large data centres pay for the grid upgrades they require. The triage premise, that the bill was merely "set to fail", is out of date. On Wednesday the chamber voted 57-43 on a motion to advance the Ratepayer Protection Act. That is three votes short of the 60 needed to overcome a filibuster. Whatever cost-allocation rules the AI industry ends up with, they will now come from state regulators and the next Congress, not from this one.

What the bill did

The Ratepayer Protection Act would amend the Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978. It would set a federal standard for "large-load customers", defined as non-residential users, mainly data-storage and computing facilities, with peak demand of 100 megawatts or more at a single site. Those customers would bear the full incremental cost of any generation, transmission or distribution upgrades needed to serve them.

The mechanism was soft. The bill asked state utility regulators to consider adopting the standard. It did not compel them to. That softness is what Democrats seized on, calling the text "toothless" and noting that it lacked enforcement provisions.

The vote

The House passed the measure earlier this month, 417-3, so the Senate outcome is a long way from a partisan split. Four Democrats voted to advance it: Maggie Hassan, Amy Klobuchar, Jon Ossoff and Raphael Warnock. The remaining Democrats voted no. Sponsors, led by Republican Senator Husted, framed the vote as a test of whether Congress would make data centres "pay their own way". Opponents said they would not vote for a bill with no enforcement behind it just before an election.

What it means for operators

For the hyperscalers, the failure cuts two ways, and the evidence supports neither extreme.

The first reading is that a threat has lifted. A mandatory federal cost-causation standard would have raised the price of connecting new load. By its own terms the bill would not have done that. It left the decision to states, and many of them are already acting on large-load tariffs. The market consequence of this vote is therefore small. It is not clear that any operator's modelled power costs changed.

The second reading is that uncertainty persists. No federal rule means no common template. Operators will keep negotiating tariffs, minimum-take contracts and interconnection terms state by state, while residential bills rise and become a campaign issue. The political pressure on utility commissions is likely to grow, not shrink, ahead of November's midterms. Whether power costs end up in operators' margins or in ratepayers' bills is still unsettled, and this vote did not settle it.

The bill's backers had hoped to give voters a visible "affordability" win before the election. Its collapse hands both parties a talking point and leaves the underlying economics where they were.

What to watch

The next catalysts are state-level large-load tariff decisions and any attempt to revive the measure, with tougher language, in the lame-duck session after the midterms. Separately, the Fed's inflation commentary and the company-level capex guidance due with third-quarter earnings should show whether operators are building higher power costs into their spending plans.
