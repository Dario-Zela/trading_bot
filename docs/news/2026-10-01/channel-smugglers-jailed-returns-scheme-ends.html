Six smugglers jailed for manslaughter over Channel disaster as UK-France returns scheme ends

In one sentence
A Paris court jailed six smugglers for six to ten years over the 31 deaths in the November 2021 Channel sinking, as the UK-France 'one in, one out' returns pilot expired.

A Paris criminal court has convicted six men of involuntary homicide — manslaughter, in British usage — over the deadliest small-boat crossing recorded in the Channel, and sentenced them to between six and ten years in prison. The verdicts, delivered on 30 September, came on the same day that the UK and France let their year-long "one in, one out" returns pilot lapse. The two events are formally unrelated. Together they show where Channel policy now stands: a bilateral returns mechanism that never reached scale has gone, and the courts are testing whether criminal liability can do some of the deterrent work.

The night of 24 November 2021

The dinghy went down in the early hours of 24 November 2021 after its occupants sent distress calls to both the French and British coastguards. It was overloaded and deflating in one of the world's busiest shipping lanes. Thirty-one people died, among them a seven-year-old child. Most were Iraqi Kurds; there were also Afghans, Egyptians and Iranian Kurds. Only two men survived, and four bodies were never recovered.

The charge sheet described a boat that was poor in quality, uncertified, unfit for the open sea, overloaded and without proper life jackets. After a four-week trial, the court found six defendants guilty of involuntary homicide. Seven others were convicted of lesser offences and received sentences of up to four years. One of the 14 defendants was acquitted.

The court said the defendants had profited from "the vulnerability of exile candidates". A lawyer for the victims' families put the case in blunter terms: "When it costs the lives of 31 people and the sea becomes a cemetery, there is a price."

The case is not closed. Seven French military personnel are separately under investigation for allegedly failing to help survivors. All deny wrongdoing.

Why the charge matters

Smuggling prosecutions on both sides of the Channel have mostly rested on immigration and facilitation offences: organising unlawful entry, running a network, laundering the proceeds. Those carry penalties, but they treat the crossing as the crime. A manslaughter conviction treats the death as the crime. The question the court answered is whether those who supplied and launched an unseaworthy boat bear responsibility for the foreseeable result.

The verdict does not make this routine. It came after nearly five years, in a case with an unusually large death toll and an unusually well-documented chain of events. Even so, it sets out a template that other prosecutors can follow. Whoever fills a boat beyond its capacity, or puts it to sea without safety equipment, can be charged with more than moving people.

The effect on deterrence is uncertain. Many of the people who run these networks are far from the beach and out of reach of any court. The defendants in the dock were the ones who could be found. Higher sentences matter to a smuggler only if arrest is a real prospect, and the evidence from five years of crossings is that it often is not. What the convictions do change is the legal exposure of mid-level organisers. They also move the public account of such deaths away from blaming migrants for the risks they accept, and away from treating them as unavoidable accidents.

A returns scheme that never scaled

The pilot was launched in August 2025 under Keir Starmer. Britain could return to France some people who arrived by small boat and had no right to stay, and in exchange it would accept an equal number of asylum seekers from France judged likely to qualify for protection. Between 4 August 2025 and 30 June 2026, 1,087 people were returned to France and 1,117 were transferred to the UK. The initial target was 2,600 over three years. Only about 4% of small-boat arrivals were returned, at an average cost reported at €65,500 per return.

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood said the numbers were "still relatively small" and that the harder part had been to "scale up very large numbers of removals every week into the French system". Detention capacity was one constraint. France reportedly wanted to end the arrangement and to negotiate a Europe-wide deal, and the prime minister, Andy Burnham, had tried to save it in talks with Emmanuel Macron in September. The scheme no longer accepts new cases, though flights for people already detained will continue.

Crossings have fallen even without the scheme doing much of the work. Some 19,135 people had crossed in 2026 by late September, a fall of 42% on the previous year. Twenty-two deaths have been recorded in Channel crossings this year.

What comes next

The government now has no bilateral returns channel with France, and any replacement is tied to a wider EU negotiation that France has said it wants. The French side has two things to watch: whether the convicted men appeal, and the outcome of the inquiry into the military personnel. The wider test is whether prosecutors in France and Britain bring homicide charges in other crossing deaths, and not only in the largest one.
