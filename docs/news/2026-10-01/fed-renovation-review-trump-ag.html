Fed review finds no misconduct in HQ renovation; Trump asks AG to study it

In one sentence
The Fed's inspector general found management failures but no crime in the roughly $1bn headquarters overrun, and Trump has asked AG Blanche to study the report anyway.

President Trump has asked Attorney General Todd Blanche to "study" a Federal Reserve watchdog report on the renovation of the central bank's Washington headquarters. The report found no basis for a criminal referral. The inspector general's review, published on 30 September, found management failures but no crime. The president responded on Truth Social by reviving his call for Jerome Powell's removal.

What the report found

The Fed's inspector general examined the overhaul of the Eccles building and the neighbouring Martin building. The central finding was blunt: "At no point during our evaluation did we find reasonable grounds to believe that a violation of federal criminal law had occurred requiring a referral to the U.S. Attorney General."

The cost findings were less comfortable for the Fed. According to the figures reported, the project's budget stood at $1.317bn in February 2020 and had risen to $2.381bn by December 2024. Construction costs alone more than doubled, from $921m to $2.018bn. That makes the overrun closer to $1bn than to the larger totals some headlines carried: roughly $1.06bn on the approved budget, and more against the original estimate for construction.

The inspector general blamed governance rather than misconduct. The Board used a contract framework designed to prevent earlier mistakes but did not apply cost controls. Project oversight was "fundamentally insufficient" for work of this complexity, and the Fed repeated errors from the earlier Martin building renovation. The recommendations are procedural: negotiate guaranteed maximum prices, set clear completion metrics, reconcile services received against billing, and create an oversight body that reports directly to the Board.

The political response

Trump wrote that he had asked Blanche to "study the report, and make a determination as to what to do about" a building he described as hundreds of millions of dollars over budget and, by the report's account, costing at least $2.5bn. He added that "This is Jerome Powell's fault, and he should be forced to resign, IMMEDIATELY!" The Fed chair had not commented on the report at the time of publication.

The request has a history. The Justice Department previously ran a criminal inquiry that alleged Powell had committed perjury over the renovation. That investigation was dropped in April after a judge quashed subpoenas issued by US attorney Jeanine Pirro. Senate Banking Committee chairman Tim Scott took a different line, welcoming accountability and noting that "Inflation does not change the Fed's responsibility to manage its resources prudently."

What it means for markets

The report itself is a governance story, and it offers little that moves rates. The market-relevant element is the pressure. A watchdog with access to the Fed's books has looked for criminality and found none, and the White House is asking the Justice Department to look again. For traders, that keeps Fed independence on the list of tail risks rather than resolving it, though there is no sign yet of a pricing effect. Other desks in this edition cover the near-term policy backdrop, including cooler core PCE and Governor Cook's remarks on inflation.

The next signal is whether Blanche's office opens any formal review, and whether it relies on the inspector general's findings or revisits the perjury theory that a court has already set back. Powell's response, when it comes, will also show whether the Fed accepts the inspector general's oversight recommendations.
