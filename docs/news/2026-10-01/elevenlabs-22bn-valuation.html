ElevenLabs doubles valuation to $22bn

In one sentence
ElevenLabs has doubled its valuation to $22bn via a $300m share sale led by Wellington and T. Rowe Price, though the company has published no headline revenue to test the price.

ElevenLabs, the London-founded voice-AI company, has doubled its valuation to $22bn in seven months. The mark came through a $300m share sale rather than a conventional fundraising. The previous reference point was the $11bn valuation set in its $500m Series D in February.

The structure matters. Wellington Management and T. Rowe Price led the transaction, which was reported as a tender: employees get liquidity, and the price is set by what incoming investors will pay. A tender raises little or no new money for the company. It does produce a clean, headline-ready price. Goldman Sachs, Singapore's GIC, EQT, Ontario Teachers' Pension Plan, Sapphire Ventures and BDT & MSD came in as first-time holders. Andreessen Horowitz, Lightspeed and Iconiq, existing backers, also took part.

What the company says it is selling

ElevenLabs began with text-to-speech and dubbing, and it now leans on a conversational-agent product, Eleven Agents. Annual recurring revenue from that platform has more than tripled since February, according to the company. It now handles more than 15 million conversations a week, also about three times the February volume.

Enterprise customers account for 55% of revenue, with a target of 60% by year-end. Named clients include Stripe, Deutsche Telekom and Admiral, plus the governments of Ukraine and Greece. The company has more than 800 staff.

Chief executive Mati Staniszewski framed the sale as a reward for staff: "As we grow, it's important to us to let our people share in some of the value they are creating for our customers and the wider world." He has also said the company aims to be ready for a possible listing within two to two and a half years. It hired Adyen's former finance chief in September, a hire that fits that ambition.

How much weight the number can bear

The figures above are growth rates and mix, not totals. The company has not, in the coverage reviewed, published a headline revenue figure for the new valuation, so the multiple cannot be calculated from public data. Triple-digit growth in one product line says little about group margins, or about how much of the revenue is usage-based and exposed to cheaper rivals.

Private marks also behave differently from public ones. A tender among a small group of institutions sets a price for a small slice of equity, and that price can run ahead of what a broader market would pay. Investors that join a tender often do so partly to secure a place in a later offering.

With those caveats, the direction is clear enough. Seven months of doubling, led by two large public-market mutual-fund managers, points to conviction in voice as a category where products can be defended and sold to enterprises. Press coverage places $22bn just below Mistral's reported $24bn, which puts a London voice specialist in the same bracket as a general-purpose model lab. That sits awkwardly with the wider caution over AI profitability, and with the Bank of England governor's warning on AI-driven market shocks (see related coverage).

What to watch

The test is disclosure. A listing within the stated timeframe would force audited revenue, margins and customer concentration into view. Before that, the next signals are whether the Eleven Agents growth rate holds as the base grows, whether the enterprise share reaches 60% by December, and whether the Series D investors from February mark their own holdings to the new price.
