UK income growth revised up, but household energy bills face the biggest rise in four years

In one sentence
UK household incomes were revised higher, but a forecast 4% rise in energy bills, the largest in four years, complicates the Bank of England's path and raises pressure on the Budget.

Two pieces of UK economic news landed on the same day, and they pull in different directions. Household incomes turned out to be stronger than the official figures had shown. Household energy bills, meanwhile, are forecast to rise by about 4%, the largest increase in four years. The first makes the second easier for families to absorb. It also makes it harder for the Bank of England to look past.

The bills

The forecast rise of roughly 4% is modest next to the spikes of the early 2020s. Its significance is in the sequence: it would be the biggest annual step up in four years, and it arrives after a stretch in which energy had stopped being the main driver of UK inflation. The move comes against volatile global energy markets and supply constraints, which are outside Westminster's control.

Energy matters to inflation out of proportion to its share of the shopping basket. It is bought by every household, it is hard to cut back on, and its price feeds into what households expect inflation to do next. A 4% rise in bills is a small number in isolation. Its effect on expectations depends on whether it looks like a one-off or the start of a run.

The political consequence is already visible. Chancellor Healey is under pressure to announce energy support measures in the coming Budget. That puts the Treasury in a familiar bind. Support for bills cushions households. It also costs money, and depending on its design it can add to demand at a moment when the central bank is trying to cool price growth.

The income revision

The other half of the picture is the Office for National Statistics revising household income growth higher than it had previously estimated. The revision points to a stronger household financial position than the earlier data showed. It cuts against the narrative of an imminent collapse in household spending, which had been a staple of recession warnings.

Markets treated it as news. Gilts and sterling repriced, which reflects a recalibration of expectations for Bank of England policy tied to the strength of the consumer. The logic is simple. Households with more income than assumed can spend more, and stronger demand leaves less room for the Bank to cut rates. The revision gives forecasters ballast for continued demand projections.

The revision does not say how that income is distributed, and the triage material does not break it down. Aggregate income can rise while the households most exposed to energy costs see little of it. Lower-income families spend a larger share of their budgets on heating and power, so a 4% rise lands harder on them than the headline suggests.

Where the two meet

The two items interact. Stronger incomes lower the risk that higher bills push consumers into a sharp retrenchment, which is the benign reading. The less comfortable reading is that resilient demand plus a fresh energy cost gives inflation fewer reasons to fall. For the Bank, an energy-driven rise in the headline rate is awkward whichever way it is read. If policymakers look through it as temporary, they risk being wrong about expectations. If they respond to it, they tighten against a supply shock.

The Budget is where the fiscal side of that choice gets made. Support for bills would signal that the Treasury is prepared to spend to protect households. It would also test how much room the public finances leave. A decision not to act would leave the Bank carrying more of the burden. Either outcome has consequences for sterling and for UK-listed companies sensitive to consumer spending.

What to watch

Three things will settle the question. The first is the shape of any energy support in the Budget: whether it is targeted or broad, and how it is funded. The second is the next inflation print, which will show how much of the energy pressure is already in the numbers. The third is the Bank of England's next rate decision, where the income revision and the bill forecast will have to be weighed together. Gilt yields will register the verdict before the official statements do.
