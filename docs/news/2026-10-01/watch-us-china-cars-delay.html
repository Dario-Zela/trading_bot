US-China: Senate vote on permanent Chinese-car ban slips to November

In one sentence
The Senate will not vote on a permanent Chinese-car ban before the elections after a holdout dispute over Mercedes-Benz, leaving the policy risk open into November.

A bill that would permanently bar Chinese-linked cars from the US market will not reach a Senate vote before the November elections. Sponsors failed to settle the objections of a single holdout. They now plan to try again when the chamber returns from recess in November.

The bipartisan measure is sponsored by Republican Bernie Moreno of Ohio and Democrat Elissa Slotkin of Michigan. It would ban the import, manufacture and sale of internet-connected vehicles, and of software and hardware tied to China or other "foreign adversaries", including Russia and North Korea. The Senate Commerce Committee approved it in July.

The holdout

The obstacle is Republican Rand Paul, who has argued the bill unfairly catches Mercedes-Benz. The committee text bars companies with more than 15% ownership by Chinese entities from selling vehicles in the US. Mercedes-Benz has nearly 20% passive Chinese ownership, so on a literal reading it would qualify.

Moreno has said the bill is not meant to ban Mercedes. Reports of the stalled talks suggest that assurance has not yet been turned into text that satisfies Paul. A drafting fix on the ownership threshold, or on how passive stakes are counted, is the likeliest route to a deal. Nothing in the reporting says one is close.

What the delay does to risk

The sector has had its pressure eased in the near term. No new restriction can arrive from this bill before the elections. That is a reprieve, not a resolution. The ban was stalled rather than defeated, and the sponsors say they will bring it back.

For trading books with China or auto exposure, the cost is a longer wait for clarity. The vote now falls after the midterms, so its fate may depend on a changed Senate. That makes the outcome harder to forecast through the fourth quarter. The reporting gives no price moves or named stocks. Any claim about how positions have shifted would therefore be inference. The ownership-threshold dispute does show that a European automaker, not only Chinese firms, sits inside the bill's potential reach.

What to watch

The date to watch is the Senate's return in November. The questions are whether revised language settles the Mercedes question and whether Paul's objection falls away. Floor time in a post-election session will also be limited, and a lame-duck calendar crowded with other business could push the bill back again.
