Swiss glaciers suffer 'disastrous' year of ice loss

In one sentence
Swiss glaciers lost 5.5% of their ice in 2026, the second-worst year on record, boosting short-term meltwater but eroding the long-term water base for Alpine hydropower.

Switzerland's glaciers lost 5.5% of their ice volume this year, the second-largest annual loss on record after 2022, according to the country's glacier monitoring network, GLAMOS. Researchers called the retreat "disastrous". The immediate effect is more water in the rivers and reservoirs that feed Alpine hydropower. The longer-term effect is the reverse, because the ice that supplies that water is running out.

A year of little snow and long heat

Two things drove the loss. The winter of 2025–26 was among the ten least snowy in Switzerland since measurements began, so glaciers entered the summer with a thin protective layer. Hot, dry weather then ran from May to September. For 76 days, heat waves pushed the freezing level above 4,000 metres, more than twice the average and a Swiss record. Melt reached even the highest elevations.

Glaciers thinned by an average of 2.5 to 4 metres, and some tongues lost as much as 10 metres of ice. The Rhône, Aletsch and Clariden glaciers each recorded their largest melt on record. Several small glaciers disappeared altogether, among them Bella Tola in Valais and Griessfirn in Glarus. Nearly a fifth of Swiss glacier volume has gone in the past five years.

What it means for water and power

From July to September the melt released about 2.2 trillion litres of water. That is more than four times what Swiss households drink in a year, and it eased the summer shortages that heat and drought would otherwise have caused. Researchers, including Matthias Huss of ETH Zurich, note that this contribution is dwindling as glacier area shrinks irreversibly.

The stakes for energy are large. Hydropower supplied about 57.6% of Swiss electricity generation in 2023, and Austria also leans heavily on Alpine water. Glacier melt tops up reservoirs through the summer. Those reservoirs are then drawn down in winter, when demand peaks and imports matter most.

The near-term picture is therefore not obviously bad for supply. Accelerated melt is filling storage faster, and this source did not report current reservoir levels, so the triage claim that storage is at a particular percentage of capacity could not be verified. The concern is structural. Studies cited by EPFL suggest that by 2100 around 30% less water will flow into Swiss hydropower reservoirs than today once the glaciers are gone. Lower snowfall, wetter winters and more frequent droughts compound the problem. Switzerland is exploring new dams and reservoirs on terrain the ice has exposed.

Why markets may care

A record melt year is, in one sense, a familiar pattern. Its energy relevance is the question of how soon the "peak water" from melting glaciers passes and inflows begin to fall. A single year cannot answer that, and this one does not show that storage is being exhausted faster than projected. It does add to the evidence that the easy surplus is being spent. Any weakness in winter hydro output would feed into European power prices at a time when households are already facing higher energy bills.

The next data points are the winter reservoir drawdown figures and the winter snowfall. A second thin snow season would leave glaciers with little protection going into summer 2027.
