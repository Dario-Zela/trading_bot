LVMH shares sink to six-year low as fashion demand stalls

Louis Vuitton's flagship store on the Champs-Élysées in Paris; the brand's fashion and leather goods division drives most of LVMH's profit. — Jorge Láscar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH stock hit a six-year low on stalling fashion demand, with investors watching Hermès and China spending data for signs of a turn.

LVMH shares dropped to their lowest level since 2020 this week, closing near €445 in Paris and extending a slide that has now wiped roughly a third off the stock's value this year. For the world's largest luxury conglomerate — owner of Louis Vuitton, Dior, Moët & Chandon and dozens of other marques — the retreat marks the clearest signal yet that the industry's post-pandemic growth story has stalled, not just paused.

In one sentence: LVMH stock hit a six-year low as fashion demand stays weak, while investors watch Hermès and China for signs the luxury slump might turn.

A one-quarter growth spurt wasn't enough

The irony is that LVMH's fashion and leather goods division — the group's profit engine, generating close to half of revenue and more than 70% of recurring operating profit — actually returned to growth last quarter. Organic sales rose 1% in the second quarter, ending seven straight quarters of decline. Group-wide organic growth came in at 3%, operating margin held at 22.5%, and net financial debt fell 19% to €8.25bn.

None of it was enough to stop the selling. As one market commentary put it bluntly: "One quarter of 1% growth from the fashion engine does not make a comeback." Investors who piled into luxury stocks on the assumption of a swift rebound have instead watched three successive false dawns — at the end of 2023, 2024 and 2025 — each built on hopes that Chinese stimulus would revive spending, each fading within months.

The China problem, in numbers

The latest disappointment traces back to China. Bernstein this week cited mall-sales data showing a sharp deceleration in Chinese luxury spending through June and July, with sales down 12% in July alone across price points and categories. The broker trimmed its 2026 and 2027 organic growth forecasts for LVMH by 66 basis points and cut earnings estimates by up to 6%, though it kept an Outperform rating with a €570 price target.

Barclays and Jefferies went further, downgrading the stock outright — Barclays to Equal Weight with a target cut to €835 from €932, Jefferies to Hold at €780 from €960. LVMH itself has reported essentially flat Chinese spending in the first half of the year, with what growth exists skewing toward prestige beauty rather than handbags and leather goods, the categories that actually move the group's earnings.

Beijing's own policy shift has compounded the problem. Since August, Chinese authorities have tightened controls on cross-border capital flows and pressed citizens to settle levies on offshore assets and investment gains — a squeeze that appears to have had, in Bernstein's words, "a chilling effect" on high-net-worth spending. Sales across the leading luxury houses in China fell more than 10% year-on-year in July, a sharper drop than June.

Hermès as the market's tell

If there is a bet still standing, it runs through Hermès. The Birkin-and-Kelly maker has held up better than peers through the downturn, and traders have treated its share price as a rough barometer for whether China's luxury consumer is turning a corner. It has not been a straight line: Hermès stock fell to a three-year low of €1,465.50 in late July before rallying more than 5% in a single August session on early signs of firmer Chinese demand, then dropping again after RBC downgraded it to "sector perform" from "outperform" on August 18, cutting its target to €1,700 from €1,900. Shares steadied later in August as some data pointed to renewed spending.

The whipsaw illustrates the market's current state of mind: no one disputes that the ultra-wealthy end of the Chinese luxury market is more resilient than the aspirational middle, where LVMH's Louis Vuitton and Dior are more exposed. The question is whether Hermès-style resilience is a leading indicator for the wider sector or simply evidence that only the very top of the market still spends freely.

What the discount is pricing in

LVMH now trades well below most analysts' fair-value estimates — one widely cited model puts the gap at roughly 27% — and the average 12-month price target across sell-side analysts sits near €566, more than a fifth above the current price. That gap is either an opportunity or a warning, depending on which side of the China debate an investor sits on. Bulls point to the fashion division's return to growth, steady margins and a cash-generative balance sheet. Bears note that higher Chinese taxes, softer aspirational demand and a valuation multiple built for double-digit growth all argue for shares staying cheap.

The next test arrives with the third-quarter sales update, expected in October, when investors will look for whether the fashion division's 1% uptick was the start of a trend or a rounding error. Until then, LVMH's share price is effectively a running vote on a single question: is China's wealthy consumer pulling back because of taxes, or losing interest in the goods altogether.
