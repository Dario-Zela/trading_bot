US auto industry group urges Congress to pass full ban on Chinese vehicles

A BYD Seal — the kind of low-cost Chinese EV gaining rapid market share in Mexico and Canada, fueling US industry anxiety — Evnerd / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Automakers led by the Alliance for Automotive Innovation are pressing Congress to pass a permanent Chinese vehicle ban by January 3, as BYD and Geely gain ground in Mexico and Canada.

The lobbying letter landed on Capitol Hill on Thursday with a blunt request: ban Chinese cars, permanently, before this Congress goes home.

The Alliance for Automotive Innovation — a trade group whose members include General Motors, Ford, Toyota, Volkswagen, Hyundai, Honda, Stellantis and Mercedes-Benz, covering most of the vehicles sold in the United States — wrote to congressional leaders urging a comprehensive, legislated ban on Chinese-made connected vehicles, along with their hardware and software, before the current session adjourns on January 3.

"Right now, Chinese automakers are dumping subsidized vehicles with connected software and hardware around the world," wrote John Bozzella, the alliance's chief executive. "This hasn't happened inside the U.S. yet, but given the scale and urgency of this threat, we urge you to enact a Chinese vehicle, software and hardware ban before adjourning this year and make this policy the law of the land." Passage, he added, would send "a clear and bipartisan message that China's strategy to dominate global automotive manufacturing will be met with a national security policy response."

From regulation to statute

The industry isn't starting from zero. In January 2025 the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security finalized a rule barring Chinese- and Russian-linked hardware and software from vehicle connectivity and automated-driving systems, with the software provisions taking effect for model year 2027 and hardware for model year 2030. What Bozzella's letter asks for is different in kind: turning that administrative rule into statute, which would make it far harder for a future administration to unwind.

The vehicle for that is a bill from Senators Bernie Moreno, an Ohio Republican, and Elissa Slotkin, a Michigan Democrat, which the Senate Commerce Committee approved in July. It would bar companies with more than 15% Chinese ownership from selling vehicles in the U.S. — a threshold that, as committee chair Ted Cruz has noted, could catch Mercedes-Benz, in which Chinese investors hold roughly 20% of shares held passively. That the alliance is lobbying on behalf of a bill that might sideline one of its own members says something about how seriously the industry rates the underlying threat; Bozzella has said the group wants to work with lawmakers on a "balanced policy" that spares companies like Mercedes while still closing the door to Chinese manufacturers proper.

Why the urgency now

Chinese automakers remain largely locked out of direct U.S. sales by existing tariffs, but the flanks are exposed. BYD now claims roughly 70% of electric and plug-in hybrid sales in Mexico, where nearly 100,000 Chinese EVs shipped last year alone; Canada has cut tariffs on an initial tranche of 49,000 Chinese EV imports from 100% to 6%, and BYD is opening 20 dealerships there this year while it studies a domestic manufacturing plant. Geely already has a foothold inside the U.S. itself, through its ownership of Volvo and Polestar — though Polestar is set to stop U.S. sales from the 2027 model year under the existing connectivity rule.

That pincer movement, more than any single event, explains the timing of the letter. Midterm elections in November will reshape the congressional math the bill needs to survive, and the alliance evidently wants a vote banked before campaign season swallows the legislative calendar.

What to watch

The Moreno-Slotkin bill still needs a floor vote in the Senate, House companion action, and a resolution of the Mercedes-Benz carve-out question before it could reach a president's desk this year. None of that is assured by January 3 — committee approval is a long way from enactment, and routine industry letters rarely move that timeline on their own. The nearer-term marker is whether the bill gets floor time before midterm campaigning crowds out the legislative session.
