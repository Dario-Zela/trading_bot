Sadiq Khan to have texts and emails searched in Palantir Met Police legal battle

Sadiq Khan, Mayor of London, whose digital communications will be searched as part of the Palantir disclosure process. — David Maynard / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Sadiq Khan will have his texts and emails searched after Palantir sued London's police authority over a blocked £50m Met AI contract, with a full hearing set for January 2027.

Sadiq Khan's phone is about to become evidence. The London mayor has agreed to let lawyers acting for Palantir Technologies search his texts, WhatsApp messages and Microsoft Teams chats as part of a High Court fight over a blocked £50 million policing contract — a sign that a procurement dispute which started as a City Hall memo has hardened into a full-blown legal siege.

The case centres on a deal the Metropolitan Police wanted to sign with Palantir in May to supply artificial intelligence tools for automating intelligence analysis in criminal investigations. The Mayor's Office for Policing and Crime, known as Mopac, stepped in and stopped it, saying the Met had breached procurement procedure by failing to present its strategy for approval and by dealing with only one potential supplier. Palantir is now suing Mopac, arguing the block amounted to an unlawful veto — one it says was actually driven by objections to the firm's "values and ethics," a factor it calls legally irrelevant.

Why Khan's inbox matters

Mopac initially resisted adding Khan as a "custodian" in the disclosure process, telling the court it wasn't necessary or proportionate since the actual decision was taken by the deputy mayor for policing and crime, not Khan himself. It relented after Palantir kept pressing. The search will cover Khan's communications from 1 September 2025 to 28 May 2026 — the run-up to and immediate aftermath of the block — alongside those of more than a dozen other officials.

That detail is the reason this brief procedural filing made headlines rather than staying buried in a court list: Palantir isn't just contesting a decision, it's trying to establish whether political considerations, rather than procurement rules, killed the deal. Mopac's defence, filed by Joseph Barrett KC, states plainly that "the decision was not taken on the basis of Palantir's 'values and ethics' or a mismatch between those values/ethics and the values of Londoners," pointing instead to what it calls the Met's "clear and serious breach" of process — and, more pointedly, an allegation that the force gave Mopac "inaccurate, misleading and/or inconsistent information."

A pattern of friction, not a one-off

For Palantir, the stakes go beyond one London contract. The company has built a lucrative government-and-defence franchise across the US, UK and elsewhere, but has also drawn recurring scrutiny in Europe over its ties to Israeli military operations and US immigration enforcement — the kind of reputational friction that appears to have shaped Mopac's original objection, whatever the legal pleadings now say about procedure. Losing a flagship UK policing deal, and choosing to fight it this hard, suggests the company sees the outcome as a template for future European public-sector bids rather than an isolated setback.

A full hearing is set for January 2027, before Mr Justice Constable. Until then, the disclosure process itself is doing double duty as a public airing of how City Hall and the Met talked to each other in the months before the block — arguably more revealing than the eventual verdict, since it will surface the internal exchanges both sides would rather have kept private.

The next marker to watch is the disclosure deadline itself: once Khan's messages and Mopac's internal files are handed over, the substance of what actually happened behind the block — political judgment or procedural failure — should start to become clear well before January's hearing.
