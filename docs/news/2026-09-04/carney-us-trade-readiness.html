Carney says Canada ready to strike US trade deal 'when Washington is ready'

Mark Carney at the White House in May 2025, before trade talks between Ottawa and Washington later broke down. — Official White House Photo / Gabriel B. Kotico
In one sentence
Carney said Canada will negotiate a US trade deal only if it brings stability and protects auto, steel and aluminum competitiveness.

Canada is open for business with Washington — but only on terms Ottawa can defend at home. Prime Minister Mark Carney, speaking at a news conference in Thunder Bay on Thursday, said his government remains ready to negotiate a new trade agreement with the United States, provided it delivers the "stability and credibility" that the last round of talks failed to produce.

"We're ready to sit down and strike that deal when the Americans are ready," Carney said, framing Canadian patience as a virtue rather than a retreat. He was explicit about where the line sits: any pact must keep Canada's automotive, steel and aluminum sectors competitive, including protections for the use of Canadian-made parts and materials — the industrial base most exposed to a tariff war that has already cost jobs on both sides of the border.

Talks that broke down, then got personal

The conditional tone traces back to August 21, when negotiations collapsed just before a midnight deadline that triggered US tariffs of 50% on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods. Carney said at the time that Washington "asked too much and offered too little," arguing the American proposal was uneconomic and called into question the reliability of any deal it might have produced. Canada answered with matching retaliatory tariffs and now faces a further threat: a 50% levy on Canadian-built vehicles and auto components from January 1, unless a new arrangement is reached.

The collapse produced a public spat as much as an economic one. US Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, joined by Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, argued on CNBC that Canada had blown up a nearly finished deal for domestic political reasons — pointing to Alberta's independence referendum in October and elections in Quebec. Carney dismissed the charge without much ceremony: "I don't think, with all respect, appointed, unelected cabinet members in the United States are experts on Canadian politics." He noted, pointedly, that Washington's own position had since softened, with earlier US demands over Canadian language and cultural protections no longer part of the conversation.

Hedging while it waits

Ottawa is not treating the standoff as a pause button. Alongside Thursday's trade remarks, Carney announced C$3.4 billion for domestic manufacturing of more than 300 passenger rail cars, part of a broader push to reduce reliance on the US market. Canada's share of exports going south has already slipped, from 72.64% to 66.35% in July, according to figures Carney cited — a modest but deliberate diversification as tariff exposure has risen.

The next marker to watch is less a date than a signal: whether Washington makes a concrete counteroffer on auto and steel terms, or whether the January tariff deadline arrives with the two sides still talking past each other. Until then, Carney's position amounts to a standing invitation with the terms already attached.
