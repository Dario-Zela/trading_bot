Milei threatens to sanction oil firms over Falklands claim

Javier Milei at his presidential inauguration in Buenos Aires, December 2023. — Juan Carlos Cardenas / Argentine Senate Communications (Wikimedia Commons, public domain)
In one sentence
Milei threatened sanctions on oil firms drilling near the Falklands, escalating Argentina's sovereignty claim after Trump wavered on US neutrality.

Argentina's president, Javier Milei, has taken his country's oldest territorial grievance somewhere it has not gone in more than four decades: onto a company's balance sheet. In a televised address on Thursday, Milei said he would send a bill to Congress to fast-track sanctions against any firm drilling for oil in waters near the Falkland Islands, and extend the penalties to their suppliers, shareholders and directors.

"The Falklands are Argentinean. Historically and legally," Milei said, adding that Buenos Aires had "months, not years" to act before rival claims to the seabed hardened into extraction. He announced funding for a new naval base in Tierra del Fuego, Argentina's southernmost province, and cast the moment as a hinge point in a dispute that last turned violent in 1982, when Argentina's invasion of the islands triggered a ten-week war with Britain that killed more than 900 people.

The trigger: an oilfield and a Trump aside

The target is Sea Lion, a field roughly 220 kilometres north of the islands being developed by Britain's Rockhopper Exploration and Israel's Navitas Petroleum. The companies took a final investment decision late last year on a project estimated to hold 319 million barrels of certified resources, with first-phase drilling due to start in 2027 and oil flowing by 2028 at a targeted 50,000 barrels a day. Milei called the project a "clear and present danger" to Argentina's claim, warning that if his government didn't move quickly, Britain and its partners "will have the material capacity to access the oil that lies underneath our sea."

The timing is not incidental. Donald Trump has twice this week declined to reaffirm Washington's long-standing neutrality on Falklands sovereignty, telling a British broadcaster he "always review[s] every position" when asked about US policy on the islands. Milei seized on the opening, telling his audience that "the United States is considering this change in position because it knows that Argentina has a reliable partner, aligned with Western values." Successive US administrations since the 1980s have recognised Britain's de facto administration of the islands while staying formally agnostic on who owns them — a posture that has underpinned the current settlement as much as British military deterrence has.

What sanctions would actually do

The proposed law would go further than Argentina's past efforts to squeeze Falklands-linked drilling, which have included asset seizures against exploration firms and a 2015 decree barring unlicensed oil companies from Argentine ports. This time the reach would extend down the supply chain — to shipping, financing and equipment providers — and could bar any company tied to islands' oil projects from operating or signing contracts in Argentina itself. Whether Buenos Aires can enforce penalties against a UK-listed firm and an Israeli one operating entirely outside Argentine jurisdiction is a separate question from whether it can pass the law; sanctions with no extraterritorial teeth would mostly raise the cost and legal risk for anyone financing or insuring the project, rather than halting it outright.

Downing Street's response, relayed to reporters, was terse: sovereignty rests with Britain, the islanders retain the right to self-determination, and the UK's position is "long-standing and unwavering." Neither Rockhopper nor Navitas has commented publicly. Milei's own political position gives the announcement extra weight to read: his approval ratings have slipped to around 34%, and he has faced criticism from Argentina's Peronist opposition that he has gone soft on the sovereignty claim in favour of courting foreign investment.

What's next

The bill's passage through Argentina's Congress, where Milei's coalition holds a minority, is the first thing to watch — a sanctions regime without legislative backing is mostly rhetoric. The second is whether Trump's ambiguity hardens into an actual US policy shift or fades as a passing remark; a formal break from Washington's neutral stance would be the bigger story than anything Argentina can legislate on its own. Sea Lion's 2027 drilling start remains the deadline both sides are now racing against.
