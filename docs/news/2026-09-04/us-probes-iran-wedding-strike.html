US investigates whether missile strike hit Iranian wedding party

In one sentence
Vance confirmed a US probe into whether an American missile hit an Iranian wedding, killing five, as independent analysis and Iran's ICC complaint deepen scrutiny of a war Washington still won't name.

The explosion tore through a home in Kuhestak, a fishing town on the Iranian shore of the Strait of Hormuz, in the early hours of September 1. Dozens of guests had gathered for the wedding of a local fisherman's daughter. When the dust settled, at least five people were dead — among them a four-year-old boy, Amirali Karimi — and more than 60 wounded, according to Iran's Red Crescent Society.

Three days later, US Vice President JD Vance confirmed what Tehran had already been alleging: that Washington is investigating whether an American missile caused the carnage. He was careful, in the same breath, to cast doubt on the source of the claim. "Iranian state media has not been a very good scribe about what's happened in the conflict thus far, so I'm extremely skeptical of this," Vance told reporters at a White House briefing.

It is a familiar posture for an administration seven months into a conflict it still resists calling a war — one now facing an independently corroborated account that points the other way.

What the evidence shows

US Central Command has said its forces struck Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps air-defense sites, radar and communications facilities in southern Iran on September 1, including what officials described as a communications tower with both civilian and military uses. CENTCOM's public position, delivered through spokesman Tim Hawkins, is that the US military "never targets civilians, unlike the IRGC" — a statement that does not directly address the Kuhestak incident.

Reuters, however, shared video and photographs from the site with four independent weapons experts. Three concluded the damage was consistent with a direct hit by a US munition rather than shrapnel from a strike on a nearby target or an errant Iranian air-defense missile. Reuters has also reported that an internal, unpublished US military assessment reached a similar preliminary conclusion. The Pentagon has not released its findings.

Iran's government has moved well beyond diplomatic protest. Foreign Ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei called the strike a "war crime," and the Red Crescent Society has written to the International Criminal Court's prosecutor asking for an investigation — believed to be the first such formal complaint filed against the US military over the conflict. Parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf used the incident to brand Washington "Satan" in a floor speech. Iran responded on September 2 with missile strikes on US-linked targets in Jordan, Iraq and Kuwait, though officials on both sides described the exchange as contained rather than a fresh escalation.

A war Washington won't name

The wedding strike lands inside a conflict now past its six-month mark, one the Trump administration continues to describe in clipped, minimizing terms. Vance told the same briefing he "wouldn't call it a war," arguing that major combat operations lasted only about six weeks after fighting began on February 28 and that "there is no active shooting" at present — a claim awkward to square with strikes and counter-strikes still occurring on a roughly weekly cadence, this one included.

The language matters beyond semantics. Under the War Powers framework, a formally acknowledged war triggers congressional notification and authorization requirements that a series of discrete "strikes" does not. Kuhestak is not the conflict's only disputed civilian casualty event — Iran has also accused the US of hitting a girls' elementary school in Minab earlier in the war, killing as many as 165 people by Tehran's count, an incident the Pentagon is still reviewing without public findings. A pattern of unresolved investigations, each one straining the administration's insistence that this is a tightly targeted campaign rather than an open-ended war.

The stakes for shipping and oil

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade normally passes, has been the conflict's central economic battleground since Iran effectively closed it in early March. Brent crude was trading around $89 a barrel in early August, some 24% above pre-war levels, and the US Energy Information Administration does not expect regional oil production to recover to pre-conflict levels before early 2027.

Vance used the same briefing to restate the administration's precondition for any negotiation: Iran must stop firing on commercial vessels in the strait before talks can begin. "We're not talking to them, and we're not going to talk to them unless they stop shooting at commercial shipping," he said. Iran, for its part, has said it will not fully reopen the strait while the US naval blockade remains in place — a standoff that leaves the wedding-strike investigation layered on top of an unresolved shipping crisis rather than separate from it.

What comes next

CENTCOM's review — reportedly examining drone footage of the strike area and the targeting procedures used — has no public deadline, and the Pentagon's unreleased Minab findings suggest similar inquiries can stall for months. The nearer-term signal to watch is whether Iran follows through on further retaliatory strikes or holds to the contained response it mounted on September 2, and whether the Red Crescent's ICC complaint gains any traction with prosecutors already occupied with the broader war. For markets, the strait's status remains the more immediate variable: any sign the blockade is hardening rather than easing would matter more to oil prices than the investigation's outcome, whatever the Pentagon eventually concludes about who fired the missile that hit Kuhestak.
