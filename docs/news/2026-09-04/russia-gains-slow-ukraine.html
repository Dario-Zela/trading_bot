Russian territorial gains in Ukraine slow over past four weeks

In one sentence
Russia's territorial gains in Ukraine slowed to roughly 40 square miles over four weeks even as strikes kept pounding a power grid already 80% damaged.

Russia's grinding advance across eastern Ukraine has lost momentum again. Over the four weeks to September 1, Russian forces netted roughly 40 square miles of Ukrainian territory, according to Russia Matters' analysis of Institute for the Study of War data — a figure so small it barely registers against the scale of the front line, which stretches for more than 600 miles. Ukraine's own DeepState tracking group, using a different methodology, puts the gain even lower, at just 5 square miles, the smallest four-week haul it has recorded in months.

The discrepancy between trackers is a reminder that front-line measurement is an inexact science pieced together from geolocated footage, unit reports and satellite imagery, not a precise survey. But the direction both agree on is unmistakable. DeepState's monthly figures have fallen in almost a straight line through 2026 — from 445 square kilometres in December to 160 in April to just 27 in July — a small fraction of the 500-square-kilometre-plus months Russia was posting at its 2025 peak. Fighting remains intense around Pokrovsk and Kupiansk, and roughly a third of Russian assaults are still concentrated on the Pokrovsk axis, but intensity is no longer translating into ground.

Attrition without breakthrough

Casualty data helps explain why. Ukrainian military estimates of Russian losses have held steady at roughly their second-half-2025 level even as the pace of advance has collapsed, meaning Russia is paying a similar price in soldiers and equipment for a shrinking return in territory. Analysts at the Institute for the Study of War and independent Russian-language outlets have pointed to Ukraine's expanded use of first-person-view drones and other asymmetric tactics as the main reason the front has hardened. Russia still holds about 19% of Ukraine's internationally recognised territory, a line that has moved only marginally in either direction for most of this year.

The grid still under fire

While the ground war stalls, the campaign against Ukraine's energy system has not let up. Energy Minister Denys Shmyhal said last month that Russian strikes have destroyed or damaged more than 80% of the country's power generation capacity since the invasion began, and has told lawmakers there is "not a single power plant in Ukraine that has not been hit by the enemy." Available generating capacity has fallen from about 33.7 gigawatts before the war to roughly 14 gigawatts, forcing Kyiv to lean heavily on its remaining nuclear stations, which now supply around 70% of the country's electricity — not because Ukraine built new reactors, but because so much else has been knocked out.

Russian forces struck energy facilities serving Ukraine's Black Sea ports at Odesa overnight on September 2, and Moscow has signalled plans for a renewed wave of strikes on the grid as autumn approaches, framing it as retaliation for Ukrainian drone attacks on Russian refineries and substations. Kyiv has made some headway on repairs — officials say close to half of the generation capacity knocked out has been rebuilt — but engineers warn that replacing high-voltage transformers can take 12 to 18 months, far slower than Russia can re-target a rebuilt substation.

The two threads point to the same underlying picture: a war that has settled into a contest of attrition on the ground and infrastructure at the rear, with neither side able to force a decisive shift. The next Russia Matters report card, due in mid-September, will show whether the slower pace of ground gains holds or whether Moscow's forces around Pokrovsk manage another push before the weather turns. On the energy side, the test comes with the first hard frost, when Ukrainian officials will find out how much of the rebuilt 4 gigawatts survives contact with a fresh bombing campaign.
