Record 271-day pig-kidney transplant hailed as bridge to human organ donation

Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, where the record xenotransplant and the subsequent human kidney transplant both took place. — Whoisjohngalt / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A gene-edited pig kidney functioned in a US patient for a record 271 days before he safely received a human transplant, doctors report.

A pig kidney, engineered with more than a dozen genetic edits to survive inside a human body, kept a New Hampshire man off dialysis for 271 days before it failed and he received a human transplant instead — the longest anyone has lived with a xenotransplanted organ, and the first documented case of a patient moving successfully from an animal kidney to a human one. Doctors at Massachusetts General Hospital, who reported the case in The Lancet, are calling it proof that pig organs can serve as a working bridge for patients who might otherwise die waiting.

"The organ shortage is the greatest crisis we have right now in transplantation," said Dr. Leonardo Riella of Mass General Brigham's Center for Transplantation Sciences, the paper's lead author.

A second kidney, from a very different donor

Tim Andrews, 66, of Concord, New Hampshire, had end-stage kidney disease and had spent more than two years on dialysis when he received the experimental porcine organ at Mass General on January 25, 2025. The kidney came from a Yucatan miniature pig supplied by the Cambridge, Massachusetts biotech firm eGenesis, and it was not a stock animal organ — it carried three categories of genetic modification: the removal of three glycan sugars on the cell surface that normally trigger immediate, violent rejection by the human immune system; the insertion of seven human genes to regulate immune response, inflammation and blood clotting; and the deliberate inactivation of porcine retroviruses that had long worried regulators as a cross-species infection risk.

The organ worked immediately. Andrews left the hospital within about a week and, unusually for a xenotransplant patient, went 271 days without a single dialysis session — filtering his blood the way a native kidney would. That is nearly double the 130-day mark set last year by Towana Looney, an Alabama woman whose pig kidney was the previous record holder, and it dwarfs the 52 days that Richard Slayman, the first living patient to receive one, survived after his March 2024 transplant at the same hospital before he died of causes doctors said were unrelated to the graft.

When the bridge itself needs replacing

The kidney's failure was not sudden or mysterious, according to Riella and Dr. Tatsuo Kawai, the paper's senior author and director of Mass General's Legorreta Center for Clinical Transplantation Tolerance. Andrews had an early bout of T-cell-mediated rejection that doctors treated and reversed. Months later, when an infection forced his medical team to scale back his immunosuppressive drugs — a routine trade-off in transplant medicine, where suppressing the immune system too hard invites infection and too little invites rejection — the kidney developed microvascular injury and gradually stopped working. Doctors removed it in October 2025.

What happened next is the detail that made this case notable rather than merely long-lived. Andrews went back on dialysis for 82 days and then received a standard deceased-donor human kidney in January 2026, which began functioning immediately. In transplant terms, the pig organ had done exactly the job its designers hoped for: it kept a patient alive and dialysis-free through the most acute stretch of the wait, without compromising his eligibility or fitness for a human organ when one became available.

Why the shortage makes this matter

The case lands against a waiting list that has outgrown the supply of donor kidneys for decades. Roughly 100,800 people in the US are currently listed for a kidney, according to organ-network data, out of more than 109,000 total transplant candidates — kidneys account for the large majority of everyone waiting for any organ. In 2023, more than 44,500 new candidates joined kidney waiting lists nationally while only about 27,300 kidney transplants were performed. Median wait times run roughly three and a half years nationally and considerably longer — into a decade in some regions — depending on blood type and geography; an estimated 12 people a day die on transplant waiting lists across all organs. Dialysis, the fallback, carries its own mortality risk and quality-of-life toll the longer patients remain on it.

eGenesis's pig kidney, designated EGEN-2784, has since received FDA clearance to enter a formal Phase 1/2/3 clinical trial — a step up from the single-patient "expanded access" pathway that covered Andrews, Looney and Slayman. That trial will enroll dialysis-dependent patients aged 50 and older who are already on the transplant waiting list, testing organ function at 24 weeks across a larger cohort rather than relying on individual case reports.

What this does and doesn't prove

It would be a mistake to read 271 days as evidence that pig kidneys are close to matching human ones. Three patients, three outcomes, none longer than nine months, is not a durability record by the standards of ordinary transplant medicine, where human-donor kidneys routinely last a decade or more. Andrews's course also shows the underlying tension has not been solved, only managed: the same immunosuppression needed to protect a foreign organ left him vulnerable to the infection that forced the drug reduction that ultimately doomed the kidney. Cost and manufacturing scale — breeding, editing and screening pigs to the sterility and genetic specifications required for human transplant — remain largely unaddressed in public reporting, and no regulatory pathway yet exists for xenotransplantation as standard, insurance-reimbursed care.

What the case does establish, more convincingly than earlier ones, is the bridge concept itself: that a xenograft can carry a patient through the hardest stretch of the wait and then step aside cleanly when a human organ arrives, without the sort of sensitization or complication that would jeopardize the second transplant. The next data points will come from eGenesis's multi-patient trial, and from United Therapeutics' rival pig-kidney and pig-heart programs, both racing toward the same regulatory finish line. Whether 271 days becomes a footnote or a floor depends on whether the next cohort can beat it.
