VW board approves record 50,000 job cuts and closure of four German plants

Volkswagen's Zwickau plant, one of four German factories now without guaranteed production beyond the early 2030s. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
VW's board unanimously backed 50,000 more job cuts and put four German plants on notice, months after rejecting a harsher 100,000-cut version of the same plan.

Volkswagen's supervisory board voted unanimously on Wednesday to back the deepest restructuring in the company's 89-year history: another 50,000 jobs cut worldwide, on top of 50,000 already being shed under a prior agreement, and no guaranteed production beyond the early 2030s at four German plants — Emden, Zwickau, Hanover and the Audi factory in Neckarsulm. It is the clearest mandate CEO Oliver Blume has had since taking the job in 2022, and it comes only seven weeks after the same board rejected a more radical version of his plan outright.

The numbers explain the urgency. Volkswagen's operating margin sat at 3.8% in the first half of 2026, roughly a third of what rivals such as Toyota post in a decent year. The new plan, internally titled "Future Plan 2030," targets a 9% margin by the end of the decade, at least €10bn in annual cost savings, and a model lineup cut by half — with the underlying parts and platform complexity that feeds it reduced by three-quarters. Volkswagen still plans to spend €135bn on capital projects and R&D between 2027 and 2031, so this is not a company retreating from the car business. It is a company trying to make far fewer models, more cheaply, at far higher volume per line.

From rejection to unanimous approval

The path here says as much as the destination. In July, the same supervisory board threw out an earlier version of Blume's plan by twelve votes to seven — a proposal that had floated cutting up to 100,000 jobs. Volkswagen's governance structure gives labour representatives and the state of Lower Saxony, which holds roughly 20% of voting shares, a combined majority on the board, and both blocked the more aggressive cuts. Blume spent the following weeks negotiating down the headline number and building in commitments that no German plant would be shut outright without first exhausting a search for alternative production.

That compromise is why Wednesday's vote was unanimous rather than contested, and why VW's preferred shares rose nearly 6% on the Tradegate exchange in early trading — investors reading decisiveness into a number that, on its face, is brutal. Roughly 25,000 of the new cuts will fall in Germany; the rest are spread across the group's roughly 170 subsidiaries worldwide. Management now has until June 2027 to find viable production concepts for Emden, Zwickau, Hanover and Neckarsulm; absent that, a staggered phase-out beginning in 2031 becomes the default.

Labour's uneasy consent

Daniela Cavallo, who chairs VW's works council, told employees that trust in management has "been damaged; not irreparably, but damaged," while accepting the restructuring as a necessity the company cannot avoid. Her counterpart at IG Metall, the metalworkers' union, was less conciliatory. Christiane Benner called Blume's 9% margin target something close to fantasy, asking how the group could hit that number "under the current geopolitical conditions," and Thorsten Gröger, the union's regional head, warned that pure headcount and budget cuts would meet resistance "with all our strength" — not ruling out strikes. That the board still voted unanimously suggests labour's representatives judged a managed, negotiated 50,000 preferable to another standoff that risked a repeat of July's rejection, and possibly a worse plan next time.

Why the whole sector is watching

Volkswagen is not cutting because demand for cars has vanished; global vehicle sales are broadly stable. It is cutting because it is losing the fight for margin on the cars it does sell. Chinese manufacturers — BYD chief among them — have taken share in Europe and China alike with EVs built at a cost base German plants cannot currently match, while VW's own electric transition has been slower and more expensive than planned. Add persistently high German energy prices and the tariff friction running through both the US and Chinese markets, and the result is a company selling roughly 9 million vehicles a year that still cannot turn an adequate profit on them.

That is the detail that should worry policymakers in Berlin and Brussels more than the job-cut number itself. Volkswagen is not a marginal player choosing to retrench — it is Europe's largest carmaker by volume, employing close to 700,000 people worldwide, roughly 40,000 of them at the four plants now in limbo. If VW's own board concluded that the company cannot compete without restructuring at this scale, smaller German and European manufacturers, with less capital and less scale to fall back on, face the same arithmetic with fewer options to solve it.

The next marker is June 2027, when Volkswagen has committed to publishing a verdict on Emden, Zwickau, Hanover and Neckarsulm — plant by plant, alternative production concepts or a confirmed phase-out. Between now and then, expect wage negotiations, works council votes at each site, and a running argument over the 9% margin target that Volkswagen's own labour chief has already called unrealistic.
