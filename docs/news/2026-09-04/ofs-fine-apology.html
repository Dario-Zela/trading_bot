Higher education regulator apologises after wrongly imposing £585,000 fine

Falmer House at the University of Sussex, whose free speech case against the regulator ended in a quashed fine and a rare public apology. — The Voice of Hassocks / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Office for Students admitted its £585,000 fine against Sussex was tainted by bias, prompting a public apology and a promised regulatory overhaul.

It is not every day that a regulator publishes a report whose central finding is that it behaved badly. But that is roughly what the Office for Students has just done, five months after the High Court eviscerated its handling of a free speech investigation into the University of Sussex — an inquiry that ended with a record £585,000 fine, a judge's finding of bias, and now a rare public apology.

The fine dates back to a long-running OfS investigation into how Sussex handled the 2021 departure of the philosophy professor Kathleen Stock, who left the university amid protests over her gender-critical views. The regulator concluded Sussex had breached its conditions of registration on free speech and governance grounds and, in March, imposed what was at the time the largest penalty in its history.

Sussex did not accept it. The university took the OfS to judicial review, and on 29 April Mrs Justice Lieven quashed the fine in a judgment that went well beyond a technical foul. She found the regulator's decision was "vitiated by bias" because it had "closed its mind to anything that would lead to not finding breaches" — in effect, that the OfS had decided on the outcome before it had finished weighing the evidence. The judge added that the regulator had set out from the start to make an example of Sussex, with a strong sanction and maximum publicity attached.

An unusually blunt apology

What followed was not the quiet walking-back regulators often prefer. The OfS's new leadership — chair Edward Peck and joint chief executives Ruth Hannant and Polly Payne, none of whom were in post when the investigation was run — commissioned a formal lessons-learned review and published it alongside a statement that pulled few punches: "We made mistakes in the University of Sussex case, and we regret this."

The review itself is more revealing than the apology. It describes how policy development and enforcement became blurred as the case progressed, and how decision-making was concentrated among a small group inside the OfS, generating what the report calls "decision momentum" — institutional jargon for a process that became difficult to stop once it was underway. Much of that momentum, according to reporting on the case, traced back to the OfS's then chief executive, Susan Lapworth, who left the post some months before the judgment landed and whose direction of the investigation was described as intense and overarching.

Reforms, and scepticism about them

The OfS has promised a package of changes: meeting with any provider under investigation at least once before enforcement action; committing to "engage early" and "communicate openly"; consolidating its regulatory guidance, previously scattered across multiple documents, into a single annual publication; and setting up dedicated teams for enforcement, financial sustainability and governance. It has also commissioned an external review comparing its structures with those of other UK regulators, due by the end of the year.

Sussex's vice-chancellor, Sasha Roseneil, welcomed the acknowledgement of fault but was pointedly unimpressed with how far it went. The report, she said, "reveals deep cultural problems within the OfS in relation to freedom of expression and internal challenge" and "does not fully reflect the breadth or seriousness of the High Court's findings." She noted the OfS had still not properly engaged with Sussex during the review process — the same complaint that ran through Mrs Justice Lieven's judgment in the first place.

Why the credibility gap matters

A regulator's authority rests on the presumption that its enforcement machinery is more careful than the institutions it polices, not less. Surveys cited alongside the OfS's own review found only around four in ten universities trust it to treat them fairly — a striking figure for a body with the power to fine, suspend registration, or in principle deregister a provider altogether. A near-£600,000 penalty overturned for bias is not a rounding error in that process; it is the kind of failure that invites scrutiny of every other enforcement decision the same team made.

The practical test now is whether the promised changes survive contact with the next contentious case. The external structural review due by year-end will show whether the OfS is prepared to separate policy-making from enforcement more durably than a single mea culpa can. Until then, universities have been given assurances rather than guarantees — and, as Sussex's experience shows, assurances are only as good as the willingness to act on them under pressure.
