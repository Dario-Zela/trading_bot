British army told to halt non-essential training exercises to save money

A British Army Challenger 2 main battle tank on exercise on Salisbury Plain — the kind of live-fire training now being scaled back. — POA(Phot) Terry Seward / UK MOD, Crown Copyright, Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Army has been told to pause non-essential training to save money, a quiet budget squeeze ahead of a defence review meant to fund Britain's bigger military ambitions.

The order, relayed through defence sources rather than announced from the despatch box, is blunt enough: stop the training that isn't strictly necessary. Units already earmarked for deployment — to Estonia, the Falklands, Cyprus — will carry on training as planned. But army formations sitting in the UK with no specific posting on the horizon have been told to shelve what one defence source called "nice to have training": large live-fire exercises, rehearsed platoon attacks on ranges like Salisbury Plain and Sennybridge, and outings for Challenger 2 tanks and Apache attack helicopters.

No single number attaches to the decision — no savings target, no end date beyond "most of this year," no tally of units affected. That vagueness is itself telling. This is not a headline defence cut announced with a press conference and a Treasury score card; it is the kind of quiet, internal rationing that happens when a budget is being managed month to month rather than planned year to year.

A department still catching up

The timing matters. The pause lands ahead of a defence review that is meant to settle, at last, how Britain pays for the ambitions set out in last year's Strategic Defence Review — more missiles, more submarines, a larger army. Those commitments were made under Sir Keir Starmer, whose handling of the defence budget contributed to his departure from Number 10 this summer; his defence secretary, John Healey, resigned over the funding plans before Andy Burnham took over as prime minister in July and installed Wes Streeting at the Ministry of Defence. Spending is on course to reach 2.5% of national income next year and 2.7% by 2027-28, but there is no confirmed path to the 3% figure Burnham has said he is committed to reaching by the end of the decade.

That gap between ambition and arithmetic is where this story sits. The MoD's capital budget — the money for new weapons programmes, submarines, munitions — is largely ring-fenced by contract. Training budgets are not. When a department needs to find in-year savings without touching procurement commitments already signed, the axe tends to fall on things that can be switched off without breaching a contract: flying hours, live-fire days, exercise bookings. It is administratively easy and politically quiet, which is presumably why it was disclosed to reporters rather than announced.

Readiness versus optics

The army insists the change is about prioritisation, not decline — training judged "most beneficial for operational readiness" continues, including newer priorities like drone and counter-drone warfare. But readiness is cumulative: skills atrophy, tank crews lose currency, live-fire proficiency needs repetition. A pause billed as temporary can harden into a pattern if the next budget settlement doesn't restore the money. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, has already used the episode to press for welfare spending to be redirected to defence — a line calculated to keep the story alive rather than to fix it.

What happens next depends less on this particular training freeze than on the defence review itself, and on whether Burnham's government can show a credible route to 3% of GDP. Until then, expect more of this kind of cut: unannounced, unquantified, and reversible in principle — administrative slack rather than strategy.
