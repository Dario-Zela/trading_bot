Jane Street strikes $13bn AI compute deal with Crusoe

In one sentence
Jane Street will pay Crusoe roughly $13bn over five years for AI compute, its second multibillion-dollar infrastructure deal this year after CoreWeave.

Jane Street, the secretive Wall Street trading firm best known for making markets in ETFs and options, has agreed to pay Crusoe Energy roughly $13 billion over five years for access to clusters of Nvidia GPUs and the data-center capacity to run them. Bloomberg reported the deal on September 3, citing people familiar with the matter — neither company has issued a formal statement, which is standard practice for Jane Street, a firm that guards its internals more closely than most trading houses guard their positions.

The number is large even by the standards of an industry now routinely signing multibillion-dollar compute contracts. It is also not Jane Street's first this year. In April, the firm committed $6 billion to rent capacity from CoreWeave and bought $1 billion of CoreWeave stock outright at $109 a share. Add the two together and Jane Street has committed roughly $19 billion to external AI infrastructure since the spring — a sum that rivals what some public cloud providers spend annually on data-center capex, from a firm whose core business is buying and selling securities a few cents apart, millions of times a day.

From flared gas to Wall Street's GPUs

Crusoe is an unlikely counterparty for a firm like Jane Street. It started in 2018 capturing natural gas that would otherwise be flared off at oil wells and using it to power bitcoin-mining rigs — a business it sold off entirely in 2024 to go all-in on AI infrastructure. Since then it has built hyperscale data-center campuses for Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and OpenAI, including a stake in the Stargate project in Abilene, Texas. Jane Street is now, by Bloomberg's account, Crusoe's highest-profile cloud customer.

The timing helps explain why. The deal landed just as Crusoe was closing a $3 billion funding round at a $30 billion valuation — nearly triple the $10 billion valuation it carried ten months earlier — co-led by Atreides Management and Valor Equity Partners, with Abu Dhabi's Mubadala Capital also participating. People close to the company say a Jane Street contract of this size helped draw investor interest in the round. Crusoe has separately met with bankers at Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, JPMorgan and Bank of America about a near-term IPO. A marquee anchor customer with a five-year, $13 billion commitment is the kind of revenue visibility that makes an IPO prospectus easier to sell.

Why a trading firm needs its own GPU fleet

Jane Street's public description of its research effort — "training large, complex models on massive volumes of noisy data" — reads like something out of an AI lab's careers page, not a market maker's. That is deliberate. The firm already runs tens of thousands of high-end GPUs and has discussed building a self-financed 200-megawatt data center of its own, according to people familiar with its planning. Machine-learning models, on this account, are no longer a research side-project with a discrete budget line; they sit inside pricing, risk and trading-signal generation across the firm.

The economics explain the appetite. Jane Street posted $16.1 billion in trading revenue and $10.3 billion in net income in the first quarter of 2026 alone — more than double the same period a year earlier — against $39.6 billion in revenue for all of 2025. Some of that was volatility-driven trading gains; some came from the rising value of the firm's private AI stakes, including an early position in Anthropic taken in 2024, whose valuation climbed from $183 billion to above $800 billion between September 2025 and this year. A firm generating that kind of cash, and holding equity in the AI boom's biggest winners, has both the means and the incentive to buy compute at a scale that would have been unthinkable for a trading shop five years ago.

The wider signal

What makes the Crusoe deal notable beyond its size is what it says about where quantitative trading firms now think their edge comes from. Speed and cleverness in code have been necessary but no longer sufficient; the newer advantage lies in training bigger models on more data, which requires the same GPU clusters that OpenAI, Meta and Anthropic are fighting over. Jane Street's rivals — Citadel Securities, Two Sigma, Renaissance Technologies and others — face a version of the same choice: match this level of infrastructure spending or risk falling behind on the modeling side of the business, even if their trading strategies remain sound. Compute has become balance-sheet infrastructure for market makers in a way that would have seemed strange even three years ago.

For Crusoe, the immediate test is delivery — standing up the GPU clusters this contract requires against a backdrop of tight Nvidia supply and its own aggressive build-out across Texas, Missouri and Wyoming. For Jane Street, the number to watch is whether the AI-driven share of its revenue keeps compounding at the rate it has this year, which would justify the spending, or whether markets normalize and the firm is left holding a very large, very fixed cost. The next data point lands with Jane Street's third-quarter numbers, and with whatever Crusoe discloses — or is forced to disclose — as it moves toward an IPO.
