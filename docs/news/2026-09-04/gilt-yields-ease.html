Gilt yields ease off two-decade highs as BoE hike bets firm

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — Adrian Pingstone (Wikimedia Commons, public domain)
In one sentence
UK 10-year gilt yields eased to 5.17% from a 2008-era high of 5.29%, even as markets fully price a BoE hike by year-end.

Ten-year gilt yields eased to around 5.17% on Thursday, pulling back from 5.29% earlier in the week — the highest level for UK government debt since the financial crisis year of 2008. The retreat is modest, and it comes with a complication: bets on a Bank of England rate rise before year-end have, if anything, gained ground.

Markets are now fully pricing in at least one 25-basis-point hike by December, with a November move seen as roughly 70% likely and a further increase by February 2027 priced at close to 80%, according to swaps data tracked by Trading Economics. That is a striking turnaround for a central bank that spent much of the past two years cutting rates, and it puts the MPC on a path few forecasters had sketched out as recently as the spring.

Why yields and hike odds are pulling in different directions

On the surface, a market convinced the Bank is about to tighten policy should be pushing yields higher, not lower. The reconciliation lies in the composition of this week's move: shorter-dated gilts eased alongside the long end, with two-year yields down 7 basis points to 4.42% on Wednesday and 30-year paper slipping to 5.87%. Traders appear to be responding less to a change in rate-hike conviction and more to a cooling in the inflation impulse that drove yields to their 2008-era peak in the first place — energy prices have eased over the past fortnight, taking some heat out of the pass-through effects the MPC has been watching closely.

That distinction matters. The Bank's July meeting minutes recorded a 6–3 vote to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, with three members already pushing for an immediate quarter-point increase — the clearest sign yet of a hawkish minority gaining traction inside the committee. Governor Andrew Bailey and colleagues have said explicitly that policy will follow the evidence on how far energy-driven price pressures generate second-round effects in wages and services inflation. CPI stood at 2.6% at the last reading but is projected to climb toward 3.2% by year-end.

A credibility question, not just a pricing one

The gap between softening yields and firming hike bets reads as a market still working out how committed the Bank actually is. Gilt investors have spent much of 2026 pricing in tightening that then failed to materialise at successive meetings — rate-hike odds for September's MPC decision, for instance, were pared back to under 15% in the days before the meeting after having been considerably higher only weeks earlier. Each time the committee has held rather than acted, the pattern has been the same: yields drift down on relief, then creep back up as the next inflation print reopens the debate.

Fiscal considerations sit underneath all of this. Thirty-year gilts remain more than 100 basis points higher than a year ago, a move that predates this month's inflation scare and reflects lingering doubts about the sustainability of the UK's borrowing path ahead of the autumn Budget. Chancellor Rachel Reeves faces the same trade-off that has shadowed gilt markets since 2025 — narrow the deficit further and risk growth, or lean on the OBR's forecasts and risk another bout of the yield pressure that followed last year's fiscal statement.

For traders, the practical upshot is that gilts are pricing two things at once — a central bank plausibly done drifting toward the exit from tight policy, and a fiscal backdrop that keeps a floor under long yields regardless of what the MPC decides. The next test lands at the Bank's November meeting, where a hike is now the market's base case; a hold would reopen exactly the credibility gap this week's price action is already hinting at.
