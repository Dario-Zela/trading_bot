Nvidia buys Hugging Face for $12.9bn in bet on open AI ecosystem

Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, whose company is paying $12.93 billion for open-source AI platform Hugging Face. — Presidential Office (Taiwan) via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
Nvidia will pay $12.93bn for Hugging Face, its second-biggest deal ever, while pledging the open-model platform will stay neutral toward rival chipmakers.

Nvidia is paying $12.93 billion for Hugging Face, the online repository where the AI industry stores, shares and downloads open-source models — and in the same breath, promising not to use its new property to lock developers into Nvidia chips.

That second part is the story. Nvidia has spent a decade building the most profitable moat in modern technology: CUDA, the software layer that ties AI workloads to Nvidia GPUs so tightly that switching to a rival's silicon means rewriting large chunks of code. Buying the platform that hosts three million AI models, half a million datasets and serves more than 18 million developers would be an obvious way to extend that moat one layer up the stack. Instead, chief executive Jensen Huang says the opposite will happen: "Hugging Face will remain an open platform for the entire AI ecosystem," he said in confirming the deal on September 3. "Nvidia compute will not be required to build on or deploy through Hugging Face."

The deal

The structure is straightforward: roughly $11.9 billion goes to Hugging Face's shareholders, with a further $1 billion in Nvidia equity set aside to retain the company's staff. It is Nvidia's second-largest acquisition on record, behind only the $20 billion deal for chipmaker Groq's assets struck last December, and well ahead of the $6.9 billion it paid for the networking firm Mellanox in 2019 — the deal that first turned Nvidia from a graphics-card maker into a full data-centre platform vendor. The transaction is expected to close in the first half of 2027, pending regulatory sign-off, which given the scrutiny that sank Nvidia's attempted $40 billion purchase of chip designer Arm in 2022, is not a formality.

Hugging Face itself was not obviously for sale. Founded in New York as a teen-oriented chatbot app before pivoting to open-source machine learning tools, it has raised more than $395 million from investors including Salesforce Ventures, and was generating an estimated $150 million in annualised revenue as of last month — a fraction of the price Nvidia is now paying. Chief executive Clem Delangue said on X that the company had gone to Nvidia, not the other way round, seeking more compute, support and visibility for the platform: "We went to talk to Jensen, who offered to do exactly that with us." Notably, Delangue had turned down a much smaller approach from Nvidia just a year earlier — a $500 million investment offer reported by the Financial Times — which suggests the terms, not just the interest, had to change substantially before he said yes.

Why Hugging Face matters

To understand why the acquisition matters more than its price tag suggests, it helps to know what Hugging Face actually is. It functions like GitHub for AI models: a place where Meta posts Llama, Stability AI posts its image generators, and independent researchers upload fine-tuned variants, alongside the libraries — Transformers, llama.cpp — that let those models actually run. Crucially, that tooling is hardware-agnostic. Hugging Face's "Optimum" libraries work with Nvidia's own TensorRT-LLM, but also with AMD and Intel chips and Amazon's cloud silicon, and the platform underpins rival inference engines such as vLLM and SGLang that compete directly with Nvidia's own software stack. That neutrality has earned Hugging Face a nickname inside the industry: AI's Switzerland — the one piece of infrastructure every lab and every hardware vendor could trust because no single one of them controlled it.

That is precisely what critics say is now at risk. The concern is not that Nvidia will crudely lock out AMD or Intel — that would draw immediate antitrust attention and defeat the platform's purpose. It is subtler: that documentation, tooling and default configurations quietly favour Nvidia hardware, or that Nvidia floods the platform with cheap Nvidia-backed compute credits, nudging developers toward its chips without ever writing an explicit rule against competitors. Huang's pledge — no Nvidia compute requirement, continued support for open-weight models — directly targets that scenario. Whether it holds is a matter of enforcement, not intention, and Hugging Face will be a wholly owned subsidiary with no independent board to hold Nvidia to its word.

From chipmaker to gatekeeper

The deal fits a pattern. Nvidia has already put more than $50 billion into AI labs building frontier models, and struck a $6 billion arrangement with the open-model startup Poolside. Huang has been explicit about the strategic logic: open models, he argues, are becoming essential infrastructure in their own right, not just a cheaper alternative to closed ones. "One of the areas where frontier models are vital is cybersecurity," he said, arguing that regulated industries and government agencies that cannot send data to a closed, third-party model API increasingly need to run open weights on infrastructure they control. Owning the distribution layer for those open models — rather than just the chips that run them — gives Nvidia visibility and influence over where the entire open-source AI ecosystem is heading, even if it never touches the hardware choice directly.

That is the bet embedded in the price: that controlling the neutral ground is worth more than controlling the hardware lock-in, because the neutral ground is where the next generation of developers, and therefore the next generation of demand for compute, actually gets decided. It is also, implicitly, a hedge against fragmentation — if Nvidia does not own the connective tissue of open AI, a rival chipmaker or cloud provider eventually might.

What to watch

Regulators in the US, UK and EU — all of whom killed the Arm deal on competition grounds — will scrutinise this one closely, and the close is not expected until the first half of 2027, leaving over a year for objections to surface. Between now and then, the test will be practical rather than rhetorical: whether AMD- and Intel-optimised tools on Hugging Face keep pace with Nvidia's own, whether compute pricing on the platform stays neutral, and whether developers who have used Hugging Face precisely because no single vendor owned it start to notice a tilt. Delangue and Huang have both said the right things. The next eighteen months will show whether Hugging Face's independence was a feature Nvidia bought to preserve, or one it bought to retire quietly.
