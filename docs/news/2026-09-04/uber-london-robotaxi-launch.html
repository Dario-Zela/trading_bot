Uber launches UK's first robotaxi service on London streets

The Ford Mustang Mach-E is the electric SUV platform Uber and Wayve are using for London's robotaxi trial. — Alexander Migl / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Uber and Wayve put 15 self-driving Ford Mustangs on London's streets, UK's first robotaxi trial — safety drivers still required at the wheel.

Fifteen electric Ford Mustangs are now driving themselves around London — with a human sitting behind the wheel just in case. On Thursday, Uber and British startup Wayve began running the UK's first public robotaxi trial, letting some Uber passengers get matched with a self-driving car instead of a regular minicab, at no extra charge.

It is a small fleet in a city with roughly 100,000 licensed Ubers, and it is not, strictly speaking, driverless. Every Mustang Mach-E carries a Transport for London-licensed safety driver who monitors the AI and can grab the wheel at any moment. But the launch matters beyond its size: it is the first time a self-driving car has been allowed to carry fare-paying passengers on Britain's roads, in a market regulators have historically approached with more caution than the United States or China.

How the trial actually works

Riders can't hail a robotaxi directly. Order an UberX, Uber Electric or Uber Comfort in London and the app may match you with a Wayve-equipped Mustang instead of a normal car; you can accept or decline. Fares are unchanged, and — in a detail that says something about how Uber wants this framed — the app doesn't prompt for a tip afterwards, since there's no human driver providing the service.

Transport for London approved the fleet, capped at 15 vehicles, for a one-year trial. Technically, the cars are licensed as ordinary private hire vehicles, not as automated vehicles. To operate without a safety driver at all, Uber and Wayve need a separate Automated Passenger Services permit from the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency — a process neither company has completed. Sarfraz Maredia, Uber's head of autonomous mobility, put it plainly when asked how long that might take: "That process is still playing out."

The legal scaffolding for eventually removing the safety driver already exists. The UK's Automated Vehicles Act, passed in 2024, sets out a framework for self-driving services and is due to be fully in force by the second half of 2027. Until then, every robotaxi on British roads — this one included — keeps a human on standby.

Behind on the world stage, ahead of it at home

Against the rest of the world, London's launch looks modest. Waymo already runs fully driverless taxis, no safety driver required, across 14 American cities. In China, Baidu, WeRide and Pony.ai operate multi-city services and are expanding into the Middle East. Uber's own robotaxi footprint elsewhere — a Zagreb launch and a Tokyo pilot with Nissan — has likewise kept a human in the car, suggesting the London trial is closer to Uber's global template than an exception to it.

What sets Wayve apart technically is its refusal to pre-map cities in the way Waymo does. Its "AI Driver" is trained end-to-end on camera footage to generalise across roads, weather and cities it hasn't specifically mapped — a bet that matters in London, where narrow streets, legal jaywalking and heavy cycle traffic make block-by-block HD mapping a bigger lift than in a US suburb. Waymo and Baidu are both said to be preparing their own London launches, which would turn the city into a genuine test bed for competing approaches to the same regulatory obstacle course.

The stakes for Wayve are not small. The Cambridge-founded company raised $1.2bn in a Series D round in February, led by Eclipse, Balderton and SoftBank Vision Fund 2 with Mercedes-Benz, Nissan and Stellantis also participating, valuing it at $8.6bn. Uber has committed up to $300m more in milestone-based capital. A London trial that goes smoothly — or badly — will shape how that money gets spent.

The cabbies aren't buying it

London's black cab drivers, who train for years to pass "The Knowledge" — the notoriously difficult exam on the city's street layout — have seen off Uber before and are unimpressed now. Steve McNamara, general secretary of the Licensed Taxi Drivers Association, called the robotaxis "a solution looking for a problem," and questioned how the service handles lost property, sick passengers or unplanned route changes without a driver empowered to make judgment calls. He also noted the vehicles are less autonomous than the marketing suggests, given remote human agents can be pulled in when something goes wrong — a fair point, since a safety driver is physically present in every car regardless.

What comes next

The real test isn't whether these fifteen Mustangs can navigate the Euston Road without incident — Uber and Wayve wouldn't have launched if they doubted that. It's whether the DVSA permit for driverless operation arrives on anything like a predictable timeline, and whether the trial's safety record over the next year gives regulators reason to move faster than the 2027 statutory deadline. Waymo's rumoured entry into the same city would turn this from a solo pilot into a genuine competitive test of two different bets on how self-driving cars learn to drive.
