Snowflake and Broadcom extend AI infrastructure boom visibility to 2028

Broadcom's San Jose headquarters. The company guided AI chip revenue to roughly $230bn by fiscal 2028. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Broadcom guided AI chip revenue to $230bn by fiscal 2028 and Snowflake posted 35% growth on AI workloads, extending the capex cycle's priced-in runway to 2028.

Two earnings reports landed within a day of each other this week, and together they did something more interesting than beat estimates: they pushed the market's mental model of the AI infrastructure cycle out to 2028.

Broadcom, reporting fiscal third-quarter results on 2 September, said revenue rose 86% year-on-year to $29.59bn, ahead of the $29.36bn analysts had pencilled in. AI semiconductor revenue — the custom chips it builds for hyperscalers and AI labs — tripled to $16.7bn, up 221% on the year. Chief executive Hock Tan then did the part that moved the stock: he put a number on 2028.

The number: $230bn

Broadcom now expects fiscal 2028 AI revenue of roughly $230bn — about four times the $58bn it expects to book this year, and roughly double the $115bn now pencilled in for fiscal 2027. That 2027 figure is itself a revision: three months ago Tan was guiding to "more than $100bn." The upgrade matters less for its size than for what it implies about visibility — Broadcom is not extrapolating from hope, it says, but from secured supply commitments matched against orders already on the books.

"Demand actually exceeds this outlook," Tan told analysts on the call, adding that supply — not customer appetite — is the binding constraint on both the 2027 and 2028 numbers. Broadcom named Google, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic as customers for its custom accelerator business, and said Anthropic is on track to overtake Google as its largest customer in 2027. Guidance for the current quarter puts AI semiconductor revenue at $21.7bn. Broadcom also guided fiscal 2028 adjusted earnings per share above $30, versus a Refinitiv consensus of $25.86 — a gap wide enough that some of Wednesday's rally, the stock's steepest since 2020, was simply the market repricing forward earnings rather than sentiment.

The honest caveat, which Tan volunteered rather than had extracted from him, is deployment risk: chips shipped are not the same as chips running. "Even as we ship the chips, are they going to be deployed on a timely basis?" he said, pointing to land and power availability — not silicon — as the more likely bottleneck further out.

Snowflake: the demand-side echo

A day earlier, Snowflake supplied the second half of the picture — not the chip supply side of the AI build-out, but what happens once that capacity gets switched on. The data-cloud company reported fiscal second-quarter revenue of $1.55bn, up 35.1% year-on-year and above the $1.48bn Wall Street expected; product revenue rose 37% to $1.49bn. Non-GAAP earnings of $0.62 a share beat estimates by nearly 39%. Shares jumped as much as 23% in the reaction.

Chief executive Sridhar Ramaswamy described three reinforcing dynamics: AI workloads landing on the platform for the first time, rapid uptake of Snowflake's own AI tools including a coding agent called CoCo, and — the more structurally important claim — that customers running AI workloads on Snowflake consume more of the underlying platform as a result. "That flywheel is accelerating," he said. Snowflake guided third-quarter product revenue to $1.588bn–$1.593bn, implying continued 37–38% growth, and lifted its non-GAAP operating margin target to 15.5%.

Why the pairing matters

On their own, either report is a good quarter. Together they cover both ends of the AI capex chain in the same week: Broadcom supplies the picks and shovels, extending its visibility on chip orders out to 2028; Snowflake shows that the workloads those chips enable are already generating paying, compounding demand two layers up the stack, in the data and software tier. That is the detail traders were parsing — not whether AI capex is real, which by September 2026 is no longer seriously disputed, but how long the spending curve runs before it flattens.

Broadcom's answer, delivered with supply contracts rather than forecasts, is: longer than the market had priced. Snowflake's answer, delivered in consumption data from live customers, is that the workloads justifying that spending are showing up, not just being promised. Both narrow the range of outcomes traders have to worry about — not to zero, since deployment bottlenecks in power and land remain a live risk on Tan's own account, but enough that a two-year extension to consensus visibility, from thereabouts 2026 to 2028, is a genuine repricing input rather than noise dressed up as news.

The next test of that thesis lands with Nvidia's fiscal third-quarter results, expected in November, and with whichever hyperscaler capex guidance comes attached to it — the numbers that will show whether Broadcom's secured 2027 and 2028 supply lines are actually being drawn down at the pace implied.
