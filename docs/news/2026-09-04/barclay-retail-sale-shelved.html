Sale of Barclay family's former retail empire shelved

In one sentence
Carlyle has shelved its £2bn auction of The Very Group, the retailer carved from the Barclay family's collapsed empire, after bidders including JD.com balked at the price.

The private equity firm Carlyle has shelved plans to sell The Very Group, the online retailer built from the wreckage of the Barclay family's high street empire, after bidders declined to meet its £2 billion asking price.

According to Sky News, potential buyers — including the Chinese e-commerce giant JD.com — walked away from the table rather than stump up Carlyle's minimum. JD.com had been seen as the most credible suitor, but has grown wary of taking on another large transaction while its bid for the German electronics retailer Ceconomy sits under regulatory scrutiny in Brussels. Elliott Advisors, the hedge fund that owns Waterstones, was also reported among the parties sniffing around. Neither Carlyle nor Very Group has commented publicly.

From £1 to £2 billion, in seven months

The numbers either side of this deal are the story. Carlyle took control of The Very Group in November 2025 for a nominal £1, after PwC was appointed administrator to the group's holding company in a restructuring that ended the Barclay family's two-decade ownership. Carlyle had been the retailer's main creditor; the pound-coin handover simply converted debt into equity.

Seven months later, in June, Carlyle hired bankers at Barclays and JPMorgan and put the same business up for sale at roughly £2 billion. That was not vanity pricing — running a sale process was reportedly a condition of the change-of-control agreement that brought Very under Carlyle's ownership in the first place. But the obligation was to launch an auction, not to accept whatever it produced, and £2 billion turned out to be a ceiling the market wasn't willing to test.

A retail empire dismantled piece by piece

The Very Group is the last significant asset to fall from what the Barclay brothers, David and Frederick, built over four decades — a portfolio that at its peak spanned the Ritz hotel, the Telegraph titles, the parcel firm Yodel, and a private island fortress off Sark complete with its own castle. The retail arm traces back further still, to Littlewoods, the catalogue and pools business founded by John Moores in 1923. The Barclays bought it in 2002, merged it with Great Universal Stores' home-shopping arm in 2005, and rebranded the combined business — Shop Direct, then The Very Group — as the family's finances came under mounting strain.

That strain proved terminal. HSBC pursued the brothers over unpaid debts, the Telegraph was forced into a sale process of its own, and by late 2025 the retail business had followed the rest into restructuring. Under Carlyle, Very still turns over more than £2 billion a year selling fashion, electricals and homeware to 4.4 million customers through its Very and Littlewoods brands, from a converted aircraft hangar in Speke that serves as its Liverpool headquarters. Interim results for the 39 weeks to March 2026 showed the retail arm barely growing — up 0.1% — with sports up 7.5% but fashion down 4.5%, a pattern familiar to anyone watching British clothing retailers this year.

What it says about legacy UK retail

A £2 billion price tag on a business with roughly £1 billion in half-year retail revenue is not, on its face, an outlandish multiple. That bidders still balked says less about Very specifically than about appetite for UK consumer credit and catalogue-retail assets more broadly, at a moment when fashion demand has been soft across the sector and financing costs remain elevated. It also leaves Carlyle in an awkward spot: technically compliant with its sale obligation, but stuck holding a business it plainly intended to flip quickly.

For now, Very stays with Carlyle, and the Barclay name recedes further from a business it controlled for more than twenty years. The next marker to watch is whether Carlyle returns to market once the Ceconomy regulatory cloud over JD.com clears, or whether — absent a fresh buyer — the private equity firm settles in for a longer hold than it bargained for at £1.
