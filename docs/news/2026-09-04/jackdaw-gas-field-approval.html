Shell's Jackdaw North Sea gas field set for approval within weeks

An offshore drilling platform in the North Sea, where Shell's Jackdaw gas field sits 150 miles east of Aberdeen. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Government sources say Shell's long-delayed Jackdaw gas field could get final sign-off from the energy secretary as soon as mid-September.

Shell's Jackdaw gas field, 99% built and sitting idle 150 miles east of Aberdeen, is about to get the paperwork it has been waiting three years for. Government sources say energy secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh could grant final consent as soon as mid-September, before Parliament breaks for party conference season — a decision that would let gas start flowing to UK homes this winter and put an end to one of the North Sea's longest-running regulatory sagas.

Three years, one court ruling

Jackdaw was supposed to be simple. The Conservative government approved it in 2022, Shell and partner Equinor built it through their joint venture Adura, and production was pencilled in for 2026. Then the law changed underneath it. In June 2024 the Supreme Court ruled, in a case brought over a Surrey oil site, that environmental impact assessments for fossil fuel projects must account for "scope 3" emissions — the carbon released when the fuel is eventually burned, not just the carbon released pulling it out of the ground. Greenpeace and the campaign group Uplift used that precedent to challenge Jackdaw and its sister project Rosebank, an oil field off Shetland, and in early 2025 the Court of Session in Edinburgh agreed: both consents were unlawful. Judges suspended the ruling to let construction continue while the government redid its homework.

Shell filed a revised assessment for Jackdaw last September. A public consultation on the new climate analysis for both fields closed in August. What's left is a ministerial signature — but signatures on North Sea gas have proven unpredictable lately, and the government doing the signing is not the one that opened the consultation.

What's actually at stake

The physical facts of Jackdaw are not in dispute. The wellhead platform and four subsea wells are tied back by a 31-kilometre pipeline to Shell's Shearwater hub; the steel jacket went in during 2023, the topside last October, and drilling finished early this year. At peak output of roughly 40,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, Shell says the field could supply around 6% of Britain's gas; Uplift puts the figure nearer 2%. The gap matters because it is the crux of the argument for and against approval — whether Jackdaw meaningfully improves UK energy security or just adds emissions without denting import dependence. Uplift's Tessa Khan frames it starkly: "The science is clear that the world already has far more oil and gas than can ever be safely burned." Estimates of the field's lifetime emissions run from 23.6 to 35.8 million tonnes of CO2 — as much as 60-90% of Scotland's entire annual output in 2023.

For Shell, the commercial stakes are more modest than the politics suggest. The company already operates under a 38% Energy Profits Levy that runs until 2029, and its shares have drifted down about 2.2% this year — worse than the wider energy sector's 4% gain, though still ahead of BP's 13.2% slide. Approval doesn't transform Shell's earnings; a field that's already 99% built mostly just needs to stop being a stranded asset on the balance sheet. What it removes is uncertainty, and a green light would tell the market that London is still willing to permit large-scale gas infrastructure even under a government that campaigned on net zero.

Burnham's balancing act

The political backdrop has shifted since Jackdaw's court defeat. Andy Burnham became prime minister this summer in a cabinet reshuffle that moved Ed Miliband — the minister most associated with blocking new North Sea licenses — out of the energy brief and into the Foreign Office, replacing him with Fahnbulleh. Burnham has been notably blunter than his predecessor about the limits of an immediate transition away from fossil fuels: "We won't be able to stop using oil and gas for some time. That's just a fact." Energy minister Kate White has kept the department's public language cautious — "The process ended in August, and the Secretary of State will be taking those decisions in due course" — but government sources briefing on a mid-September timeline suggest the direction of travel is set.

Rosebank, the larger and more contentious oil field, is being decided separately and could land at a different time. Approving Jackdaw while leaving Rosebank pending would let the government claim it is backing a project industry frames as UK-based gas security while holding off, for now, on the field campaigners see as the bigger symbolic fight over new oil extraction.

What's next

The formal decision could arrive within days of Parliament's conference recess, likely alongside or shortly before a separate ruling on Rosebank. If Fahnbulleh signs off, gas could reach the grid this winter, and the government will have set a precedent for how it treats other court-quashed fossil fuel consents still working through the system. If she doesn't, or delays past conference season, that will be read in Aberdeen and in Shell's boardroom as a sign the reshuffle changed less than industry sources currently expect.
