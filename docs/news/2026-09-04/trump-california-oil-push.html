Trump administration escalates push for more oil drilling in California

An offshore oil platform in the Santa Barbara Channel, off the California coast. — Ken Lund / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Washington is escalating pressure on California's coastal permitting system to unlock more oil, but real barrels remain years and lawsuits away.

Washington's campaign to unlock more crude from California waters is entering a more aggressive phase, with federal officials now targeting the state's coastal permitting machinery directly rather than just proposing new leases and waiting for Sacramento to comply.

The Interior Department has moved toward authorizing fracking on a 45-year-old drilling platform nine miles off Ventura County, a step the California Coastal Commission opposes. "It creates far too many opportunities for something to go wrong," commissioner Ray Jackson said of the proposal. Separately, federal officials helped a Texas pipeline operator restart a line that had sat idle since a 2015 rupture near Refugio State Beach spilled tens of thousands of gallons of crude and fouled roughly 150 miles of coastline. And in May, the administration opened a federal review of California's coastal management program itself — the decades-old state mechanism, run largely through the Coastal Commission, that has been the main chokepoint for offshore development.

The bigger leasing plan

Those moves sit alongside a far larger proposal: the first new federal oil and gas leasing in California waters since the mid-1980s. Interior's draft five-year schedule floats as many as 34 lease auctions nationwide between 2026 and 2031, with six of them along the California coast between 2027 and 2030. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick framed the push as a response to what he called California's foot-dragging, saying "obstructionist policies that delay critical national infrastructure in the name of environmental extremism are unacceptable."

Governor Gavin Newsom has called the plan "idiotic" and "dead on arrival," and has pledged to sue. The administration has also moved to buy back an offshore wind lease off the California coast, discouraging renewable capacity even as it presses to expand fossil-fuel output — of a piece with its broader cancellation of clean-energy grants nationally.

Why the numbers are still small

Even administration estimates put the near-term payoff at modest scale: analysts cited in reporting on the Ventura fracking plan put the potential output increase at roughly 1,100 to 4,000 barrels a day, a rounding error against U.S. production of more than 13 million barrels daily. California's own oil output has been in structural decline for years — the state drew nearly half its electricity from renewables in 2024 — and Sacramento has shown no sign of loosening permitting even under legal and political pressure. Newsom suspended a swath of coastal-commission permitting last year in the aftermath of the state's wildfires, but that was disaster-response streamlining, not a concession on drilling.

Stanford environmental law professor Deborah Sivas and others frame the fight in structural terms: whether Washington can override a state's coastal authority when it dislikes the outcome. "This is about whether the Trump administration can strip California's authority because they don't like the answers," said Democratic strategist Ellenberger, a characterization state officials share. The 1969 Santa Barbara spill, which released an estimated 3.5 to 4.2 million gallons of crude and helped catalyze the modern environmental movement, still anchors California's institutional resistance to offshore expansion, and the Coastal Commission has cited it repeatedly in opposing new permits.

What would actually move the needle

For energy markets, the distinction that matters is between announcements and barrels. A five-year lease schedule does not itself produce oil — auctions, permitting and construction lag any lease sale by years, and California's legal environment guarantees each step will be litigated. Attorney General Rob Bonta has already said he wants the state excluded from the 2026-2031 leasing program outright. The more immediate signal to watch is the Ventura fracking authorization, which could be finalized within months and would be the first concrete test of whether federal pressure can override state coastal review rather than simply propose around it. Until then, this remains a jurisdictional fight rather than a supply one.
