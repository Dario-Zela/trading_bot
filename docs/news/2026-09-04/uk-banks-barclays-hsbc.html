UK banks in focus: Barclays' growth push weighed against HSBC 'sell' call

One Churchill Place, Barclays' Canary Wharf headquarters — Tom Page / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Analysts split on UK banks: Barclays' expansion is drawing growth bulls while Shore Capital reiterates a 'sell' on HSBC over stretched valuation.

British banks are trading on diverging narratives this week, and the split runs right through the sector's two biggest names. Barclays is being framed by analysts as a growth story — a bank still finding new avenues to expand even as UK retail banking matures. HSBC, meanwhile, has just picked up a reiterated 'sell' rating from a UK broker that says the stock has simply run too far.

The contrast matters less for what either bank did this week — neither made fresh announcements — than for what it says about how analysts are pricing the UK banking recovery. One side sees room to keep buying; the other says the good news is already in the share price.

Barclays: still finding places to grow

Barclays shares have gained roughly 34% over the past year, a touch behind the wider banking sector's 35% but well ahead of the broader market. Zacks Research holds a Strong Buy view on the stock, and the bull case rests less on Barclays' home turf than on where it has been buying.

The bank has spent the past two years reshaping its edges: the GoHenry acquisition to get a foothold in youth banking, Best Egg for digital personal lending, a tie-up with Samsung, and earlier purchases of the Tesco Bank and GM store-card books. UK lending rose 5% year-on-year in the first half of 2026, and group income for the half came to £16.5 billion, enough for management to lift its full-year income guidance to around £31.5 billion.

Capital return has kept pace. Barclays paid out £2.3 billion to shareholders in H1 — up 61% year-on-year — as part of a £9 billion distribution programme running since 2024, funded partly by £1.7 billion of structural cost savings banked through 2024–25, with a further £2 billion-plus targeted by 2028. Even after the share-price run, Barclays trades on a trailing price-to-earnings ratio of about 10 times, against a sector average nearer 14 — which is the crux of the bull argument: growth that the market hasn't fully paid for yet. The caveats are cost inflation and regulatory changes expected in 2027, which Zacks flags as the main reasons it isn't calling the stock an outright bargain.

HSBC: a sell call on valuation, not fundamentals

HSBC's problem, according to Shore Capital, isn't the business — it's the price. Analyst Gary Greenwood reiterated his sell rating this week, lifting his target price to 1,335p from 1,250p after HSBC upgraded its banking net interest income guidance and flagged further cost savings, which pushed Shore Capital's 2026–2028 earnings forecasts up 4–6%. Even so, the new target implies roughly 13% downside from current levels.

The arithmetic behind the call: HSBC shares trade at around 2.1 times 2026 forecast tangible net asset value for a return on tangible equity near 18%. Shore Capital's argument is that this multiple only makes sense if HSBC can sustain today's elevated profitability indefinitely — and the broker isn't convinced cyclicality has left the business for good. If returns dip in a future downturn, today's price leaves no cushion.

What the split signals

Neither call is really a bet against UK banks as a group. Barclays' bulls and HSBC's bear both accept the same underlying story: net interest income has held up better than feared, cost programmes are delivering, and capital returns are generous. Where they part ways is on how much of that is already reflected in the share price. Barclays, on a mid-single-digit P/E discount to peers, still has room to re-rate if the growth initiatives land. HSBC, re-rated further already, has less margin for anything to go wrong.

That divergence is itself a signal worth watching. Broker positioning like this tends to shift with the next round of guidance — UK banks' third-quarter updates, due through October, will show whether Barclays' US card and wealth expansion is converting into earnings, and whether HSBC's net interest income momentum is strong enough to keep justifying a premium multiple. Until then, the sector's direction depends on which of these two theses turns out to be right.
