Kyrgios banned one month after positive cocaine test

Nick Kyrgios pictured during his playing days at The Queen's Club, London, 2017. — Carine06 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Nick Kyrgios accepted a one-month ban after testing positive for cocaine in June, derailing his comeback and undercutting his own criticism of tennis's doping enforcement.

Nick Kyrgios has spent the past year cast as tennis's self-appointed conscience, needling the sport's anti-doping system for treating its biggest stars with what he called disgraceful leniency. On Thursday, the International Tennis Integrity Agency handed him the same sentence he once mocked as too soft for others — a one-month ban, this time for himself.

The ITIA confirmed that a sample Kyrgios provided at the ATP 250 event in Mallorca on June 22 tested positive for benzoylecgonine, the metabolite produced when the body breaks down cocaine. He was provisionally suspended on August 4, then Kyrgios said the drug use had happened socially, on a night out before he was due to play, and had nothing to do with performance. An independent expert at a WADA-accredited laboratory reviewed the case and found the account consistent with the trace levels detected in his sample.

"I made a huge mistake and I take full responsibility for it," Kyrgios said in a statement, adding that he had entered a treatment programme.

A lighter sentence, on conditions

Cocaine is banned only in competition under the World Anti-Doping Code, and a standard violation carries a starting point of roughly three months. Kyrgios's ban was cut to one, contingent on completing the treatment programme; the ITIA said the suspension would end on September 3, a day before this article was published. He forfeits the prize money and ranking points earned in Mallorca, a token loss for a player now ranked 918th in the world, some 900 places below the No. 13 he reached at his peak.

The timing is brutal for a comeback that had barely got going. Kyrgios, 31, has played only sporadically since 2022 because of wrist and knee surgeries, and had talked openly about using low-stakes ATP 250 events like Mallorca to rebuild match fitness. A month on the sidelines, however lightly imposed, is a month he doesn't have to spare.

The irony he can't outrun

What makes the case land harder than a routine doping brief is Kyrgios's own record as tennis's loudest doping scold. He spent much of the past two years attacking the ITIA's handling of Jannik Sinner, who tested positive twice for the steroid clostebol but escaped a ban after arguing contamination from a physiotherapist's cut, and Iga Swiatek, who accepted a one-month suspension after testing positive for trimetazidine, also blamed on contamination. Kyrgios called the affair "disgusting for our sport" and accused the ITIA of applying its rules unevenly depending on a player's profile.

He now holds the exact sentence he once said was too lenient for a Grand Slam champion. The ITIA's defence would presumably be that the cases aren't comparable — Sinner and Swiatek's substances were performance-adjacent and attributed to contamination, while cocaine has no performance benefit and Kyrgios owned the mistake outright. But the optics are unhelpful for an agency already accused of moving the goalposts case by case, and Kyrgios's critics will note that the same complaint about consistency now applies to him.

What's next

Kyrgios is free to return to the tour once his suspension lapses and the treatment programme is confirmed complete, though he has not said which tournament he'll target first. Whether the ITIA can shake the perception of an ad hoc disciplinary process will depend less on this case than on how it handles the next celebrity name that fails a test — the question Kyrgios himself kept asking, right up until it was asked of him.
