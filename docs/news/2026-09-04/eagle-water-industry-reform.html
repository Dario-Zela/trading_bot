Government 'ready to change the law' to reform water industry, says Eagle

Angela Eagle, who became Environment Secretary in July 2026, says the government is ready to legislate on water industry reform. — Richard Townshend / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Environment Secretary Angela Eagle says ministers are ready to legislate for a new single water regulator, but no bill or timetable yet exists.

Britain's water companies got another reminder this week that the regulatory ground beneath them is still shifting. Environment Secretary Angela Eagle told MPs the government stands "ready to change the law" to push through reform of the sector, doubling down on plans first sketched out in January's white paper and now given fresh political weight by Prime Minister Andy Burnham.

The pledge builds on the Cunliffe review, the independent inquiry led by Sir Jon Cunliffe that reported in July 2025 and recommended scrapping Ofwat altogether. The government took up that recommendation in its white paper, A New Vision for Water, published on 20 January by Eagle's predecessor Emma Reynolds. It set out plans to fold Ofwat's functions together with water-related duties currently split across the Environment Agency, Natural England and the Drinking Water Inspectorate, creating a single body — provisionally named the Clean Water Authority — to oversee economic regulation, pollution enforcement and drinking-water standards under one roof. A Water Reform Bill has been promised to carry the changes into law, though no date has been fixed for its introduction.

A reshuffled department, a bigger stage

Eagle took over at the Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs in July, when Burnham reshuffled his cabinet after becoming prime minister. She inherits a department mid-way through the largest overhaul of water regulation since privatisation in 1989, with £104bn of private investment pencilled in over five years and a live commitment to halve sewage pollution by 2030.

What has changed is the political framing. In his first statement to the Commons as prime minister on 1 September, Burnham called the water industry "a leaking monument" to the failures of privatisation and pledged to set out, before the end of the year, a broader plan for "stronger public control" over essentials including water, energy and transport. He was careful to temper expectations, conceding that reversing three decades of private ownership would be "very difficult" — a signal that outright renationalisation remains unlikely, even as the rhetoric hardens.

Why traders should barely blink

None of this changes the investment case for listed water names — United Utilities, Severn Trent and Pennon — in the near term. No draft bill exists, no timetable has been set, and the substantive policy detail (price controls, appeal rights against the Competition and Markets Authority, dividend rules) was already flagged in January. Analysts at BNP Paribas, restarting coverage of the sector this year, said they do not view nationalisation as a plausible risk despite the noise from Westminster.

What is worth tracking is regulatory certainty, which the white paper explicitly named as a goal for attracting long-term capital into a sector that needs it. A confirmed bill timetable, or detail on how the Clean Water Authority's powers over pricing and enforcement will differ from Ofwat's, would be the first genuine catalyst. Until then, this is a statement of intent layered on a statement of intent — noteworthy for UK utilities-sector positioning, immaterial for anything trading on a shorter horizon.
