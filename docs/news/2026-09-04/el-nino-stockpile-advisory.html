Minister urges Britons to stockpile supplies ahead of 'supersize' El Niño winter

Environment secretary Angela Eagle, who has urged households to keep several days of food and water in reserve ahead of a forecast 'supersize' El Niño winter. — Richard Townshend / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Environment secretary Angela Eagle has urged Britons to stockpile food and water as the WMO forecasts a possibly record-breaking El Niño peaking near 4°C in November.

Angela Eagle, the environment secretary, has told the public to keep several days of food and water in the house before winter — advice pitched not as routine seasonal caution but as a direct response to what forecasters say could be the strongest El Niño on record.

"We're about to experience probably the largest El Niño that has ever been seen, and that will lead to drier summers, wetter winters, with more extreme storms in this country," Eagle said. "If you've got a store of a bit of food that can keep you going for a while before the emergency services can get to you, you're going to be a lot better off than if you haven't."

The UN's World Meteorological Organization has described the event as being "supersized before our eyes," in the words of secretary-general António Guterres, with Pacific Ocean temperatures already running 2.6°C above the 30-year average and forecast to peak near 4°C in November. Climate analysts cited in reporting on the forecast say no El Niño has reached that level in at least 1,000 years — a claim that, if it holds, would put this event beyond anything in the instrumental or proxy record used by modern climate science.

From guidance to instruction

What marks this out from previous UK ministerial messaging on El Niño is the specificity. Governments have long issued general resilience advice — the Cabinet Office's gov.uk/prepare site has existed quietly since 2024, offering guidance on flooding, power cuts and cyberattack — but it has rarely been front-loaded onto a named minister making a direct, urgent case to households. Eagle's comments are being folded into a wider Cabinet Office resilience campaign, due to launch more broadly later this year, that also covers non-climate risks such as hostile-state attacks on infrastructure. The government is at pains to say the advice isn't a signal that a specific emergency is imminent, but rather an attempt to build baseline household resilience so emergency services can prioritise the vulnerable during a crisis. Still, tying it explicitly to a forecast event — rather than leaving it as generic preparedness literature — is a change in register.

Alongside the stockpiling message, Eagle pointed to infrastructure spending already under way, including Havant Thicket, the first new reservoir to be built in England since 1992, as part of a broader push on water resilience and farming adaptation.

What the WMO is actually forecasting

The scale of the underlying event is what gives the advisory weight. WMO chief Celeste Saulo said El Niño "has the potential to deliver a massive blow to communities and economies across the world," and the agency's modelling puts the probability of a very strong event this autumn and winter above 90%, with roughly 69% odds it surpasses every El Niño on record since 1950. Persistence through February 2027 is judged close to certain. For the UK, the pattern typically means a wetter, stormier autumn and winter with a heightened risk of extreme rainfall events — the flooding side of El Niño, rather than the drought and wildfire risk facing the tropics and southern Africa. The UN has separately warned the event could push some 50 million people into acute hunger globally, concentrated in regions dependent on rain-fed agriculture.

The commodity read-through

For markets, the signal is less about the UK's three-day tin-of-beans guidance and more about what an official body is implicitly pricing in by issuing it. Commodity desks have already been positioning for El Niño-driven supply shocks: analysts at Citigroup have flagged cocoa could push toward $5,000–$6,000 a tonne if the West African crop is damaged, building on the surge from roughly $2,500 to $12,000 a tonne during the 2023–24 event. Wheat production in Australia is projected to fall by around 9 million tonnes in the 2026/27 season, and sugar producers in India and Thailand have historically seen 20–30% output declines in strong El Niño years, pushing them toward net imports. Some estimates suggest a 50% rise in the FAO food price index by year-end could add more than a percentage point to G7 headline inflation in 2027.

A UK government minister telling the public to stock up isn't itself a market-moving event, but it is a data point on how seriously officials are treating the underlying forecast — and officials with access to Met Office and WMO modelling are, in effect, signalling that they expect the disruption to be material enough to warrant a public information campaign, not just internal contingency planning.

What's next

The WMO's next formal El Niño update is expected in the coming weeks as the event approaches its projected November peak; a materially higher or lower reading against the 4°C forecast would be the clearest early test of how this compares with historical events. Domestically, watch for the wider Cabinet Office resilience campaign launch and whether the Treasury or Defra follow with any funding for agricultural contingency stocks — a step that would confirm the government is preparing for supply disruption beyond the household level.
