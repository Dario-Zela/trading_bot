India's avocado boom: from 'dog fruit' to supermarket darling

Avocados, once left to rot on trees in parts of India, are now a fast-growing supermarket category. — Daniel Paullll / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Once dismissed as 'dog fruit,' avocados are now a supermarket staple in India, with demand outpacing thin domestic supply nearly two-to-one.

There was a time, not so long ago, when the avocado tree at Cottanad Plantations in Wayanad, Kerala, was planted purely to shade the coffee bushes beneath it. The fruit itself fell to the ground and rotted, or was eaten by stray dogs — hence the unglamorous nickname that stuck in parts of southern India for decades: "dog fruit."

That fruit is now stacked in netted bags at supermarket checkouts from Mumbai to Bengaluru, priced like a treat rather than an afterthought. "Overnight, prices doubled and then kept rising," says Sunil Bopaiah, group manager at Cottanad, recalling the moment in 2011 when Bollywood actress Shilpa Shetty mentioned in an interview that she used avocado in her skincare routine. It was a small, throwaway remark. It reset the market.

From shade tree to cash crop

Cottanad now grows avocados across 40 acres and harvested 10 to 15 tonnes last year, with a target of 40–50 tonnes within three or four years. It is not alone. In Wayanad's Ambalavayal region, cultivation has spread from a single tree planted during the colonial era to hundreds of smallholder orchards — a shift documented by local growers like Shiju Sebastian, who pulled 3,500kg from his plot this year after starting eight years ago. Further north, in landlocked Bhopal, Harshit Godha's Indo-Israel Avocado Nursery has supplied roughly 18,000 saplings to farmers since 2021, importing cultivation techniques more associated with Israeli desert agriculture than Indian hill farms.

The economics explain the enthusiasm. Industry estimates put returns from avocado at $6,500–$13,000 per acre — several multiples of what the same land would earn under traditional crops like paddy or areca nut. Dr Ganeshan Karunakaran, a principal scientist at the government's ICAR-Indian Institute of Horticultural Research, has been fielding a steady rise in queries from farmers weighing the switch.

A market still running on imports

For now, most of that supermarket avocado is not grown in India at all. The country imports around 15,000 tonnes a year, against domestic production of only 8,000–9,000 tonnes — a gap wide enough that market researchers expect total consumption to roughly quadruple, from about 10,000 tonnes in 2024 to near 40,000 tonnes by 2026–27, with import volumes alone forecast to grow close to 5% annually over the same stretch.

That combination — soaring demand, thin domestic supply — is what has planters and nursery owners betting on avocado as a long-term crop rather than a fad. It is also what has kept prices high enough in Indian shops to make the fruit still feel aspirational, a decade and a half after Shetty's comment sent it there. Restaurants have followed the trend uphill: Hussain Shahzad, executive chef at Mumbai's Hunger Inc. Hospitality, which runs The Bombay Canteen and Veronica's, now builds menu items around a fruit his kitchens barely stocked a generation ago.

What's next

The open question is whether India's growers can close the supply gap before the import bill becomes structurally embedded, the way it has for edible oils and pulses. Groups like Cottanad and the Bhopal nursery are betting they can, backed by returns per acre that dwarf legacy crops. The trade data over the next two harvest cycles — whether import growth cools as domestic tonnage rises, or simply keeps pace with demand — will show whether this is a passing craze or the seed of a genuine new agricultural export-substitute industry.
