Shell and Chevron sign Ghana exploration deal with state oil firm GNPC

An offshore oil platform; Shell and Chevron's Ghana deal targets deepwater acreage off the country's western coast. — Berardo62 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Shell and Chevron signed a non-binding MoU with Ghana's GNPC for the South Deepwater Tano block as Accra tries to reverse a sixth straight year of oil-output decline.

Shell and Chevron have signed a non-binding agreement with Ghana's state oil company to explore for crude in one of the last big untested blocks off the country's coast, a bet that two of the industry's largest operators can help reverse six straight years of falling output.

The memorandum of understanding was signed on September 1 in Accra, on the sidelines of Africa Oil Week, by Ghana's government, the Ghana National Petroleum Corporation (GNPC) and its exploration arm GNPC Explorco, alongside Shell Overseas Holdings and Chevron Sub-Saharan Africa Ventures. It covers rights to the South Deepwater Tano Cape Three Points block, a 3,482 sq km tract in water between 2,000 and 3,500 metres deep, sitting next to the Hess-operated acreage where seven discoveries have already been made.

Nothing here is finalised. Shell has described the MoU as a framework for negotiating final licence terms, subject to regulatory approval — there is no disclosed work programme, spending commitment or drilling timetable. It is, in effect, a statement of intent from two majors who have largely sat out Ghana's upstream sector in recent years, at a moment when the government is rewriting the rules to get them back in.

A sixth year of decline

Ghana's crude output has fallen every year since 2019, when production peaked near 200,000 barrels a day. By 2025 it had dropped to roughly 150,000 b/d, with the Institute for Energy Security estimating cumulative full-year production has nearly halved from 71.4 million barrels to 37.3 million. GNPC's own revenue has fallen more than 61% as a result, compounded by a separate policy change that cut the corporation's share of petroleum income from 30% to 15%.

Energy minister John Jinapor used his Africa Oil Week address to unveil the government's answer: a package of fiscal sweeteners designed to make Ghanaian blocks more attractive against competing basins in Namibia, Suriname and elsewhere. GNPC's initial carried interest in new agreements will be cut from 15% to 10%, petroleum agreement terms extended from 25 to 30 years, the traditional upfront signature bonus replaced with a payment tied to a discovery being declared commercial, and loss-carry-forward periods doubled to ten years. Jinapor's own summary of the logic was blunt: "I'm better off having 10% of 1 billion than 15% of 1,000."

The government says it has already lined up more than $3.5 billion in investment commitments, and GNPC is due to begin drilling its own Voltain Basin acreage in October. Agreements covering the ageing Jubilee and TEN fields, Ghana's two producing deepwater developments, have also been extended through 2040.

Whether the South Deepwater Tano MoU converts into an actual exploration programme will depend on seismic results and commercial terms still to be negotiated — non-binding paper is cheap, and Ghana has watched previous investment pledges stall before a drill bit ever touched the seabed. The next marker is whether Shell and Chevron move from framework to a signed petroleum agreement, which would need ratification by Ghana's parliament, as happened with the original SDWT licence in 2018.
