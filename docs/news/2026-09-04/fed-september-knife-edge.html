Payrolls miss and Waller's dovish tilt put September Fed call on a knife-edge

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor, where rate-sensitive desks spent Friday repricing a Fed decision markets can't call. — Eduard Hueber / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
August payrolls split three ways — soft hiring, falling unemployment, hot wages — leaving Fed rate futures at a genuine 50-50 for September's hike-or-hold decision.

Federal funds futures did something they rarely do on Friday morning: they landed almost exactly on 50-50. Hours after the Bureau of Labor Statistics published August's employment report, traders in Fed funds futures and swaps were pricing the September 16 rate decision as a coin flip — a quarter-point hike to 3.75%-4.00%, or a hold at 3.50%-3.75%, each with roughly even odds. For a market that spends most of its life leaning decisively one way, that is unusual, and it is the data's fault.

A jobs report that argues with itself

The headline number disappointed: employers added 142,000 jobs in August, short of the roughly 160,000 economists had penciled in. On its own, that would read as a case for standing pat. But the rest of the release cut the other way. The unemployment rate fell to 4.2% from 4.3% in July — not the direction one expects from a labor market that's supposedly cooling. And wages ran hot: average hourly earnings rose 3.8% year-over-year, up from 3.6% in July and above the 3.7% forecast. Add in downward revisions that stripped a combined 86,000 jobs from June and July, and the report offers no single, coherent story. It reads instead like three different indicators pointing three different directions, which is exactly why the futures market couldn't settle on one.

That ambiguity matters more than usual because of who's fighting over how to read it. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh, confirmed into the role earlier this year, used his Jackson Hole address on August 28 to warn that inflation — not the labor market — deserves the Fed's full attention, arguing prices are unlikely to drift back to the 2% target on their own. Fed funds futures responded by pushing hike odds up to roughly 60%, and by the following week they'd climbed further, to around 66-68%. Warsh's position also put him publicly at odds with the White House, which has pushed for lower rates.

Waller's Lennon line

Then came Thursday. Speaking at a Reuters NEXT Newsmaker interview in Washington, Governor Christopher Waller pulled the other way. "To paraphrase John Lennon, I'm willing to give disinflation a chance," he said, adding that he'd support holding rates steady at the September meeting if incoming data over the following two weeks continued to show inflation cooling. He was careful to leave the door open — "I'm willing to sit and wait and be patient, but if the progress we've seen reverses, it's time to pull the trigger and hike rates" — but markets heard the hold case louder than the hedge. Hike odds fell roughly 12 percentage points, back toward 50-50.

Markets took the remarks as a green light. The Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed 624 points, or 1.18%, to 53,686.11 on Thursday — its best day in a month. The S&P 500 gained 1.06% to 7,747.71, its strongest session since early August, while the Nasdaq Composite jumped 1.4% to 26,584.06. Treasury yields eased alongside the rally: the two-year note slipped three basis points to 4.34%, and the ten-year eased toward 4.75%, both retreating from highs set earlier in the week.

Why the coin flip matters more than the call

Friday's payrolls data landed into that already-uncertain backdrop and did nothing to resolve it — if anything, its internal contradictions reinforced the split. For algorithmic and systematic trading desks, a genuine 50-50 read on Fed policy is a harder problem than a market leaning 70-30 either way. Rate-sensitive positioning in Treasury futures, equity index derivatives and the dollar typically clusters around a consensus view; when that consensus doesn't exist, models built to trade the gap between market pricing and fundamental data have no edge to trade, and desks are left rebalancing across asset classes without a directional anchor. That is a source of volatility in its own right, independent of which way the Fed ultimately goes.

Waller was explicit about what would settle his own vote: the August CPI report, due before the September 15-16 FOMC meeting, which he singled out as the decisive input. If inflation continues to cool, the hold camp likely wins and Thursday's rally has room to extend. If it runs hot, Warsh's Jackson Hole warning looks prescient and the hike case reasserts itself — probably with less warning built into asset prices than markets currently assume, given how quickly odds have swung on a single speech this week. Either way, the next two weeks carry more information for rate markets than the jobs report just did, and the Fed's own summary of economic projections, due alongside the September decision, will show whether Warsh has the votes to match his rhetoric.
