Brush-on fluoride treatment halts child tooth decay in seconds, trial finds

In one sentence
A 830-child US trial found a seconds-long fluoride brush-on stopped tooth decay in most treated baby teeth — no drill, needle or surgery required.

A clear liquid, brushed onto a cavity with a sponge-tip the size of a match head, held still for a few seconds. No needle, no drill, no laughing gas. In a trial of 830 children under six run across Michigan, New York and Iowa, that alone stopped decay from spreading in more than half of the baby teeth it was applied to — a result that, if it holds up under regulatory scrutiny, could change how the youngest patients experience the dentist's chair.

The compound is silver diamine fluoride, or SDF — silver, fluoride and ammonia in solution, used in Japan and elsewhere since the 1960s but only cleared for U.S. use since 2014, and even then solely as a treatment for tooth sensitivity. Dentists have prescribed it off-label for cavities anyway, on the logic that something that kills the bacteria driving decay and hardens the tooth surface ought to work on the underlying disease too. The new trial, led by Margherita Fontana at the University of Michigan and published in JAMA Pediatrics, is the largest U.S. test of that logic to date, funded with more than $12 million from the National Institute of Dental and Craniofacial Research.

Why a brush beats a drill, for some

Children were recruited through dental offices, pediatric medical practices and Head Start programmes — a deliberate choice, since untreated early childhood caries sends thousands of American toddlers to emergency rooms each year, where ER doctors can manage pain but not repair a tooth. Left alone, those cases often end up in an operating room, under general anaesthesia, for surgery many parents understandably want to avoid in a one-year-old. Applied every six months, SDF arrested progression in the majority of treated teeth without any of that.

"This is a very effective and safe treatment — even in children as young as 1," Fontana said. "For almost anyone, this can arrest the decay and stop the infection."

The catch is cosmetic rather than clinical: SDF turns the decayed portion of a tooth permanently black. For a back molar that will fall out in a few years, most parents in the trial appear to have judged that an acceptable trade against surgery. For a front tooth, the calculus is less obvious, and the trial's published results do not break out parental satisfaction by tooth location.

What has to happen before this reaches every dentist's chair

SDF's manufacturer, Elevate Oral Care, still needs to file for FDA approval as a cavity treatment specifically — the current sensitivity-treatment label leaves off-label use exposed to inconsistent insurance reimbursement, which has limited uptake outside research settings. No timeline for that filing has been announced, and nothing in the trial data yet points to which insurers, dental chains or hospital systems might move first if approval comes through. Until it does, SDF will remain what it has been for a decade: a tool dentists reach for informally, now with considerably stronger evidence behind it.
