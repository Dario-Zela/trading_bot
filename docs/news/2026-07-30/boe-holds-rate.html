Bank of England holds rate at 3.75% as services inflation keeps hike risk alive

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which held its benchmark rate at 3.75% on 30 July. — Wikimedia Commons / geograph.org.uk
In one sentence
The BoE held its Bank Rate at 3.75% on 30 July, but persistently elevated services inflation and a widening hawkish minority on the MPC kept the door open to a rate rise later this year.

The Bank of England left its Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% on Thursday, the fourth straight hold since December's cut, but the accompanying minutes and a fresh Monetary Policy Report made clear the committee is no longer treating a rate rise as a remote possibility. Services inflation — the stickiest, most domestically-driven slice of the price index — ran at 3.7% in May, more than 80 basis points above where the Bank needs it if headline inflation is to settle durably at 2%. Governor Andrew Bailey's press conference, moved to Bloomberg's London offices this month while the Bank's own conference centre has its AV system replaced, was as much about explaining why a hike is now a live option as why the committee isn't pulling the trigger yet.

Where the pressure is coming from

Headline UK inflation has, on the surface, been behaving. The consumer price index eased to 2.6% in June, a 15-month low, pulled down by softer food and fuel costs. But the Bank's own preferred gauge of underlying price pressure — services inflation, which strips out the volatile goods and energy components — moved the other way, climbing from 3.2% in April to 3.7% in May before easing only fractionally to around 3.6% in June. That is the gap the MPC has been staring at all summer: a headline number drifting toward target while the services component, which captures everything from haircuts to hotel rooms to hospitality wages, stays stubbornly elevated.

The Bank's new Monetary Policy Report trimmed its forecast for peak inflation this year to just over 3.25%, down from an earlier projection of 3.6%–3.7%, and pencilled in CPI running a little under 3% in the third quarter before rising again to a little over 3.25% in the fourth. That is a modest downgrade in the inflation threat, but not a retreat from it — and it leaves the committee explaining a rate path that neither confirms cuts are coming nor rules out a hike.

A widening hawkish minority

The vote itself tells the more interesting story. The MPC was unanimous in holding rates as recently as March. By April, chief economist Huw Pill had broken ranks to vote for an increase. In June, external member Megan Greene joined him, producing a 7–2 split in favour of holding at 3.75%. Thursday's decision came with what the Bank's own materials describe as a continued two-way debate on the committee — the hawkish wing pushing to tighten further even as the majority judges current policy restrictive enough to keep squeezing inflation out of the system gradually.

Pill had already signalled where he stood. Asked on BBC Wales' Walescast earlier this month whether rates would need to rise over the coming year, he gave a blunt answer: "The short answer is yes." His concern, he said, is that UK economic growth has been running ahead of the economy's productive capacity, sustaining the kind of inflationary pressure that doesn't resolve itself. It's a minority view on the MPC, but a growing one — and precisely the dynamic markets are now pricing into short-sterling futures and gilt yields.

Oil, Westminster and what happens next

The backdrop hasn't made the committee's job easier. The Iran–US conflict has kept the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed for months, and Brent crude's spike above $100 a barrel earlier this summer forced the MPC to weigh an energy shock against an already-elevated services number — the exact combination Bailey has previously called the Bank's "most difficult" scenario, where higher fuel costs risk feeding into wage and price expectations rather than passing through and fading. Crude has since eased back from its highs, taking some of the acute pressure off, but two-year gilt yields were still trading near 4.35% and sterling around $1.327 heading into Thursday's decision, reflecting a market pricing close to even odds on further tightening this year.

Domestically, the decision lands just as Prime Minister Andy Burnham's new government has scrapped VAT on household energy bills, a move that should mechanically pull the headline inflation rate lower in the months ahead even as it does nothing to address the services-price stickiness the MPC is actually worried about. The Bank isn't alone in confronting this pattern, either: the US Federal Reserve held rates on Wednesday with three officials dissenting in favour of a hike, the widest such split in nearly a decade, suggesting the reflex among major central banks has shifted from debating the pace of cuts to debating whether cuts are still the right call at all.

The next test comes with August's labour market data and the September MPC meeting, when the committee will have a clearer read on whether June's services print was a plateau or a peak. A hike is not the base case — Bailey and the seven-member majority still see current policy as sufficiently restrictive — but for the first time since the cutting cycle began, it is a case the Bank is visibly rehearsing rather than dismissing.
