Sam Altman to meet White House's Wiles ahead of AI framework deadline

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, who is meeting senior Trump administration officials in Washington this week. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Sam Altman meets White House chief of staff Susie Wiles this week, days before Trump's AI framework deadline lands on 1 August.

Sam Altman is spending this week in Washington doing what chief executives do when a regulatory deadline is bearing down: showing up in person. The OpenAI boss told CNBC he will meet Susie Wiles, the White House chief of staff and one of Donald Trump's closest advisers, before the administration finalises a framework for policing powerful AI models — a deadline the president himself set at 60 days, and one that lands on Friday.

The framework traces back to an executive order Trump signed on 2 June, titled Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security. It directs the National Security Agency, working with the Treasury, the Pentagon and the Department of Homeland Security, to build a classified benchmarking process that decides which AI systems count as "covered frontier models" — the ones judged capable enough to pose a national security risk. Developers who agree to the framework would give the government early looks at new models, up to 30 days before public release, in exchange for a voice in how the rules get applied to them. Crucially, the order stops short of creating any licensing or preclearance regime; participation is voluntary, at least on paper.

That distinction is exactly why Altman's week of meetings matters. A voluntary framework is still being drafted by people who can be talked to, and Altman is talking to a lot of them. Besides Wiles, he is due to see Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, and he met Senator Ted Cruz and a group of Senate Democrats on Wednesday. He's also expected to sit down with Senator Mark Warner, the top Democrat on the Senate Intelligence Committee, who has separately been pushing legislation that would require mandatory safety testing before advanced models go public — a considerably harder line than the White House's voluntary approach.

A lobbying push with a live backdrop

Altman says he has already seen a draft of the framework and is using the trip to discuss OpenAI's next generation of models, cybersecurity and what he's called the need for the US to "stay ahead" in AI globally — language that doubles as an argument against tighter rules. The timing is not incidental. OpenAI is still working through the fallout from a disclosed incident in which one of its systems behaved unexpectedly during internal testing, the kind of episode that hands ammunition to lawmakers like Warner who want binding checks rather than a voluntary handshake. Over 1,100 current and former AI lab staff, including OpenAI employees, have separately petitioned Washington to build a mechanism for slowing deployment if warning signs appear.

None of this guarantees Altman gets the light-touch version he's after. But eleventh-hour access to the people writing the rules is worth more than a comment period, and OpenAI knows it. The framework is due by 1 August; what it says about mandatory testing, and whether Warner's harder line gets any purchase before then, is the next thing to watch.
