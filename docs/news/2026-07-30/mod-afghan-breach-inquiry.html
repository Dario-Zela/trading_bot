MoD Afghan data breach was a 'foreseeable' failure, inquiry finds

The Ministry of Defence Main Building on Whitehall, where the 2022 data breach originated. — Harland Quarrington / MOD, Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Commons inquiry brands the 2022 MoD leak of 30,000 Afghan resettlement records a 'foreseeable' failure, tied to dozens of deaths and years of delay.

The email was meant to reach 150 people. It reached one — the wrong one — carrying details on more than 30,000. That single mistake, made from UK Special Forces headquarters in London in February 2022, has taken almost four and a half years, a secret £7 billion evacuation scheme, an unprecedented superinjunction and, now, a parliamentary inquiry to fully surface. On Thursday, the Commons Defence Committee delivered its verdict: the breach was not a one-off blunder but a foreseeable systemic failure, born of risks the Ministry of Defence had already been warned about and chose not to fix.

The committee's report, titled Shifting heaven and earth?, is the fullest official account yet of how a spreadsheet holding the resettlement applications of Afghans who had worked alongside British forces ended up outside government control — and of the human cost that followed. Committee chair Tan Dhesi said the inquiry "began with a data breach, but became about the real lives affected by delay, secrecy and flawed decision-making."

A warning ignored

The MoD had been handling applications to the Afghan Relocations and Assistance Policy (ARAP), the scheme set up in 2021 for interpreters, drivers and others who had risked their lives working with UK forces, through Excel spreadsheets stored on SharePoint — a system the department itself knew was unsuited to sensitive personal data. The Commons Public Accounts Committee, reporting in November 2025, found that breaches serious enough to require reporting to the Information Commissioner's Office had already occurred in 2021, before the scheme was even a year old. Sir Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, that committee's chair, described "the farrago of errors and missteps that led to, and followed, the Afghan data breach."

The Defence Committee's findings go further, framing the failure in language with legal weight. Calling a disaster "foreseeable" is not the same as calling it unlucky: it implies the department knew, or should have known, what was coming and had the opportunity to stop it. That distinction matters for any Afghan claimant now pursuing the MoD through the courts, and for the ministers and officials who signed off on the department's data practices between 2021 and 2022.

The breach itself was only discovered by accident. A man in Afghanistan posted on Facebook in August 2023 naming nine people from the leaked list and suggesting he could release the rest — what government sources at the time called "essentially blackmail." The following month, the MoD sought an injunction; Mr Justice Robin Knowles granted a superinjunction so sweeping it barred reporting of the injunction's own existence. It held for nearly two years, only lifted by the High Court in July 2025 once the resettlement scheme it had been shielding was wound down.

The human toll

What the superinjunction obscured was not an abstract data-protection lapse but a threat to people the Taliban had reason to hunt down. Of 231 individuals formally notified by the MoD that their data had been compromised, research submitted to Parliament found 49 reported that a family member or colleague had since been killed in connection with the leak. Another 99 said their own lives had been directly threatened, and around 200 more reported threats against themselves or relatives. The MoD's own risk assessment at the time put the number of people potentially endangered — applicants and their dependants combined — as high as 100,000.

The response, run under cover of the injunction, was the Afghanistan Response Route, a resettlement channel the government has spent an estimated £7 billion administering. Yet the committee found the scheme has moved slowly: of roughly 27,278 people judged eligible for relocation because of the breach, only 3,383 had arrived in the UK as of the report's cut-off, leaving close to 24,000 still waiting, many in hiding or in third countries, years after their exposure.

What accountability looks like now

The Defence Committee is not confident the MoD has fixed the underlying weaknesses — a conclusion the Public Accounts Committee reached independently eight months earlier when it warned a repeat incident was still plausible. Both inquiries point to the same root cause: a department that treated data protection as an administrative afterthought while running a scheme whose entire purpose was to protect people from identification.

The government now has until 30 September to respond formally to the committee's findings, a deadline that will force ministers to state, on the record, what changes have actually been made since 2022 rather than what was promised in select-committee hearings. For the Afghans still named in that original spreadsheet, the more immediate question is simpler: whether "foreseeable" translates into faster resettlement, or merely into a sharper line in a report. Legal claims against the MoD, including the first formal case lodged by affected families, will test whether the finding carries consequences beyond Whitehall's own paperwork.
