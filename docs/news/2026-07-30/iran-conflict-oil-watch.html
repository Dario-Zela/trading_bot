IRAN WATCH · By M. Halloway · 2026-07-30 · 3 min read

Iran-US conflict grinds into a fourth week as Brent nears $91

A US carrier strike group transits the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint now at the centre of the oil market's risk pricing. — Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class J. Alexander Delgado / US Navy, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brent jumped nearly 8% to $90.74 after fresh US-Iran strikes and tanker attacks near Hormuz, with a fourth week of fighting keeping crude's risk premium elevated.

Brent crude jumped almost 8% to $90.74 a barrel on Wednesday, its sharpest one-day move since the war reignited, after the United States carried out what Central Command called a "heavy wave" of strikes against Iran and Tehran attempted a missile attack on American forces that was intercepted before impact. The exchange marks the fourth week of renewed hostilities since a ceasefire brokered in the spring collapsed in early July, and the oil market is once again pricing supply risk that briefly looked like it was fading.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles on Tuesday night at Jordan's Muwaffaq Salti air base, a key US hub in the region; Jordan's military said all five were intercepted. Trump told reporters in the Oval Office the following morning that "we're going to be hitting them very hard because it's our turn to hit them. They know it's coming," and US forces struck Iranian targets that night. The retaliatory cycle has now run since the collapse of the June 17 memorandum of understanding that had briefly reopened the Strait of Hormuz — a truce that lasted barely three weeks before Iranian forces attacked three commercial vessels transiting the strait on July 6-7 and hostilities resumed.

A chokepoint under sustained fire

The renewed fighting has turned the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas normally passes, into an active shipping hazard rather than a background risk. At least a dozen tankers have been struck in and around the strait this month, killing at least two seafarers, according to maritime risk trackers, with nine vessels attacked since the July 6 breakdown alone. Traffic has thinned sharply: transits fell to eight ships on one recent day, down from 15 the day before, with many crews now switching off transponders to avoid being targeted. The chief executive of maritime risk firm Marisks described the period since the MOU's collapse as "the worst phase of this conflict for merchant shipping" — a judgment borne out by insurers, who have pushed war-risk premiums on Gulf transits sharply higher.

The toll and the trajectory

The human cost has climbed alongside the market reaction. The Iranian Red Crescent Society puts the war's death toll in Iran at roughly 787, while at least six American service members and ten people in Israel have been killed since fighting first erupted in February. Trump has said the current bombing campaign, now in its third week following the ceasefire's collapse, could continue for weeks more. That timeline matters for traders as much as for the region: the longer the campaign runs, the harder it becomes for shipping and insurance markets to treat Hormuz disruption as temporary.

What it means for pricing risk

Crude's move from below $84 during the brief ceasefire back above $90 illustrates how quickly the geopolitical premium can reprice when a chokepoint is directly contested rather than merely threatened. Unlike earlier scares in this conflict, the current phase combines two distinct risks — direct US-Iran military exchange and physical attacks on tankers — which compound rather than substitute for each other. For portfolios and algorithmic systems pricing energy and inflation-sensitive assets, that combination argues for treating the current premium as sticky rather than transient until there is evidence shipping volumes are recovering, not just that a ceasefire has been announced.

The next marker to watch is whether Washington and Tehran attempt to revive talks now that the MOU has failed once already, and whether Hormuz transit volumes show any recovery in the coming days — the more reliable signal, given how quickly the last truce broke down.
