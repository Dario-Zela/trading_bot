Burnham reshapes cabinet, scraps energy VAT and won't rule out tax rises as gilts swing

Andy Burnham, who became UK prime minister this month, reshaping the cabinet and opening economic policy with a VAT cut on electricity bills. — Wikimedia Commons / official portrait
In one sentence
New PM Andy Burnham reshuffled the cabinet, scrapped VAT on electricity bills and won't rule out tax rises, as gilt yields hit two-month highs.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for barely ten days, and already the bond market has rendered its verdict on the change of government. Britain's 30-year gilt yield climbed to 5.75% this week, its highest in two months, while the 10-year moved back above 5% — levels that leave the UK with the highest borrowing costs in the G7, ahead even of Italy. The proximate cause was a single phrase from the new prime minister about wanting "flexibility" within the fiscal rules he inherited. Traders, still carrying scar tissue from the 2022 Liz Truss crisis, did not wait for the fine print.

What has followed is a government finding its shape in public: a reshaped cabinet, a tax cut on energy bills funded by cancelling a flagship digital identity scheme, a pledge to renew the UK's nuclear deterrent, and an admission that taxes may have to rise again to fix social care. Burnham has framed all of it as a return to "stability." The gilt market, for now, disagrees.

A cabinet built in his own image

Burnham's reshuffle removed the clearest symbols of the government he replaced. Rachel Reeves, the chancellor who spent two years insisting the UK's fiscal rules were inviolable, is gone from government entirely. So are David Lammy and Peter Kyle, both senior figures under Keir Starmer. In their place, Burnham has installed John Healey — until this month the defence secretary — as chancellor, and Angela Rayner as housing secretary.

The personnel changes matter because of what they signal about direction, not just tone. Reeves had built her chancellorship around a self-imposed reputation for fiscal discipline — a deliberate contrast with the market turmoil of the Truss premiership. Her removal, paired with Burnham's own remarks about wanting room to manoeuvre within the rules rather than simply obeying them, is precisely the kind of signal gilt investors are paid to price. Burnham has publicly committed to keeping his predecessor's fiscal framework intact. Investors are behaving as though they expect it to bend.

Sterling told the same story in miniature: the pound slipped as much as 0.17% against the dollar to $1.3429 on Burnham's initial comments, before clawing back some ground once Healey's appointment was confirmed — a chancellor with a defence brief and a reputation as a Treasury-orthodoxy figure read by markets as a modest reassurance. One strategist, tracking the swings, put it plainly: the market is watching for anything that touches the fiscal rules, and reacting fast.

The first policy: cheaper power, an unfunded gap

Burnham's opening move as prime minister was a cost-of-living measure rather than a fiscal one on paper — scrapping VAT on domestic electricity bills, cutting the rate from 5% to zero for six months from 1 October 2026 to 31 March 2027. The Treasury says a typical household will save around £45 over the period, and Healey has argued the cut will help pull down headline inflation as well as easing bills directly.

The financing is where the policy gets uncomfortable. Downing Street says the roughly £850m cost will be met by cancelling the previous government's Digital ID programme — but that scheme had already burned through an estimated £600m, leaving a shortfall of around £250m that has not been explained. It is a small number by Treasury standards, but it is exactly the sort of loose thread that gilt investors, already nervous about the government's fiscal instincts, tend to pull on.

Trident, and the argument that spending is not the same as loosening

Against that backdrop, Burnham has also doubled down on one of the most expensive commitments in government — the Dreadnought-class submarine programme that will carry Britain's Trident nuclear deterrent into the 2030s. Visiting the BAE Systems yard at Barrow-in-Furness, he confirmed a further £8.4bn phase of contracts, which the government says will support 18,000 additional defence jobs by 2030 and 22,000 apprenticeships by 2035, on top of the roughly 47,000 jobs already tied to the programme.

The political logic is straightforward: defence spending plays well with NATO allies unsettled by any hint of a change in British commitment, and Barrow's shipyards are a tangible, photogenic story about industrial jobs. The fiscal logic is less comfortable. Trident renewal is a decades-long, multi-billion-pound commitment layered on top of a VAT cut with a funding gap and a chancellor who has floated more flexible borrowing rules. None of these are enormous sums individually against a roughly £1.1 trillion annual budget. Collectively, they are the kind of accumulation that has, in the recent history of British fiscal crises, mattered more than any single line item.

Social care, and the tax question nobody wants to answer yet

The most consequential admission of Burnham's first fortnight may be the one dressed as the least urgent. Asked directly whether taxes would need to rise to fund an overhaul of adult social care, he did not rule it out. "Some of what we would want to do in the fuller sense will require difficult decisions," he said, "but we will be honest with people about those, we will put them before the country at the right time, and we will hopefully proceed with people's consent." He has been sharper about the scale of the problem than the remedy, describing England's care system as "as unfair as American healthcare," in which, he said, "the vulnerable pay with everything and it can completely leave them with nothing" — a reference to the £23,250 savings threshold below which the state begins to help with costs.

Burnham has also signalled a sequencing choice with its own political weight: he wants palliative and social care fixed before Parliament revisits the assisted dying debate, arguing the country should not be choosing an end-of-life option under financial duress. It is a values-based case, but its immediate effect is to push a difficult and expensive reform — one with no rate, no threshold and no timeline yet attached — further into the future while flagging, unmistakably, that it will cost money the government does not currently have earmarked.

What ties it together

Each thread on its own is defensible. A VAT cut helps households through winter. Nuclear deterrence is a long-term strategic commitment, not a discretionary spend. Social care is genuinely broken and has resisted three governments' attempts to fix it. But gilt investors do not price policies one at a time — they price a government's revealed appetite for spending against its willingness to raise the money to pay for it, and a new prime minister who has removed his fiscal-hawk chancellor, opened his premiership with an underfunded tax cut, committed billions to submarines and left a major spending pledge dangling on "difficult decisions" has given them a fairly clear answer already. The yield curve is the market's running commentary on that answer, updated in real time. The next data points worth watching are the autumn budget, where Healey will have to reconcile the VAT shortfall and any social care costings, and whether 30-year gilts hold above 5.75% into the September auction calendar — a level that, if sustained, starts to constrain the room for manoeuvre Burnham has just spent his first fortnight in office claiming for himself.
