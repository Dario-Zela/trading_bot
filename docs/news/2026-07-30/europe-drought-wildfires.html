Drought grips half of England as wildfires rage from Crete to Suffolk

A reservoir bed exposed by drought conditions in England, illustrative of the low reservoir levels reported across the country this summer — Robert Wade / Geograph / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Drought now covers 51% of England after the driest July on record, as wildfires kill firefighters in Greece and force evacuations from Bordeaux to Suffolk.

Half of England woke up this week to an official word that has not applied to London and the Thames Valley in this way for years: drought. The Environment Agency confirmed on 29 July that seven regions — covering 51.4% of the country, from East Anglia and Hertfordshire down through Hampshire and the Isle of Wight, across Devon and Cornwall, the West Midlands and Wessex — have crossed into drought status. Behind the declaration is a simple, stark number. July's rainfall came in at just 7% of the long-term average across England, and in the south of the country at barely 1%. Parts of Devon have gone 50 days without meaningful rain; Hampshire, 49.

The Agency is calling it a "flash drought" — one that builds fast on the back of low rainfall stacked on top of unusual heat, rather than a slow multi-year decline. It is also the second consecutive summer that parts of England have tipped into drought, after 2025, a pattern that officials and farmers alike are starting to describe less as an anomaly and more as a baseline.

The reservoirs are the tell

Rainfall deficits are one thing; what turns them into a crisis is storage. England's reservoirs stood at 75.3% capacity as the drought was declared — 7.4 percentage points below where they should be for this point in summer — with nearly half of all reservoirs classified as below normal. Wimbleball and Blagdon are among the worst hit; Wimbleball's level had fallen to roughly a fifth of capacity by some recent readings, close to the record low it set in 2022.

"The hot and dry weather means we are currently using water faster than nature can replenish it," said Helen Wakeham, the Environment Agency's director of water. Seven water companies have now imposed temporary use bans — hosepipe restrictions, in plain terms — covering roughly 23 million customers, about 40% of England's population, with South East Water, Yorkshire Water and Southern Water among those that pulled the trigger first. Water firms have been told to prioritise leak repairs, a pointed instruction given how much of the shortfall in a well-managed system should never reach the customer's tap at all.

Fire follows drought across the map

The same dry, hot air mass sitting over Britain has been baking the rest of western Europe for weeks, and this week it turned lethal. Two Greek firefighters died on Wednesday battling a blaze near Krya Vrysi in the Rethymno region of Crete, trapped in their vehicle as they tried to move between fire fronts; a third died in a separate fire in the Peloponnese. It was Spain's fourth heatwave of the summer and, by some counts, the fourth widespread heatwave to hit the UK so far in 2026 — an unusually high cadence for what used to be occasional extreme-weather events.

In France, a wildfire that began on 22 July in the Gironde and Landes departments near Bordeaux has burned across an area roughly four times the size of Paris — part of some 115,000 hectares scorched nationwide this year — and forced tens of thousands of further evacuations as it flared again this week. "The situation remains complicated. That's clear. We're not out of the woods," the Gironde prefect, Sophie Brocas, said. President Macron convened a crisis cabinet meeting and has pledged to "rebuild a different kind of forest" once the fire is out, an acknowledgment that the pine monoculture that fuelled the blaze may not be replanted as it was.

England's own fire risk showed up on Wednesday at Dunwich Heath on the Suffolk coast, where a blaze on National Trust land was declared a major incident. Fourteen fire appliances and around 90 firefighters — reinforced by crews from Norfolk — worked through hot, gusty conditions to stop flames spreading toward St James' Church, using Unimogs, drones and quad bikes to reach terrain fire engines could not. Homes near the Coastguard Cottages were evacuated, along with a nearby caravan park, as smoke was reported as far away as Lowestoft.

Why traders should be watching the rain gauge

Drought translates into cost through two distinct channels, and both are already visible. The first is water infrastructure itself: utilities facing depleted reservoirs, higher pumping and treatment costs, and regulatory pressure over leakage will be building those costs into the price reviews Ofwat sets every five years — a slow-moving but directional cost pressure on customer bills and, in turn, on utility earnings assumptions. The second is agriculture. The National Farmers' Union says dairy and livestock farmers have already lost some 18.5 million litres of milk yield in recent weeks to heat stress, while arable growers report crop quality and yield damage serious enough that early harvesting has become routine rather than exceptional. UK feed wheat futures for November delivery were quoted at £188.50 a tonne, up £9 over two weeks, with tight global stocks — compounded by separate disruption to Black Sea grain exports — adding further support to a market already nervous about domestic supply.

"Farmers and growers have always had to contend with the weather, but what they are experiencing now is climate change in action," said NFU deputy president Paul Tompkins. "Water is a vital ingredient in food production, and without a secure supply, we cannot build resilient farm businesses or strengthen the country's food security."

None of these threads — a UK drought, a Greek firefighter death toll, a French wildfire the size of a small English county, a Suffolk heath ablaze — is unrelated to the others. They are the same weather system expressed through different infrastructure: reservoirs in one country, forests in another, a National Trust heath in a third. What connects them for anyone pricing risk is that each is a case study in an assumption climate models have made for years and financial markets have been slow to cost in: that water scarcity and fire risk in temperate Europe would stop being once-a-decade events and start showing up most summers. The next data points are close at hand — the Environment Agency's next national drought status update is due in late August, alongside water company statements on whether further use bans follow, and the first hard yield estimates from this year's wheat harvest, now underway early across much of southern England.
