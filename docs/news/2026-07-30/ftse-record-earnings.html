FTSE 100 hits record as StanChart, Rio Tinto, Reckitt, Greggs and Unilever all beat

The London Stock Exchange in Paternoster Square, where the FTSE 100 notched an intraday record as commodity and earnings strength converged. — London Stock Exchange / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The FTSE 100 hit a record on oil and mining strength as StanChart, Rio Tinto, Reckitt, Greggs and Unilever all beat earnings forecasts on the same day.

London's blue-chip index spent Wednesday doing something it rarely manages twice in one earnings season: rising on both the macro and the micro at once. The FTSE 100 touched an intraday record of 10,951.06 points before settling at 10,908.41, up 0.34% on the day and a whisker below February's closing high. The push came from two directions that don't usually pull together — a jump in oil prices and a clutch of unrelated corporate results that all landed the same way. Standard Chartered beat profit forecasts. Rio Tinto posted its best half-year earnings in four years. Reckitt raised its dividend. Greggs shares jumped as much as 18%. And Unilever, a company not known for surprising anyone, delivered its strongest quarterly growth in more than a decade.

Oil and metal do the heavy lifting

The index's record owed plenty to a commodities market rattled by geopolitics rather than economics. Brent crude settled 7.9% higher at $90.74 a barrel as fighting in the Middle East resumed and US forces reported intercepting an Iranian missile attack on personnel in Jordan — the kind of escalation that pushes traders to price in supply risk rather than demand destruction. Energy stocks on the index rose 2.9% in response.

Mining did its own share of the work, and here the story was less about geopolitics than about what companies are actually digging up. Rio Tinto reported first-half underlying earnings of $6.85 billion, up 42.5% from $4.81 billion a year earlier and its highest half-year profit in four years. The surprise was in the composition: copper Ebitda surged 84% to $5.7 billion, helped by ramping production at the Oyu Tolgoi mine in Mongolia, while iron ore — long the company's core earner — generated $6.8 billion, down 1%. Copper, aluminium and lithium now account for more than half of group Ebitda, a threshold Rio Tinto has never crossed before.

"We achieved a step-change in performance in the first half, which, alongside favourable commodity prices, delivered a 28 per cent increase in underlying EBITDA and a 75 per cent rise in free cash flow," chief executive Simon Trott said.

The company lifted its interim dividend to $2.11 a share from $1.48, a 43% increase in first-half shareholder distributions and its highest interim payout in four years.

The read-through matters beyond Rio Tinto's share register. A mining major making more money from electrification-linked metals than from steelmaking's raw material is a data point for the argument that AI infrastructure and grid build-out are creating real, priced demand — not merely a stock narrative.

Banking and consumer names beat in step

Standard Chartered's numbers were, in the words of one analyst note, a clean beat. Pre-tax profit for the second quarter came in at $2.33 billion against a consensus estimate of $2.08 billion, 13% ahead of expectations, while earnings per share of 77.4 cents beat forecasts by 17%. Wealth Solutions income rose 43% to $1.06 billion, comfortably outpacing the bank's own guidance. Standard Chartered lifted its interim dividend 66% to 20.4 cents a share and announced a fresh $1 billion share buyback, alongside guidance for operating income growth around the middle of its 5–7% target range for the year.

Reckitt, the maker of Dettol and Nurofen, rose around 7% after quarterly sales beat forecasts, raised its dividend by 5% and added a £500 million buyback of its own. Greggs went further still: shares surged as much as 18% after the bakery chain reported total sales up 7.2% to £1,101.5 million, like-for-like growth of 2.1%, and operating profit up 22.9% to £86.5 million — enough to hold its full-year guidance steady in what management has repeatedly called a tough consumer market.

Unilever's decade-best quarter

The most striking number of the day belonged to a company generally associated with slow, steady, unglamorous growth. Unilever reported second-quarter underlying sales growth of 5.8%, against a consensus estimate of 4.3%, driven by underlying volume growth of 5.5% — its strongest quarterly volume performance in more than ten years. Shares jumped as much as 6.5% to 4,925.5p, their highest level since March, and the company raised full-year guidance to within its 4–6% multi-year sales growth range, up from a previous forecast of growth at the bottom of that band.

The timing is notable. Unilever is mid-transition into a leaner household-and-personal-care business, having agreed in March to combine its food arm — Knorr, Hellmann's, Cholula — with McCormick in a $44.8 billion deal that leaves Unilever shareholders holding just over half of the combined entity. The group is also running its largest sports marketing push in years as official personal-care sponsor of the 2026 FIFA World Cup, activating more than 35 brands including Dove and Axe across over 120 markets. Neither initiative shows up directly in the Q2 numbers, but both are aimed at the same goal the results suggest is working: proving that a slimmed-down Unilever can grow faster than the conglomerate it is leaving behind.

What the breadth signals

What ties a copper miner, an emerging-markets bank, a bakery chain and a soap-and-mayonnaise conglomerate together is not a shared sector story — it's that all four beat, on the same day, for different reasons. A single strong earnings report is noise; four unrelated ones landing together, alongside a commodities market pricing in resilient demand rather than a slowdown, is closer to signal. For a FTSE 100 still heavily weighted to mining, energy and banking, that combination is precisely what a record close is supposed to represent.

The next test comes quickly. Brent's move was driven by an Iran-US flare-up that could just as easily reverse if de-escalation talk returns, and the Bank of England's rate decision this week keeps the domestic inflation question live. Whether Wednesday's record was a genuine turning point or a one-day confluence of good news will depend on whether the earnings beats keep coming when the results calendar thins out in August.
