Carmakers delay UK factory investment pending EV mandate review

BMW's Mini Plant Oxford, where a £600m electric vehicle investment has been paused amid uncertainty over UK EV sales quotas. — Lobster1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK carmakers are freezing factory investment until ministers say whether EV sales quotas will be relaxed, with BMW's £600m Mini Oxford upgrade already paused.

British carmakers are holding back final investment decisions on new UK models, according to the industry's chief lobbyist, until ministers decide whether to loosen the sales quotas that force a rising share of electric vehicles onto forecourts each year.

"They're waiting for the mandate, certainly," said Mike Hawes, chief executive of the Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT). "There's investment decisions on next model, next generation, which need a resolution, need an easing of the mandate." Those decisions, he noted, are for production runs stretching well past 2030 — meaning manufacturers are trying to plan a decade out against a policy the government itself is still rewriting.

The gap between quota and market

The zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) mandate requires 33% of new car sales this year to be fully electric, climbing to 38% in 2027 and 52% in 2028. Actual battery-electric market share is running at 23.9% of cars and 9.5% of vans — a shortfall manufacturers have so far closed by discounting. The SMMT puts the industry-wide cost of that discounting at more than £12bn, money it argues would otherwise have funded new models and factory upgrades. Miss the targets outright and manufacturers face fines, which the government has already trimmed once this year in a partial concession.

The clearest casualty so far is BMW's Mini plant at Cowley, Oxford, where a planned £600m upgrade to build electric Coopers and Acemans has been paused, the company citing "multiple uncertainties facing the automotive industry." BMW also turned down an associated £60m government grant. Toyota, which has built the Corolla at its Derbyshire plant since 2019, and Nissan, weighing a possible Chery partnership at Sunderland, are named by the SMMT among firms sitting on comparable calls. Jaguar Land Rover has raised similar concerns publicly.

UK vehicle output fell 7.5% in the first half of the year to 386,000 cars and commercial vehicles, though SMMT figures show the decline stabilised in the second quarter as exports to the EU picked up — a reminder that the investment freeze is a forward-looking signal, not yet a production collapse.

Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds, back in the role under Prime Minister Andy Burnham, has signalled the government is likely to ease the mandate, with a consultation already under way. No date for a decision has been set.

Why the delay matters more than the mandate

The substance of the dispute — how fast to force EV adoption — is a familiar industrial policy argument playing out across Europe. What is less ordinary is manufacturers openly citing regulatory uncertainty, rather than demand or cost, as the reason capital sits idle. That is a different kind of signal: not a sector in decline, but investors and manufacturers pricing in the chance that UK industrial policy can be renegotiated under pressure. Set against a government also weighing tax rises and gilt markets already jumpy over its spending plans, a stalled £600m factory upgrade reads less as an automotive story than as one more data point on how firms are pricing UK policy risk.

The consultation's conclusion — no fixed date yet, though officials have promised speed — is the next marker. Until it lands, factory investment across the sector stays parked.
