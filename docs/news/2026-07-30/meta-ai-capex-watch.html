Meta shares slide again as investors question AI infrastructure spending

Mark Zuckerberg, whose defence of Meta's AI spending has leaned on long-term conviction more than near-term numbers. — Jeff Sainlar / Meta, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Meta shares fell as much as 11% after it raised AI capex guidance to $130bn-$145bn even as free cash flow collapsed 91%, deepening investor doubt in Zuckerberg's AI bet.

Meta's answer to nine straight days of investor doubt was supposed to be Wednesday's earnings report. Instead, it made things worse.

Shares in the company fell as much as 11% in after-hours trading once the numbers landed — a reaction that, for a stock already down roughly a quarter from its high, confirmed rather than calmed the market's central worry: that Mark Zuckerberg's AI spending has stopped looking like investment and started looking like a drag.

The headline numbers were not bad on their own terms. Revenue for the April-to-June quarter grew 28% year-on-year to $61bn. But profit fell 14% to $6bn, and free cash flow — the cash left over after Meta pays for its own operations — collapsed 91% to $784m, from $8.5bn a year earlier. That is the lowest quarterly free cash flow the company has generated in at least five years, and it happened despite the revenue growth, not because of a lack of it. The gap between the two is capital expenditure: server farms, chips and data centres, almost all of it aimed at AI.

The bill keeps growing

Meta didn't just spend heavily — it told investors it plans to spend more. The company lifted its full-year 2026 capex guidance to a range of $130bn–$145bn, up from the $125bn figure it gave just three months ago. That is on top of a raise from the quarter before that. Each earnings call this year has produced the same shape of surprise: a bigger number than the one promised last time.

Mike Proulx, an analyst at Forrester, put the quarter's tension plainly: "What it generated in cash this quarter almost all got eaten by AI infrastructure spending. Investors now have to decide whether Meta's growing list of AI initiatives represents company diversification or distraction." He drew a pointed comparison to Meta's Reality Labs years — the multi-billion-dollar metaverse bet that has yet to turn a profit — describing the current AI build-out as spending "ahead of proven product demand" in much the same way.

Zuckerberg's pitch, and the product to prove it

Zuckerberg's defence leaned on faith more than figures. "I get that this is a big bet across the industry," he told analysts. "My personal bet is that the people who invest in this will feel very good." Chief financial officer Susan Li offered a firmer, if distant, timeline: "By 2028, we'll have turned over a lot of cards," she said, asking investors to wait roughly two more years before judging the payoff.

The evidence Zuckerberg pointed to in the meantime is Muse Spark 1.1, a model Meta released earlier this month under Alexandr Wang, the former Scale AI chief who now leads Meta's AI efforts. Wang has called it the "strongest model for agentic and coding work yet," and its pitch to developers is price: Meta is charging roughly a quarter of what OpenAI and Anthropic charge for comparable access, undercutting rivals by about 75%. It marks Meta's first real push to sell AI capability directly to other businesses, after years spent giving Llama models away for free. Zuckerberg said the near-term priority is making Muse Spark "easier for companies to integrate" — the first plank of a plan to turn the AI build-out into a revenue line of its own, rather than just a cost centre supporting Instagram and Facebook's ad engine.

The read-through

What makes Meta's slide notable is that it isn't an isolated data point. A week before its own report, Alphabet's shares fell more than 7% after it too raised capex guidance, and BMO Capital has already flagged Meta as having the "least visible return on investment story" among its AI-spending peers. Investors are no longer taking capex increases as a sign of confidence; they are starting to price them as a warning.

Susan Li's 2028 marker gives the market a rough date to hold the company to. Until then, the number to watch each quarter is not revenue growth, which remains healthy, but free cash flow — the figure that just fell 91% and the one that shows, more than any model release, whether the spending is actually converting into something shareholders get to keep.
