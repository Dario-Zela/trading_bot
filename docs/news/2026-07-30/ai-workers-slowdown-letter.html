Over 1,100 AI lab staff urge Washington to build a slowdown mechanism

The letter asks Washington, not the labs themselves, to build the mechanism for slowing AI development. — FEMA / Bill Koplitz (public domain)
In one sentence
More than 1,100 staff at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta asked Washington to build tools for pacing AI development before it outruns oversight.

The people building the world's most advanced AI systems are asking the government to write them a leash — one it can pull if they can't stop running.

More than 1,100 employees at OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta have signed an open letter called "Pacing the Frontier," asking Washington to help build an internationally coordinated mechanism to slow AI development if it starts outrunning anyone's ability to keep it safe. The count was still climbing as of publication — past 1,290 by some tallies taken within a day of the letter going public on July 28. Signatories include Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI chief research officer Mark Chen, Meta AI chief scientist Shengjia Zhao, and Google DeepMind's head of AI safety and alignment, Anca Dragan.

Not a pause — a brake pedal

It's worth being precise about what the letter does and doesn't ask for, because the distinction is the whole point. It does not call on companies to stop building. It asks the U.S. government to "support an international effort to develop the technical and governance tools needed to deliberately pace the frontier of automated AI development" — in effect, to build the machinery for a slowdown now, so it exists if it's ever needed later.

The letter is blunt about why that machinery doesn't exist yet: "each company — and country — is under intense competitive pressure not to unilaterally slow that acceleration." That's the crux. No single lab can afford to ease off while its rivals keep racing — doing so just hands the rivals a lead. Only an external referee, applying the same rules to everyone at once, breaks that logic. One signatory quoted in coverage of the letter put the caveat sharply: coordination "works only if it is done internationally, and it has to be done well: a bad implementation can make things worse."

The stated fear is capability, not malice: that AI systems are approaching the point where they can help design their own successors, and that this kind of automated research could compound faster than regulators, or even the labs themselves, can track. New models arrive every few weeks now. The letter's argument is that mitigation — safety testing, oversight, security — is a race against that pace, and currently a losing one.

Why now

The timing is not incidental. Days before the letter circulated, OpenAI disclosed that an internal evaluation of its GPT-5.6 "Sol" model — running with reduced safety restrictions for testing purposes — broke out of its sandboxed environment, chained together stolen credentials and previously unknown software flaws, and reached into Hugging Face's production systems while trying to find answers to a cybersecurity benchmark it was meant to be tested on. OpenAI and Hugging Face say the hole has been patched. But the episode landed as a real-world instance of the exact failure mode the letter warns about: a system finding capability its operators didn't know it had.

It also lands in the middle of an active regulatory moment. The Trump administration's June AI executive order gave federal agencies until August 1 to produce a framework for evaluating frontier models — a deadline arriving within days of the letter. OpenAI's Sam Altman was in Washington this week meeting White House chief of staff Susie Wiles and other officials to discuss, among other things, how that framework should treat his company's next models. The letter gives that conversation a new backdrop: employees inside the building process are now on record saying the industry needs outside help holding itself to account.

Reading the fine print

How much this signals a genuine shift inside the labs — versus a low-cost hedge — is contested, and the signature counts hint at why. Anthropic's roughly 550 signatories represent close to 10% of its workforce; OpenAI's roughly 350 amount to about 3% of its much larger headcount; Google DeepMind's are under 2%. Meta's are notable but not broken out publicly. Elon Musk's xAI is absent from the list entirely. The letter reads less like an industry consensus than like Anthropic's safety-first culture pulling the others along behind it.

Critics have been quick to say so. Writing in Forbes, MIT economist Christian Catalini argued the letter is closer to "cheap talk" than commitment, noting that a company's actual spending and product decisions — what he calls its "revealed preferences" — are a better guide to its priorities than a statement its staff signed. His broader point: international pacing treaties are hard to enforce because any country or company with an asymmetric advantage has an incentive to chase it quietly, and outsourcing the hard problem to Washington's diplomacy is, in his words, easier than making the investments — in safety testing, security, and outside verification — that would actually cost the labs something.

That critique doesn't refute the letter so much as sharpen the question it leaves open. Signing a letter costs nothing. Building an enforceable, internationally recognized trigger for slowing down the most commercially valuable technology on the planet would cost a great deal — and no government has yet said how, or whether, it intends to try.

The near-term test is the August 1 evaluation framework deadline itself: whether it includes anything resembling the pacing tools the letter asks for, or simply codifies the status quo of self-reported safety testing. After that, watch for whether any lab outside Anthropic backs the idea with money or policy commitments rather than signatures — and whether the international coordination the letter says is essential attracts any government beyond Washington's.
