Burnham government sets 28 October Budget date, unveils mayoral tax-devolution plan

HM Treasury, 1 Horse Guards Road — where Chancellor Healey will set out the government's first Budget on 28 October. — Wutthichai Charoenburi / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Healey set a 28 October Budget and pledged to hold Reeves' fiscal rules, even as Burnham's mayoral income-tax plan adds a new claim on the same tight headroom.

Chancellor John Healey confirmed on Thursday that he will deliver his first Budget on 28 October, and paired the date with a pledge that will matter more to bond traders than the date itself: the government intends to keep the fiscal rules it inherited from Rachel Reeves, even as bills for defence and social care pile up on the Treasury's desk. In the same week, Prime Minister Andy Burnham put flesh on his devolution agenda, confirming that regional mayors will get a cut of the income tax raised in their own patches — with the fine print, inconveniently for anyone hoping to model it now, pushed back to the same October date.

Taken together, the two announcements tell a single story about the Burnham government's first real test: whether it can fund an ambitious redistribution of power to the regions without giving markets a reason to doubt its discipline on the public finances.

In one sentence: Healey set a 28 October Budget and vowed to hold Reeves' fiscal rules, even as Burnham's mayoral tax-sharing plan adds a new, uncosted claim on the same tight headroom.

A date, and a promise

Healey's line was carefully chosen: the Budget, he said, "will be built on fiscal discipline. It will meet our fiscal rules." Treasury officials briefed reporters that the government intends to stay "well within" the two targets Reeves set before Burnham took over from Keir Starmer last month — balancing day-to-day spending against tax revenue by the end of the decade, and getting debt falling as a share of GDP.

The trouble is the room to manoeuvre inside those rules. Reeves left her successor a £22 billion buffer at her last fiscal event, but persistent inflation tied to the Iran conflict has been eating into it, and gilt markets have already registered their unease: the 10-year yield pushed back above 5% on Healey's appointment in July, and the 30-year hit roughly 5.75%, a two-month high. Sterling slipped as investors weighed whether a chancellor drawn from Labour's centre-left, seen by desks such as MUFG as a moderating influence, could actually hold the line against demands from his own cabinet for more defence spending and a fully funded social care settlement. Shadow Chancellor Mel Stride took the opposite tack, arguing the delay to October leaves the country facing "89 more days of unfunded spending commitments and damaging tax speculation."

The devolution gambit

The policy Burnham chose to headline that same fiscal backdrop is not a spending line so much as a plumbing change: mayors in Greater Manchester, Liverpool City Region, South Yorkshire, Cambridgeshire and Peterborough, and the newer mayoralties covering Greater Essex, Hampshire and the Solent, and Greater Lincolnshire will keep a slice of the income tax and business rates generated in their areas, rather than seeing it all flow to the Treasury and back out through grants.

"More of the taxes raised in a community will stay in that community," Burnham said, framing it as the "biggest transfer of power" of his premiership — notable coming from a former Greater Manchester mayor who spent years lobbying Westminster for exactly this. Healey, standing alongside him, put it more plainly: "For the first time we're giving mayors a share of income tax so communities directly benefit when their economy grows."

The mechanics, though, are still a placeholder. The specific share of revenue each mayor keeps won't appear until the October Budget white paper; an equalisation mechanism is promised to stop poorer regions losing out, and Downing Street has stressed that "the rate of income tax which billpayers face will not change as a direct result of the reforms." Actual cash won't move until spring 2027. The IPPR called the plan "extraordinarily bold"; Conservatives dismissed it as "top-down Manchesterism" of uncertain value outside major cities.

What the fiscal rules have to absorb

The two stories collide at the Treasury's spreadsheet. Devolving revenue is not, in itself, new spending — Downing Street's insistence that headline tax rates won't move is the tell — but the equalisation payments, the administrative cost of running a dozen new local tax regimes, and the political cost of any mayor complaining about their share all land on the same balance sheet as the defence increase and the social care settlement Burnham has also promised. A government with a shrinking buffer and rising borrowing costs has just added a structurally significant, multi-year commitment to a Budget it swears will still meet the old rules.

That is the bet markets are now pricing: that fiscal orthodoxy holds and the devolution numbers, when they land in October, are calibrated to fit inside it rather than force a rule change or a tax rise to accommodate them. Gilt investors will get an interim read at the August auction; the fuller picture — how much revenue mayors actually get, and how the OBR scores the defence and care commitments against the £22 billion buffer — arrives with the Budget itself on 28 October.
