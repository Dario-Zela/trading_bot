Trump signs four-year tariff-rate quota on imported quartz surfaces

Engineered quartz has become the most common material for U.S. kitchen countertops, ahead of granite and laminate. — Stilfehler / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Trump's four-year tariff-rate quota on imported quartz surfaces takes effect 15 August, pushing the tariff fight into kitchen countertops.

President Donald Trump signed a proclamation on 31 July establishing a four-year tariff-rate quota on imported quartz surface products, the engineered stone that has become the default material for American kitchen countertops. The measure takes effect at 12:01 a.m. on 15 August, and it marks the tariff fight's clearest move yet from factory inputs into the stuff people actually renovate their homes with.

The action follows a Section 201 safeguard investigation by the U.S. International Trade Commission, triggered by a petition from the Quartz Manufacturing Alliance of America. The ITC found that a surge in imported quartz slabs was a substantial cause of serious injury to domestic producers — the same statutory trigger Trump used for the 2018 washing-machine and solar-panel tariffs. Under the quota, the in-quota duty starts at 25% and the above-quota rate at 40% in year one, both declining by a percentage point annually through year four. Permitted quota volume rises from 140 million square feet in year one to 169 million by year four, allocated on a quarterly basis. Canada, Mexico, Australia, South Korea, Israel, Singapore, the CAFTA-DR bloc and a handful of smaller developing-country suppliers are carved out.

Who actually pays

Quartz accounts for roughly 36% of U.S. countertop installations, ahead of granite and laminate, which makes this a safeguard measure with a direct line to household renovation budgets. An analysis circulated by the Competitive Enterprise Institute ahead of the ruling estimated the duty could roughly double the material cost embedded in a typical quartz countertop job — from around $504 to $1,036 — and put the aggregate hit across roughly 540,000 annual kitchen projects at $272 million to $559 million. The same analysis put prospective job losses in fabrication and installation at over 6,400, against an estimated 500 gained in domestic quartz manufacturing — a ratio its authors framed as thirteen to one.

The quota is also, in one sense, a mop-up operation. Chinese quartz slabs have carried duties above 200% since 2018, and Commerce imposed anti-dumping and countervailing duties on India and Turkey in 2019 after those countries absorbed the diverted trade. India's exports to the U.S. have since fallen sharply; Brazil, largely on natural quartzite rather than engineered quartz, has picked up share. This proclamation extends coverage to the rest of the field — a broader net than a country-specific dumping case, applied to nearly the entire remaining import base at once.

The next marker is 15 August, when the new rates apply to goods entered for consumption. Fabricators and importers will be watching first-quarter quota allocations for signs of how tightly the volume caps bind; retailers and homebuilders will be watching whether the cost shows up in listed remodel prices before the November data catches it.
