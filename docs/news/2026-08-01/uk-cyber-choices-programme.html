UK police cyber-prevention scheme diverts at-risk teen hackers from crime

Woolwich Crown Court, where two teenagers were jailed for five and a half years over the 2024 hack on Transport for London. — N Chadwick / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The NCA is scaling up free 'Cyber Choices' courses to divert at-risk teens from hacking after TfL and M&S attacks put teenagers in court.

The pitch is simple: if a bored, technically gifted teenager is going to spend hours online building skills, better that he ends up in a cybersecurity apprenticeship than in Woolwich Crown Court. That is the logic behind Cyber Choices, the National Crime Agency's prevention programme, which police forces across England are now pushing harder — with free school sessions, teacher toolkits and parent guides aimed squarely at boys and young men judged at risk of drifting into hacking.

The timing is not subtle. In June, two teenagers — Owen Flowers, 18, and Thalha Jubair, 19 — were each jailed for five and a half years over a 2024 attack on Transport for London that cost £29 million to clean up and knocked out more than 140 internal systems. Prosecutors said the pair, who claimed affiliation with the hacking collective Scattered Spider, threatened to lock TfL out of its own network — a system that carries an average of nine million journeys a day. It was the first successful prosecution under Section 3ZA of the Computer Misuse Act, the offence covering attacks that risk serious damage to human welfare.

Scattered Spider's fingerprints turned up again in the hack that hit Marks & Spencer this spring, an attack the retailer has said cost it roughly £300 million in lost sales and remediation. Four suspects were arrested in connection with that case and related attacks on Co-op and Harrods; three were teenagers, including a 17-year-old from the West Midlands. Investigators increasingly describe both incidents as products of the same loose criminal network — sometimes called "the Com" — that recruits technically able teenagers through gaming and Discord servers, first with small dares, then with cash.

From punishment to prevention

Cyber Choices itself is not new — the NCA has run some version of it since 2013 — but the emphasis is shifting. Referral data covering 2017 to 2025 shows more than a thousand young people have been flagged to the programme's Prevent teams, with a median age of 15 and the youngest just nine. A survey cited by the NCA found one in five children aged 10 to 16 admit to activity that would breach the Computer Misuse Act, rising to one in four among those who game heavily.

Devon and Cornwall Police is among the forces now running hour-long "Cyber Prevent" sessions in schools, aimed at students who show a strong interest in computing but might be tempted toward stresser and booster sites — cheap tools that let anyone launch a denial-of-service attack. Rather than simply blocking searches for such terms, participating schools redirect pupils to Cyber Choices material explaining what is legal, what is not, and where the same skills lead in a paid career: penetration testing, esports, software engineering.

There is early evidence the approach works better than jail time alone. An NCA evaluation published in February found that young people who went through Cyber Prevent reoffended at 0.58%, against a benchmark youth cyber-offending rate of 1.14% — roughly half. That is a small, self-selected cohort, not a controlled trial, but it is the strongest data the agency has offered yet for diversion over prosecution.

What it doesn't fix

The harder problem is what happens once a recruiter reaches a teenager first. Scattered Spider and its associated networks operate largely on gaming platforms and encrypted chat apps, well outside the reach of a school assembly, and the financial rewards — six-figure ransom cuts, sometimes paid in crypto — are hard for a careers pamphlet to compete with. The NCA's own strategic assessment this year named the wider network a priority target, alongside the prevention push, suggesting the agency does not expect education alone to close the pipeline.

The next test comes as more Scattered Spider-linked cases reach sentencing, and as the NCA publishes further reoffending data from Cyber Prevent's expanding cohort — the clearest signal yet of whether diversion is denting recruitment or merely running alongside it.
