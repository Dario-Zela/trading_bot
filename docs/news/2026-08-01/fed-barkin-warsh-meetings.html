Fed's Barkin calls rate stance a 'close call' as Warsh weighs fewer meetings

Fed chair Kevin Warsh, whose ambiguous guidance after last week's rate hold rattled bond markets and who is now weighing fewer FOMC meetings. — Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Barkin says it's a 'close call' whether rates are high enough as three FOMC members dissent for a hike and Warsh weighs fewer Fed meetings.

The Federal Reserve held interest rates steady last week for the fifth meeting running, at 3.50%-3.75%. But the vote that produced that outcome — 9 to 3 — was the most openly divided the committee has been in nearly a decade, and the days since have shown just how far apart its members sit.

On one side: three regional presidents, Lorie Logan of Dallas, Beth Hammack of Cleveland and Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis, who dissented in favour of a 25 basis-point hike. It was the first time since September 2016 that three FOMC members broke ranks with a unified hawkish view, and their argument is straightforward — inflation has run above the Fed's 2% target for five straight years, and the current funds rate sits roughly 100 basis points below what a standard Taylor Rule calculation would prescribe.

On the other: Richmond Fed president Tom Barkin, who told the Wall Street Journal it is "a close call whether interest rates are high enough" and that he is "skeptical that the labor market has strengthened meaningfully." Asked directly about employment conditions, Barkin said plainly: "It doesn't feel like a tight labor market." He added that he isn't sure he would have joined the three dissenters, and that "price increases are moving through the economy unevenly" — a hedge, but a telling one from a policymaker who has spent the past year worrying more about jobs than prices.

That split matters because it is playing out just as markets have soured on the man supposed to referee it. Fed chair Kevin Warsh's press conference after Wednesday's decision left investors with no clear signal on the Fed's reaction function, and the reaction was ugly: the S&P 500 fell 1.52%, the 30-year Treasury yield jumped to 5.23% — a 19-year high — and Bank of America's Mark Cabana called it "a classic central-bank credibility shock." PCE inflation currently stands at 4.1%. CME FedWatch pricing now puts roughly 65% odds on a hike at the September meeting, a startling reversal for a central bank that has held rates in place all year.

Fewer meetings, same argument

Against that backdrop, Bloomberg and the New York Times report that Warsh raised a structural idea at this week's gathering: cutting the FOMC's schedule below its current eight meetings a year. It's a deliberation, not a proposal on paper — Warsh has not stated a preferred number, and he told senators at his April confirmation hearing only that "four is not enough," while declining to say how many would be. He has also floated fewer press conferences and set up five task forces to review the Fed's communications, data practices and balance sheet.

The timing is awkward. A committee this openly split, led by a chair whose own guidance just triggered a bond-market selloff, is an unusual moment to propose giving policymakers fewer chances to speak. Fewer meetings would mean fewer opportunities to resolve — or paper over — disagreements like Barkin's with the dissenters, and less frequent recalibration if incoming data moves quickly in either direction.

The next scheduled test is September, where futures markets are already leaning toward a hike Barkin isn't sure he'd support. Whether Warsh uses that meeting to clarify the Fed's reaction function, or whether the frequency question overshadows it, will say much about which faction is actually setting the tone.
