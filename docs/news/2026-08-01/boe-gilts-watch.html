BoE hold stands, but gilt yields push through 5% into August auction

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
BoE held at 3.75% but hawkish dissent grew to three of nine, as gilt yields above 5% and an oil-driven inflation risk collide ahead of a 4 August DMO auction.

The Bank of England left Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% on 30 July, the fifth hold of the year — but the vote behind that stillness is moving. Three members of the Monetary Policy Committee, external member Catherine Mann, chief economist Huw Pill and external member Megan Greene, voted for an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%, one more dissenter than June's split and the largest hawkish bloc since the tightening cycle turned. The headline number didn't move. The committee did.

In one sentence: The BoE held at 3.75% but the hawkish dissent grew to three, and gilt yields already above 5% suggest markets are pricing the direction the vote is drifting.

A hold with a widening crack

Governor Andrew Bailey framed the decision as a case for patience: UK CPI inflation had fallen to 2.6% in the month before the meeting, undershooting the Bank's own forecast, which he called "encouraging." But patience was not unanimous, and the minutes made plain why. Oil prices have averaged almost 50% above their pre-conflict level in the five months since fighting resumed in the Middle East, and European gas stocks are running below seasonal norms alongside falling global refining output. The Bank's own language put it plainly: risks to energy prices "lie to the upside."

That is the crux of the three dissents. Monetary policy, as Bailey noted, "cannot influence energy prices" — but it can shape how the economy absorbs the shock, and Pill, Mann and Greene judged that absorption is running hot enough to warrant tightening now rather than waiting for confirmation in the data. Bailey pushed back against reading too much into the split, telling reporters there was "no evidence of second-round effects" feeding from energy costs into wages and core prices, and that he could not "draw too much comfort" from the inflation undershoot alone. The next scheduled decision falls on 17 September; there is no Monetary Policy Report attached to that meeting, so the committee will be voting on a partial picture, with the next full forecast round not landing until 5 November.

The gilt market isn't waiting

While the MPC parses second-round effects, the bond market has already made its own adjustment. Ten-year gilt yields rose 28 basis points across July and closed the month above 5%, a level last breached amid the same Middle East-driven inflation anxiety. Thirty-year yields went further, touching 5.73% — a fresh high in a cycle that has repeatedly tested investors' appetite for long UK duration. The move tracks the same energy-price logic underpinning the hawkish dissents: if oil-driven inflation proves sticky, the path of Bank Rate implied by the futures curve looks too low, and long-dated gilts are repricing to match.

That repricing puts the UK Debt Management Office's 4 August auction under closer scrutiny than usual. Details of the specific gilt on offer were due for release on 1 August, but the timing is pointed regardless of size: the DMO will be testing demand for UK government debt within days of a rate decision at which a third of the MPC voted to hike, and at yields already sitting at levels last seen when the Iran conflict first jolted markets. A soft auction — a wide tail, weak bid-to-cover — would read as confirmation that investors want more compensation for holding gilts through an inflation shock the Bank itself cannot control at source.

What ties it together

The through-line is that the bond market and the hawkish minority on the MPC are reading the same signal, just via different instruments. Both are pricing an energy-driven inflation risk that sits outside the usual levers of UK monetary policy, and both are arriving at the same conclusion: the balance of risk is shifting toward higher-for-longer, not lower. A 6–3 vote is still a comfortable majority for the doves, but the trajectory — from a narrower split in June to three dissents in July — is the kind of pattern that tends to compress before it breaks. The September 17 decision, made without a fresh Monetary Policy Report to lean on, will show whether Bailey's camp can hold the line or whether the hawks pick up a fourth vote. Between now and then, the 4 August auction offers the more immediate read: whether the market that has already pushed yields through 5% is willing to keep buying at the price it just set.
