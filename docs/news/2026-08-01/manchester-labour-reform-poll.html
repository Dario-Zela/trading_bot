Labour's Manchester landslide coincides with Reform losing its national poll lead

Bev Craig, elected Mayor of Greater Manchester with 66.3% of the vote in a record-low turnout contest. — Manchester City Council / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bev Craig won Manchester's mayoralty 66.3% to 33.7% on record-low 25% turnout, days before polls showed Reform UK losing its 16-month national lead to Labour.

Bev Craig stood before a half-empty count hall in Manchester on Thursday night and won by a margin that would, in most democracies, be read as a mandate. She took 66.3% of the vote in the runoff against Reform UK's Sian Astley, who managed 33.7% — a two-to-one drubbing in a contest for one of Britain's most closely watched regional offices. Only one in four eligible voters bothered to cast a ballot.

Both facts are true at once, and neither cancels the other out. That tension is the story: a Labour machine that still knows how to win a fight it chooses to show up for, in a political environment where fewer and fewer voters are showing up at all.

A landslide with an asterisk

Craig, formerly leader of Manchester City Council, was chasing a job made vacant in the strangest way possible: her predecessor, Andy Burnham, left the mayoralty not for retirement or scandal but for 10 Downing Street. Burnham became prime minister on 20 July after Keir Starmer's resignation, triggering the first Greater Manchester mayoral by-election since the post was created in 2017 — and the first time the race has ever gone to a second round.

Under the region's supplementary vote system, Craig led the first round with 47.15% against Astley's 20.99%, short of the outright majority needed to win there. When second preferences from five eliminated candidates were redistributed, Craig's total swelled to 309,525 votes against Astley's 157,178. It took counters fifteen hours to get through it.

What didn't grow was the electorate's appetite for the exercise. Turnout came in at 25%, the lowest since direct elections for the post began — worse than the 34.7% Burnham drew in his last full contest in 2024, and a long way from the sort of participation that lets a winner claim to speak for a city region of three million people. Craig, to her credit, did not pretend otherwise. "For those of you that didn't vote for me, I will still be your mayor," she told supporters. "I'm honoured, I'm humbled, and I stand ready to serve the three million people that live across this fantastic city region."

The Burnham bounce

The Manchester result did not stay a local story for long. Four days later, two separate polling firms reported that Reform UK had lost its national lead for the first time since March 2025 — a run of dominance that had lasted sixteen months and outlasted a change of Labour leadership.

More in Common's weekly tracker, fielded 24–27 July among 2,061 respondents, put Labour on 28%, up four points on the previous week, with Reform down two points to 24%. The Conservatives held steady on 22%. DeltaPoll, working separately, put Labour and Reform level at 28% apiece. Both pollsters pointed to the same cause: Burnham's arrival in Downing Street, and a first week in office stacked with retail policy — a £2 bus fare cap and a cut to VAT on energy bills among them. "After a jam-packed week of high-profile policy announcements and media appearances, the Labour Party has surged in our weekly poll," said Luke Tryl, UK director at More in Common.

The scale of the swing is worth sitting with. A month earlier, Labour trailed Reform by eight points nationally. Reform's slide comes as Nigel Farage faces his own distractions — his personal finances are under parliamentary scrutiny ahead of an August by-election in Clacton, the seat that first sent him to Westminster.

Two readings of the same result

It is tempting to fold the Manchester result and the poll swing into a single tidy narrative — Labour resurgent, Reform in retreat — and Downing Street will be tempted to sell it that way. The two data points do reinforce each other: a mayoral landslide in England's second city gave Burnham's first fortnight in office a visible, countable win, and pollsters explicitly credit that momentum, not any single policy, for the national shift.

But a 66-34 win on 25% turnout is not the same instrument as a national poll, and it measures something different. Reform's national polling slide reflects genuine attrition — voters moving from one column to another. Manchester's turnout collapse reflects something closer to disengagement: Labour's core vote held up (or simply outlasted a Reform base that has yet to build the same ground organisation in a Labour-stronghold city), while a large share of the electorate simply stayed home. A council estate turnout crisis is not disproven by a strong topline percentage; it is masked by one.

What comes next

The two threads reconverge in October. Burnham's government has already set a Budget date of 28 October, alongside a proposed devolution of tax powers to mayors — a policy Craig will now administer rather than merely campaign on. Farage's Clacton by-election in August offers the next real test of whether Reform's national slide is a blip tied to one bad news cycle or the start of a longer erosion. Neither event will resolve the turnout question hanging over Craig's own mandate, which only a fuller local contest — the next scheduled Greater Manchester election — will actually test.
