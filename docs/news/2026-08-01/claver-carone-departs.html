Trump's unofficial Venezuela adviser Claver-Carone departs role

Mauricio Claver-Carone, who wielded outsized informal influence over U.S. Venezuela policy before his departure from the role. — Keegan Barber / White House (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Mauricio Claver-Carone, the unofficial power broker behind Trump's post-Maduro Venezuela policy, has left the role amid conflict-of-interest questions.

For seven months, one man effectively ran Washington's Venezuela policy without holding a single government title. That arrangement ended this week. Mauricio Claver-Carone, the Miami businessman who emerged as the Trump administration's de facto point person on Venezuela following the January raid that captured Nicolás Maduro, has stepped back from the role, Reuters reported, leaving unresolved who — if anyone — inherits the sprawling portfolio he built.

Claver-Carone insists he went voluntarily. "I'm retired on Venezuela," he told Reuters, framing his exit as a return to private life after months of informal service, much as he says he did during Trump's first term. But multiple people familiar with the matter told the news agency he was pushed out over the past several weeks, and the State Department's insistence that he never held a formal role — technically true, and part of what made his influence so hard to check — reads as much as a distancing as a defense.

From maximum pressure to viceroy

Claver-Carone's history with Venezuela goes back years. As a National Security Council official in Trump's first term, he was credited with building the original "maximum pressure" sanctions campaign against Maduro. He later ran the Inter-American Development Bank until governors voted him out in 2022 over an ethics probe into an alleged workplace relationship, which he disputed. When Trump returned to office, Claver-Carone briefly held the title of special envoy for Latin America before moving into private equity — yet kept reporting informally to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, a longtime ally, on Venezuela matters.

After U.S. forces captured Maduro in the January 3 raid that installed Vice President Delcy Rodríguez as interim president, that informal channel became something closer to a viceroyship. Claver-Carone shaped decisions worth potentially billions of dollars: debt restructuring for Venezuela's roughly $200 billion in obligations, and approvals for oil companies seeking to re-enter the country's fields, home to the world's largest proven reserves. One deal drew particular scrutiny — a more than $150 million restructuring contract awarded to Centerview Partners without competitive bidding.

Friction inside the tent

The arrangement drew critics even among Trump loyalists. Former ambassador Richard Grenell raised concerns directly with Trump in early July about Claver-Carone's commercial reach, and questions circulated about his use of Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt — who has faced corruption and money-laundering scrutiny — as a go-between with Rodríguez's government. Others inside the administration questioned the optics of a private citizen running a private-equity fund steering which American firms got access to Venezuelan business, though Claver-Carone has denied holding a financial stake in the country himself. His hands-on style also "ruffled feathers" at the State Department, according to Reuters' sourcing.

Rubio's team says an inter-agency group, backed by a reconstituted U.S. embassy presence in Caracas, will now run the policy directly, and officials handling other parts of the Venezuela file show no signs of following Claver-Carone out. Whether that means a more conventional, institutional approach to sanctions relief and oil licensing — or simply a reshuffling of who holds informal sway — is the question markets and oil majors weighing Venezuelan re-entry will be watching for in the coming weeks.
