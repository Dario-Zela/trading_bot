BP launches formal sale process for North Sea oil and gas business

Meg O'Neill, who became BP's chief executive in April 2026, is driving the company's push to shed non-core assets and cut debt. — Ian Regnard / BP, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BP has launched a formal sale of its UK North Sea business — five hubs, ~117,000 boe/d — expected to fetch around £2bn as CEO Meg O'Neill cuts debt and narrows the portfolio.

BP has put its UK North Sea oil and gas business up for sale, a formal step that confirms months of speculation about the company's appetite for one of its oldest production regions. The unit up for grabs comprises five hubs — two in the central North Sea, three west of Shetland, including Clair, the largest oilfield on the UK continental shelf — producing around 117,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day. That is roughly 5% of BP's 2.3 million-barrel global output, extracted from a basin the company has worked for more than six decades.

The process, launched on 31 July, is the clearest marker yet of the direction new chief executive Meg O'Neill intends to take BP: smaller, more concentrated, and considerably less indebted.

What's on the block

BP has not disclosed a valuation or a timetable, but people close to the process, and analysts at Rystad Energy, have put the business at somewhere near £2 billion — a figure consistent with earlier, unsuccessful talks. The Financial Times reported in the spring that BP had held advanced discussions with Ithaca Energy over a near-identical package at a similar price; those talks broke down without a deal. That history suggests £2 billion is less a target than a starting point, and that BP may need to accept a discount, split the portfolio between several buyers, or wait longer than it would like.

The unit employs about 1,100 people. In a statement, O'Neill was careful to separate the commercial logic from any judgment on the North Sea's importance to Britain: "The North Sea remains integral to the UK's energy system. However, as we focus our portfolio and direct capital to our highest-value opportunities, we believe our North Sea business will be better positioned as part of another company." It is the standard language of divestment — not unwanted, just no longer strategic — but it lands differently for a business BP has operated since the 1960s and that still anchors Clair, a field holding an estimated seven billion barrels in place.

O'Neill's arithmetic

O'Neill, formerly of Woodside Energy and a 23-year Exxon veteran, took over as CEO in April and has moved quickly. BP has since collapsed its three-way divisional structure into two — upstream and downstream — and is working toward $20 billion in disposals by 2027, a bracket that already includes the planned sale of Castrol, the lubricants business. Share buybacks were suspended in February. Net debt stood at $22 billion in the most recent quarter, down from $26 billion a year earlier, against a company target of $14–18 billion by the end of 2027. A £2 billion North Sea sale would not close that gap on its own, but it moves the needle and, more importantly, signals to investors that non-core assets are genuinely for sale rather than merely under review.

The basin itself gives O'Neill additional reason to sell rather than hold. The Energy Profits Levy, extended and raised in November 2024, has pushed the effective tax rate on North Sea production to 78% — among the highest headline rates applied to oil and gas anywhere. Offshore Energies UK, the industry body, estimates the levy will deter roughly £50 billion of investment if left in place. Combined with a moratorium on new exploration licences under the previous government, the basin has become a place for extracting cash from mature fields rather than building new ones — exactly the kind of asset a company chasing debt reduction would rather convert to a lump sum.

Not the only one leaving

BP is not acting alone. Shell, Equinor and ExxonMobil have each trimmed North Sea exposure in recent years, part of a broader consolidation that has left the basin increasingly the preserve of specialist independents such as Ithaca and Harbour Energy, which has cut more than 600 jobs since 2023 as margins compress. The political backdrop has shifted too: Manchester mayor Andy Burnham's government has reopened consultations on the Rosebank and Jackdaw developments, an olive branch to an industry that spent much of the past two years warning it was being taxed and regulated out of the UK shelf. Whether that shift arrives in time, or with enough force, to change the economics for BP's exiting hubs is an open question — reopening a consultation is not the same as cutting a tax rate.

For BP, the sale is less a verdict on the North Sea's prospects than a statement about its own. O'Neill inherited a company that had spent under her predecessor's climate strategy diluting its oil and gas identity without fully committing to a low-carbon one; her answer so far has been to shrink the portfolio toward the assets that generate the most cash per barrel of capital employed, and sell the rest. The North Sea, taxed at 78% and requiring years of further investment to sustain 117,000 boe/d, does not clear that bar.

The immediate catalyst to watch is who actually bids. Ithaca's failure to close a deal in the spring leaves the field open to Harbour Energy, private-equity-backed independents, or a break-up of the five hubs among several buyers — any of which would test whether £2 billion was ever realistic. BP has given no timetable, which itself says something: a company confident of a quick, full-price sale usually says so.
