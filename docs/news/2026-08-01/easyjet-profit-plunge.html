EasyJet profit plunges 70% on Middle East-driven fuel costs, shares hold up

An easyJet Airbus A320, the workhorse of a fleet increasingly exposed to unhedged fuel costs. — Ad Meskens / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
EasyJet's pre-tax profit fell 70% to £85m on a £105m Iran-conflict fuel hit, yet shares held up on resilient bookings and an unrelated £5.7bn takeover battle.

EasyJet told the market on 23 July what most travellers could have guessed from the price of a summer fare: fuel is expensive, and the airline is paying for it. Pre-tax profit for the three months to 30 June fell 70%, to £85m from £286m a year earlier — a number that would, in most other circumstances, have been treated as a profit warning in all but name. Instead, the shares rose.

The arithmetic behind the collapse is straightforward. Fuel costs rose £105m in the quarter to £732m, a 17% increase, as the unhedged portion of easyJet's fuel consumption was bought at prices inflated by the conflict between Israel and Iran. Jet fuel briefly touched around $1,800 a tonne in April, close to the highest levels since the aftermath of Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Cost per seat rose 8% to £85.97, and the airline's load factor — the share of seats filled — slipped 1.3 percentage points to 88.9%, evidence that higher fares and skittish Middle East-linked demand were doing some quiet damage to volumes as well as margins.

A profit halved, a share price unmoved

What makes the release notable is not the size of the fall — airlines have absorbed worse — but the market's shrug. Shares initially rose almost 6% on results day, though that figure needs a caveat: it was recovering ground lost the previous session, when a Reuters report that the European Union might tighten airline-ownership rules knocked 12% off the stock. Strip out that noise and the standalone reaction to a 70% profit miss was closer to neutral than euphoric — but neutral is itself the story. A year in which pre-tax profit fell by five-sixths in a single quarter would normally invite a sharper repricing of the stock. It didn't happen, largely because investors have already concluded that the shock is temporary and close to its peak.

Chief executive Kenton Jarvis framed the quarter as a management job rather than a crisis: the airline said it had "continued to manage the impact of the Middle East conflict, and its effect on fuel prices and booking trends," while pointing to "strong late booking demand for our flights and holidays." That late-booking pattern is doing real work in the numbers. Group revenue actually rose 2%, to £2.98bn, even as costs outran it — the classic shape of a margin squeeze rather than a demand collapse. Jarvis also said the airline was seeing "the load factor gap close for peak summer and an extension of the booking curve," and confirmed fares for July, August and September would run flat year-on-year despite the fuel bill, a pricing discipline analysts read as confidence rather than desperation.

Holidays carry the group, bookings hold the line

The division doing the heavy lifting is easyJet Holidays, the package-tour arm built to diversify away from pure seat-selling. Its pre-tax profit came in at £84m, down just £2m on last year (and up 7% excluding currency effects), on customer growth of 8%. Hargreaves Lansdown's research note on the update put it plainly: costs are rising faster than revenue can offset them across the group, but the Holidays business is demonstrating what the company calls the resilience of a "capital-light" model — no aircraft to fund, higher margins, and a growth rate now running in double digits. That division alone accounts for more than a third of group profit, a proportion that has crept up with every difficult quarter for the core airline.

Forward indicators gave the market more reason to look through the fuel spike. Fourth-quarter airline bookings stood at 68% of seats sold, only 2 percentage points behind last year, with ticket yields flat — not the pattern of a business managing collapsing demand. Holidays bookings were stronger still, at 87% of capacity. EasyJet left its full-year capacity guidance unchanged, targeting roughly 6% growth in available seat kilometres, and restated its medium-term target of pre-tax profit above £1bn once conditions normalise — a target some way from this year's run rate, with analysts' full-year forecasts clustering around £129m, but one management is not walking back.

A takeover battle sitting underneath the numbers

The results also landed inside an unrelated but consequential distraction: a bidding contest for the airline itself. Apollo Global Management has tabled a £5.7bn, £7.15-a-share all-cash offer, trumping a prior £6.90-a-share approach from Castlelake, and easyJet's board has backed the Apollo terms as superior. Both bidders face the same obstacle — EU rules require airlines flying within the bloc to remain majority-owned and controlled by EU nationals — which is why Wednesday's Reuters report of possible tightening on that front did more to the share price in a day than the earnings release did the day after. Castlelake must say by 3 August whether it will proceed or step aside; Apollo's deadline follows on 7 August, a week from this report and a nearer-term catalyst for the stock than any further fuel-price move.

That sequencing matters for reading the market's reaction correctly. Some of the share price resilience around the results is genuine conviction that Middle East-driven fuel costs are peaking rather than compounding; some of it is simply a takeover premium sitting on top of the stock, insulating it from a bad quarter that would otherwise have drawn blood. Untangling the two will get easier once the EU ownership question and the bid deadlines resolve in the coming days — at which point the shares will be trading on fundamentals again, and the fuel bill, not the deal, will decide whether this quarter's optimism was justified.
