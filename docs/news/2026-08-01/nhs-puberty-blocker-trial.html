UK High Court clears NHS-funded puberty blocker trial to proceed

The Royal Courts of Justice in London, where Mr Justice Chamberlain heard the two-day judicial review challenge. — David Castor / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A High Court judge rejected an emergency bid to block the NHS-funded PATHWAYS puberty blocker trial, clearing 226 children to begin enrolling from 1 August.

A High Court judge has cleared the way for NHS-funded researchers to begin dosing children with puberty-suppressing drugs as part of a clinical trial, rejecting a last-ditch legal challenge from campaigners who call the study unsafe and unethical.

Mr Justice Chamberlain refused permission for a judicial review of the PATHWAYS trial on 31 July, finding that none of the grounds put forward by the claimants had a realistic prospect of success. The ruling followed a two-day hearing at the Royal Courts of Justice and clears recruitment to begin from 1 August — the date originally scheduled before the legal challenge intervened.

A ban, then a trial

The PATHWAYS study exists because puberty blockers effectively do not exist anymore for new NHS patients outside a research setting. Following the Cass Review — the independent inquiry led by paediatrician Dr Hilary Cass into NHS gender identity services for children — the government imposed an emergency ban on prescribing gonadotropin-releasing hormone analogues, or GnRH analogues, to under-18s in 2024, later made indefinite. Cass's report concluded the evidence supporting the drugs' use in adolescent gender dysphoria was, in her words, "remarkably weak," and recommended that any future use happen only inside a properly designed clinical trial.

PATHWAYS, run by King's College London and South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust, is that trial. Announced in November 2025, it aims to enrol around 226 children and young people — trans boys from age 11, trans girls from age 12 — who have already been through NHS Gender Services and received sustained psychological and social support. One group will receive puberty blockers immediately for 24 months; a second will wait 12 months before starting treatment, allowing researchers to compare outcomes on quality of life, mental health, and gender-related distress. The protocol has clearance from the Health Research Authority and the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency.

The case against it

The challenge came from the Bayswater Support Group, which represents parents of children who identify as transgender, alongside psychotherapist James Esses and Keira Bell, who detransitioned after being treated at the Tavistock's former gender identity service and later won a landmark case over informed consent. Their argument was that regulators failed to properly weigh the trial's foreseeable risks — to bone density, cognitive development, and fertility — against its anticipated benefit, and that the decision-making process behind the trial's approval had been conducted with only partial disclosure of minutes and papers.

Esses called the trial "the antithesis of child safeguarding" and said he would consult lawyers about an appeal. Bell described the ruling as a "tragedy."

Mr Justice Chamberlain was unpersuaded. In his judgment, he noted that every child entering the trial would have been assessed over "many months" by specialist doctors who had independently "formed the opinion that a child has a reasonable prospect of receiving a clinical benefit from the treatment" — a safeguard, he found, that sat on top of the ordinary consent protections required of any clinical trial involving minors. Legal scholars who reviewed the design, including Professor Sir Jonathan Montgomery of UCL, have separately noted that the trial's structure — independent clinician assessment ahead of any research-team involvement, plus parental consent — tracks the Court of Appeal's guidance from the earlier Bell v Tavistock litigation.

Institutional resolve, tested

What the ruling settles, more than any single scientific question, is a question of institutional nerve. The Cass Review landed as a rebuke to how NHS gender services had operated for years, and its recommendation of a trial rather than a blanket resumption of prescribing was itself a compromise — evidence-gathering instead of either an outright ban or a return to the pre-2024 status quo. Campaigners' attempt to stop that compromise in court, arguing it did not go far enough to protect children, tested whether the NHS and its regulators would hold that line under legal and political pressure. Chamberlain's refusal to grant even permission for a full judicial review — the lowest bar a challenge must clear — suggests the courts see the trial's safeguards as adequate, not merely defensible.

Expert reaction from clinicians and researchers not party to the case was broadly supportive. Dr Cass herself said the trial was necessary precisely because families and clinicians continue to seek the treatment despite the weak evidence base. Professor Dougal Hargreaves of Imperial College London called it "a robustly-designed new study" addressing a genuine research gap, while Professor Rod Mitchell of the University of Edinburgh cautioned that PATHWAYS is built to measure short-term psychological wellbeing rather than long-term physical effects such as bone health — meaning the trial answers one question while leaving others for later studies.

What comes next

Recruitment was expected to open around 1 August, subject to the ruling standing. Esses has signalled an intention to seek legal advice on an appeal, which would go first to the Court of Appeal rather than directly to the Supreme Court; whether that materialises, and on what grounds, is the immediate thing to watch. Beyond the courtroom, the trial itself will take years to report — the 24-month treatment arm alone pushes any meaningful outcome data well into 2028 — so the practical significance of Wednesday's ruling is less about puberty blockers reaching patients quickly and more about confirming that the research the Cass Review called for can actually proceed.
