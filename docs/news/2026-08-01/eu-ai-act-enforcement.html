EU AI Act enters full enforcement, GPAI penalty powers activate

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission and the AI Office's home institution. — Euro Pictures / Sarra Benyaich, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
From 2 August, Brussels can fine general-purpose AI providers up to 3% of global turnover — not the widely cited 7% — while its toughest AI rules get delayed to 2027-28.

From Sunday, the European Commission's AI Office stops being a rulebook and starts being a regulator with teeth. Under the EU's Artificial Intelligence Act, the Office gains the power to investigate, demand documents, run technical tests on AI models, and fine the companies behind them — the first time any government body anywhere has had this kind of direct financial leverage over general-purpose AI providers such as OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Microsoft and Meta.

The date — 2 August 2026 — has been circled on compliance calendars for two years. What actually changes on it is narrower than the headline version doing the rounds, but still consequential: it is the day Brussels' enforcement machinery switches on, not the day the underlying obligations first applied.

What actually starts on Sunday

Two separate things happen at once, and conflating them is the easiest way to get the story wrong.

First, the Commission's power to fine providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models — the foundation models underneath products like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude — becomes operative. Under Article 101 of the Act, those fines top out at 3% of a provider's global annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher. That is the figure that applies specifically to the GPAI enforcement regime activating this week. The often-cited 7% ceiling is real, but it sits in a different part of the Act (Article 99) and applies to the most serious category of violation — deploying an AI system for an outright banned purpose, such as social scoring or manipulative subliminal techniques — a bar most mainstream chatbot providers are not anywhere near tripping. For GPAI providers specifically, 3% of global turnover, or €35 million/7% only if a firm's conduct is separately judged to cross into prohibited-practice territory, is the more accurate ceiling to watch.

Second, Article 50 of the Act — the transparency rules — takes effect on the same day, independent of the fining powers. Chatbots and voice assistants must now tell users, in the first exchange, that they are talking to a machine rather than a person. AI-generated deepfakes need a machine-readable label. Emotion-recognition systems have to disclose themselves when in use. These duties fall on both the companies that build the systems and the businesses that deploy them, and they apply regardless of whether the underlying system counts as "high-risk" under the Act's separate risk-tiering.

Notably, the GPAI obligations themselves — safety documentation, copyright policies, systemic-risk assessments for the most powerful models — are not new; they have applied since August 2025. What was missing until now was the mechanism to punish anyone who ignored them.

A regime already being softened even as it arms up

The timing is more complicated than "regulation gets tougher." In June, EU member states signed off on a "Digital Omnibus" package that delays the Act's toughest provisions — the compliance rules for high-risk AI systems used in areas like hiring, credit scoring and law enforcement — from their original 2026 timetable to December 2027 for stand-alone systems and August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, has framed the rewrite as keeping Europe economically competitive without lowering the bar, following pressure from industry and, reportedly, from Washington.

Crucially, the omnibus left the transparency rules and the GPAI fining powers untouched — negotiators kept them out of the deferral because they sit outside the Act's risk-tiered structure, attaching instead to specific functions (a system that talks to people, a system that generates synthetic media) rather than to a risk classification. The result is a regulator that goes into enforcement mode on its most visible, chatbot-facing rules at the exact moment it is granting itself more room on the industrial, back-office rules that critics consider the Act's real substance.

The compliance industry has already picked sides

Most large model developers reached an accommodation with Brussels well before this week. OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Microsoft and Mistral all signed the Commission's voluntary Code of Practice for general-purpose AI when it was published in July 2025, a document meant to give companies a clear path to demonstrating compliance rather than negotiating it case by case. Meta refused, arguing the code would "throttle the development and deployment of frontier AI models in Europe" — a bet that its exposure to EU fines is worth taking rather than accepting terms it considers commercially damaging.

Whether the AI Office can actually make good on its new powers is a separate question from whether it has them. At least a dozen member states missed the August 2025 deadline to appoint the national authorities that are meant to work alongside the AI Office on day-to-day surveillance, and several, including France, Germany and Ireland, had still not passed the national legislation needed to operationalise enforcement as of last November. The Commission has built some machinery in the meantime — a public complaints portal and, since November 2025, a whistleblower tool through which employees at AI companies can anonymously flag suspected breaches, with retaliation protections under the EU's Whistleblower Directive kicking in for AI Act reports from this week onward.

What happens next

No fine has been issued yet, and the Act gives the Commission latitude on timing — investigations into systemic-risk models can run for months before a penalty is even proposed. The real test is less the 2 August date itself than what follows it: whether the AI Office opens a formal investigation into a named provider before the year is out, and whether it reaches for the fining power at all or leans on the softer tools — mandated documentation, model access requests, market-withdrawal threats — that sit below a headline penalty. Given the parallel move to delay the Act's harder high-risk provisions, the more telling signal may come from how aggressively Brussels treats the transparency rules it chose to keep on schedule.
