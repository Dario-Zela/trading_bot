Michael Burry expands short bets against Nvidia, Micron, Tesla and SOXX

Nvidia's Santa Clara headquarters. Burry has expanded short bets against the chipmaker and its AI-infrastructure peers. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Michael Burry has deepened short bets against Nvidia, Micron, Tesla, Caterpillar and semiconductor and Nasdaq ETFs, betting AI valuations have outrun demand.

Michael Burry, the investor whose 2008 subprime short became Wall Street shorthand for calling the top before anyone else, spent July piling onto a stack of bearish bets against the companies building out artificial intelligence infrastructure. Over a series of disclosures, he expanded existing short positions in Nvidia and Micron, added a fresh short against Tesla, and widened his wager against the semiconductor trade through put options on the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) and, separately, the Invesco QQQ Trust, which tracks the Nasdaq-100 — two distinct vehicles, not one and the same, despite how the trade is often shorthanded.

One thing has changed since Burry's last headline-grabbing call: it isn't arriving via a 13F. He deregistered his hedge fund, Scion Asset Management, with the SEC in November 2025, closing off the quarterly filing requirement that used to make his book public on a two-month lag. He now writes his positions up himself, on a paid Substack called Cassandra Unchained, at $39 a month. The market is trusting his own account of his trades rather than a regulatory filing — worth flagging for anyone treating the numbers as audited fact.

The trades

The basket took shape on June 30, when Burry disclosed new shorts in Nvidia, Applied Materials, Caterpillar and SOXX, plus a short against Tesla at $416.22 a share. Caterpillar was a first-time short; he told subscribers the machinery maker's stock had "nearly doubled" on AI-linked capital-spending optimism it hadn't earned. Two days later he shorted Micron outright rather than through options, saying puts had gotten too expensive, with the stock near $1,051.

By late July he had built on all of it: December 2026 Nvidia puts struck in the low $100s, January 2027 QQQ puts struck in the high $500s, and an enlarged Micron short taken near $880 as the stock slid. He also rolled his SOXX puts out to March 2027 and lifted the strikes into the low-to-mid $400s, tracking the ETF's own climb before the pullback.

Vindication, or just early

The July sell-off has so far rewarded the bet. Tesla fell roughly 24% on the month, closing near $319.69 after a weak earnings report knocked 15% off the stock in a single session. Caterpillar dropped about 16%, to $894.54. CNBC counted four of Burry's five disclosed July trades moving in his favor by month's end.

Burry's stated case, citing the Bank for International Settlements' 2026 annual report, is that a meaningful share of Nvidia's demand isn't coming from end customers at all but from off-balance-sheet financing arrangements among AI infrastructure players — vendor financing and circular deals he argues inflate apparent demand. He has also pointed to the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index's extension above its 200-day moving average, a level he says hasn't been seen since 2000, as a technical echo of the dot-com peak.

None of that proves a turn. Burry's 2008 call made him $100 million personally and his investors more than $700 million, but his more recent record is patchier — he has opened and closed Tesla shorts before, on both sides of the trade. His positions also remain a small counterweight to a market still setting records elsewhere: chip stocks hit fresh highs on blowout cloud earnings even as he was adding to his shorts. The next real test lands with Nvidia's and Micron's upcoming earnings, when actual AI-infrastructure demand — rather than the financing structures behind it — gets measured against the bookings.
