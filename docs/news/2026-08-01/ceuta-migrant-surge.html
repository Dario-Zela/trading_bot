Spain's PM blames traffickers as 60,000 migrants cross into Ceuta

The border fence separating Morocco from the Spanish enclave of Ceuta, the physical barrier at the centre of this week's record crossing. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
60,000 migrants crossed into Ceuta in two days — a record Sánchez blames on trafficking gangs — as France and Italy tighten Schengen checks with Spain.

The Spanish enclave of Ceuta has a population of roughly 85,000. In the space of about 48 hours this week, some 60,000 people crossed into it from Morocco — swimming around a breakwater fence into the Mediterranean, scaling border barriers, or slipping through gaps in patrols that could not keep pace with the crowds. It is, by any measure, the largest single migration event Ceuta has recorded, dwarfing the roughly 10,000 arrivals that alarmed Spain in May 2021.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's government says the surge was no accident. Trafficking networks, he told reporters, seized on a recent Spanish Supreme Court ruling — which found that migrants intercepted at sea en route to Ceuta or Melilla cannot simply be turned back to Morocco — and twisted it into a rumour that the border was effectively open. "They deceive many young people," Sánchez said of the smugglers, "many of whom find death, whether in the ocean or at this border."

A beach, a fence, and a stampede

Most of those who crossed were young Moroccan men, aged roughly 14 to 25, drawn less by a coordinated plan than by a viral claim spreading on social media: that Spain's highest court had thrown the door open. It had not — the ruling narrowly restricted a specific return procedure for sea arrivals — but the distinction was lost in the crush at Tarajal beach, where a barrier juts into the water and where much of the crossing, and much of the dying, happened.

The death toll is contested in the way these figures often are in the first chaotic days: Ceuta's regional president put it at 34, a Spanish government representative at 57, with drownings and a trampling incident at the breakwater fence cited as the main causes. Ceuta's mayor, Juan Vivas, said reception facilities built for a few hundred people were running at close to 2,400 percent of ordinary capacity. Spain scrambled troops to the enclave for logistics and crowd control, and by Friday evening had returned more than 48,000 of the arrivals to Morocco — a scale of removal that itself signals how thin the line between crisis and containment has become.

Morocco's ambassador to Spain, Karima Benyaich, insisted the crossing happened against her government's wishes, saying Rabat has "always prioritised legal, orderly and safe migration." That statement sits awkwardly against the memory of 2021, when a similar surge followed what was widely read as Morocco deliberately loosening border controls amid a diplomatic dispute with Madrid over Western Sahara. Spanish officials have not made that accusation explicit this time, but the echo is doing a lot of the political work regardless.

Paris and Rome move first

The response outside Spain came fast, and it came from the two governments most exposed to onward movement through the passport-free Schengen area. France said it would increase its border-police presence with Spain roughly fivefold, to 334 officers and gendarmes, and tightened checks at crossing points. Italy went further: Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's government suspended its Schengen arrangement with Spain for a month, imposing checks on non-EU nationals arriving from Spanish airports and seaports, while coordinating with Paris on the Franco-Italian frontier. Other member states, including Finland and Austria, called for Spain to be suspended from Schengen altogether — a demand Madrid has no intention of entertaining, but one that reveals how quickly a border event in one country becomes a legitimacy question for the whole passport-free zone.

What the enclave exposes

Ceuta and Melilla are the European Union's only land borders with Africa, an accident of sixteenth-century colonial geography that has made two small Spanish cities on the Moroccan coast the pressure point for a much larger set of migratory currents. Their small size is precisely what makes them so combustible: a few tens of thousands of arrivals can overwhelm Ceuta in a way that the same number would barely register in mainland Spain. That asymmetry has repeatedly forced Madrid into ad hoc, high-visibility responses — troop deployments, mass returns, ministerial visits — that look less like migration management and more like crisis containment.

Sánchez has framed this episode around criminal exploitation of a court ruling rather than state failure, which is also a way of avoiding a harder argument about Morocco's cooperation and the EU's asylum architecture more broadly. But the France and Italy response suggests other capitals are not waiting for that argument to be settled. Suspending Schengen commitments, even temporarily and even at the margins, is the kind of hardline reflex that tends to outlast the crisis that triggered it.

The immediate marker to watch is whether Italy's one-month suspension gets renewed or allowed to lapse in early September, and whether Brussels intervenes with anything beyond statements of concern. A slower-moving one is whether Spain's Supreme Court ruling — the technical trigger smugglers exploited — gets revisited by lawmakers now that it has been blamed, rightly or not, for dozens of deaths.
