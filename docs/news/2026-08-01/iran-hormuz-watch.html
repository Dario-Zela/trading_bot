STANDING WATCH · IRAN · By C. Tanaka · 2026-08-01 · 3 min read

Iran watch: strikes weighed, prices rise, Treasury eyes frozen assets

Abadan, home to one of Iran's largest oil refineries and a potential target if a renewed U.S.-Israel bombing campaign targets energy infrastructure. — Iranian Department of Publication and Broadcasting / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
US and Israel are reportedly weighing renewed strikes on Iran's energy infrastructure within days, and oil, consumer prices and frozen-asset diplomacy are already moving on the rumor.

The rumor mill on Iran is running hot again, and this time the number that matters is the price of a barrel of oil rather than the number of missiles.

CBS News, citing multiple U.S. sources, and the Times of Israel report that Washington and Israel are weighing a fresh bombing campaign against Iran's energy infrastructure — power plants and refineries — with strikes potentially coming as soon as this weekend. At a Camp David cabinet meeting, President Trump struck an aggressive note: "We'll be hitting them very hard. At some point, they're going to say, 'We just can't take it anymore.'" Asked about reviving diplomacy instead, he replied, "I think we just want to win." One option under discussion, according to officials briefed on the planning, is an intensive campaign running one to two weeks. An Israeli official pushed back on the framing, saying Israel is "unaware of a decision to restart full military operations," nor had it been asked to join. Nothing is confirmed, and nothing is official — but the pattern is familiar. Since Israel and the U.S. killed Iran's supreme leader in a February strike, the conflict has cycled through ceasefires, collapses and re-escalations, with Iran declaring the Strait of Hormuz closed in March and the Revolutionary Guard reportedly hitting two more tankers there on Thursday.

Markets are trading the possibility, not the fact. WTI crude settled at $84.67 a barrel and Brent at $90.12 on Friday, both up more than 1% on the day and part of a roughly 22% climb over the past month. The CBOE Volatility Index pushed toward the 20 level, and one-month implied volatility on oil — a gauge of how violently traders expect prices to swing — spiked as high as 68% last week before easing to 51%. Options desks report demand for out-of-the-money crude calls, a bet that a weekend strike would send prices sharply higher still.

The bill is already being passed on

That premium isn't just showing up in futures screens. The Wall Street Journal reports that Boston Beer, Sherwin-Williams, International Paper and Unilever have all raised or announced plans to raise prices on beer, paint, packaging and fried-food staples, citing Hormuz-related disruption to shipping and raw-material costs. A Reuters tally since the war began counts 32 companies signalling price increases, 31 warning of a financial hit, and 21 withdrawing or cutting guidance altogether. The framing from executives is uniform: these are temporary, cost-driven increases, not a demand story — but multiplied across beverages, coatings and packaging, it looks less like isolated pricing power and more like a broad-based passthrough that could complicate the Federal Reserve's read on inflation just as it weighs its next move.

Who pays for the damage

A third thread is quietly hardening from theory into practice. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has directed staff to collect damage estimates from Gulf allies — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar and Oman — with a view to compensating them for Iranian strikes using Iran's own frozen assets, estimated at $100–120 billion worldwide. One figure circulating puts Gulf damage at $58 billion. Tehran has long called the frozen funds theft and is demanding their return as a condition of any settlement; steering them to Riyadh and Abu Dhabi instead would be one more reason for Iran to stay away from the table.

Taken together, the three threads describe the same trade: a market pricing in a war that keeps almost-ending and almost-escalating, companies passing the cost to consumers before the shooting even resumes, and a U.S. Treasury positioning to make Iran pay for it either way. The next data point is blunt — whether the weekend passes with strikes or without them, and what oil does at Monday's open.
