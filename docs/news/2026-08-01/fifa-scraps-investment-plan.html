FIFA scraps $20bn World Cup investment plan after UEFA boycott threat

FIFA president Gianni Infantino, who abandoned the $20bn World Cup investment plan after days of backlash. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
FIFA abandoned its $20bn plan to sell a stake in the World Cup to Kushner-linked investors after UEFA's boycott vote and a senior adviser's resignation.

Gianni Infantino spent three days defending a plan to sell a fifth of FIFA's World Cup business to outside investors. On Friday, he abandoned it entirely.

The FIFA president confirmed the reversal in a brief statement, saying the proposal "has created divisions of a nature that, regardless of the level of support, are no longer in the interest of the objective set out in the first place." What went unsaid was more telling: within 48 hours of the plan going public, UEFA's 55 member federations had voted to boycott FIFA competitions, a senior adviser with a direct line to the White House had quit, and the UK's prime minister had called for Infantino's head.

A $20bn valuation, and one anchor investor

The plan, unveiled Tuesday, would have spun FIFA's commercial operations — World Cups and Club World Cups, men's and women's — into a standalone unit valued at $20bn. FIFA intended to sell roughly 20% of it to outside investors, raising as much as $4.2bn. The identity of the lead backer did most of the damage: Thrive Eternal, a vehicle tied to Thrive Capital founder Joshua Kushner, brother of Jared Kushner and thus a Trump family connection sitting at the centre of world football's flagship asset. Infantino himself stood to gain tens of millions of dollars had the deal closed, according to reporting on the proposal's terms.

For an organisation that markets itself as the custodian of a global public good, selling a stake in the World Cup to a politically-connected New York investment firm was always going to read badly. UEFA didn't mince words, calling it "irresponsible and indefensible that a proposal of such significance for football was conceived in secret." The Asian Football Confederation and CONCACAF lined up behind it within days.

The resignation that stung

Opposition from continental federations is one kind of pressure; defection from inside the tent is another. Carlos Cordeiro, a former Goldman Sachs banker who represented FIFA on the White House's World Cup task force and had accompanied Infantino on visits to see President Trump, resigned Friday in protest. "Football has been central to my life," he said, "and after more than 35 years in banking, I understand both the value of this asset and the consequences of giving part of it away." He called it "a bad deal for football." FIFA's chief operating officer, Kevin Lamour, went further internally, telling staff they had been "deceived" and describing the scheme as "a project of one person."

Into that mix stepped Andy Burnham. The UK prime minister, whose government is already tangled in a separate energy and budget agenda this week, called Infantino "the wrong man to lead the organisation" and said the sell-off plan was an "outrageous suggestion." It's an unusual intervention — a head of government publicly demanding the resignation of a sports administrator — but it captured how far the row had travelled beyond football's usual boardrooms.

What the collapse actually signals

Strip away the personalities and the episode is a case study in how quickly a coalition of stakeholders can kill a mega-deal once it's perceived as secretive and self-dealing. FIFA didn't lose this fight in a courtroom or a shareholder vote — it lost it in five days, to a boycott threat and a wave of resignations, before a single dollar changed hands. That matters beyond football. Institutions that sit atop valuable, quasi-public assets — broadcasting rights, event franchises, data — are discovering that monetisation plans conceived behind closed doors carry reputational and governance risk that can move faster than the deal itself.

For anyone pricing risk around major sporting and media events, the takeaway is less about the $20bn number and more about FIFA's internal fragility. An organisation that can be blindsided into a five-day capitulation over its own commercial strategy is one where broadcasting rights negotiations, World Cup hosting arrangements, and sponsorship structures all carry a wider band of uncertainty than governance-by-committee usually implies.

What's next

Infantino's term runs through FIFA's presidential election, now scheduled for 18 March 2027 in Morocco, with candidate declarations due by 18 November 2026. Cordeiro's resignation and the open criticism from Sheikh Salman bin Ebrahim Al Khalifa's camp — Infantino's 2016 rival — and Concacaf's Victor Montagliani have already stirred talk of a challenge. Whether Infantino stands again, and whether FIFA revisits some slimmed-down version of outside investment once the immediate anger cools, are the two threads worth watching between now and the vote.
