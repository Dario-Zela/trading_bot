Chelsea's Mudryk cleared to play again after doping-ban appeal resolved

Mykhailo Mudryk playing for Ukraine's under-21 side in 2023, before his move to Chelsea and the doping case that sidelined him for nearly two years. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Mudryk's four-year doping ban ended not in vindication but a settlement crediting time served, after WADA's own reporting rules changed under him.

Mykhailo Mudryk is free to play football again, almost exactly twenty months after a doping test knocked him out of the game. But the way his case ended says as much about the machinery of anti-doping enforcement as it does about the winger himself.

The Football Association confirmed on 30 July that it had resolved its disciplinary proceedings against the Chelsea forward "with the agreement of" the World Anti-Doping Agency, clearing him to return "with immediate effect." There was no exoneration, no finding that the original charge was mistaken. Instead, the case was settled on the basis that WADA's own rules for reporting the substance in question had since changed — and that under the new rules, Mudryk's sample would never have triggered a violation at all.

A four-year ban, undone by a technical document

Mudryk's out-of-competition sample, collected on 22 October 2024, returned a low concentration of meldonium, a heart medication banned by WADA since 2016 for its purported endurance-boosting effects. The FA notified him in December that year and imposed a provisional suspension; an independent Regulatory Commission subsequently found an anti-doping rule violation and handed him a four-year ban in January 2026. He had not played a competitive match since November 2024.

Mudryk took the case to the Court of Arbitration for Sport. While the appeal was pending, WADA updated its technical document governing minimum reporting levels — the threshold concentration above which a lab is required to report a substance as present. Under the revised standard, known as TD2027MRL, the FA said plainly that "if Mr Mudryk's sample was collected today, the concentration of meldonium in the sample would not have been reported and no anti-doping rule violations would have resulted."

That change was not made retroactive, so it could not simply void the earlier finding. Instead, the FA, WADA and Mudryk settled: he accepted that he had committed the violation as charged, but agreed to a period of ineligibility equal to time already served — in effect, a suspended sentence dressed as a technical correction. "After a long battle," Mudryk said in a statement, "the four-year ban that was imposed on me has been rescinded, and I am free to resume my career with immediate effect. I am grateful that this process has reached a conclusion."

What Chelsea get back

Chelsea, who had kept Mudryk on their books throughout but away from first-team facilities, called the saga "an extremely difficult period for Misha, both professionally and personally" and pledged to support his "reintegration into the squad." The club is currently on a pre-season tour of Australia and south-east Asia under new manager Xabi Alonso, and has the option of loaning Mudryk out first to rebuild match fitness rather than throwing him straight into a Premier League squad that has managed without him for the best part of two seasons.

For a Chelsea side still shaping its identity under Alonso, the practical upside is real: a 25-year-old wide forward signed for a club-record fee in January 2023, now returning with no fee, no minutes lost to another club, and a full pre-season to reintegrate. Fantasy and betting markets had already priced him as effectively unavailable; his sudden reappearance widens Chelsea's attacking options at a stroke.

The uncomfortable question

The harder story is what the settlement implies about the system that produced it. A player served roughly twenty months of a four-year ban for a violation that, under WADA's current standards, would not be a violation at all — and the fix was a negotiated settlement rather than a formal reversal. WADA's meldonium reporting threshold has been contentious since the drug produced a wave of positive tests after its 2016 ban, many involving trace, possibly lingering concentrations rather than clear doping. Whether other pending or recent meldonium cases get the same treatment as Mudryk's — a quiet settlement crediting time served — is now a live question for anyone tracking how consistently WADA applies its own rule changes.

Chelsea's medical and legal staff will want Mudryk playing again as soon as he is fit; the next marker to watch is whether he features on the remainder of the club's pre-season tour or is eased in via a loan. The more consequential marker sits elsewhere — in whether WADA or national federations revisit any of the other meldonium cases sitting in the same window as his.
