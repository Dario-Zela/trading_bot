High Court rules China's London 'mega-embassy' approval was lawful

The former Royal Mint building on Tower Hill, the site China plans to convert into Europe's largest embassy compound — Steve Cadman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The High Court dismissed a residents' challenge to China's planned Royal Mint Court mega-embassy; opponents now aim for the Supreme Court.

The Royal Mint stopped making coins on Tower Hill nearly six decades ago, but the site next to the Tower of London is still generating friction — this time over who gets to build there, and what it might be used for.

On Friday, the High Court dismissed a judicial review brought by residents opposed to China's plan to convert the 20,000 square-metre former Royal Mint Court site into what would be the largest embassy compound in Europe. Two judges, ruling remotely, found that both the housing ministry and Tower Hamlets council had acted lawfully in granting planning permission back in January. The Royal Mint Court Residents' Association — representing roughly 100 nearby properties — says it isn't done.

"Beijing thinks this judgment is the final word. It isn't. The appeal is already in the works."

That was Luke Pollard, the association's spokesperson, signalling the group's intent to seek permission to appeal, with the explicit goal of reaching the UK Supreme Court. It has relaunched a crowdfunding campaign, having already raised more than £220,000 to fund the legal fight.

A long-running planning saga

China bought the historic site — a former Cistercian abbey ground, then home to the Royal Mint from 1809 until operations moved to Wales in 1967 — for a reported $327 million in 2018. Its ambitions for the plot have run into resistance at every stage since. Tower Hamlets council unanimously rejected the original application in December 2022 on security grounds, prompting Beijing to appeal directly to central government. Housing secretary Steve Reed ultimately called in and approved the scheme in January, clearing the way just ahead of a China visit by the prime minister at the time.

That approval came despite a notably blunt intervention from Britain's intelligence establishment. MI5 director general Ken McCallum and his GCHQ counterpart wrote jointly to the home secretary and foreign secretary warning that security risks tied to the site "cannot be wholly eliminated," while judging the government's mitigation package "expert, professional and proportionate." Objectors have pointed to the embassy's proximity to fibre-optic cables carrying financial data between the City and Canary Wharf, and to the prospect that a 200-strong compound staff could conceal undeclared intelligence officers.

The residents' case, and why it failed

Before the High Court, the residents' association argued that planning conditions attached to the approval were effectively meaningless, since the Vienna Convention shields diplomatic premises from routine enforcement — meaning China would face no real sanction for breaching them. They also argued that two documents underpinning the decision, covering a security assessment and a terror-blast risk analysis, were withheld from public view, denying residents a fair chance to comment during the planning process.

The judges were not persuaded on either point, finding the process followed by ministers and the council to be lawful. It is a narrower legal question than the political one that has dogged the project for years — whether the UK should be granting China more diplomatic real estate at all, given the broader tension between courting Chinese investment and managing security concerns identified by its own spy agencies.

What's next

The residents' association must first secure permission to appeal before any Supreme Court hearing becomes possible, a process that could stretch well into next year. In the meantime, the ruling leaves the government's January approval intact and gives China a clearer, if not yet final, path to building its embassy metres from the Tower of London. The next marker to watch is whether an appellate court agrees there is a legal question substantial enough to warrant reopening — until then, the diggers stay parked.
