Sainsbury's agrees £120m sale of Argos to Swift Partners

In one sentence
Sainsbury's sells Argos to ex-Co-op boss Richard Pennycook's Swift Partners for £120m — a tenth of the £1.4bn it paid in 2016.

Ten years ago this September, Sainsbury's completed the £1.4bn takeover of Argos, promising a marriage of grocery footfall and catalogue-showroom convenience that would remake British shopping. On Friday, it sold the whole thing for £120m — enough, as one City analyst noted, to buy roughly a tenth of what it paid, with most of even that sum arriving in instalments over the next three years.

The buyer is Swift Partners, a vehicle built around three retail veterans — Richard Pennycook, Trevor Strain and Matt Truman — backed by the investment firm True Capital. Pennycook, who ran the Co-operative Group through its own post-crisis rebuild a decade ago, becomes Argos's executive chairman, splitting his week three days to the new job. It is, depending on your appetite for pattern recognition, either a specialist rescuing a business a supermarket never understood, or another turnaround man about to discover why the last one failed.

The arithmetic of a decade-long miscalculation

The terms are modest by design: £70m on completion, expected in February 2027, plus £50m deferred over the following three years, plus proceeds from selling an Argos distribution centre. Sainsbury's will also book a non-cash impairment of around £350m on the transaction itself — on top of the £852m writedown Home Retail Group, Argos's former parent, had already taken before the 2016 deal even closed. Add it up and the effective destruction of value across the Argos era approaches £1.3bn, most of it simply the gap between what Sainsbury's paid in 2016 and what the business fetched in 2026.

Sainsbury's chief executive, Simon Roberts, framed the sale in the language every departing shareholder learns to translate: "This agreement allows us to focus all our resources and investment on the significant opportunities ahead." What it means, more plainly, is that a decade-long bet on general merchandise diversification has been abandoned, and the company is going back to selling food. Shore Capital's Clive Black — Sainsbury's own house broker — put it more bluntly, calling Argos a "suboptimal performer from a financial perspective" and the sale process itself "challenging and prolonged." Investors seemed to agree with the relief thesis: Sainsbury's shares jumped as much as 7% on the news before settling around 3% up.

A sale that almost didn't happen

This is not Sainsbury's first attempt to be rid of Argos. Talks with the Chinese e-commerce giant JD.com collapsed within a day of starting last September, after JD.com pushed for terms Sainsbury's judged not to be in shareholders' interests. That failed courtship left Argos in limbo for nearly a year — a period in which its general merchandise sales kept sliding even as Sainsbury's core grocery business grew. In the most recent quarter cited by analysts, overall group sales rose 3.1% while Argos sales fell 0.5%, a gap that has become the defining feature of the group's results for years.

What Swift Partners is actually buying is not a shrinking rump. Argos retains roughly 20 million active customers, about 80% of its sales now happen online, and the business still turned an underlying operating profit of £9m in the last financial year — thin, but not nothing. The deal covers 201 standalone stores, 466 concessions inside Sainsbury's supermarkets, and more than 450 collection points, alongside the Nectar loyalty tie-in and the Habitat homewares brand. Full separation from Sainsbury's systems and logistics is not expected until February 2029, meaning the two businesses will be untangling supply chains and store space for the better part of three more years even as they operate under separate ownership.

Why a specialist might succeed where a supermarket didn't

The case for Pennycook succeeding rests on a fairly narrow argument: that Argos's problems were ones of fit, not fundamentals. Under Sainsbury's, the catalogue retailer was a general-merchandise sideline inside a grocery-first culture, competing for capital and management attention against a food business that, as Roberts likes to point out, had to be "rebuilt" after years of its own underperformance. As a standalone company again, with owners whose sole job is making Argos work, the theory goes that its click-and-collect network and price-comparison positioning against Amazon could finally get the focused investment a diversified retailer never gave it.

The counter-case is that this is exactly the story Home Retail Group's own management told before Sainsbury's arrived, and exactly what a decade of falling like-for-like sales, store closures and now two separate abandoned or steeply discounted ownership attempts would suggest is wishful thinking. Argos has spent ten years being told its problems are structural — the wrong parent, the wrong strategy, the wrong moment in retail history — without anyone quite proving they are fixable. Pennycook's three-day-a-week chairmanship will be the next test of that proposition, and probably the last one anybody is willing to fund at scale.

What comes next

The deal needs to clear customary conditions before completion in February 2027, with the full IT and logistics separation running through to 2029 — a long runway during which Argos remains, in practice, half-attached to the group it just left. Watch Sainsbury's next set of results for whether the market rewards the simplification thesis with a sustained re-rating, and watch Swift Partners' first year for the shape of its actual turnaround plan, beyond the supportive quotes issued on announcement day. If Argos's sales keep falling under genuinely independent, retail-specialist ownership, the conclusion will be hard to avoid: this was never a portfolio problem. It was the business.
