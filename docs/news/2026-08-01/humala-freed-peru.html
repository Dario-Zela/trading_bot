Peru's ex-president Ollanta Humala freed as corruption sentence overturned

Ollanta Humala at a military parade in Lima during his presidency, July 2015. — Ministerio de Defensa del Perú / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Peru's Constitutional Court freed ex-president Ollanta Humala, ruling his Odebrecht-linked conduct wasn't legally defined as money laundering when it occurred — not that he didn't do it.

Ollanta Humala walked out of a Lima prison on Friday evening, seven years to the month since the corruption dragnet from Brazil's Odebrecht scandal first swept up a Peruvian head of state. He did not walk out because judges found him innocent. He walked out because Peru's Constitutional Court ruled that what he did — however it is described in the earlier verdict — did not meet the legal definition of money laundering at the time he did it.

That distinction is the whole story. Humala, who governed Peru from 2011 to 2016, and his wife Nadine Heredia were sentenced last year to 15 years each after the National Superior Court found they had funnelled close to $3 million from Odebrecht and, earlier, from the Venezuelan government into Humala's 2006 and 2011 campaigns. It was the first conviction of a Peruvian ex-president to come out of the Odebrecht affair, a scandal so vast — some $788 million in bribes paid across a dozen Latin American countries, and a $3.5 billion settlement with US and Brazilian prosecutors in 2016 — that it eventually touched four Peruvian presidents.

A technicality, not an acquittal

Humala's lawyers filed a habeas corpus petition arguing that the criminal statute used to convict him did not exist, in the relevant form, when the campaign contributions were made. In a ruling dated 15 July and made public on 31 July, the Constitutional Court agreed, declaring "the entire criminal proceedings against Ollanta Moisés Humala Tasso null and void." Humala left the prison in a vehicle under police escort at around 7:40pm local time.

The ruling does not touch the underlying facts — that Odebrecht and Caracas money moved into Humala's campaigns is not in dispute. It touches only the legal label attached to that conduct, and whether prosecutors could apply it retroactively. Heredia, who ran both campaigns and was convicted alongside her husband, had already sought diplomatic asylum in Brazil last year rather than face prison; it is not yet clear how the ruling affects her case.

A pattern prosecutors know well

For a judiciary that has spent nearly a decade trying to make Odebrecht convictions stick, this is a familiar kind of setback. Peru has cycled through presidents jailed, prosecuted, or driven to suicide by the same scandal — Alan García shot himself as police arrived to arrest him in 2019 — without landing a conviction that survives appeal. Humala's case was meant to be the one that held. Instead it joins a list of prosecutions unwound on process rather than facts, a pattern that does little to reassure investors weighing how durable anti-corruption enforcement in Peru actually is.

What happens next runs through the same court: prosecutors can appeal the habeas corpus ruling, though Constitutional Court decisions are typically final. The more consequential question is whether Peru's legislature moves to close the retroactivity gap the ruling exposed, or whether the next corruption case now has a ready-made exit.
