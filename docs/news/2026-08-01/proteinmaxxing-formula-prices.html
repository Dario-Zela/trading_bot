'Proteinmaxxing' trend pushes up UK infant formula prices

Whey protein, used to adjust infant formula's protein ratio toward that of breast milk, has become a tightly bid dairy commodity. — National Institute of Korean Language / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0 KR)
In one sentence
GLP-1 users bulking up on whey protein have driven wholesale whey prices up over 100% in a year, pushing UK infant formula prices up 4.8%.

Britain's supermarket baby aisle has become an unlikely casualty of the Ozempic era. Wholesale prices for whey protein — a workhorse ingredient in both protein shakes and infant formula — have surged over the past year, and the increase is now showing up on receipts for parents who have never touched a protein bar in their lives.

According to Office for National Statistics data, the average price of baby formula rose 4.8% over the year to mid-2026, with a standard 750g box now costing £12.11, up from £11.55. Industry analysts and campaigners trace a good part of that increase back to a dairy ingredient few shoppers think about at all: whey protein concentrate, the powder left over when milk is processed into cheese.

A by-product turned hot commodity

Whey protein concentrate 80 — 80% pure protein, the industrial-grade benchmark — cost €11,733 (£10,050) per tonne in July 2025. By this summer it had reached €25,875, according to dairy market analyst Expana, with some sales of the highest-grade material touching €29,000 and traders bracing for €30,000. Three or four years ago, the same tonne of powder went for under €5,000.

The scale of the move has turned what was once a commodity by-product — whey used to be treated almost as cheese-making waste — into a tightly bid ingredient. "GLP-1 has changed who buys protein, and the whey market is still catching up," one dairy intelligence platform observed, pointing to the millions of new patients on drugs such as Ozempic, Wegovy and Mounjaro who are advised to raise their protein intake to preserve muscle mass while losing fat rapidly. That demand has combined with a broader "protein-first" consumer trend — protein added to everything from breakfast cereal to coffee and Pop-Tarts — to push sports-nutrition buying from a niche market into a mainstream, structural source of demand rather than a seasonal blip.

Where the formula makers get squeezed

Infant formula manufacturers sit downstream of that same whey market, and they have far less room to substitute or absorb the cost. Whey is used in formula specifically to adjust the protein ratio and improve digestibility so the product more closely resembles breast milk, and there is no easy swap for it.

"Whey is a major ingredient in infant formula, so significant increases in its price risk increasing the price of formula itself," said Dr Vicky Sibson, director of the First Steps Nutrition Trust. Some manufacturers have responded by reformulating recipes to lean on D90 demineralised whey powder, a cheaper alternative that still supplies the required whey-to-casein ratio — but as more producers pile into that substitute, its price has begun climbing too. Jose Saiz, a dairy analyst at Expana, said manufacturers now describe current whey costs as "economically unsustainable at existing retail price levels," a signal that further formula price rises may already be in the pipeline.

The timing is awkward for an industry already under regulatory scrutiny. The UK's Competition and Markets Authority found in its infant formula market study that manufacturers had raised prices by 25% over two years during the cost-of-living crisis, with profit margins running between 50% and 75%. The CMA stopped short of recommending a price or profit cap, and the government's response last December accepted only six of its eleven recommendations. Sibson has pointed to price-cap precedents abroad, including Greece, as evidence that tighter intervention remains an option regulators have simply chosen not to use — which means the whey cost pass-through is landing on a market with essentially no price ceiling to blunt it.

The wider lesson for commodity watchers

For traders, the interesting part isn't the baby-formula aisle itself but the transmission mechanism. GLP-1 adoption is a pharmaceutical and consumer-health story; whey is a dairy by-product market most people have never priced. The two have collided because whey sits at a genuine bottleneck — it's a fixed co-product of cheese output, not something dairies can simply produce more of on demand — and formula makers, sports-nutrition brands and now a widening tier of "protein-added" packaged food are all bidding for the same limited pool.

That is the pattern worth filing away: a demand shock concentrated in one corner of a supply chain (weight-loss drug users buying protein powder) doesn't stay contained to that corner. It reprices an upstream input shared by an entirely unrelated end market, and the businesses least able to pass on or hedge that cost — formula makers selling to price-sensitive parents under regulatory watch — absorb it last and loudest.

The next data points to watch are the ONS's monthly food inflation release, due in the coming weeks, and Expana's whey benchmark, which will show whether the €25,875 print holds, extends toward €30,000, or eases as cheesemakers respond to the incentive by running more milk through the vat. Any formal government move on formula pricing — a live question since December's partial acceptance of the CMA's recommendations — would also determine how much of this cost increase manufacturers are ultimately allowed to pass through.
