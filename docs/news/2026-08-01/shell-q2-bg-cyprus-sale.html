Shell beats on Q2 profit, agrees BG Cyprus sale to MOL

Shell Centre, the company's headquarters on London's South Bank. — Marek Ślusarczyk (Tupungato) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0
In one sentence
Shell beat Q2 profit forecasts with $9.84bn in earnings while agreeing to sell its Cyprus gas stake to MOL for up to $720m, trimming non-core assets from a position of strength.

Shell posted a second-quarter net profit of roughly $9.84bn on Thursday, comfortably ahead of the $8.92bn analysts had pencilled in, and followed the announcement two days later with a decision that says as much about the company's next decade as the earnings say about the last three months: it is selling its stake in a Cypriot gas discovery it has held for more than a decade to Hungary's MOL Group for up to $720m.

The two announcements, arriving within days of each other in the final week of July, tell a single story from opposite ends. One shows Shell's current businesses throwing off cash faster than expected. The other shows management deciding, in real time, which businesses get to keep that cash and which get cut loose.

A quarter the Middle East made stranger, not weaker

Shell's adjusted earnings of $9.8bn compared with $6.9bn in the first quarter, a jump the company attributed to higher oil and gas prices, stronger refining margins and unusually active trading desks — the last of those a direct product of the volatility that has gripped energy markets since strikes on Iranian and regional infrastructure disrupted flows through the Strait of Hormuz earlier this year. Revenue rose 45% to $94.7bn. Cash flow from operations came in at $21.4bn, and free cash flow at $17.5bn, enough for Shell to cut net debt to $41.8bn from $52.6bn in the first quarter, taking gearing down to 19%.

The segment detail bears out the trading story. Integrated gas, which houses Shell's LNG trading book, delivered adjusted earnings of $2.69bn, up 55% on the year — the business best positioned to profit when regional supply gets jumpy and cargoes get rerouted at short notice. Upstream added $3.49bn and chemicals and products $2.88bn; renewables and energy solutions, by contrast, contributed a token $79m, underlining how far Shell's low-carbon arm still sits from moving the group's overall numbers. Chief executive Wael Sawan called it "very strong results during another quarter of severe disruption in global energy markets," a description that fits Chevron and ExxonMobil's own outsized quarters this earnings season about as well as it fits Shell's.

The board approved a new $3bn share buyback tranche — Shell's nineteenth consecutive quarter of buybacks at that size or larger — plus $1.2bn of deferred repurchases carried over from prior periods, while holding 2026 capital spending guidance at $24bn-$26bn. Shareholder approval for the roughly $10bn all-stock acquisition of Canadian gas producer ARC Resources also cleared this quarter, with completion expected in the third quarter — a reminder that the disciplined-capital-allocation language Shell uses around Cyprus applies just as much to what it's buying as to what it's selling.

Selling Cyprus to buy conviction elsewhere

The divestment announced Friday hands MOL, the Budapest-based refiner, full ownership of BG Cyprus Limited — a Shell subsidiary whose only real asset is a 35% non-operated interest in Block 12 offshore Cyprus, home to the Aphrodite gas field. Shell will receive up to $720m, made up of an upfront payment plus milestone-linked contingent payments tied to the field's eventual development, with the deal expected to close in early 2027.

Aphrodite has been a discovery in search of a final investment decision since Noble Energy first found it in 2011. Estimates put the resource at around 104 billion cubic metres of gas equivalent — some 632 million barrels of oil equivalent — but a lack of export infrastructure and disputes over maritime boundaries in the eastern Mediterranean left it stranded for years. That changed on 22 July, when Chevron, which operates the block, secured a fully signed memorandum of understanding with Egypt's state gas company, EGAS, to buy the field's entire output by pipeline under a supply deal expected to run 15 to 20 years. The partners are now working toward a binding agreement and final investment decision, targeted for September, with first gas pencilled in for 2031. NewMed Energy holds the remaining 30% of the block.

In other words, Shell is exiting just as the project's commercial path has become clearer than at any point in its history — which is itself the point management is making. "Our decision to exit is driven by disciplined capital allocation and portfolio choices, as we focus on opportunities that strengthen our integrated LNG value chain," said Cederic Cremers, Shell's integrated gas president. Aphrodite is a non-operated, minority stake in a single offshore field feeding one buyer; it does not touch the liquefaction, shipping and trading network that let Shell's gas division post a 55% earnings jump this quarter. Selling it converts a passive holding into cash for buybacks, debt paydown or the LNG assets Shell does want to run directly — among them the still-undecided second phase of its Canada LNG project, on which the company says it will rule by the end of the year.

Part of a wider clearout

Shell's move follows a similar logic to BP's decision this year to put its North Sea oil and gas business up for formal sale, and sits inside a broader pattern of the majors trimming legacy or non-core positions even as headline profits hold up — Chevron and Exxon both posted their own outsized quarters this period, helped by the same Hormuz-driven price volatility that lifted Shell's trading arm. The difference is that Shell's Cyprus exit is not a response to weakness; it's a company using a strong balance sheet to walk away from an asset it was never going to operate, in favour of ones it can.

The next marker for how far this reshaping goes is Shell's final investment decision on Canada LNG's second phase, due by year-end, alongside the ARC Resources deal closing in the third quarter — both bigger tests of capital discipline than a $720m Cypriot stake, and both likely to shape how investors read Shell's next set of quarterly numbers.
