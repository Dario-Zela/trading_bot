STANDING WATCH · IRAN-HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-13 · 3 min read

Oil holds near $100 as Hormuz standoff spreads to Saudi pipeline

Satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint that carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. — NASA MODIS Land Rapid Response Team / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Oil neared $100 after Iran hit US Navy ships and Saudi's Hormuz-bypass pipeline was struck; Goldman flags $120 oil in 2027 as a tail risk, not its base case.

Brent crude touched $101 a barrel this week and settled Friday at $104.61, capping a rally of more than 8% as Iran widened its confrontation with the United States from the Strait of Hormuz to Saudi Arabia's main pipeline network. It is the sharpest run-up in oil since the war between Iran and Israel first shut the strait in February, and it has produced a forecast from Goldman Sachs that is more unsettling than the headline price move itself: crude above $120 a barrel not this month, but in 2027.

The strait and the pipeline, both squeezed at once

The immediate trigger was military. Iran said it struck a US Navy vessel and, separately, hit tankers it linked to American forces near Hormuz — claims that followed the US destroying three Iranian oil tankers and, days later, five more, according to reporting reviewed this week. Traffic through the strait, which carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, thinned to a handful of ships a day.

Then, on September 10-11, drones launched from Iraq's Maysan province — which borders Iran — struck Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline near Riyadh and Medina, touching off fires. Aramco had already pushed the line, known as Petroline, to its maximum roughly 7 million barrels a day to help customers route crude to the Red Sea port of Yanbu and around the Gulf chokepoint entirely. Saudi Arabia shut it down as a precaution. Iraq has since dismissed a military commander over the incident and opened an investigation; Riyadh says it will not retaliate for now, at Baghdad's request. For a few days, the two main routes out of the Gulf were both partially or fully impaired at once — precisely the scenario the pipeline's Hormuz-bypass design was never sized for.

A price already off its highs

Oil markets have absorbed worse this year. Brent peaked near $126 in the spring, during the acute phase of the wider Iran war, before easing back. This week's move is a second spike layered on an already fragile market, and it cooled somewhat Friday after Iran said it would meet Gulf states and Iraq in Oman on Monday to discuss a longer-term shipping arrangement for the strait — the kind of diplomatic signal that has calmed prices before, only for attacks to resume weeks later.

That pattern is what makes Goldman's note, from commodities co-head Daan Struyven, notable. The bank's actual base case has Brent easing to the $80s by December and into 2027 as non-OPEC supply grows. The $120-plus figure is explicitly a tail scenario — one that requires Gulf output to stay 4 million barrels a day below pre-war levels for a full year, driven by "more intense shipping attacks in Hormuz and the Red Sea." Struyven's advice to clients was not simply to bet on higher crude, but to hedge geopolitical risk through long positions in natural gas and refined products, which move differently than crude in a supply-outage scenario.

The distinction matters for anyone repricing energy exposure this week. A market betting on a spike prices options and short-dated futures; a market pricing sustained disruption reaches for structural hedges further out the curve. Monday's talks in Oman are the next test of which one this is.
