Burnham's fiscal strategy faces test as Budget Day nears

Prime Minister Andy Burnham's official portrait, taken at 10 Downing Street shortly after he took office in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / Crown Copyright (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham promises fiscal discipline but won't rule out more borrowing, leaving markets to guess until Healey's 28 October Budget.

Andy Burnham has spent his first two months in Downing Street on the front foot — capping bus fares, cutting VAT on energy bills, promising business-rate relief for music venues, dangling devolved income tax powers for regional mayors. The one thing he has not yet done is say how he will pay for it. That question now has a deadline: Chancellor John Healey delivers the government's first Budget on 28 October, and the gap between Burnham's spending instincts and his fiscal promises is starting to show.

Both men have repeated, almost word for word, that they will stick to the fiscal rules inherited from Rachel Reeves — balancing day-to-day spending against tax receipts by the end of the Parliament, and getting debt falling as a share of GDP. Healey has called fiscal discipline his "No. 1 priority." But neither has ruled out borrowing more to invest, and Burnham has openly talked about using whatever "flexibility" the rules allow. Markets noticed immediately: on the day he signalled it, from his first week in office, 30-year gilt yields jumped 10 basis points to 5.73%, a two-month high, the 10-year rose to 5.02%, and sterling slipped 0.3% against the dollar.

A shrinking cushion

The room to manoeuvre has since narrowed further, and not because of anything Burnham did. Pantheon Macroeconomics estimates Healey's fiscal headroom has been roughly halved since the spring — from about £23.6bn at the Spring Statement to closer to £13bn — as a global bond-market selloff, driven partly by the Hormuz standoff pushing oil back near $100 a barrel, has lifted borrowing costs across the board. Every extra £1bn the Treasury borrows now costs roughly £50m a year in debt interest, which eats into the very room Burnham wants to use.

Into that gap, the Treasury is reportedly exploring a technical route rather than an open breach: reclassifying more infrastructure and equity spending as investment rather than day-to-day cost, a mechanism Reeves herself built into the rules, potentially freeing up some £9bn a year by lifting investment borrowing from £129bn to £138bn. It is the kind of move that satisfies the letter of a fiscal rule while testing what markets consider its spirit.

What Healey actually said

Asked to define the Budget, Healey has offered a formula rather than a number: "This will be a budget that moves money and power out of Westminster and into every postcode around Britain. It will be built on fiscal discipline." That is a mission statement, not an arithmetic one — and it leaves open exactly how tax rises, spending restraint and any extra borrowing headroom will be divided up on 28 October.

The Office for Budget Responsibility's pre-Budget forecast, due alongside Healey's statement, will be the moment the ambiguity resolves one way or the other. Until then, gilts and sterling are trading on rhetoric rather than numbers — a state of affairs that tends not to last much beyond the next data point, or the next unguarded comment from No. 10.
