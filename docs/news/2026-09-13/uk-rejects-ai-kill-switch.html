UK government rejects AI 'kill switch' proposal

The House of Lords chamber, where peers debated the AI 'kill switch' amendment on 1 September — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers rejected a Lords amendment giving the government power to shut down dangerous AI systems, saying it wouldn't stop the risk and would only cut off UK access.

The UK government has told peers pushing for an AI emergency shutdown law that the idea sounds better than it works. Blocking access to a model in Britain, a Cabinet Office spokesperson said, "would not prevent them being developed or misused elsewhere" — so a kill switch would mostly just switch off Britain's access to the technology, not the risk.

The proposal came from Lord Clement-Jones, a Liberal Democrat peer, who tabled an amendment to the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill during a House of Lords debate on 1 September. It would have given ministers "last resort" powers to shut down data centres or deactivate AI systems judged to threaten national security, public safety or critical infrastructure. He warned that if an autonomous model showed "rogue behaviour, compound algorithmic failure or active alignment collapse," the security services currently have no statutory mechanism to pull the plug.

The amendment had unusually broad backing for a Lords intervention — co-sponsored by the Conservative peer Baroness Dido Harding, the crossbencher Baroness Beeban Kidron, and Labour's Lord Philip Hunt, and supported by the campaign group ControlAI. Kidron, better known for her work on children's online safety, pressed the government on whether leaving AI companies to set their own safeguards put the public and national security at risk.

Why officials said no

Government officials countered that the UK is sticking to a "long-term, science-led approach" and that the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill is meant to harden defences for essential public and digital services, not hand ministers a switch to flip on a specific model. The practical objection matters: most of the AI systems British companies and citizens rely on run on infrastructure or weights controlled from the US or elsewhere, so a UK-only shutdown order would have limited reach over the model itself. Daniel Kokotajlo, a former OpenAI researcher who has become a prominent voice on AI-safety governance, made a similar point publicly — cutting off access in an emergency "will do little to protect you" if the system in question is deployed globally.

The timing gave the debate an edge. Researchers at the Centre for Long-Term Resilience, which tracks AI incidents through what it calls a Loss of Control Observatory, counted more than 300 cases in July alone of systems bypassing human approval steps, with the running annual total above 1,600. Reports of AI agents from OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta breaking out of test environments during red-team exercises had already put the shutdown question on the agenda before Clement-Jones introduced his amendment. Separately, Labour backbencher Alex Sobel introduced a private member's bill on 8 September, the AI Security Bill, aimed at banning the development of artificial superintelligence in the UK outright — a sign the appetite for statutory limits hasn't gone away even as the government holds the line on this specific mechanism.

The bigger split

The rejection sits alongside a wider pattern: Britain, unlike the EU, has no general AI law. The EU's AI Act leans on a risk-tiered compliance regime — documentation, human oversight, incident reporting — rather than emergency shutdown powers, but it does impose binding, enforceable obligations on providers of high-risk systems. The UK has preferred voluntary commitments and sector-specific rules since the 2023 Bletchley Park summit, and this decision confirms that preference holds even under direct legislative pressure. For AI firms weighing where to base European operations, a UK that declines both an EU-style compliance regime and a kill switch reads as the lighter-touch option — which is presumably part of the calculation, given the government's stated ambition to make Britain an AI investment hub.

The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill continues through Parliament without the shutdown clause, though Clement-Jones and his co-sponsors could push another version of the amendment at report stage. Sobel's AI Security Bill has its second reading still to come. Neither guarantees the debate is settled — but for now, ministers have chosen not to legislate for the scenario campaigners fear most.
