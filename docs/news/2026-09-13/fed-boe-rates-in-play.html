Fed and BoE rate decisions loom as hike bets creep back

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, whose hawkish Jackson Hole remarks reversed rate-cut expectations within days. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech pushed the odds of a Fed rate hike this week from roughly 56% to 85.5% in two weeks, upending a market that had priced in cuts.

Eight weeks ago, futures markets had all but written off a Federal Reserve rate rise this year. A weak July jobs report had pushed traders toward bets on cuts, not hikes. Then Kevin Warsh walked up to the podium at Jackson Hole.

"Summer inflation readings were better than expected, but they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved."

That one line from the Fed chair's August 28 speech at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium did more to move markets than months of data releases. Within days, the probability of a quarter-point hike at this week's meeting — bringing the federal funds rate to a range of 3.75-4.00% from its current 3.50-3.75% — jumped from roughly 56% to over 66%. By September 12, with the Fed's decision due Wednesday, CME's FedWatch tool put the odds at 85.5%.

From dissent to consensus, in reverse

The reversal is notable partly for its speed and partly for what it undoes. At the Fed's July 29 meeting, the committee held rates steady, with the debate largely framed around dissenting voices who wanted to see rates already moving down. Warsh, who took over as the 17th Fed chair in May, used Jackson Hole to reframe that debate entirely — recommitting to the 2% PCE inflation target and declining to rule out further tightening if disinflation continues to stall. Traders took the hint. A market that had been pricing an easier path for the rest of 2026 is now pricing the opposite, with the Fed's two-day meeting concluding Wednesday, September 16, at 2pm Eastern.

The timing matters. A shift this large this close to a live decision leaves little room for the Fed to disappoint whichever side of the market it now faces — a hike would validate hawks who moved early; a hold would strand traders who piled into positions on the strength of one speech.

The fuel problem underneath

Warsh's hawkishness has an obvious trigger: fuel. Energy costs have stayed elevated even as broader disinflation has proceeded, widening the gap between headline and core inflation readings and undercutting the Fed's preferred narrative that price pressures are fading on their own. Oil prices near $100 a barrel, driven in part by the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz, have kept transport and heating costs sticky in a way that core services inflation alone can't explain away. For a central bank trying to declare victory on inflation, a fuel bill that won't cooperate is an awkward complication — and one reason Warsh's speech leaned on underlying trends rather than headline prints.

Not just a Fed story

The pivot in Washington is arriving alongside a broader turn away from the easy-money era. The European Central Bank has already moved, lifting its rate to 2.65% as eurozone inflation ran hotter than expected. In London, the Bank of England — which meets a day after the Fed, on September 17 — has held its base rate at 3.75% since July, but gilt markets are behaving as though further tightening, not cuts, is the live risk. The 10-year gilt yield is sitting near 5.1%, with the 30-year at 5.93%, close to levels last seen in 2007. Yields spiked as high as 5.295% earlier this month on the same oil-driven inflation concerns squeezing the Fed, and have shown little sign of retreating since, with investors largely holding position until both central banks report.

Taken together, the pattern is one central banks spent a decade unlearning: rates that go up, not down. Algorithmic and systematic strategies built through years of falling-rate assumptions are being forced to recalibrate at the least convenient moment — in the days immediately before two of the world's most closely watched policy decisions land almost back to back.

What's next

The Fed announces Wednesday at 2pm Eastern; the Bank of England follows Thursday at noon London time. Whichever way the Fed goes, the size of the market's repositioning since Jackson Hole means the reaction — in rates, in the dollar, in equities pricing on the assumption of easier policy — is likely to be sharper than the 25 basis points nominally in play.
