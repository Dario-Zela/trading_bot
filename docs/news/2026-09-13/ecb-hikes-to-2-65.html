ECB raises rates to 2.65% as eurozone inflation runs hot

Christine Lagarde, President of the European Central Bank, which raised rates 25bp to 2.65% on 10 September 2026. — Lemrich / European Central Bank
In one sentence
The ECB hiked rates a quarter point to 2.65% as inflation hit 3.3%, but Lagarde flagged downside growth risks alongside the upside inflation risk — leaving the next move genuinely open.

The European Central Bank raised its key interest rates for the second time since the Iran war began, lifting the deposit rate a quarter point to 2.50% and the main refinancing rate to 2.65%. The move was unanimous, fully priced in by markets beforehand, and — in President Christine Lagarde's words — "a no-brainer." What was less straightforward was the message that came with it: a central bank simultaneously worried that inflation is running too hot and that growth is running too cold.

"The decision that we took today, which was, by the way, a unanimous decision, was a no-brainer," Lagarde told reporters in Frankfurt on 10 September. But pressed on where rates go next, she declined to give any steer. "We are not taking a view as to which direction we go at our next meeting," she said, repeating that the Governing Council would proceed "meeting by meeting."

An inflation problem with a familiar cause

Eurozone inflation hit 3.3% in August, its highest reading in almost three years, up from 2.9% in July. The proximate cause is energy: prices in that category rose 14.3% year-on-year, up from 10.3% the month before, as the fighting around the Strait of Hormuz continues to disrupt Gulf supply and keeps oil hovering near $100 a barrel. Core inflation, which strips out energy and food, actually eased slightly to 2.4% from 2.5% — a detail that matters, because it suggests the price pressure is concentrated in one channel rather than broadening across the economy.

That distinction shaped the ECB's own updated projections, released alongside the decision. Staff now expect headline inflation to peak at 3.6% in the fourth quarter of this year before easing to 3.0% for 2026 as a whole, 2.5% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028 — still above target for years, but trending the right way once the energy shock washes out of the annual comparison. All 65 economists polled by Reuters in the days before the meeting had forecast exactly this outcome: a 25-basis-point hike, no surprises on the headline number.

The growth caveat

What distinguished this meeting from July's was the explicit hedging on growth. "The risks to the inflation outlook are to the upside... due in particular to the Middle East conflict," Lagarde said, "[while] the risks to the growth outlook are to the downside... Renewed disruption of energy supplies could cause energy prices to rise further." It is an unusual position for a central bank to occupy: raising rates to fight inflation while flagging that the same shock driving that inflation could also tip growth lower.

For now, the data hasn't forced the ECB's hand. Staff lifted their 2026 growth forecast to 0.9%, with 1.4% penciled in for 2027 and 1.5% for 2028 — figures Lagarde described as sitting "at the top of the range" of outside forecasts, helped by German defence and infrastructure spending and a labour market that has held up better than expected. "We have been surprised by the resilience of our economy," she said. The caveat is that resilience has a shelf life if energy costs keep climbing, and the ECB's own statement acknowledges as much.

Markets read the fine print

The rate move itself barely moved the euro — EUR/USD dipped modestly and steadied near 1.1610, unsurprising given the hike was fully baked into pricing beforehand. The more interesting market reaction is in rate expectations for December and beyond, where the absence of forward guidance leaves traders to price a genuine two-way bet rather than a well-signposted third hike. Lagarde was also asked about elevated government bond yields across the euro area, and was careful to frame it as a global phenomenon rather than a currency bloc-specific stress: yields are high for "multiple causes," she said, and are "not a euro-specific issue... happening across the world."

For trading desks running EUR exposure, the practical takeaway is that the easy read — ECB hiking, buy the euro — no longer holds cleanly. A central bank that won't commit to a direction is a central bank that could just as easily pause or reverse if the Hormuz standoff eases and growth data disappoints, which raises the cost of hedging any position built on continued tightening.

What comes next

The Governing Council's next scheduled decision falls in late October, by which point another month of inflation and PMI data — plus whatever happens to the Hormuz standoff and the wider Gulf energy picture — will have arrived. Lagarde's refusal to pre-commit means that data, more than any pre-set path, will determine whether this is the last hike of the cycle or merely the second of several.
