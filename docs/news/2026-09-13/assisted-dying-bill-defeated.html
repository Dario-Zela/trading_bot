MPs vote down assisted dying bill by narrow margin

The House of Commons chamber, where MPs voted 286-270 against the revived assisted dying bill — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
MPs rejected the revived assisted dying bill 286-270, reversing last year's 314-291 majority, after a Parliament Acts gambit drove abstentions rather than conversions.

The House of Commons has, on rare occasions, allowed itself to be surprised by its own vote count. Thursday's was one of them. MPs rejected the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill at second reading by 286 votes to 270 — a majority of 16 against — little more than a year after the Commons passed what was substantially the same bill by 314 to 291 at third reading in June 2025.

"The case for law change remains unshakeable, the public mandate remains overwhelming and the direction of travel remains clear," Dignity in Dying said afterwards, promising to keep campaigning. But the arithmetic told a starker story: a policy that had cleared the Commons twice in the previous parliament — first at second reading in November 2024 by 330 votes to 275, then at third reading last June — has now been stopped in its tracks.

What actually flipped

The reversal was not, on the numbers, a mass conversion. Analysis of the voting lists shows just five MPs who backed the bill in 2025 switched to oppose it this time, and none moved the other way. What decided the vote was turnout: 43 previous supporters did not vote at all on Thursday, against only 15 lapsed opponents. A bill that depends on a coalition of the mildly persuaded is fragile precisely because abstention costs it more than opposition does.

Labour's own numbers illustrate the drift. Around 190 Labour MPs backed the bill this time, down from roughly 234 in 2025, while 153 opposed it, up from 147. Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who took office in July after succeeding Keir Starmer, did not vote — a decision his office said was meant to avoid "unduly influencing the debate" on what the government has treated throughout as a conscience issue, free of the whip.

A procedural gambit that backfired

The bill's return was itself the tell. After the original legislation, sponsored by Kim Leadbeater, passed the Commons last year only to stall in the House of Lords before the session ended, its backers — this time led by Labour MP Lauren Edwards — brought back an almost identical text rather than starting from scratch. The calculation was that a second successive Commons majority for the same bill would let supporters eventually invoke the Parliament Acts, which allow the Commons to override a recalcitrant Lords without further amendment.

That move seems to have cost votes rather than banked them. Many MPs who had reservations about the Lords' handling of the bill read the re-run as an attempt to sideline scrutiny rather than secure it, and several who opposed it said so explicitly. "MPs have voted against this Bill because it wasn't an abstract debate but a decision about a real Bill that was unsafe and unworkable," a group of opponents said in a joint statement. Edwards pushed back afterwards, arguing that "the objections raised today were not about the principle of assisted dying but about parliamentary process" — in effect conceding that the procedural strategy, not the underlying policy, had decided the outcome.

An old argument, on the same date

The defeat fell, coincidentally, eleven years to the day after the Commons last rejected assisted-dying legislation outright — a 2015 bill voted down 330 to 118. Between those two dates lies a decade in which public opinion polling has consistently shown large majorities in favour of a law change, and in which the Commons itself twice signalled it agreed. Thursday's vote does not reverse that public mood so much as expose how thin and contingent the parliamentary majority for translating it into law had become.

For campaigners, the practical consequence is that the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill is now dead for this parliamentary session, and any revival would need to start over rather than lean on the Parliament Acts shortcut. For MPs on both sides, the more durable lesson may be about coalition management on conscience votes generally: a majority built on marginal, mildly-supportive members can evaporate through absence alone, without a single mind changing.

The next marker to watch is whether Dignity in Dying and allied MPs attempt a fresh bill in a future session, and whether they do so with amended safeguards designed to win back the 37 Labour MPs who backed the policy in 2025 but could not be brought to the lobby this time.
