Shell sells $715m New England energy stake to Constellation

A natural gas-fired combined-cycle power plant, illustrative of the RISEC facility Shell is selling to Constellation Energy. — Peoplepoweredbyenergy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Shell is selling its 609MW Rhode Island gas plant to Constellation for $715m while buying smaller Pennsylvania plants, trading scale for return.

Shell has agreed to sell its stake in a New England gas-fired power plant to Constellation Energy for $715 million, the latest in a run of deals reshuffling who owns America's electricity generation as data centres and AI infrastructure push demand higher.

The asset is RISEC Holdings, owner of the Rhode Island State Energy Center in Johnston — a 609-megawatt combined-cycle plant that has been the state's largest single source of electricity since it came online in 2002. It runs on natural gas, burns cleaner and more efficiently than older single-cycle turbines, and sells its output and capacity into the ISO New England wholesale market. Constellation will pay the full $715 million in cash, though the effective outlay is closer to $580 million once roughly $135 million in first-year tax benefits are counted. The deal is expected to close in the first quarter of 2027, pending regulatory sign-off.

Why Constellation wants it

Constellation, the largest nuclear operator in the United States, has spent the past year buying its way deeper into the fossil and geothermal generation businesses it once did without — most notably through its $16.4 billion acquisition of Calpine, completed in January, which added roughly 23 gigawatts of gas and geothermal capacity and pushed the combined company's fleet above 60 gigawatts. Chief executive Joe Dominguez framed RISEC in similarly practical terms: the plant sits "on both the electric grid and the natural gas pipeline system," giving Constellation a dispatchable asset it can lean on as its New England customer business — increasingly built around long-term power contracts with hyperscale tech firms — keeps growing. Constellation says the purchase will be immediately accretive to operating earnings, with returns above its 10% unlevered hurdle rate, and won't touch the $5 billion share buyback programme it has running through 2027.

Shell's side of the trade

For Shell, the sale is less a retreat from U.S. power than a rebalancing. In the same announcement, the company said a subsidiary would buy 100% of Hunlock Creek Generating in Pennsylvania — two gas plants totalling 169 megawatts, including a 44-megawatt peaker that earns revenue through capacity markets — in a deal Shell expects to clear its own internal return threshold. Chief executive Wael Sawan has spent his tenure trimming assets that don't earn their keep: this year alone Shell has also sold Jiffy Lube in the U.S., its SPRNG renewables business in India, and marketing operations in South Africa. The RISEC-for-Hunlock Creek swap fits the same logic — trade a large, low-growth New England asset for a smaller one with a clearer return profile, while keeping a foothold in U.S. gas-fired generation and power trading rather than exiting it outright.

What it signals

Taken together, the two deals are a tidy illustration of where capital is moving in U.S. power markets. Constellation is scaling up to sell firm, dispatchable capacity to data-centre operators and industrial customers who need power now, not decades from now — hence its appetite for gas and nuclear assets with existing grid and pipeline connections. Shell, by contrast, is optimising a trading-and-generation book rather than building market share, happy to cycle out of a large plant if the price is right and a smaller one pencils out better. Neither move is a bet against natural gas or New England power demand; both are bets on which balance sheet is better suited to hold a given asset.

The next marker will be regulatory approval, expected ahead of the Q1 2027 close — worth watching given how closely New England's grid operator and state regulators track ownership concentration in a region already tight on both gas pipeline and generation capacity.
