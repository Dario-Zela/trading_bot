MARKETS · EV SUPPLY CHAIN · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-13 · 3 min read

Europe's battery makers keep losing ground to Chinese rivals

Northvolt's Ett gigafactory near Skellefteå, Sweden, during construction in 2024 — before the company's 2025 bankruptcy and sale to US startup Lyten. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Chinese firms now supply over 70% of global EV batteries as Europe's own champions — Northvolt, ACC — collapse or get sold off.

Europe set out to build a homegrown battery industry that would power its electric-vehicle transition and keep the value chain on the continent. Instead it is watching that industry hollow out, plant by plant, while Chinese manufacturers consolidate a lead that is now closer to unassailable than merely large.

The numbers are stark. In the first half of 2026, CATL alone held 39.9% of the global EV battery market, with BYD adding another 14.4% — meaning two Chinese firms now supply more than half the world's EV batteries between them, according to data from CnEVPost. Widen the lens to the top ten global suppliers and seven are Chinese, controlling 72.6% of the market. Europe, by contrast, has no manufacturer in the same league.

The casualties list keeps growing

Northvolt, once Europe's best-funded battery hope, filed for bankruptcy in March 2025. Its remaining Swedish, Polish and German assets — including the Skellefteå gigafactory and the Västerås R&D hub — were bought in February 2026 by Lyten, a US lithium-sulfur startup backed by Stellantis and FedEx, for a fraction of the roughly $5bn once invested. Lyten has pencilled in a restart of cell production for the second half of 2026, but the episode confirmed that Europe's flagship independent cell-maker no longer exists as a European company.

ACC — the Stellantis, Mercedes-Benz and TotalEnergies joint venture once planning three gigafactories worth €7.6bn — fared little better. Construction at its Kaiserslautern site in Germany, part of a €1.8bn project that had drawn €437m in German federal and state subsidies, was frozen in May 2024 and permanently abandoned in February 2026, alongside a twin project in Termoli, Italy. ACC cited slower-than-expected EV demand and rising cost pressure across the value chain. Industry researchers estimate Europe has lost roughly 700 gigawatt-hours of announced gigafactory capacity in eighteen months.

Why Chinese cells stay cheaper

The underlying problem is cost, not capital. Chinese battery packs averaged $84 per kWh in 2025, against roughly $131 in Europe — a gap of about $41–43 per kWh, or some 35% cheaper, according to analysis from Transport & Environment. Manufacturing efficiency, built on a decade of mass-production experience and highly automated lines, accounts for more than 40% of that gap on its own. T&E projects the gap could narrow to around $14 per kWh by 2030, but only if European output actually scales — the same condition that has repeatedly failed to hold.

Meanwhile Chinese producers are simply building inside the tariff wall. CATL and Stellantis broke ground on a €4.1bn, 50-GWh lithium-iron-phosphate plant in Zaragoza, Spain, targeting production by the end of 2026 — a joint venture that gives Stellantis cheaper cells but does nothing to create an independent European supplier.

Brussels reaches for policy

The European Commission's response, the Industrial Accelerator Act unveiled on 4 March 2026, proposes "Made in EU" content requirements for batteries and a Battery Booster Facility to de-risk gigafactory financing, alongside earlier moves such as CINEA's €643m in funding agreements signed with five project sponsors this year. Whether phased local-content rules can be enforced without breaching trade law — or without simply raising costs for European carmakers already losing ground on price — is now under negotiation between Parliament and the Council.

The next marker is the Zaragoza plant's scheduled start-up at the end of this year, alongside progress reports on the Industrial Accelerator Act as it moves through EU legislative process. Until either delivers, Europe's battery sector remains a policy ambition propped up by Chinese capital and Chinese cells.
