Student loan repayment terms to be clarified in England

Dame Meg Hillier, chair of the Treasury Committee, whose inquiry found student loan marketing 'amounted to mis-selling' — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
England will rewrite student loan guidance after MPs found its marketing to teenagers 'amounted to mis-selling', but a threshold freeze angering graduates stays in place for now.

For more than a decade, the government's pitch to teenagers weighing up university was that a student loan was a manageable thing — repayments pegged to earnings, no more painful, one Department for Education slide suggested, than a £14-a-month phone contract. That comparison is now at the centre of a row over whether ministers misled a generation of borrowers about what they were signing up to.

On Friday the government confirmed it would rewrite the guidance given to prospective students in England, after a Treasury Committee inquiry concluded that the way loans had been marketed to teenagers — more than five million of whom hold Plan 2 loans taken out between 2012 and 2023, worth upwards of £200bn combined — "amounted to mis-selling" in places. The fix on offer is narrow: better information going forward, not compensation or a change to existing terms.

What the committee found

MPs on the committee, chaired by Dame Meg Hillier, found that DfE and Student Loans Company materials routinely compared monthly repayments to small everyday costs — a phone contract, a cinema trip — while omitting a more consequential fact: that the terms of the loan, including interest rates and repayment thresholds, are set by legislation and can be rewritten by any future government at will. Many borrowers, the report said, took out tens of thousands of pounds in debt believing the deal was fixed when it was not.

That omission became a live grievance after November's Budget, when the then chancellor Rachel Reeves froze the Plan 2 repayment threshold at £29,385 for three years from April 2027, rather than letting it rise as borrowers had been led to expect. The freeze effectively increases monthly repayments for graduates earning above that level, at a moment when many are also stretched by rent and the search for a first mortgage.

A narrow fix

The government's response, agreeing with only some of the committee's recommendations, commits to redesigning the guidance shown to new applicants so it states plainly that repayment rules can change and that different career paths lead to very different lifetime costs. It rejected calls to apply Financial Conduct Authority-style consumer-protection standards to loan marketing, to scrap RPI-linked interest, or to split the cost of tuition evenly between graduates and the state. Crucially, it did not commit to reversing the threshold freeze itself.

Hillier welcomed the guidance changes as "an important step forward" but said they do nothing for graduates "facing punitive repayment terms on a loan which keeps growing" while trying to save for a pension or a deposit. Her committee had gone further, telling ministers they had a "moral obligation" to undo the freeze. "Our report is a signal to the Treasury and the Department for Education that this can no longer be ignored," she said. "Patience has run out."

What this episode captures, more than any single number, is how thin the line has worn between explaining a policy and selling one. Loan terms were always changeable — that was true when the scheme launched — but a system that only says so once challenged by a parliamentary inquiry has already lost the argument about trust, whatever it puts in next year's prospectus.

The unresolved question is the freeze itself. Hillier is pressing the chancellor to use the next Budget to give graduates "some much-needed breathing space"; the Treasury says the wider student finance system remains under review but has not ruled a reversal in or out.
