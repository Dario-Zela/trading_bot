5G 'fast lanes' could push up UK phone bills, Telegraph warns

An EE store on a UK high street; EE launched the country's first consumer 5G 'fast lane' network-slicing plan in August. — Hsamtc / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK operators are selling 5G 'fast lane' speed tiers for £3-£5 a month, and Ofcom's net neutrality review will decide how far that pricing can go.

British mobile operators want to sell speed the way airlines sell legroom — pay more, jump the queue. Regulators and consumer groups are asking whether that queue-jumping is going to cost everyone else.

The technology behind it is called network slicing, a feature of 5G Standalone networks that lets an operator partition its infrastructure into virtual lanes, each with guaranteed bandwidth and priority, layered on top of the same physical masts. EE became the first UK operator to sell this directly to consumers and small businesses last month, launching Fast Lane on 20 August after two years of trials at Wembley Stadium, the Bafta Awards and Sail GP in Portsmouth. Customers get dedicated capacity when the network is busiest — for an extra £5 a month via EE's new Full Works Plus plan. Vodafone got there first commercially in April with SuperMobile, promising speeds up to four times faster than standard 5G for an extra £3 a month.

The regulatory fight underneath

What unnerves consumer advocates isn't the £3 or £5 add-ons themselves — those already exist within Ofcom's 2023 net neutrality guidance, which carved out room for "premium retail packages" as long as customers know what they're buying. The bigger question is what operators are asking for next: the right to prioritise specific apps and services, not just specific customers. BT, Vodafone and Virgin Media O2 have all lobbied Ofcom to loosen rules that, as one Virgin Media O2 executive put it, were "developed for a very different era" — roughly two decades ago, before video calls and cloud gaming existed. VMO2 chief executive Lutz Schüler has called the current regime "outdated," arguing it limits innovation and holds back the value of the 5G infrastructure operators have already built.

Ofcom's board opened a fresh consultation on network slicing in June, and it is still gathering responses. Its own monitoring so far has found no major consumer harm, real-world use remains concentrated in enterprise contracts rather than mass-market phone plans. But the direction of travel is what's being contested: campaigners such as Alex Tofts at Broadband Genie warn that letting operators sell priority for specific apps risks creating "commercial gatekeepers" and a two-tier internet, where standard-tier speeds get quietly squeezed to make the premium tier look more attractive by comparison.

Why traders should care

For equity investors, network slicing has been pitched as a genuine new revenue line rather than a rebrand of existing tariffs — analysts at one point put the global addressable market for slicing-based services at tens of billions of pounds, split between enterprise private networks and consumer premium tiers. BT, Vodafone and Three have all built some assumption of margin expansion from these services into medium-term guidance, on the logic that 5G capital spending finally gets monetised beyond flat-rate data bundles.

That assumption depends on Ofcom leaving room to price for priority. Matthew Howett of Assembly Research, who tracks the sector, is sympathetic to operators' basic argument — that networks capable of guaranteeing quality should be able to charge for it — but he's also blunt that what's on sale today looks more like an airline baggage allowance than a genuine service tier: a modest add-on rather than the kind of differentiated product that would justify a re-rating. If Ofcom's consultation lands on tighter restrictions, or if take-up of Fast Lane and SuperMobile disappoints, the margin case for slicing shrinks back toward marketing exercise, and any premium currently priced into UK telecom valuations for it would need to come out.

What's next

Ofcom has not set a firm deadline for concluding this year's net neutrality review, but a decision — or at least a clearer statement of intent — is expected before the next annual monitoring report, normally published in February. Investors in BT Group, Vodafone and Telefónica-backed Virgin Media O2 will be watching for early subscriber numbers on Fast Lane and SuperMobile as the first real test of whether UK consumers will actually pay for a faster lane, or whether operators end up selling a feature nobody notices missing.
