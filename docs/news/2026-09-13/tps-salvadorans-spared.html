Trump administration spares Salvadorans from TPS termination

President Trump hosts El Salvador's President Nayib Bukele in the Oval Office — a relationship advocates say helped Salvadorans avoid TPS termination. — Daniel Torok / Official White House Photo (public domain)
In one sentence
The Trump administration quietly let a deadline to end TPS for ~200,000 Salvadorans pass, even as it pressed ahead with cuts for Haitians and Syrians.

Roughly 200,000 Salvadorans who have spent up to a quarter-century building lives in the United States under Temporary Protected Status got an unusual reprieve this week: nothing happened. The Trump administration let a September 9 deadline to terminate their protections pass without action, telling U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services that a decision would come "at the appropriate time" and that, until then, TPS holders retain their status and work permits.

That quiet non-decision stands out because it is so out of step with the administration's pattern. Since January 2025, the Department of Homeland Security has moved to end Temporary Protected Status for more than a million people across 14 countries, including Venezuela, Honduras, Afghanistan and, most recently, roughly 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians — terminations the Supreme Court cleared DHS to carry out in a June ruling. Salvadorans, the single largest TPS population and one of the longest-standing, are for now the exception.

A hardline agenda with a soft spot

The administration has not explained the divergence, and DHS's public language on El Salvador undercuts any obvious rationale. The department's own prior assessments cited continuing food insecurity, a weak macroeconomic environment and lingering damage from the 2001 earthquakes — the same kind of "conditions have improved" framing used to justify ending protections elsewhere. Nothing in that record explains why El Salvador gets more time while Haiti and Syria do not.

What does explain it, more plausibly, is pressure from industries that depend on Salvadoran labor. TPS holders from El Salvador are heavily concentrated in construction, food processing, hospitality and transportation — sectors already straining under labor shortages. Foreign-born workers make up more than a third of the residential construction trades, according to the National Association of Home Builders, which flagged the September deadline to its members as a live business risk. The American Business Immigration Coalition and hospital and eldercare groups lobbied along similar lines. Roughly 90% of Salvadoran TPS holders are in the workforce, contributing an estimated $5.4 billion a year to U.S. GDP and $1.5 billion in taxes, by immigration-advocacy groups' figures — numbers that translate directly into payroll gaps and project delays if the status lapses.

The Bukele factor

Diplomacy is the other obvious variable. El Salvador's president, Nayib Bukele, has made himself one of Trump's most useful allies, opening the CECOT mega-prison to planeloads of U.S. deportees the administration has accused — not always accurately, subsequent reporting found — of gang ties. Bukele reportedly raised the TPS question directly with Trump during a July visit to the White House. Salvadorans in the U.S. sent home $9.9 billion in remittances last year, equivalent to about a quarter of El Salvador's GDP, giving Bukele his own stake in keeping the arrangement alive. Haiti and Syria have no comparable relationship with Washington, nor a leader positioned to ask a favor in person.

What it signals

Neither explanation is officially confirmed, and DHS has been careful to frame this as a delay rather than a reversal — Salvadoran advocates note the administration has still not committed to extending TPS beyond the current pause. But the pattern is instructive for reading the rest of the administration's immigration agenda: campaign rhetoric about ending TPS broadly has run into two concrete constraints — organized business lobbying in labor-tight sectors, and bilateral relationships the administration values. Where both are present, enforcement slows. Where neither is, it proceeds on schedule, as Haitians and Syrians have discovered.

The next marker is whatever DHS means by "the appropriate time." Until Secretary Kristi Noem's department issues a formal redesignation or termination notice, El Salvador's roughly 200,000 TPS holders remain in the same legal limbo they have occupied for weeks — able to work, unable to plan much beyond it.
