Barclays lifts S&P 500 target as valuations cool and ETF inflows pick up

The New York Stock Exchange facade in Lower Manhattan, where the S&P 500's earnings-driven rally has kept strategists raising targets. — Jeffrey Zeldman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Barclays lifted its S&P 500 target to 7,950 on earnings strength, and ETF buyers are following — a bet history says gets harder once a downturn actually hits.

Barclays raised its year-end 2026 target for the S&P 500 to 7,950, up from 7,800, in a note that credited a "standout earnings season led by Tech." The 150-point bump — a little over 4% above where the index was trading when the call went out — arrived the same week Vanguard's flagship index fund pulled in tens of billions of dollars from investors who apparently agree the market still has room to run.

Taken together, the two data points describe the same bet: that corporate earnings can keep growing fast enough to justify prices that no longer look cheap, or that, on one narrow measure, now look almost reasonable. History is not entirely on that bet's side.

The upgrade

Barclays strategist Venu Krishna lifted the bank's 2026 EPS estimate to $365 a share from $337, and pushed the 2027 estimate to $414 from $389, while leaving the 2027 index target unchanged at 8,800. The bank's bull case for 2026 sits at 8,350; its bear case at 6,750 — a spread wide enough to acknowledge real uncertainty even as the headline number moves up.

The justification is concrete. Big Tech earnings grew 35% year-on-year in the second quarter, accelerating from 30% the quarter before, while the rest of the technology sector posted an 88% jump. Krishna's own arithmetic makes the mechanism explicit: assuming 30.8% EPS growth in 2026 and a price-to-earnings multiple of 21.8 times, "earnings do most of the heavy lifting in our target increase." In other words, Barclays isn't calling for investors to pay more for each dollar of profit — it's betting the profits keep showing up.

Money following the multiple

The flows back that story up, at least on the surface. The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) has absorbed roughly $131 billion in 2026, closing in on the $137 billion annual record it set the year before, and investors added $43.6 billion over a single trailing 30-day stretch even as the fund dipped and pundits warned of an overdue selloff, according to Bloomberg's Eric Balchunas. A large share of that money is structural — VOO's 0.03% expense ratio and its default status in workplace retirement plans mean most of it arrives on autopilot rather than conviction, and roughly nine in ten Fidelity plan participants made no allocation changes at all through last year's volatility.

What has changed is the entry price. VOO's forward price-to-earnings ratio has slipped to about 19.1, a touch below its five-year average of 19.8, according to FactSet data reported by Benzinga. That is not a deep discount — the index remains close to its ten-year average multiple of roughly 19 — but after two years of the "expensive market" narrative, a valuation that has drifted back to historical norms is itself notable, and it dovetails neatly with Barclays' argument that the market isn't asking investors to overpay for the earnings growth it expects.

The catch

The entire framework rests on earnings holding up through whatever macro turbulence 2026 brings, and that is the part with the weaker track record. In a typical postwar recession, S&P 500 earnings have fallen 25–30% from peak to trough; in the 2007-09 downturn the decline reached 57%, from roughly $91 a share to under $40. Barclays' own $365 EPS estimate for next year implies growth of nearly a third from current levels — the opposite of what earnings have historically done once a downturn actually bites.

None of this means the call is wrong. Tech earnings have beaten expectations for two consecutive quarters, AI-linked capital spending shows no sign of slowing, and the industrial backdrop Krishna cites is real. But a forecast built on earnings resilience is only as good as the assumption that this cycle behaves differently from the last several. Barclays is one bank among many still raising targets on the same logic; whether that becomes consensus, or a warning sign, will be visible in the Q3 earnings season and in how the Federal Reserve's coming rate decision shapes the growth outlook it's all resting on.
