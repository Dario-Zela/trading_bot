Charlie Kirk killing fuels wave of neighbour-on-neighbour doxing

Charlie Kirk speaking at a Turning Point USA campus event in April 2024, months before his assassination at Utah Valley University. — Shoot for the Stars / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A year after Charlie Kirk's killing, doxing campaigns have cost over 600 Americans their jobs, $2m in settlements — and some say they'd post it again.

A year on, the ledger from Charlie Kirk's assassination is still being tallied — not in courtrooms weighing a murder charge, but in HR offices, group chats and small towns where someone finally worked out who posted what.

Kirk, the 31-year-old founder of Turning Point USA, was shot in the neck and killed on 10 September 2025 while debating students at Utah Valley University in Orem. Tyler Robinson, then 22, was charged with the killing. What followed was not confined to the manhunt. Within hours, a parallel campaign had begun online to identify and punish anyone who seemed to celebrate, joke about, or simply decline to mourn his death — and a year later, that campaign's aftershocks are still working their way through American workplaces and courts.

A database, a rebrand, a shutdown

The clearinghouse for this effort was a site that launched under the name "Expose Charlie's Murderers" and was quickly rebranded the "Charlie Kirk Data Foundation." By its final update on 14 September, it claimed 63,648 submissions of people it said were "happy about an innocent man's death." Prominent accounts amplified the hunt — Libs of TikTok, with its 4.4 million followers on X, was among the loudest — and Vice-President JD Vance urged supporters to go further than naming names: "call their employer," he said.

The site did not survive long. Its registrar, Epik, pulled it down after determining it had been registered with false information and had drawn "verifiable DDoS threats." By then the damage was largely done: names, employers and, in some cases, home addresses had already circulated. Ryan Fournier, national chair of Students for Trump, had to walk back an accusation against a Wisconsin elementary school teacher after acting on an unverified tip — one of several cases of mistaken identity. Ali Nasrati, a Walmart IT technician wrongly linked to a mocking post, was suspended with pay after threats sent his mother and sister fleeing their home. The accused shooter, notably, came from a Republican-leaning family with no apparent ties to the political left — a detail that undercut the premise much of the campaign was built on.

Universities became a particular flashpoint. Legal scholars tracking the fallout counted 37 faculty and staff members identified as harassment targets, with at least 24 terminated, suspended or placed on leave — at Clemson, Auburn, Wake Forest, the University of South Carolina, East Tennessee State and elsewhere. Republican members of Congress, including Senator Lindsey Graham and Representatives Russell Fry and Nancy Mace, publicly pressed for firings; President Trump reposted calls to defund Clemson. "The size of the activity, the pressure campaign and the short period of time is highly unusual," said Keith Whittington, a Yale Law scholar who studies academic freedom.

"I don't regret what I said"

What distinguishes this episode from earlier social-media pile-ons is what has happened since — both the scale of the punishment and the defiance of some who were punished. Researchers tracking the fallout put the number of Americans fired or disciplined over Kirk-related comments above 600, with roughly $2 million paid out in legal settlements as of this year and at least 14 federal First Amendment lawsuits filed against public employers.

Some of those settlements have been substantial. Austin Peay State University reinstated professor Darren Michael and paid $500,000 to resolve his claims. The University of Tennessee agreed to pay $1.9 million to anthropologist Tamar Shirinian over a post in which she said the country was "better off" without Kirk. Smaller settlements — $835,000, $485,000, $225,000, $125,000 — have gone to a Tennessee retiree who was wrongly charged with making threats, a Florida biologist, a Ball State administrator and an Iowa public defender.

What has struck reporters revisiting the story a year later is how many of those who lost jobs refuse to recant. One woman told the BBC she didn't regret the sentiment behind her post: "I am not sad. And I refuse to be made to feel like I have to be sad that someone who was so hateful was killed." Another said simply that it's "important to push back on things that are steeped in hatred," referring to Kirk's rhetoric on race and immigration. The writer Gretchen Felker-Martin, who lost a DC Comics book deal over her posts, has likewise stood by her comments. Rutgers historian Mark Bray, branded "Dr Antifa" by campus activists despite denying any organisational ties, ultimately left the country for Spain rather than continue fighting the harassment — a quieter, sadder form of the same refusal to apologise on demand.

The neighbour problem

What researchers who study online harassment say makes this wave distinct is not its politics but its intimacy. "Your neighbour found you," said Shauna Dillavou, chief executive of the digital-safety group Brightlines, describing a pattern in which tips came not from strangers trawling the internet but from people who recognised a colleague, a former classmate, a member of their own church or homeowners' association. Kat Green of the digital-rights group Endora said multiple copycat sites tried to replicate the original database's model even after it went dark. Shannon Hiller, of Princeton's Bridging Divides Initiative, has argued that amplification from elected officials — not just anonymous trolls — is what gave the campaign its speed and reach, collapsing the distance between an offhand post and a call to an employer's HR department.

The practice eventually lost momentum, partly through legal pushback and partly, researchers note, because the internet's meme culture overtook the tracking effort — the same mechanism of virality that built the databases went on to bury them in noise. But the underlying capability — thousands of strangers crowdsourcing a target's identity and workplace within hours — has not gone away, and free-speech lawyers expect it to resurface at the next flashpoint, left or right.

What happens next runs mostly through the courts. Discovery in several of the pending First Amendment suits against public universities is expected to produce internal records showing how administrators decided who to fire and how fast — testimony that will shape whether "political pressure campaign" becomes a recognised defence, or a liability, the next time a killing turns into a hunt for its critics.
