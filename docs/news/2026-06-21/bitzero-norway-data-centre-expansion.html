Bitzero pushes ahead with 110 MW Norway data centre as transformer foundations go in

In one sentence
Bitzero is pouring transformer foundations for its 110 MW Norway campus, backed by a $2.6 B lease letter with GPU operator OneQode — but the definitive contract isn't signed yet.

Construction at Bitzero Holdings' Namsskogan campus in Norway's Trøndelag region is advancing to where you can see it from the ground. Foundations for two 60 MW transformers are in, with the hardware itself due for delivery in September. The regional grid operator is simultaneously expanding its substation with Siemens GIS switchgear to handle the load once the facility comes online.

Bitzero, which listed on Nasdaq on June 9 under the ticker AIBZ, chose Namsskogan for reasons that are straightforwardly physical. The site sits beside the Tunnsjødal Hydroelectric Power Station — the largest in central Norway — and draws power at roughly one US cent per kilowatt-hour, which runs 40–60% below what operators pay in Germany or France. In an industry where electricity is the largest single cost, that spread is real money. The climate handles much of the cooling work for free.

The lease that still needs a contract

The commercial context shifted in May, when Bitzero signed a binding letter agreement with OneQode Networks — a Singapore-based high-performance cloud provider — for a 15-year lease of the full 110 MW. The headline numbers are large: roughly US$2.6 billion in total revenue over the lease term, and an estimated US$151 million in annual net operating income at full occupancy, implying an 85% NOI margin. OneQode, which earlier this year announced a partnership with AMD to deploy Instinct MI355X GPUs at scale, is targeting the Namsskogan site for AI training, inference, and sovereign AI contracts, with service delivery planned for the first half of 2027. As OneQode's chief executive Matthew Shearing put it when the letter was announced: "The AI market's bottleneck is no longer just GPUs — it's power, network, cooling, land, and deployment capability."

The caveat is worth stating plainly: a binding letter is not a definitive contract. The deal remains subject to technical and legal due diligence, finalised data-hall designs, regulatory consents, and credit support arrangements. None of that is unusual for a transaction of this size — but it means the revenue figures, while plausible, are contingent. Bitzero itself is still loss-making and has carried going-concern disclosures in its recent financial history. The Nasdaq listing improves access to capital markets; whether it narrows the gap between letter and executed lease is the operational question the market is currently pricing.

For context, Bitzero is building into a Norway data center market that has attracted serious capital from larger players. Microsoft committed US$6.2 billion to Norwegian AI infrastructure last year. A consortium backing "Stargate Norway" is planning a 100,000-GPU facility entirely on hydropower. Bitzero is not a hyperscaler, but the power economics that draw the big names are the same ones underpinning the Namsskogan site — this is capacity expansion into demonstrated demand, not a strategic pivot.

The next concrete milestone is transformer delivery in September. If the equipment arrives on schedule and the substation upgrade completes alongside it, the facility moves from foundations to operational infrastructure. The definitive OneQode lease, once executed, converts a promising letter into a bankable revenue stream — and clarifies whether this is a growth story or a capitalisation risk.
