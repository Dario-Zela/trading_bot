Bolivia declares state of emergency after two months of road blockades

In one sentence
Bolivia's 50-day road blockade crisis forces a state of emergency, putting $50M/day in damage and the world's second-largest lithium reserve at political risk.

Bolivia's president Rodrigo Paz declared a nationwide state of emergency on Saturday, granting the military authority to clear road blockades that have paralysed the country for fifty days and left seventeen people dead — most of them victims not of direct confrontation but of disrupted medical supply chains.

The crisis was lit in December 2025 when Paz removed a long-standing fuel subsidy as part of an austerity push aimed at shrinking the deficit. What began as farmers protesting a land-mortgage law in April broadened into a nationwide coalition of teachers, miners, transport workers and indigenous groups demanding Paz's resignation. At its peak, protesters erected more than 3,500 roadblocks across Bolivia, severing 67 highways and stranding over 5,000 trucks — economic losses estimated at $50 million per day.

"This is not a state of emergency to restrict people's lives," Paz said Saturday. "It is a state of emergency to give people back their freedom."

The emergency runs ninety days but can be lifted earlier if "violence and threats against the population come to an end," the government said. By Saturday evening, security forces had begun moving to clear blockades around La Paz, El Alto and Cochabamba — the three cities most severely isolated.

Lithium in the crossfire

For commodity investors, the crisis carries weight beyond Bolivia's borders. Bolivia holds an estimated 23 million tonnes of lithium reserves — second only to Argentina globally — concentrated in the Salar de Uyuni, a 10,500-square-kilometre salt flat that has attracted billions in foreign mining interest as global EV demand drives fierce competition for battery materials.

The blockades have not directly halted lithium extraction, which remains at an early development stage compared to neighbouring Chile and Argentina. But the surrounding political disorder — three cabinet resignations, miners detonating explosives near the presidential palace, former president Evo Morales leading a 190-kilometre march into La Paz — raises a question the mining industry has been slow to confront: can Bolivia's frontier lithium assets ever be insulated from the country's chronic political volatility?

Paz came to office last November promising to open Bolivia's mineral wealth to foreign capital, a deliberate break from the resource nationalism that defined the Morales era. That pitch is harder to sustain today. Analytics firm Geomechanics.io noted that the protests have "injected fresh political and security risk" into Salar de Uyuni development specifically, with project schedules and supply logistics already disrupted.

What to watch

The ninety-day emergency window is the near-term test. If security forces reopen highways without triggering a new round of casualties, Paz may stabilise enough to revive his investment agenda. The Bolivian Workers' Center signed a preliminary agreement with the government on June 19 — whether that compact holds is the first signal worth tracking. If it fractures, the crisis could deepen and stretch mining development timelines by months or years.

For EV supply chains already strained on lithium carbonate, Bolivia's upheaval underscores a broader reality: the countries with the largest deposits frequently carry the highest political premium — and that premium just repriced.
