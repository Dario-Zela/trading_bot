European stocks lead global peers as Middle East de-escalation eases stagflation fears

The Frankfurt Stock Exchange, home of the DAX index. European equity markets have staged their strongest relative performance against US peers in months as Middle East ceasefire hopes ease energy risk premiums. — Ank Kumar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
European stocks are outperforming US peers on Middle East de-escalation hopes, but Eurozone data — 3.2% inflation, 0.1% Q1 growth — has yet to confirm the move.

European equity markets are posting their most convincing stretch of outperformance against US peers in years, lifted by hopes that a fragile peace process in the Middle East could release the energy price pressure that has held the eurozone in a slow-growth, high-inflation bind since late winter. The thesis is coherent. The facts, so far, are provisional.

The Stoxx Europe 600 gained 3.9% on April 8 — its best single session since March 2022 — when Washington and Tehran announced a two-week ceasefire conditioned on reopening the Strait of Hormuz. Since that initial deal, and through a 60-day memorandum of understanding signed by both presidents on June 17 in Islamabad, the index has added roughly 1.5% this month while the S&P 500 has lost 1%. The relative swing is modest in absolute terms, but the direction is notable: European equities had been trailing US peers for months since the war began in late February.

The arithmetic is not complicated. Europe imports a large share of its energy through supply chains that run through the Persian Gulf. When Brent crude traded above $110 a barrel at the height of the conflict, that premium fed directly into factory input costs, utility bills, and consumer prices. With Brent now around $80 — down roughly 30% from its recent peak — the calculus is shifting, at least at the margin.

The Energy Equation

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil trade passes, has been only partially reopened since the ceasefire. After the April deal, Iran reportedly charged transit tolls exceeding $1 million per vessel and maintained effective blockade control, with tankers resuming only slowly. The waterway remains operational but fragile.

As of the weekend of June 20-21, Iran threatened again to close the strait over Israeli activity in Lebanon — a dispute over whether Lebanon was included in the original ceasefire terms. Vice President JD Vance, who had been scheduled to lead a new round of US-Iran nuclear and Lebanon talks at the Burgenstock resort in Switzerland, delayed his arrival after Tehran cancelled the meeting. He subsequently arrived and talks were expected to begin in the days following.

The stop-start quality of the peace process is visible in oil markets. Brent rebounded from a brief dip toward $77 when news of the Swiss postponement broke, illustrating how much of the recent price decline rests on expectations rather than settled supply. Marina Zavolock of Morgan Stanley has described the strait's reopening as "only partially reflected in European equities, suggesting the possibility for further index gains" — a note of conditional optimism that cuts both ways.

Stagflation's Arithmetic

The energy story matters for Europe in a way it does not, to the same degree, for the United States, because the eurozone entered this period already flat on its feet. The bloc's economy grew just 0.1% in the first quarter of 2026. Euro area annual inflation reached 3.2% in May — the highest reading since September 2023 — with energy costs up 10.9% year-on-year accounting for most of the overshoot above the ECB's 2% target.

That combination forced the European Central Bank's hand. On June 11, the ECB raised its deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.25%, the first increase since its previous tightening cycle ended in September 2023. The bank's own projections put full-year GDP growth at just 0.8% and headline inflation at an average of 3.0% — a squeeze with no clean exit while energy costs remain elevated.

This is stagflation in its textbook form: weak growth and stubborn inflation pulling policy in opposite directions simultaneously. It is also the specific trap that a genuine de-escalation in the Middle East could unlock. If energy inflation — which accounts for most of the gap between current eurozone CPI and the ECB's target — retreats meaningfully, the central bank could pause its tightening, releasing the brake on business investment and consumer spending. Ulrich Urbahn at Berenberg puts it directly: Europe's outperformance may continue "as long as markets keep pricing in a peace dividend."

A Discount in Waiting

The valuation case for European equities is not new, but it becomes more actionable if the growth picture brightens. The Stoxx Europe 600 currently trades at roughly 15 times forward earnings — a 25% discount to the S&P 500. That gap has persisted for years, partly justified by Europe's lower exposure to high-margin technology firms and its greater sensitivity to energy and trade conditions.

Banks, industrial goods, and media stocks have led the recent advance; energy shares have lagged as investors recalibrate oil price assumptions downward. Meanwhile, Citigroup's economic surprise index — which measures whether incoming data beats or misses forecasts — shows European readings inflecting higher while US data is peaking, a pattern historically correlated with periods of regional equity outperformance.

The counter-argument from the data is plain. Euro area inflation in May rose, not fell. The Islamabad Memorandum is an initial framework, not a final agreement — it launches 60 days of further talks on Iran's nuclear program, sanctions relief, and the possible unfreezing of up to $25 billion in Iranian assets. Previous ceasefire milestones in April and May were followed by repeated violations by both sides. The Strait is not reliably open. None of the structural conditions for sustained eurozone disinflation have yet been confirmed.

Raphael Thuin at Tikehau frames the European case partly in terms of what it avoids rather than what it offers: "When you invest in Europe you are diversifying away from the risk in tech and so we like our exposure to Europe." At 15 times forward earnings, that diversification is cheap — provided the growth side of the stagflation equation actually improves.

What to Watch

The Switzerland talks are the near-term pivot point. Vance arrived at Burgenstock with Lebanon's status and Iran's nuclear enrichment levels as the headline items — the same two unresolved threads that have repeatedly disrupted the peace timeline since April. A durable resolution on Lebanon could remove the Iranian threat to re-close the strait, and a sustained Brent price in the $70–75 range would, by most ECB models, pull eurozone headline inflation back toward target within two to three quarters.

The more immediate data test comes at the end of this month: the June flash CPI print for the eurozone. Any softening in the energy component — reflecting the oil price moves of recent weeks — would give markets the first hard evidence that the stagflation arithmetic is actually shifting, rather than merely being repriced on hope. Until that number lands, the European equity rally is a wager on a peace that has not yet been made.
