Embraer weighs a bet on premium regional jets to challenge the Boeing-Airbus duopoly

A Widerøe Embraer E190-E2 at Helsinki Airport — the launch operator of the E2 family, which Embraer now eyes as the platform for a larger commercial push — Valentin Hintikka / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Embraer's commercial chief confirms airlines are asking for a narrow-body jet — but the Bombardier cautionary tale looms over any decision to bet the company.

There is a gap in the global aviation order book roughly the size of a Boeing 737, and Embraer thinks it fits. Brazil's largest planemaker — third-largest in the world by deliveries — has been fielding unsolicited calls from airline customers asking whether it might, finally, build a proper narrow-body aircraft to compete directly with the Airbus A320 family and the 737 Max.

Arjan Meijer, CEO of Embraer's commercial aviation business, confirmed the inbound interest this month. "We are getting inbounds from customers saying it would be great if you built a narrow-body," he said, adding that any formal decision remains "under constant review." The phrasing is careful — and deliberately so. Meijer has described launching an entirely new aircraft programme as akin to "betting the company" on a single project. Recent history suggests that caution is warranted.

A queue ten years long

Airbus and Boeing between them control approximately 95% of the global commercial jet market. Right now, both are struggling to satisfy the demand they already have. Delivery slots for the A320neo family and the 737 Max stretch out close to a decade, as post-pandemic travel demand collides with a supply chain that was hollowed out during the COVID-19 grounding years and has yet to fully recover. Airlines expanding routes in Asia, Africa, and Latin America are hunting for alternatives wherever they can find them.

Into that gap, Embraer's E195-E2 has been making a quiet case for itself. The largest member of the E-Jet E2 family seats up to 146 passengers in a typical single-class configuration — fewer than the 150-200 typical of an A320 or 737, but with meaningful fuel efficiency gains and, critically, near-term availability. Embraer claims trip costs on the E195-E2 run 22% below those of the A320neo and 24% below the 737-8. Its order backlog reached $32.1 billion in the first quarter of 2026, a sixth consecutive all-time high and more than 50% above the prior year, driven by a 3.0x book-to-bill across the E175 and E2 platforms.

Francisco Gomes Neto, Embraer's chief executive, told the Financial Times that he expects the long-term market for narrow-body aircraft to sustain "three or four" manufacturers rather than the current two. That is the strategic frame the company is working from: not a frontal assault on Boeing and Airbus, but the proposition that a constrained duopoly and three decades of fleet expansion across the emerging world have simply outgrown a two-supplier market.

The ghost of Bombardier

The reason Embraer's executives speak carefully is visible in every Finnair, Air Baltic, and Air Canada Rouge fleet: the Airbus A220. For most of its development life it was the Bombardier CSeries — a premium, fuel-efficient aircraft designed to do precisely what Embraer is now weighing. The CSeries was technically credible. It proved financially unsurvivable.

Development costs swelled to approximately $3.5 billion. Production delays eroded launch customer confidence. Boeing filed dumping complaints that complicated the aircraft's entry into the United States market. By 2018, Bombardier had surrendered a majority stake to Airbus for $1 Canadian. The aircraft became the A220; the manufacturer that built it ceased to exist in any meaningful commercial sense.

Embraer knows this story from the inside. Boeing spent years negotiating a joint venture that would have folded Embraer's commercial arm into an 80%-Boeing-owned entity. In April 2020, Boeing walked away. Embraer was left independent, which proved — in retrospect — a more viable position than absorption.

The lesson the Bombardier episode teaches is not that a third player cannot survive in commercial aviation. It is that the development economics of a modern single-aisle aircraft are so extreme that only a company with exceptional resources — or exceptional backing — can absorb the exposure. A new narrow-body programme would require multi-billion-dollar development investment, FAA and EASA certification campaigns running five to seven years, global supply chain commitments, and enough launch customer orders to make production economics work before a single aircraft rolls out.

What Embraer has to work with

The bull case for Embraer attempting this step is stronger now than in earlier cycles when the company floated similar ambitions. Revenue reached $7.6 billion in 2025, and guidance for 2026 points to $8.2–8.5 billion, with adjusted EBIT margins approaching 9%. The E-Jet E2 programme has accumulated more than 500 firm orders — including 18 E195-E2s recently selected by Finnair for its European fleet renewal, and a June 2026 order extension from lessor Azorra for 15 more with purchase rights for an additional 15. The military C-390 transport provides an engineering base and government relationships that matter when regulatory hurdles arise.

Embraer is also operating in a different geopolitical environment than Bombardier faced. Supply-chain nationalism, reshoring pressure, and the ambitions of Latin American and Asian governments to build domestic industrial capacity have made a credible Brazilian aerospace champion more strategically attractive to potential state backers and anchor customers than it once was.

The risks, however, remain structural. China's COMAC is already producing the C919 narrow-body with export ambitions — adding a third competitor that Embraer would need to distinguish itself from in markets it currently serves. And Pratt & Whitney's geared turbofan engines, which power the E2 family, have experienced combustor degradation across the broader A220 and A320neo fleet, a reminder that even well-designed aircraft can be held hostage to supplier problems outside any manufacturer's control.

For now, Embraer's public position emphasises financial discipline and incremental growth through the existing E2 platform. The more revealing signal is that its commercial aviation chief is openly acknowledging the customer demand — and not dismissing it. The watch items are concrete: whether Embraer secures a capitalisation commitment from Brasília, whether a large airline or lessor offers a letter of intent large enough to anchor a business case, and what the 2027 supply-chain recovery at Boeing and Airbus actually delivers. If the duopoly's queue shortens, the case for a new entrant weakens. If it does not, the inbounds to São José dos Campos will keep coming.
