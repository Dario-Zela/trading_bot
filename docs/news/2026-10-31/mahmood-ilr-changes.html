Mahmood expected to change indefinite leave to remain rules

In one sentence
Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is set to announce changes to settlement rules, but the BBC gives no detail beyond the 2025 proposal to double the ILR wait to ten years.

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is expected to announce changes to the rules on settlement in the UK, according to the BBC. The reporting gives no detail on what those changes are. Until the rules are published, the substance of the announcement is unknown. The context is better established than the news itself.

What is known

Indefinite leave to remain, or ILR, is the status that lets a migrant live in Britain without time limits. It is the usual step before applying for citizenship. For most work-route migrants the qualifying period has been five years.

Mahmood became Home Secretary in September 2025. Later that month she set out plans to "fundamentally reform" settlement in a speech at Labour's party conference. In November 2025 the Home Office published proposals for an "earned settlement" model. Its central feature was to raise the standard qualifying period for ILR from five years to ten.

Reporting on the November proposals described conditions attached to settlement. These included a clean criminal record, a high standard of English, National Insurance contributions, no reliance on welfare, and voluntary work in the community. Some coverage said the new rules could reach people who arrived in recent years, an estimated 1.6 million since 2021, rather than applying only to new arrivals. That retrospective element is the most contested part of the plan.

What is not known

The BBC's description of Mahmood as "set to announce" changes does not say whether the announcement confirms the ten-year baseline, softens it, or sets out transitional protections for people already in the country. Reports from earlier in 2026 suggested the timetable for the changes had slipped, but this article could not confirm a current implementation date or the final shape of the rules from any official source.

That gap matters. A ten-year baseline with shorter routes for higher earners or public-service workers would be a different policy from a flat doubling. The treatment of people who arrived under earlier rules, and whether they are held to the new test, will determine how many people are affected and how large any legal challenge is likely to be.

Why it carries weight

Settlement rules decide when migrants gain permanent security of residence, access to most benefits and a path to citizenship. They also sit at the centre of the government's argument that it can bring net migration down while keeping to its pledges on fairness. Any change will be read for what it says about that balance.

What to watch

The test is the published detail: the qualifying period, any exemptions, and the transitional arrangements. Those will appear in the Home Office's statement of changes to the immigration rules or an accompanying policy paper. This article will be updated when they are published.
