UK ministers weigh partial ban on backyard fireworks over noise

In one sentence
Ministers are weighing a partial ban on the loudest backyard fireworks over noise, building on a consultation launched in July, though final scope and timing are unconfirmed.

Ministers are weighing a partial ban on the fireworks that households let off in their own gardens, with noise the main complaint. The question has come to a head five days before Bonfire Night on 5 November. The idea is not a ban on fireworks as such. It is a narrower cut that would take the loudest consumer products out of private hands and leave them to professional operators.

The Guardian's Politics desk has reported that ministers are considering the step. The full detail of that report — the exact scope, any timetable for legislation — could not be confirmed independently for this article. What can be established is the policy backdrop, and it shows the idea is the latest move in a review that has been running since the summer.

A review already under way

The government launched a review and public consultation on 16 July. Consumer Protection Minister Kate Dearden led it, after complaints that late-night fireworks were distressing veterans, families and pets. Reporting on the consultation says the options include limiting the loudest consumer fireworks to professional displays and restricting more high-risk products from general sale. The consultation was set at 12 weeks. That would put its close around early October, though that is an estimate from the launch date and not a published one. The government has said it will decide on changes after weighing the responses.

Dearden has framed the exercise as a balance. In the Commons she said the aim was to minimise the harm fireworks cause while letting people keep using them responsibly for celebrations. That balance explains why a partial ban is the likelier shape of any change than a full prohibition.

Why noise, and why now

The current UK-wide noise ceiling for consumer fireworks is 120 decibels. It is set in the Pyrotechnic Articles (Safety) Regulations 2015. Campaigners want it cut to 90 decibels. A petition to that effect gathered 184,376 signatures before it closed on 4 March 2026. The consultation covers several options for lowering the 120 decibel figure. It is not clear that the government has adopted the 90 decibel number.

The welfare argument carries much of the weight. Dogs Trust says almost half of UK dogs are affected by fireworks each year. MPs including Andrew Pakes and Judith Cummins have welcomed the review as a step forward.

There is also a question of jurisdiction. Scottish ministers have said it is beyond the Scottish Parliament's powers to legislate for a complete sale ban. Product safety rules are largely reserved to Westminster, so any change to what can be sold would most naturally be made at UK level.

What a partial ban would touch

The consequences would be felt by three groups. Households would lose access to the loudest products, though quieter ones would stay on the shelves. Retailers, who do most of their annual fireworks trade in a few weeks around 5 November, would have to rework their ranges. Public displays run by councils and professional firms would probably be untouched, and might gain business if loud effects migrate to organised events.

The wider signal is about how consumer-product rules are made. Fireworks regulation has long turned on injuries and fire risk. Treating noise, and its effect on animals and vulnerable people, as grounds for restriction would set a precedent. The story's reach is limited, though. It is a British policy matter tied to a single night in the calendar.

What to watch

The first marker is the government's response to the consultation, which will show whether ministers adopt a lower decibel cap, a restriction on sales, or both. Any change would need secondary legislation amending the 2015 regulations, and that would not take effect for this Bonfire Night. The trade, and the campaigners, will be looking for a date.
