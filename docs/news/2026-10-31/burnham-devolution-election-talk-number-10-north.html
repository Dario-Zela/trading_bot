Burnham's 'no nation left behind' pledge meets devolution pushback, early-election talk and a Number 10 North hiring spree

In one sentence
Burnham's 'no nation left behind' pledge meets the Scottish and Welsh first ministers' insistence that devolved decisions are theirs alone, amid election talk and a northern hiring drive.

Andy Burnham has told the UK's devolved leaders that no nation will be left behind. The Scottish and Welsh first ministers replied, in effect, that decisions on devolved matters rest with Holyrood and Cardiff alone. A pledge made on behalf of the whole country has met governments that do not accept a UK-level claim over their own affairs.

The prime minister's phrase came after a meeting with the first ministers in Glasgow, according to BBC Politics and Guardian Politics. The detail available is thin. The reports as summarised give a headline and a one-line account of the first ministers' position. They do not set out the exact wording of either side, any funding commitment or any change to how the four governments consult each other. What follows is therefore about the shape of the argument, not a settled policy.

A pledge without a mechanism

The difficulty is structural. "No nation left behind" is a promise about outcomes: growth, investment and public services reaching Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland as well as England. Delivering it depends on levers that, in the areas that matter most, are not Westminster's to pull. Health, education, much of housing and a good deal of transport policy are devolved. A UK prime minister can set a national direction and fund it through the Treasury. He cannot direct how Edinburgh or Cardiff spend what they receive.

The first ministers' stated position, that devolution decisions rest solely with Holyrood and Cardiff, sharpens that tension. On a generous reading it is a defence of settled constitutional ground. Devolved governments have long objected when Westminster spends UK money in devolved areas, as with the Internal Market Act and the old levelling-up funds. On a less generous reading it tells the prime minister that a pledge to everyone is welcome only so long as it does not include a say over how the money is used.

So the test is practical. A pledge means something if it comes with a mechanism: a funding formula, a forum with real standing, or a commitment on how UK-wide spending interacts with devolved budgets. Nothing in the reporting seen so far says Burnham has offered one. Until he does, the phrase is a statement of intent that the Scottish and Welsh governments can welcome and still ignore.

The early-election question

The politics around the pledge are not calm. BBC Politics reports that MPs continue to discuss an early general election even though Burnham has publicly ruled one out. The reporting available names no new poll, vote or event behind the renewed talk, and the reason it persists is unclear.

That matters for the devolution argument. A prime minister who has to keep denying an election is in a weaker position when asking devolved leaders to take a UK-wide commitment on trust. Both first ministers have their own electoral calendars and their own incentives to show independence from Westminster. If the denials are holding inside the parliamentary party, the talk is background noise. If they are not, the "no nation left behind" line risks sounding like campaign language, and devolved leaders will read it that way.

Number 10 North and the reach of the centre

A third thread is about where power sits. BBC Politics reports that Burnham's Number 10 North is on a multi-million pound hiring spree. The figures are unconfirmed, and no headcount, budget line or list of roles has been published in the material available. The project fits Burnham's background as a mayor with a regional power base, and it tests whether a prime minister can build a second centre of gravity outside Whitehall.

There are two ways to see it. A northern operation could back the claim that Westminster is serious about places far from London. The same operation, funded centrally and staffed by Number 10, could look to Edinburgh and Cardiff like more central capacity pointed at regional questions, with the UK government's reach growing and the devolved governments' share unchanged. Cost is a further question. A multi-million pound recruitment drive invites scrutiny of what the posts will do and who they answer to.

Europe and the pace of the reset

The Guardian reports a related signal from the business secretary, who said the UK must understand the EU's direction before signing up to closer ties. This was a comment, not a government decision. It nonetheless suggests caution on how deep and how fast the UK-EU reset will go, and the wording as reported should be checked against the full article.

The link to devolution is practical. Closer EU ties would touch devolved areas such as agriculture, food standards and parts of the environment. Scottish and Welsh ministers have said before that they expect to be consulted on any arrangement that touches them. A slower, more cautious reset leaves less to argue about for now. It also postpones the first real test of whether the UK government will give devolved governments a voice on a UK-wide deal.

What comes next

The next evidence will come from what follows Glasgow: whether Burnham's government puts a mechanism behind the pledge, and whether the first ministers accept one. Any structured forum, formula or funding commitment would show the phrase has content. The published details of Number 10 North and any further movement on an early election will show how much of the government's weight sits in Westminster and how much it intends to share.
