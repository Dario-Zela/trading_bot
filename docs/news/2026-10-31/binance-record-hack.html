Binance hit by record $6.3bn hack, with North Korea the prime suspect

In one sentence
Binance says about $6.3bn was stolen in a 20-minute raid blamed on North Korea's TraderTraitor group; roughly half is frozen, and recovering the rest will take legal process.

Binance, the world's largest cryptocurrency exchange, said this week that about $6.3bn was taken from its wallets in a single evening. That would make it the biggest crypto theft on record, roughly four times the $1.5bn Bybit raid of February 2025. Blockchain analysts say the culprit is almost certainly North Korea. About half the money has been frozen so far, and what happens to the rest is the open question.

The numbers differ slightly by source. Binance and most outlets say $6.3bn. TRM Labs puts it at $6.36bn and Chainalysis at about $6.4bn. This article uses $6.3bn, and the final tally may move.

What happened

The attack began on 29 October. TRM Labs says funds left Binance wallets across ten blockchains, most of them in about 20 minutes. Chainalysis dates the first outflows to around 4:20pm Eastern time and says freezes began at 5:14pm. One transaction alone exceeded $2.5bn, according to Wikipedia's account of the incident.

Binance suspended deposits and withdrawals while it investigated. It has said it will cover all losses and that user funds are safe.

How the attackers got in is still unsettled. Binance's first investigation pointed to a compromised engineer's laptop, which let someone authorise transactions without permission. Chainalysis describes a more technical route on Ethereum. The attacker used a wallet funded through the Tornado Cash mixer to submit signed authorisations that let a Binance hot wallet run code the attacker had deployed. The attacker then swept the wallet's assets. Wikipedia reports that Binance CEO Richard Teng has described the use of a protocol called EIP-7702, and that the attack was probably prepared at least 36 hours in advance. These accounts may fit together, but none has been confirmed in full.

Hot wallets are the part of an exchange that stays connected to the internet so customers can trade and withdraw. They are the exposed end of the business, and they are where thieves usually strike.

Why North Korea

TRM Labs attributes the attack "with high confidence" to TraderTraitor, a North Korean state-backed unit linked to the Lazarus Group. Chainalysis reached the same view. Its strongest evidence is that it watched the Binance loot mix in real time with funds from last month's Bitget theft, which had already been pinned on North Korean actors. The FBI previously tied TraderTraitor to the Bybit heist.

This is an attribution by private analysts and has not been confirmed by a government. Still, the laundering pattern fits the group's record. Chainalysis saw thousands of transactions in a short window. Funds circled back through earlier addresses, and many addresses first received exactly one TRX token before receiving larger sums, a sign of automation. Within minutes the attacker had moved about $500m in stablecoins to a swap wallet. About $325m of it was converted into roughly $285m of ether within 20 minutes.

The broader question is whether one state-linked group can do this repeatedly. On this evidence it can. A second nine-figure-plus theft by the same group within 20 months suggests that the largest exchanges' defences are failing at the human and operational layers rather than at the cryptography.

The freeze-and-trace race

Binance says it has frozen about $3bn of the $6.3bn. Chainalysis cites a lower figure of about $2.8bn. Stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle have frozen hundreds of millions of dollars in coins tied to the hack, and the US Secret Service says it is working to track the funds. Chainalysis says issuers, law enforcement and industry firms, itself included, froze funds in parallel.

A freeze has limits. Stablecoin issuers can blacklist addresses, which is why they matter in the first hours. Bitcoin and ether cannot be frozen that way. Chainalysis notes that part of the stolen value sat in those assets, which limited the response. Fundstrat's Sean Farrell estimates about $3.3bn remains unfrozen.

TRM's Ari Redbord put the distinction plainly: "A freeze stops the money from moving, and recovery follows through legal process." Frozen coins are not returned coins. Getting them back means court orders and cooperation across jurisdictions, and that takes months. Binance has made small recoveries before, such as $5.8m from the Axie Infinity theft in 2022, but nothing near this scale. As of 30 October, the stolen crypto had not been converted to cash.

Markets and what comes next

Bitcoin fell about 5% on 30 October and traded below $78,000 at points during the day, with Coinbase and Robinhood shares also lower. The move looks like a counterparty-risk repricing rather than a panic. Farrell does not see a major solvency threat, noting that Binance's roughly $1.2bn SAFU emergency fund could cover about a third of the remaining loss and the exchange's own balance sheet the rest. His test is behavioural: "the key tell will be how quickly withdrawals resume."

That gives the next catalysts. Binance plans to publish proof of reserves on 2 November, and Teng has said he aims to restore services within about 40 hours. If those hold, the incident reads as a costly security failure. If they slip, doubts about liquidity will grow. Beyond that, watch for any US government attribution and for sanctions or enforcement aimed at the intermediaries that launder North Korean proceeds. The amount frozen, and how much of it is eventually returned, will show whether a theft this size can be reversed at all.
