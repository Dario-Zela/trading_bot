AstraZeneca and Lilly earnings give defensives a catalyst as US futures slide

In one sentence
Lilly raised 2026 revenue guidance by $5.5bn and AstraZeneca held its outlook, but the share-price reaction on a weak US day could not be confirmed.

The headline as framed was a preview. By the time of writing, both companies had reported, and the reports give a clearer picture than the "in focus" label did. Lilly delivered a large beat-and-raise. AstraZeneca delivered steady growth and left its guidance unchanged. Neither set of figures, on the evidence available, settles whether they moved the shares or the sector on a day when US futures were weak. The share-price reaction could not be confirmed, and the size of the US futures move was not established.

Lilly: a larger raise

Lilly reported third-quarter results on 29 October. Revenue rose 39% to $24.4bn, driven mainly by its obesity and diabetes drugs Mounjaro and Zepbound. Reported earnings per share rose 16% to $7.22. On a non-GAAP basis they rose 7% to $7.48.

The quality of that quarter needs a footnote. Revenue included roughly $900m of one-off benefits, mostly $410m of Jardiance milestones. Earnings absorbed $4.57 a share of acquired in-process R&D charges, which explains why EPS growth lagged revenue growth by so much.

Guidance was the main event. Lilly raised the midpoint of its 2026 revenue range by $5.5bn, to $91.0bn–$92.0bn. It lifted non-GAAP EPS guidance by $4.07 at the midpoint, though the R&D charge more than offset that increase. It also reported positive topline Phase 3 results for retatrutide against Ozempic 2 mg in type 2 diabetes. Earlier previews had put revenue guidance at $85bn–$87bn, so the new range sits well above where the market had been working.

AstraZeneca: steady, not a surprise

AstraZeneca published nine-month and third-quarter results on 30 October. Nine-month revenue was $46.9bn, up 7% at constant exchange rates. Core EPS rose 14% to $8.00, and reported EPS was $5.62. Third-quarter revenue was $16.2bn, also up 7%, with core EPS of $2.79.

Growth came from oncology, rare disease, and respiratory and immunology. It was partly offset by loss of exclusivity on Farxiga and Brilinta and by volume-based procurement in China. The company reaffirmed full-year guidance for revenue and core EPS. It cited 12 positive Phase III readouts this year and 47 regulatory approvals in major regions since its Q4 2025 results.

A reaffirmed outlook is not a catalyst in itself. It removes a risk without adding an upside surprise, which is a defensible but modest thing to offer a risk-off tape.

What the evidence supports

The case that pharma is acting as a defensive refuge rests on two claims. One is that the results were good, and on the numbers they were, particularly Lilly's. The other is that investors bought the stocks in response while the broader market sold. That second claim is not established here. No post-results share-price moves for either company were confirmed, and the claim that AstraZeneca is the FTSE 100's heaviest healthcare name, while plausible, was not checked against an index weighting. The same applies to the scale of the US futures decline. The weak backdrop is consistent with the week's technology sell-off, in which Alphabet's capex worries pulled the Nasdaq down more than 3%.

The two sets of results also differ in kind. Lilly's is a growth story with a raised forecast, and growth stocks can fall with the Nasdaq regardless. AstraZeneca's is a steady compounder, closer to the textbook defensive profile, but it gave no new information on the outlook.

What to watch

The test is the next session's price action: whether AstraZeneca and Lilly hold gains if the technology sell-off extends, or trade down with the wider market. Further indications of the sector's role should come from other large-cap pharma reporting in the coming days and from the Fed's rate path, which bears on valuations across defensive and growth names alike.
