Andreas Malm condemns UK travel ban as 'drift into authoritarianism'

In one sentence
Climate writer Andreas Malm calls his UK entry ban a 'drift into authoritarianism', but the legal basis and any link to his activism remain unconfirmed.

Andreas Malm, the Swedish climate writer and activist, has been barred from entering the United Kingdom. He has called the decision a "drift into authoritarianism". The ban, and his description of it, were reported by the Guardian's politics desk. The reasons for it, the legal power used and the date it took effect had not been established at the time of writing. The Home Office's account of its own decision has not been confirmed either.

What is known

The outline is short. Malm, an academic and author who has argued for a more confrontational climate movement, has been refused entry to Britain. He has publicly framed the refusal as political rather than administrative. His phrase, "drift into authoritarianism", casts it as one step in a wider pattern, not an isolated immigration decision.

That framing is his own and is contested ground. Governments have broad discretion over who may enter. Ministers can exclude a non-citizen on the ground that their presence is not "conducive to the public good", a power that has historically been used against people accused of extremism or of stirring up hatred. Whether any such power was used here, and on what evidence, has not been made public in anything seen for this report.

Why the argument matters beyond one man

The significance lies less in Malm than in the precedent. If an entry ban rests on his writing or campaigning, then border powers are being used against a climate advocate for his views. That is a civil-liberties question that applies to any foreign speaker, academic or campaigner who might be invited to Britain.

If instead the ban rests on something unrelated to his advocacy, the authoritarianism charge looks weaker, and the government's task is to say so. The difficulty is that exclusion decisions are often made with little public explanation. The person affected can see the outcome but rarely the reasoning, and that gap is where suspicion grows.

Britain's climate protest movement has been in a long argument with the state over policing and sentencing, so Malm's claim lands in a prepared debate. His own writing, which defends property damage as a tactic against fossil-fuel infrastructure, is the kind of material a government might cite if it chose to defend the ban. Whether it has done so is not yet known.

What to watch

Three things would settle the question. The first is a statement from the Home Office on the legal basis for the ban and whether it is tied to Malm's activism. The second is any legal challenge. Exclusion decisions can be contested, and a challenge would force the reasoning into the open. The third is whether MPs or civil-liberties groups press ministers in Parliament for an explanation. Until one of these happens, the ban remains an assertion by one side, and the "drift" Malm describes is a claim that has yet to be tested against the facts.
