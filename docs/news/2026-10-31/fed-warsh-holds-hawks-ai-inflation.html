Warsh holds rates and calls prices 'too high' as Fed officials split over AI-driven inflation and more hikes

In one sentence
Fed Chair Warsh held rates and called prices 'too high' while Trump says inflation is 'way down', and hawks and AI-demand worries inside the Fed complicate the path ahead.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh has left interest rates unchanged and said prices remain "too high". On the same news cycle, President Donald Trump said inflation is "way down". Both statements cannot describe the same economy equally well. With the midterm elections weeks away, the gap between the White House account and the central bank's has become a market variable in its own right.

One caveat about the record. The reporting behind this piece comes from Benzinga alone. It carries no policy-rate level, no consumer price index reading and no meeting date, and the primary Fed statement was not available to this desk. The article therefore describes the shape of the disagreement and avoids numbers it cannot source.

A hold, and a pointed description of prices

A hold is the least dramatic thing a central bank can do. What matters here is the language that came with it. A chair who keeps rates steady while calling prices too high is saying the job is unfinished, and that the pause is a judgment about timing rather than a declaration of victory.

That sits awkwardly beside the President's line. Two readings of the same data are available. Inflation may have fallen from its peak, which supports "way down", while the price level remains well above where households remember it, which supports "too high". Rates of change and levels are different measures, and the two sides appear to be quoting different ones. The next CPI and PCE releases will show which framing the data favours, and the Fed's own published projections will show how far it expects to go.

The political stakes are plain. A Fed that publicly contradicts the White House on prices, shortly before voters go to the polls, invites questions about whether it is acting independently or being drawn into the campaign. A Fed that stayed quiet would invite the opposite question.

Inside the committee: hawks and the AI argument

The committee does not speak with one voice either. Two further threads, both also sourced to Benzinga, point towards a firmer stance than a simple hold implies.

Lorie Logan of the Dallas Fed and Alberto Musalem of the St. Louis Fed are reported to have argued that inflation is unacceptably high and that financial conditions are accommodative. On that view, gradual rate rises should continue. The report supplies no date, quote or inflation figure, and the meaning of "soon" in its headline is unexplained. The FOMC decides by majority, so two presidents' views, however forcefully stated, do not set policy on their own. They do show that part of the committee sees the next move as up rather than down, which matters for any market that has priced the tightening cycle as finished.

The second thread is more novel. Raphael Bostic's colleagues Tom Barkin of the Richmond Fed and Mary Daly of the San Francisco Fed are reported to see the surge in AI investment as a source of demand-side inflation. They say the capital-spending boom is straining supply chains. They tie the point to the Fed's two mandates, price stability and maximum employment.

This cuts against a comfortable story in which AI is disinflationary through productivity gains. If data-centre construction and equipment orders are adding to demand faster than supply can respond, the investment boom works against the case for lower rates. It also links monetary policy to the equity market's biggest theme. Alphabet's worst week since 2011, which took the Nasdaq down more than 3%, has already shown how sensitive prices are to doubts about AI spending, and a Fed that blames that spending for inflation adds a second source of pressure.

What the split means for markets

For investors the practical question is the direction of the next move. A chair who holds while calling prices too high, hawkish regional presidents calling for more hikes, and officials identifying a new demand-side driver all point to a higher bar for cuts than the White House's message implies. Rate-sensitive sectors and long-dated bonds are most exposed if that reading takes hold.

The independence question is slower-moving but larger. The Fed's credibility rests on its being seen to follow the data regardless of who is in office. A public disagreement over what the data say tests that, and the test arrives at a politically sensitive moment.

The next checkpoints are concrete: the next CPI and PCE inflation releases, the minutes of this meeting, and the next FOMC decision after the midterms. Those will show whether "too high" was a statement about the level of prices, a signal on rates, or both.
