Standoff in Poland as police deploy over control of the top court

In one sentence
Police were deployed at Poland's Constitutional Tribunal after its president was suspended in a dispute the court's leadership calls an unlawful coup, in a long fight over judicial control.

Police were sent to the grounds of Poland's Constitutional Tribunal in Warsaw on Friday, after a fight over who runs the court spilled out of the courtroom and onto the street. It is the most physical moment so far in a dispute that began in 2015, when the parties first clashed over judicial appointments.

The immediate trigger was the suspension of the court's president, Bogdan Święczkowski, on 30 October. Reports, citing Reuters, say the court's vice-president, Bartłomiej Sochański, was also affected. The court's leadership rejects the move. In a statement, the Tribunal said Święczkowski remains president and Sochański remains vice-president. Its official X account called a takeover of the court's website unlawful and said that "all those participating in these actions are committing the crime of a coup d'état." The reports I found do not say who made the suspension decision or on what legal basis. One report attributes the disputed decision to Judge Anna Korwin-Piotrowska. Neither the government nor the court has so far given a full account of the legal mechanism, and details may shift.

What happened on the ground

Police say they entered the Tribunal's grounds but not the building, and that they acted at the request of an authorised person. They say they did not move against the court's own guards. They detained three people who climbed the fence. Police also reported disturbances outside, including dangerous objects thrown at officers. Their stated aim was to keep demonstrators away from the building.

The politics moved quickly. President Karol Nawrocki cut short a trip to Senegal. "At a moment like this, the President must be in Poland," he wrote. "I am cutting short my flight to Senegal. I am returning." Law and Justice leader Jarosław Kaczyński described events as a step towards a police state. Prime Minister Donald Tusk framed the government's side as the undoing of years of political interference: "Restoring the rule of law is our duty."

Why the court is contested

The Tribunal was reshaped under the previous Law and Justice government, which filled it with judges its opponents regard as improperly appointed. Tusk's government, in office since late 2023, has refused to recognise the court's legitimacy. The Tribunal has in turn blocked its agenda. In November 2025 it struck down a government bill to overhaul the National Council of the Judiciary, the body that proposes judges.

That sequence explains why the current dispute is so hard to settle. Each side can point to a legal argument, and each side disputes whether the other's institutions can lawfully rule. A court that the government calls illegitimate cannot be the neutral arbiter of its own composition. Nor can a government that ignores the court's rulings easily claim that it is only applying the law.

Who decides

The deployment shows what happens when legal argument runs out. Somebody has to open the doors, hold the keys and decide who may walk in, and that is a job for the police. Whoever gives them their instructions, in effect, settles the question for the day. That is a thin basis for authority. It also hands the other side a ready charge of a coup, as the Tribunal has already made.

The wider stakes are with the EU, which has pressed Warsaw for years over judicial independence and which has an interest in Poland having a court whose rulings are accepted. A government that restores order by force, even in defence of its reading of the law, will find that reading harder to defend abroad.

What to watch: whether Nawrocki, who is expected to be back in Warsaw within hours, uses his powers to side with the Tribunal's leadership. Also whether the government publishes the legal basis for the suspensions, and whether the police presence at the court is lifted or extended.
