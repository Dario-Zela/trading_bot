UK faces 10 dry days after 'wettest week in two years'

In one sentence
After England's reported wettest week in two years, high pressure should bring mostly dry weather into early November, a window for flood recovery before a likely unsettled return.

The weather has turned. After what the Environment Agency has reportedly described as England's wettest week in two years, the UK is heading into a spell of high pressure that could bring as many as 10 days without significant rain. For people mopping out flooded homes and for the agencies tracking rivers, the dry run matters more than the sunshine does.

The wet week

The rain came in a run. Storms Austen and Boelo crossed the country in quick succession, followed by another area of low pressure on Monday. Reports citing the Environment Agency put the seven-day England average at about 55mm to 27 October. Those figures come from secondary coverage of the BBC's reporting, and the original Environment Agency release has not been checked here. The comparison with the past two years is the agency's, as relayed, and the regions hit hardest are not set out in the material available.

An England-wide average hides a lot. Saturated catchments in the wettest places will have taken far more than 55mm, and the flooding that followed was local: rivers rising in a day or two, surface water pooling where drains could not cope.

What the forecast says

The Met Office's outlook, published on 28 October, is more careful than the headline suggests. From Thursday through the first week of November, high pressure should keep central, southern and eastern areas largely dry, with occasional interruptions from weak fronts. The forecaster notes that "high pressure will have its greatest influence farther south." The north and west stay more exposed to Atlantic weather. A band of locally heavy rain is expected across western and northern Scotland and Northern Ireland on Friday, with stronger winds.

The weekend brings a front moving south-east, but its rain should be light and patchy. By Monday, drizzle may linger in the south. Light winds make fog a recurring feature, especially across central and eastern England from Thursday night, and temperatures should stay near or slightly below average, with a touch of overnight frost possible.

The Met Office's detailed outlook runs to around 6 November. A full 10 dry days is therefore the BBC-attributed framing, not something the forecaster's own text commits to. It is better read as "mostly dry, with exceptions" than as an unbroken run.

Why dry matters more than fine

A dry fortnight does not undo a wet week. It does give recovery work a chance.

  • Clean-up. Drying out properties, repairing roads and drains, and inspecting flood defences are all easier without fresh rain.
  • River levels. Large rivers drain slowly. Many keep running high for days after the rain stops, so flood warnings can persist into a dry spell. Falling levels, not the absence of rain, are what lift them.
  • Ground storage. Saturated soil cannot absorb more. A dry spell lets it drain somewhat, which means the next band of rain meets more capacity.

The Met Office article does not discuss flood warnings or reservoir stocks. How far one wet week has altered water-resource positions is a separate question, and the answer is not in the sources available.

The catch

The respite looks temporary. The Met Office says that later in November "an unsettled regime is considered more likely than not," with wetter-than-average conditions more likely for most areas, though confidence in locations and amounts is low.

The working window for repairs and defence checks is therefore roughly the first week of November. The next markers are the Met Office's updated extended outlook and the Environment Agency's flood-warning picture as river levels fall.
