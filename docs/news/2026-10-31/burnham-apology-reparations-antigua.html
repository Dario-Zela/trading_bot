Burnham open to a slavery apology but rules out reparations as protests greet the King in Antigua

In one sentence
Burnham says a formal slavery apology is possible but reparations are ruled out, as Caribbean campaigners protest around the Antigua Commonwealth summit and demand 'reparations now'.

Britain goes into the Commonwealth summit in Antigua with a position that satisfies neither side of the argument. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is open to a formal apology for the slave trade. He has also said, flatly, that the UK will not pay reparations. Campaigners in the Caribbean, who have spent more than a decade building towards this gathering, say the two cannot be separated.

No apology has been issued, and no date or wording has been given. What exists is an opening, offered in advance of a summit that Caribbean leaders have already described as theirs.

What Burnham has said

In an article for the Guardian, Burnham said he was open to calls for a formal apology and offered what he called "structured dialogue". He drew the line at money. British people alive today, he argued, cannot be held liable for historical wrongs, so the UK will not pay reparations.

The formulation is narrower than it first sounds. An apology is a statement and reparations are a transfer, and Burnham has offered to discuss the first while closing off the second. Because the apology is only described as possible, the government keeps control of whether it happens and when.

The King's position is different. He has acknowledged the "great suffering of the past" during his Caribbean tour, which took in the Bahamas and then Guyana. But Buckingham Palace told Dorbrene O'Marde, who chairs Antigua and Barbuda's reparations support commission, that UK reparations policy is a matter for the British government. Whatever the monarch says in St John's, the substance will be set in Whitehall.

The Caribbean demand

For Caricom, the Caribbean community, an apology is the start of a larger sequence. Its 10-point plan for reparatory justice puts a "full and formal apology" first. O'Marde has said an apology would open the way to negotiations, which is why he called the government's earlier "no apologies" stance "shameful" and accused ministers of "verbal gymnastics".

Hilary Beckles, who chairs the Caricom reparations commission, has called the summit "the Chogm of reparations" and "the first round of reparations negotiations". On that reading, Burnham's offer of dialogue without reparations is a partial concession that leaves the core dispute where it was.

The campaigners also have more international backing than they did at the last summit. On 25 March a UN resolution described the trafficking and enslavement of Africans as the gravest crime against humanity, with 123 states in support. In August the UN committee on racial discrimination recommended that countries consider reparations for the slave trade. In September, Jamaican officials petitioned the King for legal advice on a reparations claim, which the Privy Council is due to hear in March.

The Caribbean side has not always been able to carry the wider membership with it. Its governments failed to win unanimous Commonwealth support for foreign ministers to discuss historical harms, a reminder that the 56-member group does not speak with one voice.

Protest and its limits

Reparations messages are planned on billboards, placards and T-shirts, with a candlelight vigil for Africans trafficked to Antigua and Barbuda. Organisers have also planned a beach vigil and the launch of a "global reparations caravan". Neither is on the official programme. Demonstrators have been demanding "reparations now". Groups including the Repair Campaign and the law firm Leigh Day are due to meet outside the main summit, alongside Open Society Foundations and the UK all-party parliamentary group for Afrikan reparations.

James Knight of Citizens for Reparatory Justice said the royal family and the UK government had made protest necessary. He called the timing of the "no apology, no reparations" announcement "extremely insulting" and expects the issue to be unavoidable in the summit rooms.

Not every voice in the region is persuaded that an apology matters much. Speaking in Guyana, the dramatist Deandra Daniels said an apology alone would not be enough.

What to watch

The test is whether "possible" becomes a commitment. Three things will show it: whether the summit communiqué carries language on historical harms or reparatory justice, whether Burnham attaches any timetable or form of words to his apology, and whether "structured dialogue" acquires a venue and a mandate. The Privy Council hearing in March gives the Jamaican petition a legal clock that the political talks lack. If the apology comes without a process behind it, Caribbean leaders are likely to treat it as the end of the conversation Burnham says he wants to begin.
