Chicago PMI eases to 55.3, just under forecasts

In one sentence
October's Chicago PMI printed 55.3 against a forecast of 55.8, a small miss on a regional gauge that still sits well inside expansion territory.

A regional gauge of factory and business activity in the Midwest came in a touch weaker than economists expected for October, though it remains comfortably in expansion territory. The Chicago purchasing managers' index read 55.3, against a consensus forecast of 55.8, a miss of half a point.

What the number says

PMI-style surveys are diffusion indices. A reading of 50 separates expansion from contraction, so 55.3 means that, on balance, respondents in the Chicago area reported improving conditions. The shortfall against forecast is 0.5 points on a scale where month-to-month moves of several points are not unusual. Forecasts for such a volatile series carry wide error bands, and a miss of this size sits well within them.

The distance from the 50 line matters more than the distance from the forecast. At 55.3, the gauge is more than five points above the threshold. Even a modest pullback from expectations leaves it describing a business sector that is growing, not stalling.

Why it is not a turning point

A single reading can only be called a trend if the series around it supports one. The details available for this release do not include the prior-month figure or the sub-indices for new orders, production, employment and prices paid. Without them there is no basis for saying the gauge is decelerating, and none for saying the miss is the start of anything. On the evidence available, it is one data point that undershot by a small margin.

The Chicago survey is also a regional snapshot, not a national one. It is one input among several that analysts weigh alongside the broader national manufacturing and services surveys, and it has limited power on its own to shift views on growth.

What it means for markets and policy

For policymakers, the question is whether activity is cooling enough to change the inflation picture. A 55.3 does not suggest it is. Officials at the Federal Reserve are currently debating how much price pressure remains, as covered in Warsh holds rates and calls prices 'too high', and a regional gauge that stays well above 50 does little to settle that argument either way.

The stronger test lies in the next releases. The national manufacturing survey and the following month's Chicago reading will show whether October's slight miss was noise or the first step down. If the index slips toward the low 50s over the coming months, the story changes. At 55.3, it does not.
