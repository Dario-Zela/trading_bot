Avanti West Coast paid out £20m in delay-repay refunds this summer

In one sentence
Avanti West Coast reportedly paid about £20m in Delay Repay refunds over summer 2026, but without a baseline the figure shows cost rather than trend.

Avanti West Coast paid about £20m to passengers in Delay Repay compensation over the summer of 2026, according to a report in the Guardian. The figure is a plain accounting of what late and cancelled trains cost on one of Britain's busiest intercity routes. The report gives no comparison year, no route breakdown and no operator response, so the number is easier to quote than to interpret.

What the number is

Delay Repay is the scheme under which passengers claim money back when a train arrives late. Operators pay it out of their own revenue. The £20m is therefore compensation paid to passengers, not a regulatory fine, and it measures how many people applied for refunds and how large those refunds were. It is not a direct measure of how many trains ran late.

Avanti runs the long-distance services on the West Coast Main Line from London Euston to the north-west of England, North Wales and Scotland. Those journeys are long and often expensive, so each refund tends to be sizeable. A delay of 30 minutes or more on a pricey ticket can mean a large repayment.

Reading it against the past

The summer figure can be set against earlier published totals, with a caveat about how far they compare. Rail Magazine reported that Avanti and LNER were the two biggest payers of Delay Repay among train operators, each handing over just over £32m across a 12-month period. That is an annual total, not a seasonal one, and it covers a different period from the 2026 figure. Even so, a £20m summer would be a large share of a year's payouts if the earlier scale still holds.

Several factors push the totals up without necessarily meaning more delays. Operators have made claiming easier, passengers have become more used to claiming, and any fare rise raises the value of each claim. Without a like-for-like summer from a previous year, the £20m cannot be read as a worsening or an improvement.

What is missing

The triage material and the available reporting leave several questions open:

  • A baseline. Whether summer 2026 was better or worse than summer 2025 is the first thing a reader would want to know.
  • Causes. Delays on the West Coast Main Line come from several sources, including track and signalling faults on Network Rail's infrastructure, fleet availability and industrial action. Delay Repay is paid by the operator regardless of whose fault the delay was. A large bill therefore does not by itself show where responsibility lies.
  • Operator and government responses. No comment from Avanti or the Department for Transport was available to put alongside the figure.

What to watch

The Office of Rail and Road publishes operator-level performance and compensation data, and Avanti publishes its own period-by-period Delay Repay tables. Either would show whether summer 2026 sits above or below earlier periods. Those releases, and any statement from Avanti on the figure, will decide whether £20m is a one-season spike or part of a continuing pattern.

Sources

  1. Guardian UK (original report of the £20m figure)
  2. Rail Magazine: train operators pay £138m in Delay Repay compensation
  3. Avanti West Coast: Delay Repay tables, April 2025 to March 2026
