Streeting denies 'kicking the can down the road' in defence spending row

In one sentence
Streeting's denial that the government is 'kicking the can down the road' on defence spending shifts the row to whether the funding timetable, and so gilt supply and orders, is credible.

A Labour minister has rejected the charge that the government is "kicking the can down the road" on defence spending. The denial, reported by BBC Politics, shows the argument has moved from how much Britain will spend to when the money arrives and whether the Treasury can be believed about the schedule.

The detail available is thin. The account rests on a single outlet, and this article has not been able to check the exact wording, the setting of the remarks or any new figures against a second source. The reporting below therefore separates what is known from what is inferred.

The accusation

"Kicking the can down the road" is a charge about timing. Critics use it when a government announces a large ambition but puts the costly part beyond the period in which it can be held to account. In defence, that usually means a headline target for a share of national income, with the real increases back-loaded into later years.

Streeting's denial suggests ministers think the charge is landing. A government confident in its schedule rarely feels the need to deny it in those terms. That is an inference from the shape of the exchange, not something the source states.

Why the timetable matters

The UK has already committed publicly to raising defence spending as a share of GDP, and it has signed up to NATO's longer-term goals. Those commitments are not in dispute. What is in dispute is the path: how fast the budget rises, and in which years.

The path matters because defence outlays compete with everything else the Treasury has promised, under fiscal rules that leave little headroom. A faster path means more spending sooner, which means either higher taxes, cuts elsewhere or more borrowing. A slower one eases that pressure now and leaves the harder choices to a later budget.

That is the market link. The pace of defence spending feeds into how many gilts the Debt Management Office has to sell, and into when contractors see budget-backed orders rather than pledges. No issuance figures, yield data or company names accompany the original report, so the size of any such effect cannot be estimated here. The direction is clear enough: a slip in the timetable would, other things equal, mean less near-term borrowing and later order books.

What credibility requires

A spending path is credible when it is written down in departmental budgets with dates and sums, not only in speeches. Defence procurement is lumpy and slow. Contracts take years to place, and a promise made for a distant year does little for a supplier deciding whether to expand a factory now. Suppliers and investors will look for money allocated to specific years.

The row therefore turns on documents rather than rhetoric. The government can end the can-kicking charge by publishing a year-by-year path. Its critics can sustain the charge only by showing that the early years are lighter than the headline implies.

What to watch

The next test is the government's fiscal statement and the departmental allocations that follow it. The year-by-year defence figures there will show whether the money is front-loaded or deferred. They will also show how the Treasury has reconciled the spending with its borrowing forecast, which is what the Office for Budget Responsibility's assessment will turn on. Until then, Streeting's denial is a statement of intent, not evidence.
