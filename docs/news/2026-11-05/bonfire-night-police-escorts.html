Police to escort fire crews in Bonfire Night crackdown

In one sentence
Police are escorting fire crews on Bonfire Night after years of attacks on firefighters, a sign the annual disorder now shapes how emergency cover is deployed.

Police forces are escorting fire crews on Bonfire Night this year, according to BBC reporting, after years of attacks on firefighters around 5 November. The arrangement is described as part of a wider crackdown on disorder. It reflects how serious the problem has become: crews now need protection to do their work.

This article rests on the BBC's account and on a search of earlier, publicly reported Bonfire Night incidents. It does not draw on this year's force statements or figures. They were not available at the time of writing, so no numbers or locations for 2026 are given here.

A recurring problem

The attacks are not new. Reports going back several years describe fire crews called to bonfires being hit with fireworks, bricks, stones and bottles. Scotland has been the focus of much of this coverage. Edinburgh and Ayrshire have been named repeatedly, and Fire Scotland has publicly described crews coming under attack in the run-up to the night. In one reported Edinburgh case, two crews had to call for police back-up at a large, out-of-control bonfire.

Some fire services have said that in certain areas crews will not enter a scene without a police escort. Campaigns such as Scotland's #DoNotAttackMe, an appeal from firefighters and their families, have tried to change behaviour. They have not ended the attacks.

What escorts change

Escorting crews alters the logic of emergency cover. A fire appliance normally goes to the incident. Under an escort arrangement it may have to wait for officers to secure the route and the scene, which can slow a response.

The cost also falls on other services. Fire services have warned that a damaged appliance may be unavailable for other emergencies. Police officers tied up as escorts are likewise not available for other calls on one of the busiest nights of the year.

There is a case for the policy. Escorts let crews tackle fires that would otherwise burn unchecked, and they put officers where trouble is most likely. The risk is that protection becomes a standing feature of the night rather than a response to a spike in violence.

What to watch

The test is the aftermath. Forces and fire services typically publish figures on attacks and arrests in the days after 5 November. Those numbers will show whether the escorts deterred attacks or only managed them. The sharper question for councils and communities is whether the organised displays, policing and youth work that precede the night can cut the number of unsupervised bonfires. Until then, the escorts treat the symptom.
