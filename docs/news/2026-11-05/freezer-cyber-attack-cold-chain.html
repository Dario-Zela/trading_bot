Cyber-attack on supermarket freezers hits UK meat industry and French blood storage

In one sentence
Hackers have overheated supermarket freezers across Europe and reportedly hit UK meat suppliers; a French blood-storage link is unconfirmed and no one has been officially blamed.

A wave of attacks on the control systems that keep supermarket freezers cold has spread across Europe. It has now reached the meat trade, with reports that UK suppliers have been hit. A claim that French blood storage was also disrupted could not be confirmed. Nobody has credibly been named as responsible.

What is established

The first big incident was in France in October. Hackers got into internet-connected refrigeration controllers and, overnight when shops were closed, pushed temperatures in freezers and fridges as high as 50C. Reports put the number of affected stores at somewhere between 500 and 700. Stores threw away large quantities of meat, dairy and frozen food, and losses at some sites reportedly exceeded €130,000.

The controllers were mainly made by the Danish firm Danfoss. France's cyber agency, ANSSI, later said equipment from two other manufacturers was affected too. The BBC reported that the affected units were older models whose security software could not be updated. ANSSI advised operators of the affected models to disconnect them from the internet, or restrict access to them, and to apply updates.

In November, similar temperature spikes were reported in Sweden, Belgium, Luxembourg, Ireland and Estonia. In Ireland, 13 SuperValu stores had refrigeration systems affected. Whether these incidents share a cause with the French one is not yet clear.

The meat trade

The Grocer reported that several UK meat suppliers had seen their fridges tampered with overnight. The equipment was run through Dixell XWEB software, owned by Copeland. The Association of Independent Meat Suppliers said the extent of the problem was not yet clear. The report gives no tonnages, company names or dates that I could verify.

That matters because a supermarket freezer and a meat wholesaler's cold room can run on the same kind of remote-management software. A shop loses a few thousand pounds of stock. A processor or distributor holds far more product, and a failure there can empty shelves across many stores. The National Cyber Security Centre has said it is working with retailers to guard against the threat. I found no confirmed report of a UK supermarket itself being hit, though one outlet said the same hackers had tried to attack Co-op and Harrods.

The blood-storage claim

The triage brief for this story said the disruption had also reached French blood storage. I could not find a report that supports this. Searches turned up no account of any hospital or blood service losing stock to a refrigeration attack.

The risk itself is not new. A 2019 warning about exposed refrigeration systems from the vendor RDM noted that such systems are used to store blood and drugs in hospitals. That warning described exposure, not an observed attack. Until a health authority or blood service says something on the record, the medical angle is a plausible worry and nothing more.

Who is behind it

No government or agency has publicly attributed the attacks. One report names the hacking group Scattered Spider, using DragonForce ransomware. I found no official confirmation, and no ransom demand has been cited in the coverage I could find. French investigators say their inquiry is continuing.

The pattern points less to a sophisticated break-in than to a basic weakness: control boxes reachable from the open internet, some running software that cannot be patched. That is an inference from the ANSSI and Danfoss details above, not a finding from investigators.

What to watch

Three things will settle how serious this is. The first is whether ANSSI or the NCSC publishes a technical advisory naming the affected products and a common point of entry. The second is whether any UK meat supplier or health body confirms losses, with figures. The third is whether anyone claims responsibility or issues a demand, which would show whether the aim is money or disruption. If the blood-storage report is confirmed, regulators of hospitals and blood services would face the same question grocers now do: how many cold rooms can be reached from outside.
