Supreme Court told Palestine Action ban undermined confidence in anti-terror laws

In one sentence
The Supreme Court is hearing Palestine Action's challenge to its terror ban, with the court told the ban undermined confidence in anti-terror laws; no judgment has been given.

The United Kingdom's highest court is examining whether the government's decision to ban Palestine Action as a terrorist organisation was lawful. The question carries a second one with it: how far counter-terrorism powers can reach into protest that breaks the law without, on the group's account, being terrorism. According to the BBC's report of the hearing, the court was told the ban undermined confidence in the UK's anti-terror laws. The hearing is an exchange of arguments. No judgment has been given.

The precise wording of that submission, and the side that made it, should be checked against the BBC's full account. Reports differ on the schedule. One lists the hearing for 4-5 November. The Supreme Court's own order for an expedited case pointed to a window running from October to December. No judgment date has been announced.

How the case got here

Palestine Action was proscribed under the Terrorism Act 2000 in July 2025. It was a direct-action group whose members damaged property, in the main at sites linked to Israeli arms firms. Being a member of a proscribed organisation, or expressing support for it, is a criminal offence. Huda Ammori, a co-founder, challenged the decision, arguing it was unlawful and disproportionate.

The litigation has swung back and forth. In February 2026 a High Court panel ruled against the ban. In June, five senior judges led by the Lady Chief Justice overturned that decision. The Court of Appeal held that proscription was lawful given the group's support for violence. On 30 July the Supreme Court granted permission to appeal and ordered the case expedited. It refused permission on a separate ground, that the ban interfered with freedom of speech and freedom of association.

That refusal narrows the case. The justices are being asked to examine the legality of the proscription decision itself, not to rule broadly on protest rights. In practice the two are hard to separate.

Why confidence matters

Proscription is unusual among state powers. It is made by the home secretary and approved by Parliament, and it turns support for a group into an offence without any trial of the group. The regime depends on the public accepting that the label is reserved for organisations that pose a terrorist threat. If that acceptance weakens, prosecutors and police pay for it.

The government's position, as reported, is that the ban rests on Palestine Action's criminal activities, not on support for the Palestinian cause. Critics respond that criminal damage, however serious, is a different category from terrorism. They also say that a ban sweeping in people who hold placards in its name stretches the definition beyond what Parliament intended.

The practical consequences are large. More than 3,500 people have been arrested over alleged support for Palestine Action, and their cases have reportedly been put on hold pending a final ruling. Large numbers of arrests at protests continued while the litigation ran; one wire report put the figure at 117 at a single demonstration. A ruling either way will decide what happens to that backlog.

What to watch

The first thing to watch is the judgment. The Supreme Court expedited the appeal, so a decision may come within months, though no date has been fixed. If the ban is upheld, Ammori has said she would take the case to the European Court of Human Rights. If it is quashed, the government would have to decide whether to re-proscribe on a different footing, and prosecutors would have to decide what to do with the stalled cases. Either outcome will set a marker for how far proscription can reach a protest group.
