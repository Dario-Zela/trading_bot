Ministers target 'wild west' of e-bikes blocking pavements

In one sentence
UK ministers are planning measures to curb e-bikes blocking pavements and being misused, though the specific powers, penalties and timetable have yet to be confirmed.

Ministers are preparing measures to curb e-bikes left blocking pavements and ridden carelessly. The Guardian reports that they have described the current situation as a 'wild west'. The detail of what is planned (powers, penalties, timetable) was not available when this piece was prepared, so the substance below is context rather than a description of the policy.

A street-level nuisance

The complaint is a familiar one in British towns and cities. Hire e-bikes, many of them dockless, are dropped on footways where they can block a wheelchair user, a buggy or a person with a white cane. Campaigners for disabled and blind pedestrians have argued for years that a bike lying across a narrow pavement is an obstacle and a hazard, not an inconvenience. A think-tank report has described dumped e-bikes as dangerous for disabled people.

Councils have mostly had to improvise. Several London boroughs have pressed operators to remove badly parked bikes. Wandsworth has threatened to seize bikes parked 'unacceptably'. Kensington and Chelsea has pushed operators to act and has added dedicated e-bike bays after public consultation. Others have tried fines on hire firms for bikes left on pavements.

What national government can add

The patchwork is the argument for action at national level. Powers over where a bike can be left, and who pays when it is left badly, sit awkwardly between councils, operators and the police. The Deputy Prime Minister has previously announced that regional mayors would be given new powers to crack down on e-bikes and e-scooters dumped on pavements. Any new package would build on that groundwork and on the pressure councils have applied to hire firms.

'Misuse' is a wider category than parking. It covers riding on pavements, and the modified or high-powered machines that police and ministers have warned about, which are a separate legal problem from tidy parking. The one-line summary of the Guardian report refers to both obstruction and misuse. Which of the two the measures will actually reach is the open question.

What to watch

A plan is not a law. The questions that decide whether this matters are specific. Will liability for badly parked bikes fall on operators, on users, or on both? Will enforcement be left to councils that are already stretched? Will bay-only parking become a requirement rather than an option? Those details will come in any consultation or legislation that follows, and they will show whether 'wild west' is being answered with rules that can actually be enforced.

This piece will need updating once the government's own announcement, with its measures and dates, can be read in full.
