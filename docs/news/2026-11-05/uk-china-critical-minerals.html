UK 'dangerously' reliant on China for critical minerals, report warns

In one sentence
MPs say Britain is dangerously dependent on China for minerals vital to defence, renewables and AI, and want a central strategy and a dedicated finance facility.

Britain is "dangerously" dependent on China for the raw materials that go into military hardware, renewable-energy infrastructure and artificial intelligence, according to a cross-party parliamentary inquiry. The finding turns a long-running trade worry into a question of national security.

The report comes from MPs on the House of Commons business committee. As reported by the Guardian and syndicated outlets, they say the UK has no sufficiently coordinated policy for securing minerals such as lithium and rare earths. They want an urgent, centralised strategy and a dedicated "critical minerals finance facility" to fund mines, processing and diversification.

Why the framing has shifted

Critical-minerals dependence has been flagged before. A 2023 Foreign Affairs Committee report found that 12 of the UK's 18 critical minerals came from China, and blamed successive governments for failing to assess their vulnerabilities. What is different now is the emphasis. The committee describes the exposure as a risk to defence and to the compute and hardware behind AI, not only to the green transition.

Its evidence rests partly on recent demonstrations of leverage. The MPs point to China's 2025 curbs on rare-earth exports and to chip-related restrictions that, they say, left Europe's car industry days from stoppages. Beijing, in their reading, has shown it will restrict supply when it suits it. According to the secondary reports, China dominates processing in seven mineral categories, which matters because even ore mined elsewhere is often refined in China.

Demand is set to climb. MPs were told that UK lithium demand could rise 45-fold by 2030 and rare-earth demand 16-fold as Britain rearms and electrifies. Those are witness estimates reported second-hand, and the full report should be consulted for their basis.

What MPs want, and the government's reply

The committee sets out a 10-point plan covering domestic mining, refining, recycling, investment and permitting, plus closer coordination with G7 and Indo-Pacific partners. It also complains about opacity: MPs say they could not be confident that ministers had met any of the plan's 10 tests, given how little the government has disclosed.

Ministers reject the charge that they lack a strategy. A government spokesperson pointed to £400m "to ramp up domestic production and recycling" and to new international partnerships. The stated 2035 goals are to meet 10% of demand from domestic production and 20% from recycling, with no more than 60% of any critical mineral coming from a single country.

The gap between those targets and the committee's alarm is the substance of the dispute. A 60% cap is a long way from a market in which one country dominates refining, and 2035 is a long way from the next export-licensing decision in Beijing.

What to watch

The government is expected to respond formally to the committee, which will show whether it accepts the finance facility. That would be the first concrete test of whether the security framing translates into money. Defence procurement and AI-infrastructure plans, which depend on these inputs, are where any shortfall would show first.

This account draws on syndicated and secondary coverage of the Guardian's report rather than the committee's text, so figures should be checked against the report itself.
