UK commits to 'time-bound' talks on slavery apology with Caribbean states

In one sentence
At the Antigua Commonwealth summit the UK agreed to structured, time-bound reparations dialogue with Caribbean states within a year, but has ruled out paying and set no end date.

Britain has, for the first time, agreed to a dated process for talking about slavery with the Caribbean states that were built on it. It has not agreed to pay for it. At the close of the Commonwealth summit in St John's, Antigua, on 4 November, the communiqué described a dialogue on reparatory justice that is "structured, time-bound, inclusive and good-faith", with talks between the UK and Caribbean nations due to begin within a year. Whether the word "time-bound" means anything depends on details the summit did not publish.

From refusal to dialogue

The shift is real, though it is easy to overstate. In October 2024, at the Samoa summit, Sir Keir Starmer resisted giving reparations a place on the agenda. He called the subject "one paragraph in 20-something paragraphs" of the final communiqué and said none of the discussions had been about money. Rachel Reeves, then chancellor, said the UK would not pay. Even so, 56 leaders signed a text saying "the time has come for a meaningful, truthful and respectful conversation".

Two years on, the prime minister is Andy Burnham, and the posture is different. Days before the summit he wrote in the Guardian that he was "offering a structured dialogue with those affected by the transatlantic slave trade". He added that "listening to calls for a formal apology on behalf of the British state will form an important part of this process and I am open to them". He called the slave trade a "brutal and barbaric violation" of enslaved people's rights. Britain has never formally apologised for slavery, and it has long pointed to its leading role in abolition.

The same article closed the door on money. Reparations were not "the right answer", Burnham wrote, and people in Britain today cannot be held liable for wrongs "that happened in a different age under different laws". He offered a different kind of help: a drive on debt restructuring for the poorest Commonwealth countries, led by the former Labour prime minister Gordon Brown.

The King's tour of the Caribbean ran alongside. In the Bahamas Charles acknowledged "the great suffering of the past", and in Guyana he visited a colonial-era execution site. He was not expected to say anything about the change in government policy, and the apology question was left to ministers.

What was agreed, and what was not

The published detail is thin. Reports of the summit's outcome say the dialogue should start within a year. They give no end date, no named negotiators and no agenda. Nothing I found states what would count as a result. The apology is described as part of the process rather than its guaranteed product, and the government has said only that it is "open" to it.

The Caribbean reaction was welcoming and wary in equal parts. Gaston Browne, Antigua and Barbuda's prime minister, called the offer a "significant and welcome development" and later spoke of a consensus on a way forward. He also warned that "a dialogue that begins by placing reparations outside the room cannot be a complete dialogue." Mia Mottley of Barbados committed her government to the talks while conceding that "our views differ" on reparations. Philip Pierre of St Lucia, the Caricom chair, made the same point about completeness.

The Caribbean side has a long-standing case to bring. Caricom asked European governments in 2013 for a formal apology and a development programme. A UN judge said in 2023 that Britain might owe more than $18 trillion across 14 Caribbean countries, though that is one jurist's estimate and not a negotiating position. Denis O'Brien of the Repair Campaign called the government's opening statement "disappointing" and said London must "stop obfuscating and give a full and formal apology now".

Does a deadline matter?

In diplomacy, a time limit does two things. It makes delay costly, because a missed date is a public failure. It also forces both sides to decide what the talks are for. A dialogue with no limit can run for years and produce nothing, as the two decades since the 2006 expression of sorrow by Tony Blair suggest.

But a deadline for starting talks is not a deadline for finishing them, and the reporting so far supports only the first. "Within a year" is a promise to turn up. The harder questions are the ones the communiqué leaves open: who sits at the table, whether Caribbean governments can put a financial remedy on the agenda, and what Britain will do if they insist.

Domestic politics narrows the room. Kemi Badenoch, the Conservative leader, argued there was "no need" to revisit a formal apology, and warned that one would give other countries a reason to demand compensation. Burnham's own ruling-out of payment gives him cover against that charge, but it also hands Caribbean leaders their sharpest complaint.

The precedent, if there is one, lies in the process. Other former colonial powers, including the Netherlands and France, have faced similar demands. A dated, multilateral dialogue with the largest of them is a template others could be asked to match.

What to watch

The first marker is the announcement of the terms of reference, covering who leads for each side and what the agenda includes. The second is whether the promised start date falls within the year. The third, and probably decisive, is whether the government's apology review concludes before or after the talks open. If it is delivered inside the process, it becomes a bargaining chip. If it comes first, it may be offered in place of a harder conversation about repair.
