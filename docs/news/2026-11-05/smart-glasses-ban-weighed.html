Government weighs smart-glasses ban over secret filming concerns

In one sentence
Ministers are reportedly considering restrictions on smart glasses over covert-filming fears, but no bill, timeline or specific measure has been disclosed.

Ministers are weighing whether to restrict smart glasses over fears that they can be used to film people without their knowledge. That is the whole of what is currently known. No bill, consultation, timetable or named minister has been attached to the idea, so it is better read as a signal than as policy.

The report comes from the Guardian's politics coverage. It gives no incident, figures or on-the-record quotes behind the concern, and this article has not been able to check it independently. What follows is therefore context, not confirmation of any plan.

What the devices are

Smart glasses look like ordinary spectacles but carry a small camera, microphones and speakers. The best-known mainstream examples, such as Meta's Ray-Ban line, can record photos and video hands-free, and many models can stream or hand footage to an AI assistant. They also carry a small indicator light meant to show when recording is under way.

That light is the crux of the privacy argument. A phone held up to film is visibly a camera. A pair of glasses is not, and a small LED is easy to miss in daylight, or to cover. Critics have long argued that the design makes covert filming easier. Manufacturers have pointed to the light and to rules against tampering with it as safeguards.

What a restriction could mean

"Restrictions" could cover a wide range of measures, and the report does not say which are under discussion. Possibilities include:

  • Rules on where recording glasses can be worn, such as changing rooms, schools, hospitals or courts.
  • Hardware requirements, such as a brighter, tamper-resistant recording indicator.
  • Changes to data protection or harassment law to address filming people without consent.
  • A broader ban on sale or use, the most drastic option and the least likely to be settled quickly.

These are illustrations of the range, not reported proposals. Existing law already touches parts of the problem: filming in public is generally lawful in the UK, but using footage to harass, or to capture images in private settings, can bring other offences into play. Any new regime would have to define where a wearable camera differs from a phone, which is not a simple line to draw.

Why it is worth watching

If the idea moves from discussion to measure, it would be among the first attempts by a government to regulate wearable recording devices as a category rather than case by case. That would matter to device makers, to privacy regulators and to employers and venues that already set their own rules. The effect on markets is likely to be small, since the product category is still niche next to smartphones.

The point to bear in mind is how early this is. Ministers "considering" something can mean anything from a passing remark to a drafted proposal. The signs of real movement would be a government consultation, a named bill or amendment, or guidance from the Information Commissioner's Office. None has been reported so far, and the Guardian's original report is the first thing to check for further detail.
