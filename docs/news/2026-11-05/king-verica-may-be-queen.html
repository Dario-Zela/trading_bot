Historians suggest King Verica, who urged Rome to invade Britain, may have been a queen

In one sentence
A reappraisal by archaeologist Miles Russell argues Verica, whose appeal to Rome helped trigger the AD 43 invasion, may have been a woman, though the evidence is interpretive.

A ruler long remembered as a fugitive king may have been a fugitive queen. Verica, the southern British leader whose flight to Rome handed the Emperor Claudius a reason to invade in AD 43, is the subject of a reappraisal arguing that the ruler was a woman. The case rests on a name ending, a coin inscription and a coin design. It is an argument, not a finding.

The man who went to Rome

The outline is old and fairly secure. Verica issued coins bearing the name VERICA, and is widely associated with the Atrebates, whose power centred on Calleva, modern Silchester. Suetonius, in his life of Claudius, and Cassius Dio both record that the ruler was driven out by their own people and fled to Rome to ask for help. Dio calls the exile Bericus, a variant generally taken to be the same person, though that identification is not wholly certain.

Aulus Plautius duly led Roman forces across the Channel in AD 43. Verica's appeal is usually listed among the justifications. A ruler who had been recognised as an ally of Rome and then expelled gave Claudius a tidy pretext, one he arguably needed, since the new emperor was keen for a military triumph of his own.

The case for a queen

The argument comes from Miles Russell, an archaeologist at Bournemouth University, who sets it out in his book Caratacus: Great King of Britain. As reported, it has three strands.

  • The name. Russell links the "-a" ending to women's names in Iron Age Britain.
  • The inscription. Coins were first read as Commios filius, "son of Commios". Russell says the abbreviation could equally stand for Commios filia, "daughter of Commios".
  • The imagery. One coin type shows a slender, bare-breasted rider. Russell says no other coin-issuing leader in Britain put a female warrior on their money.

He also notes that the Latin rex, usually rendered "king", was not reserved exclusively for men.

None of this is conclusive, and the reasoning invites caution. Names ending in "-a" are no guarantee of sex in a Celtic language rendered through Latin spelling. Abbreviated inscriptions are by nature open to more than one reading. A rider on a coin may be a ruler, a deity or a convention. Verica is also known only through a handful of Roman sources, none of which says the ruler was a woman, and Suetonius and Dio were not given to remarking on such things when the matter was an exile's petition.

What would change

The coverage found so far is largely press reports of Russell's claim. It does not include published responses from other numismatists or Roman historians, and standard references still describe Verica as male. If the reading holds, the practical effect on the story of the conquest is modest: the appeal, the exile and the invasion happen as before. What shifts is the picture of the people involved, and of how much authority a woman could hold in the pre-Roman south, a world that also produced Cartimandua of the Brigantes and, later, Boudica.

The test will come in print. Specialists in Iron Age coinage will want to examine the inscription readings coin by coin, and the reception of Russell's book in the classical and archaeological journals will show whether the reappraisal becomes a serious rival to the standard account or stays an interesting minority view.
