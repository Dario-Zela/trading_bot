Binance: fake job interviews used in $6.3bn hack

In one sentence
Binance says a staged job interview gave attackers a foothold that ended in a roughly $6.34bn wallet theft; the FBI blames North Korean hackers.

Binance says the biggest theft in crypto history began with a job offer. According to the exchange's own account of the breach, a software engineer was approached through a job-hunting site, put through two staged interviews and handed a "coding assignment" that turned out to be malware. Five weeks later, attackers had reached the wallet systems and moved about $6.34bn in digital assets.

The account comes from Binance's incident report, as relayed by BleepingComputer, NBC News and others. The BBC Technology report named in the brief could not be located in this search. The details below rest on Binance's own statements, which independent security firms have been brought in to review, and the investigation is continuing.

How the approach worked

Binance says the first contact came in September. Someone posing as a Project Director at a fictitious company messaged the engineer. His profile mentioned past Binance experience, which seems to be why he was chosen.

There were two interviews. In the first, a fake HR representative held a live video call and shared a social media account full of personal photos and videos, apparently to build trust. In the second, a person claiming to be the company's CEO ran the conversation over a chat app and asked the engineer to download a coding assignment from an online code repository.

Binance says that code installed custom malware that endpoint security tools did not detect. It reportedly swapped a legitimate macOS Homebrew pyenv executable for a malicious one, which let the attackers run commands from an outside server.

From one laptop to the wallet system

By October 20, Binance says, the attackers had used the engineer's machine and an internal AI tool to compromise two more employees on the wallet infrastructure team. That gave them credentials and a container deployment token. On October 29 they used those to deploy a malicious script, reach the wallet signing service and move funds across several blockchains.

Binance also says the attackers abused Ethereum's EIP-7702 authorisation mechanism to keep draining assets after the exchange suspended withdrawals.

The chain matters more than the first step. The interview was only the way in. The damage came from the engineer's laptop having a path to production credentials, and from a few staff holding keys that mattered.

Who, and how much has come back

Binance has not tied the employee compromise to a specific campaign. It says the tactics resemble documented North Korean operations known as "Contagious Interview" and "Operation Dream Job". The FBI has separately attributed the theft to North Korean state-sponsored hackers, a group tracked as TraderTraitor, Lazarus, Jade Sleet and APT38. Pyongyang has rejected such claims. That attribution is the FBI's, not Binance's.

As of November 3, about $2.85bn of the stolen assets had been frozen or intercepted. Binance says 98% of the affected token assets had been replenished by October 31, and that users will not bear the losses. The reports do not say how the replenishment was funded or which users were affected. Coverage also varies between $6.3bn, $6.34bn and "about $6bn", and the exact figure may still change.

What to watch

The test is whether the independent reviews confirm Binance's timeline, and whether the FBI or Binance publish indicators of compromise that other firms can check against their own systems. If the account holds, the practical lesson for exchanges and banks is narrow. Staff who hold signing or deployment access need hiring-style contact handled as untrusted, and they should not run outside code on machines connected to production.
