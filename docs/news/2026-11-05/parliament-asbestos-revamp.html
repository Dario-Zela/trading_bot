More asbestos found in Parliament as MPs urged to get on with the revamp

The Palace of Westminster, seen from the dome of Methodist Central Hall. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The known asbestos count in Parliament has risen to over 1,650 despite removals, and a Commons committee says MPs should vote on the restoration plan before the end of 2026.

The Palace of Westminster has more known asbestos than it did five years ago, despite a sustained effort to remove it. That finding, from a Freedom of Information request by the BBC, has landed in the same week as a parliamentary report urging MPs to settle how, and when, the building will be repaired.

The number of known asbestos items across the Palace and the wider parliamentary estate now stands at more than 1,650, up about 500 since 2021. Roughly 700 items were removed over that period, so the rise reflects new discoveries rather than neglect of the old ones. Surveys of a building this size tend to find more the harder they look.

A risk managed, not removed

Under Health and Safety Executive guidelines, most of the material is classed as low risk because it is concealed and undisturbed. Almost 200 items are rated medium risk and may need remedial work. The Commons authorities say an asbestos-free building "cannot be achieved in practice".

That is partly a matter of age. The Grade I listed Palace, designed by Charles Barry and Augustus Pugin and largely completed in the 1870s, has been patched and rewired repeatedly over the 20th century. Asbestos was widely used in building work until it was banned in the UK in 1999. Its presence is handled under safety plans while MPs, peers and thousands of staff continue to work there.

Campaigners are not reassured by the "low risk" label. Gledhill, whose mother, the former Labour MP Alice Mahon, died of mesothelioma in 2022, said "asbestos should never be considered low risk". Dr Hannah Moody, chief executive of Mesothelioma UK, backs a national asbestos register and phased removal from public buildings.

The price of waiting

The asbestos is one strand of a larger problem. The Restoration and Renewal programme exists because the building's Victorian mechanical, electrical and plumbing systems are near the end of their life. The Public Accounts Committee published its inquiry on Wednesday 4 November and called it "crunch time". It put the cost of each further year of delay at £420m.

The committee's chair, the Conservative MP Geoffrey Clifton-Brown, was blunt about the fire risk. Despite round-the-clock fire patrols, he said, if a small fire broke out "the whole lot would go up". He described a "real and rising... grave risk" and criticised a "political allergy" to refurbishment.

The options on the table differ sharply in scale:

  • Full decant: MPs and peers move out for up to 24 years from 2032, at a cost of up to £15.6bn.
  • Lords-only move: only the House of Lords leaves, at a cost of £39bn over 61 years.

The most expensive full-refurbishment scenarios run to as much as £40bn over 61 years.

The committee also identified "critical" setbacks. These include an overcomplex decision-making process and delays in securing a temporary home for the Lords at the government-owned Queen Elizabeth II conference centre.

What happens next

The committee says MPs and peers already have enough information to vote on a way forward before the end of 2026. That vote would not settle the whole programme. It would approve up to £3bn of initial works and narrow the four options, with a final decision not due until 2030.

The BBC understands that government whips have discussed a debate and vote before the year ends, but no date has been set. The asbestos count is unlikely to change the choice between options by itself. Its weight is political: each new find is another reason MPs will struggle to argue that the status quo is safe. Whether a vote is scheduled in the coming weeks is the thing to watch.
