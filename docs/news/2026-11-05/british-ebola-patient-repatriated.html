British man infected with Ebola in DR Congo to be flown back to UK

In one sentence
A British national infected in the DR Congo Ebola outbreak is to be repatriated for treatment; no change in UK protocol or sign of wider case export has been reported.

A British national who has been infected with Ebola in the Democratic Republic of Congo is to be flown back to the United Kingdom for treatment, according to BBC reporting. The patient's identity, condition and destination hospital, and the date of the flight, were not available to this desk. They are not stated here.

This article rests on that single report. Independent confirmation of the case from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) or the Department of Health could not be found at the time of writing.

What is known

Repatriating an infected national is established practice for high-consequence infectious diseases. The UK has done it before: in August 2024's precedent-setting predecessor, a British healthcare worker infected in Sierra Leone in 2014 was flown home on a specially equipped RAF C-17 and treated in the isolation unit at the Royal Free Hospital in London[^1]. Nothing in the available reporting suggests a change of protocol this time.

There is also a recent, related episode. In July 2026 UKHSA announced that a UK-resident humanitarian worker, who had been treating Ebola patients in the DRC, had been evacuated on a dedicated chartered aircraft after a possible healthcare-related exposure. That person had no symptoms and was monitored in isolation at a London hospital. Richard Pebody, UKHSA's director of epidemic and emerging infections, said at the time: "The risk to the general public remains low"[^2]. That was a precautionary evacuation, not a confirmed infection, and there is no evidence it concerns the same person.

The outbreak behind it

The disease is Bundibugyo-virus Ebola. The World Health Organization declared the outbreak a public health emergency of international concern on 17 May 2026. Unlike Zaire-strain Ebola, Bundibugyo has no approved vaccine or specific therapeutic, which narrows what clinicians can offer a patient and raises the importance of supportive care[^3].

The outbreak is large. WHO reported 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths as of 30 July, and described it in early August as the largest Ebola outbreak recorded in the DRC. Later figures reported by secondary outlets run higher, including 6,686 confirmed cases and 3,226 deaths in a September report citing the Congolese government. Those later numbers have not been checked against WHO situation reports, and a current count should be taken from WHO or the DRC health ministry[^3].

What would change the picture

An individual medevac of this kind is not, on its own, evidence of wider case export. Patients moved under strict isolation, by dedicated aircraft, into specialist units pose a very low onward risk; the UKHSA line on July's evacuation was that no confirmed Ebola cases existed in the UK. The story would become more significant on three conditions: if the patient was infected through community rather than occupational exposure, if contacts on the way to the airport or in transit were identified, or if UKHSA announced new handling or screening arrangements for travellers.

The Ebola incubation period runs from two to 21 days, so any contacts would be monitored for that full window. The next useful data points are a UKHSA statement on the patient's condition and contact tracing, and the next WHO situation report on case counts and geographic spread.
