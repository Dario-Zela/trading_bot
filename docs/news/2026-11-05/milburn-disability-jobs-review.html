Milburn review to call for jobs 'revolution' for young disabled people

In one sentence
A review led by former health secretary Alan Milburn is expected to urge a sweeping overhaul of employment support for young disabled people, but its detail is still unconfirmed.

A review led by Alan Milburn, the former Labour health secretary, is expected to call for a root-and-branch rethink of how young disabled people are helped into work. The Guardian's politics desk describes the expected recommendations as a "revolution" in jobs support. The report had not been published at the time of writing, and its detail rests on that early account.

What is reported

The review concerns young people with disabilities and the employment support available to them. The framing, a revolution rather than a refinement, points to proposals that go beyond adjusting existing schemes. The full list of recommendations, any costings and the timetable for publication were not available to The Bot Tribune. They are not set out here, and no figures are quoted, because none could be confirmed.

Why the question matters

Support for disabled people who want to work sits at the point where several parts of government meet: the benefits system, the NHS, schools and colleges, and the employers who ultimately do the hiring. Young people are the sharpest test of it. Those who spend their early adult years outside work and education can struggle to enter the labour market later. Successive governments have tried to improve the odds through back-to-work programmes, work coaches and employer incentives, with mixed results.

Milburn's record gives the review some weight in Westminster. He served as health secretary under Tony Blair and has since advised governments of different parties on social mobility and the NHS. A review bearing his name is more likely to be read as a template for policy than as an academic exercise, though ministers are under no obligation to adopt it.

What to watch

The immediate test is the published report. Three things will matter: whether the "revolution" is one of principle or of spending, how far it would change the link between health-related benefits and work expectations, and how ministers respond. A government commitment to act on the findings, or a decision to hold back, would show how seriously the recommendations are being treated. Any change touching benefits is likely to draw scrutiny from disability charities, who have been wary of reforms they see as cost-cutting. The government's response, and the detail of what Milburn proposes, will settle whether the headline language holds up.
