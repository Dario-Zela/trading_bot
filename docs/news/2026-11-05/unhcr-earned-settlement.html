UNHCR criticises UK's 30-year 'earned settlement' plans for refugees

In one sentence
The UN refugee agency says Britain's earned-settlement rules, with waits of up to 30 years for some refugees, raise significant concerns and risk harming refugees' security and stability.

The United Nations refugee agency has publicly criticised the British government's plans to make refugees wait as long as 30 years for permanent settlement. It is the sharpest international objection yet to the "earned settlement" regime unveiled this week by Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood.

The agency, UNHCR, told the Guardian that lengthening the qualifying period "raises significant concerns". It said the change was "likely to be detrimental" to refugees' sense of security, belonging and stability. It also reminded ministers of a principle that sits at the centre of the dispute: "Refugees are not migrants." The Guardian did not name a UNHCR spokesperson.

What the rules would do

Under the current system, most people granted protection can apply for indefinite leave to remain after five years. Earned settlement replaces that fixed point with a sliding scale. According to the Guardian's account, refugees face an upper limit of 30 years before they can settle. Entering work or study can shorten the wait. People who arrived by irregular means, such as small boats or hidden in lorries, would carry an additional 10-year penalty.

Ms Mahmood softened some of the wider immigration proposals when she presented the package. One carve-out lets care workers apply for settlement after five years. But she kept the strict core of the rules drawn up last year. Other reporting on the consultation has described shorter baselines for some refugee categories, including a period of around 20 years, so the precise figure that applies to a given person depends on the route they took and what they go on to do. The Home Office's final rule text should be read against the newspaper's headline number.

Why the agency objects

UNHCR's argument is practical as well as legal. Settlement is the point at which a refugee stops renewing short-term leave, stops reapplying and can plan a life: take on a mortgage, change jobs without worrying about status, and eventually apply for citizenship. A longer road means more years on temporary permission, and the agency has said before that rolling short grants create prolonged uncertainty that affects work, housing and integration.

It welcomed the government's new legal routes. But it said the short-term stay arrangements and last year's changes would stop many people ever reaching citizenship. The Guardian reports that the government has sharply reduced safe and legal routes over time, and that the new ones will be open only to a small minority of those seeking sanctuary.

There is a treaty angle too. The 1951 Refugee Convention, to which Britain is a party, says states should as far as possible facilitate the assimilation and naturalisation of refugees. It also bars penalising refugees for irregular entry where they come directly from danger and present themselves without delay. A 10-year surcharge for boat arrivals sits uneasily with that wording, though ministers have not conceded the point and the Guardian piece carries no direct Home Office statement beyond the announcement itself.

A widening front of criticism

UNHCR is not alone. Kamena Dorling, policy director at the Helen Bamber Foundation, said she was "deeply alarmed and disappointed". She said ministers had "doubled down" despite evidence of harm, called the extension "simply inhumane", and argued it would not deter people fleeing war or persecution. Its main effect, she said, would be to leave recognised refugees facing years of uncertainty. Earlier this year more than 120 organisations, coordinated by the Joint Council for the Welfare of Immigrants, objected to the proposals in similar terms.

The government's case rests on public confidence. Ministers argue that settlement should be earned through contribution rather than granted by default, and that a harder line on irregular arrivals is needed to restore trust in the asylum system.

What to watch

The next test is the detail. The immigration rules changes have to be laid before Parliament, and the final text will show how long each category of refugee actually waits, and how much work and study credit can reduce it. Whether MPs, including some on the government benches, press for amendments will show whether UNHCR's intervention changes the argument or simply adds to a long list of objections.
