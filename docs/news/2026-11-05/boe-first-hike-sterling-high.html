BANK RATE · By M. Halloway · 2026-11-05 · 4 min read

Bank of England expected to raise rates for the first time in over three years as sterling hits a ten-year high

In one sentence
The Bank of England is expected to lift Bank Rate to 4.00% today, its first rise since August 2023, with the vote split and guidance showing how far the MPC now prioritises inflation.

The Bank of England is expected to raise Bank Rate by a quarter point to 4.00% at midday today. That would be its first increase since August 2023 and would reverse the cutting cycle it began in August 2024. Markets treat the move as nearly settled. Overnight index swap pricing cited on 4 November implied about a 96% chance of a 25 basis point rise, and prediction-market odds were in the low 90s. The open question is how many of the nine members of the Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) vote for it, and what the Bank says about what follows.

A cycle in reverse

The last rise came in August 2023, to 5.25%. It was the 14th in a row, a run that began in December 2021. The Bank then held for a year and began easing in August 2024. By this autumn Bank Rate stood at 3.75%.

The committee did not arrive at today's decision suddenly. At its 16 September meeting it voted 6–3 to hold at 3.75%, with the three dissenters voting for a rise to 4%. That vote is the best evidence for the interpretation at the heart of today's decision: a committee in which a third of the members already judged inflation the larger risk, and which has had seven weeks to move toward them.

Today is also a Monetary Policy Report meeting, so the Bank will publish fresh forecasts alongside the decision.

What the vote will show

The headline rate is priced in, so the vote split and guidance carry the information. Forecasts differ. ING expects a 7–2 vote for the rise. Ebury has said a divided committee is likely and that a 6–3 split would be a negative risk for the pound.

The arithmetic of the split is simple, though its reading is not:

  • A 7–2 or wider vote would suggest the September dissenters brought colleagues with them, and that the hike is a shift of priorities rather than a one-off correction.
  • A narrow 5–4 or 6–3 vote would suggest the committee is still divided over whether inflation or weak growth is the bigger danger. A small majority makes a quick follow-up rise harder to sustain.
  • Any dissent in the other direction, for holding rather than raising, would be a signal that the hike is contested at the margin.

The forward guidance matters at least as much. Money markets are not pricing a single rise. According to LSEG data cited by FX analysts, they fully price four quarter-point increases by September 2027, which would take Bank Rate to 4.75%. If the Bank's language or forecasts lean against that path, the gap between pricing and policy could close quickly.

Sterling and the dollar

The pound has been rising into the decision, though accounts of how far differ. The BBC has described sterling as reaching its strongest level since the June 2016 Brexit referendum. FX desk commentary on Wednesday put the pound at its highest against the dollar since 15 September. These are not necessarily contradictory, since the two statements may use different measures. The BBC's exact level and measure could not be confirmed here, and the ten-year-high description rests on that single report.

Both accounts credit two drivers. One is the expected Bank of England tightening, which makes sterling assets more attractive. The other is a weaker dollar, which analysts tied this week to the Democrats' midterm gains. The Federal Reserve held at 3.75%–4.00% on 28 October, with two officials, Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack, voting for a quarter-point rise. The Fed is therefore not clearly easing, so the dollar's softness is unlikely to be explained by rates alone.

The relative weight of the two drivers cannot be separated from the available data. Some market commentary suggests much of the optimism is already in the price, and that further gains may depend on the vote and guidance rather than the rise itself. That makes the pound's reaction a test of the second point, whether the hike path is as steep as markets assume.

Lenders and households

A rise passes through quickly. Variable-rate mortgages and loans follow Bank Rate directly, and savings rates usually rise with them, though banks tend to pass on increases to borrowers faster than to savers.

Bank shares are in focus for the same reason. Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest earn margin on the gap between lending and deposit rates, which usually widens as rates rise. One stock-analysis publisher, Simply Wall St, has pointed to the three as exposed to the outlook. That is not a corroborated assessment of how the margins would move. A steeper rate path would also raise concerns about borrowers' ability to repay, which cuts against the margin benefit.

What to watch

The decision and vote are due at 12:00 UK time, with the Monetary Policy Report and the Governor's press conference following. The key figures are the vote count, the Bank's inflation projection and whether it endorses the four-rise path in market pricing. Next in the calendar are the data releases that feed the committee's next meeting: the inflation and labour-market figures that will test whether the 25 basis point rise was the start of a tightening cycle or a single correction.
