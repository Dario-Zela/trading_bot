Jamal Musiala reveals treatable neurological condition after second on-pitch collapse

Jamal Musiala playing for Germany at the 2026 World Cup, two months before his back-to-back collapses in Bayern Munich preseason friendlies. — Bryan Berlin / WikiPortraits, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Musiala collapsed twice in four days and disclosed treatable absence seizures — but Bayern had already cleared him to play once before the second collapse.

Jamal Musiala went down without contact in the 66th minute against Heidenheim on Tuesday night, eight minutes after coming on as a substitute. Bayern Munich's medical staff sprinted onto the pitch. Teammates formed a loose ring around him, the way footballers do when something looks more serious than a knock to the shin. He got back to his feet unaided, jogged briefly, then was substituted off. It was the second time in four days it had happened.

On Thursday, the 23-year-old explained why, in a statement posted to Instagram rather than through the club. "I have been diagnosed with brief, temporary — but treatable — absence seizures caused by a neurological dysfunction," he wrote, adding that "there is no further health risk involved" and that he was "receiving excellent medical care" from a team he described as keeping him "very optimistic." He said the decision to keep playing was his own, made "with the medical clearance of the neurologists."

Two collapses, one explanation missing

The first episode came on Saturday against RB Leipzig, in a preseason friendly that drew little attention until Musiala hit the turf. At the time, head coach Vincent Kompany attributed it to a combination of heat and what he called Musiala's "recent medical struggles," and said tests afterward had turned up nothing significant. On that basis, Kompany named him in the squad for Heidenheim four days later. Musiala collapsed again.

Absence seizures are a form of generalised epilepsy in which the brain briefly interrupts conscious activity — typically for only a few seconds, sometimes with no obvious external sign beyond a blank stare or a stumble, sometimes severe enough to drop a person to the ground. They are common in children, less so in adults, and are usually managed successfully with medication once diagnosed. Clinically, they're considered one of the more benign points on the epilepsy spectrum. Guidance from sports neurologists is fairly consistent on the underlying principle, too: contact and collision sports are not inherently more likely to trigger a seizure, and athletes with well-controlled epilepsy are routinely cleared to compete. The caveat sits in that word "controlled." Standard advice is that seizures should be stabilised before an athlete returns to play — precisely the condition that a second collapse, four days after the first, suggests may not yet have been met.

A disclosure that didn't change the plan

What makes the Musiala case an interesting test of institutional practice, rather than just a health story, is the order of events. Bayern's medical staff examined him after Leipzig, found nothing they judged disqualifying, and played him again. Only after the second collapse — and Musiala's own public disclosure — did a specific neurological diagnosis attach itself to what had happened. The club has not published its own account of the clearance process, referring instead to Musiala's statement and confirming it is "supporting him alongside his medical team and personal network." Kompany has not said whether he would have selected Musiala for Heidenheim knowing what is now known.

That sequencing is the uncomfortable part. Player-driven medical transparency, of the kind Musiala offered on Instagram, is generally treated as a good outcome — athletes disclosing conditions rather than concealing them, on their own terms. But transparency after the fact doesn't answer the question of whether the club's return-to-play threshold was set correctly the first time. A collapse attributed to heat, cleared within days, followed by an identical collapse in the very next match, is the kind of pattern that governing bodies and independent medical panels — not the player alone — are usually asked to sign off on before a return, not after.

What Bayern do next

Musiala is not a marginal player for either Bayern or Germany. He is the youngest player to have scored on his Bundesliga debut, the youngest to score in the Champions League, and shared the Golden Boot at Euro 2024 with three goals on home soil. He started for Germany at this summer's World Cup. Whatever medical protocol Bayern applies to him now will be scrutinised well beyond the club, not least because Bayern face Borussia Dortmund in the DFL Supercup on Saturday, then open their Bundesliga title defence days later — the first real test of whether "treatable" and "cleared" hold up once the games start counting.

The next marker to watch isn't a scan or a statement — it's simply whether Musiala takes the field this weekend, and whether he does so with a public account, from Bayern rather than from him, of what changed in the assessment between Leipzig and Heidenheim.
