Ukraine names new defence minister as sacked predecessor calls for elections

Mykhailo Fedorov, sacked as Ukraine's defence minister in July, called for wartime elections in a video address on 18 August. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Hours after Zelensky named Yevhenii Khmara defence minister, his popular predecessor Mykhailo Fedorov broke wartime consensus to demand Ukraine find a way to hold elections.

Kyiv spent Tuesday absorbing two announcements that, on paper, belonged to separate news cycles. In the morning, President Volodymyr Zelensky sent parliament the paperwork to formally install Yevhenii Khmara as Ukraine's defence minister and confirm Andrii Sybiha as foreign minister, closing out a five-week vacancy at the top of the wartime cabinet. By evening, the man Khmara replaced — Mykhailo Fedorov, sacked from the same job in mid-July — had posted a video address arguing that Ukraine should find a way to hold a presidential election before the war is over.

The two events are, in effect, one story. Fedorov's video landed hours after Zelensky's nomination became public, and Ukrainian political circles read the timing as deliberate: the ex-minister waiting for his replacement's confirmation to force his own re-entry into the conversation. Whatever the intent, the juxtaposition is uncomfortable for a government trying to project unity while fighting a grinding war — a defence ministry finally settled, and in the same breath, its most popular recent occupant challenging the legal basis on which the presidency itself continues to sit.

A minister undone by his own reforms

Fedorov, 35, arrived at the defence ministry in January after three and a half years as deputy prime minister for digital transformation, where he built Ukraine's Diia app and became the public face of the country's drone procurement drive. He lasted six months. Zelensky dismissed him on 15 July, citing friction with commander-in-chief Oleksandr Syrskyi and weapons purchases made against the military's wishes. Fedorov has told a different story: that his push to overhaul procurement and break up entrenched interests inside the ministry — a notoriously corruption-prone institution even in wartime — triggered the backlash that ended his tenure.

Whichever account is closer to true, the dismissal backfired politically. Protests broke out across Ukrainian cities within days, and Fedorov's trust ratings — by some polling, up from roughly 35% to 65% in the week after his ouster — overtook Zelensky's own. A survey in late July put Fedorov in third place for a first-round presidential vote, behind Zelensky and former commander-in-chief Valerii Zaluzhnyi, but ahead in a hypothetical runoff: one poll had him beating Zelensky 63.8% to 36.2% head-to-head. Analysts caution the surge is likely a protest-driven spike rather than a durable base, but it is large enough that Kyiv has had to take it seriously.

"We must not let Putin decide"

Fedorov's Tuesday address did not announce a candidacy — Ukrainian law bars elections under martial law, and no vote can be scheduled while it remains in force. Instead, he argued that the suspension itself needs revisiting. "We must not let Putin decide when Ukrainians can choose their government," he said, adding: "Democracy is not a peacetime luxury. Democracy is part of what we are fighting for today." He went further on the substance of his own removal, saying "power must mean responsibility" and that "people do not demand that the state never make mistakes. But they have a right to the truth."

It is, by most accounts, the first public call of its kind from a senior Ukrainian political figure since the full-scale invasion began in 2022 — a genuine break from the wartime consensus that elections wait until the guns stop. Zelensky's own five-year presidential term technically expired in 2024; he has governed since under the martial-law provision that freezes elections, a position most Western allies have accepted as reasonable given active Russian occupation of Ukrainian territory, displaced voters, and frontline soldiers who cannot easily cast ballots. Fedorov's address does not dispute the logistics so much as the principle that the debate should stay closed.

Filling the ministry, not the argument

Khmara, a 45-year-old major general, is a security-services rather than a political appointment. He led the 2022 operation to recapture Snake Island, commanded the SBU's Alpha special-operations unit, and briefly served as acting head of the SBU itself before Zelensky moved him into the defence ministry as caretaker on 16 July, the day after Fedorov's dismissal. He has now agreed to leave active military service, a legal requirement for a civilian ministerial post, clearing the way for a parliamentary confirmation vote. Sybiha, who has run Ukrainian diplomacy since September 2024, stays in place — the nomination is largely a formality that closes out the foreign-ministry side of the reshuffle rather than changing course.

The contrast in profiles is itself a signal. Where Fedorov was a reformer who picked fights with procurement insiders and the general staff, Khmara is a special-operations veteran with no comparable record of institutional confrontation. Zelensky's choice reads as a bet on continuity and operational focus over further disruption to a ministry that has now had three effective leadership changes in seven months — Fedorov's predecessor Rustem Umerov, Fedorov himself, and now Khmara.

What the argument is really about

Strip away the personnel details and the two threads point at the same underlying anxiety: what happens to Ukrainian democratic legitimacy once, or if, the war winds down. Fedorov's polling strength gives his elections argument teeth it would otherwise lack — this is not a marginal critic making an abstract constitutional point, but a man whose runoff numbers suggest he could plausibly beat the sitting president if a vote were held tomorrow. That combination — genuine popularity plus a direct challenge to the legal basis of the current government's mandate — is precisely the kind of internal fissure Ukraine's Western backers have hoped to avoid airing in public while the war continues, and precisely the kind that becomes far harder to contain once a peace process, however partial, gets underway.

For now, nothing changes mechanically: martial law stays in force, no election can be scheduled, and Khmara's confirmation vote in the Verkhovna Rada — expected within days — will fill the defence ministry regardless of what Fedorov says next. But the political undertow is now visible in a way it was not a month ago. The next marker to watch is whether other senior figures, particularly Zaluzhnyi, who has so far stayed above the fray, follow Fedorov in questioning the wartime consensus — and whether Zelensky's office responds with anything beyond silence, which is what it has offered so far.
