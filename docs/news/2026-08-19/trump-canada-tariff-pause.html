Trump pauses 50% Canada tariffs for three days pending deal finalisation

President Trump and Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney meet in the Oval Office. — Daniel Torok / Official White House Photo
In one sentence
Trump paused 50% tariffs on Canadian beer, cheese and other goods for three days after a last-minute call with Carney — but the two leaders gave conflicting accounts of whether a deal actually exists.

The 50% tariffs on Canadian beer, cheese, cement and hockey sticks were due to take effect at midnight Tuesday. They did not. Hours before the deadline, President Trump announced he was pausing the levies for three days, citing an agreement with Ottawa that both sides describe rather differently — a gap that tells its own story about how unsettled this remains.

"I have paused the 50% Tariffs against Canada, that were scheduled to kick in tomorrow morning for a three day period, based on the fact that Canada and the U.S.A., subject to the finalization of documents, have a DEAL!" Trump wrote on Truth Social late Tuesday. He added, for good measure, that the Keystone XL pipeline could be "awoken from the grave."

Prime Minister Mark Carney's statement, released around the same time, was notably more clipped. "Substantial progress has been made, although there is important work still to be done," he said, confirming only that the tariffs would be held off until the end of the day Friday, August 21. No deal, in other words — a truce.

A law from 1930, revived

The tariffs at issue were never ordinary. Signed on July 20 under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 — a Smoot-Hawley-era provision that, by most trade lawyers' accounts, had never actually been invoked before — the measure imposed an additional 50% duty on close to $20 billion of Canadian goods, roughly 5% of total U.S. imports from Canada. The list ran well past the headline categories of autos, alcohol and dairy: cement, plywood, furniture, clothing, swimming pools and fishing rods all made the annex.

The administration framed the move as retaliation for Canadian trade practices — provincial liquor board bans that had cut U.S. alcohol exports to Canada by roughly 81%, and a dairy quota system that Washington says still shuts out American producers despite USMCA commitments. Ottawa disputes both characterizations, and a USMCA dispute panel has previously split the difference, with each side notching a win.

The backdrop matters: USMCA is currently under its mandated 2026 review, after Washington declined to simply renew it outright. Auto tariffs — a Section 232 measure separate from the Section 338 duties — are part of the same negotiation, with talk of cutting the rate on Canadian vehicles from 25% to 15%, scaled to U.S. content. The beer-and-cheese tariffs functioned as leverage inside that larger, slower negotiation, not as a stand-alone dispute.

What moved and why

The Canadian dollar firmed on the news, trading USD/CAD down toward the 1.388 area, close to its 200-day moving average, though the move owed at least as much to oil as to tariff relief. Crude climbed to a near three-week high on the Strait of Hormuz standoff, and Canada's status as a major exporter means firmer oil tends to lift the loonie almost mechanically. Separating the tariff-relief signal from the oil-price signal is not straightforward here — both pushed the same direction at once.

Wheat futures moved more cleanly on the tariff news itself: Chicago SRW contracts rose 5 to 7 cents, Kansas City HRW gained 8 to 10 cents, and Minneapolis spring wheat added 5 to 6 cents, consistent with a market pricing out — for now — a disruption to North American grain and dairy trade flows. Agricultural markets tend to react fast to tariff headlines and slow to their reversal, which is itself a reason to watch the Friday deadline closely rather than treat this week's move as settled.

Three days is not a resolution

The asymmetry between Trump's "DEAL" and Carney's "important work still to be done" is the whole story compressed into two sentences. Trump did not specify what the agreement covers, nor commit to cancelling the tariffs outright if the paperwork is signed. Carney did not confirm a deal exists at all — only that talks continue and the clock has been reset to Friday.

That leaves traders with a binary sitting three days out: either negotiators land on paper what Trump has already announced in substance, or the 50% duties reactivate automatically with no further notice required, since the underlying proclamations were merely paused, not withdrawn. Canadian officials met for nearly two hours this week with U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick — a sign the remaining gaps are in the technical language of quotas and rules of origin, not in broad political will on either side.

What to watch

Friday's end-of-day deadline is the immediate catalyst — either a signed agreement lands, tariffs resume, or the pause gets extended again, which would itself say something about how close the two sides actually are. Beyond that, the USMCA review process continues independently and will likely determine whether this week's dispute is remembered as a one-off flashpoint or the opening skirmish in a longer renegotiation. Until documents are finalized, positioning in CAD, North American grain futures and Canadian export-linked equities should be read as reflecting a three-day reprieve, not a settled outcome.
