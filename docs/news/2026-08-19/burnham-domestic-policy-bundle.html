Burnham unveils domestic policy push: rough sleeping drive, bus concessions and public ownership

Andy Burnham, who became UK prime minister on 20 July 2026, has made rough sleeping his signature domestic policy. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham pledged £442m to house every rough sleeper by Christmas, alongside 24-hour free bus travel for disabled people and a West Midlands bus buyout — social policy with no defined success metric.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for exactly a month. He has spent a good chunk of it on buses.

On Tuesday his government unveiled its first coordinated push into domestic policy since he entered Downing Street on 20 July — a three-pronged rollout that touches rough sleeping, disability transport and, a few hundred miles north-west of Westminster's usual concerns, who owns the buses in the West Midlands. None of it moves a gilt yield. All of it says something about the kind of prime minister Burnham intends to be, and about the limits he's already running into.

A bed for everyone by Christmas

The centrepiece is a pledge that rough sleeping in England will be met with "a route off the streets" for every person by Christmas. Burnham has framed it as his personal mission — reportedly his first instruction to officials on taking office was to end rough sleeping — and the government has now put money behind it: £442 million in funding, on top of £340 million already committed over three years, to roll out the A Bed Every Night model Burnham built as mayor of Greater Manchester across the whole of England.

"No one should have to bed down in a doorway or outside a station," Burnham said in announcing the scheme.

Shelter's chief executive, Sarah Elliott, called it "a game-changing move." Big Issue founder Lord John Bird was warmer but more wary, cautioning that people taken off the streets this winter must not simply "be sent back out again" once the cold snap passes, without the mental-health and addiction support to make it stick.

That caution is grounded in Burnham's own record. The Greater Manchester scheme he is now exporting nationally cut rough sleeping there from 268 people to 151 between 2017 and 2019 — a genuine result — but the count had climbed back to 197 by last autumn. Nationally, the latest official snapshot put rough sleeping at 4,793 people on a single night last autumn, a record high and the fourth consecutive annual rise. Downing Street has been careful not to attach a number to "real progress" by Christmas, which leaves the pledge without the kind of benchmark that would let anyone mark it a success or a failure in four months' time.

Buses, twice over

The second strand is smaller in ambition but larger in immediate reach: free bus travel for disabled pass-holders in England will run 24 hours a day, up from the current 09:30–23:00 weekday window that has long excluded early shifts, morning hospital appointments and school runs. The change is funded at £60 million — £40 million from the Department for Work and Pensions, £20 million from the Department for Transport — and is expected to benefit around a million people.

"Disabled people face extra costs every day and many need to travel in the morning for work, education and healthcare," said James Taylor of the disability charity Scope. "Making free bus travel available around the clock is a welcome step that will put money back in people's pockets and remove barriers to everyday life."

The third bus story is really someone else's, though Burnham has been happy to stand next to it. In the West Midlands, mayor Richard Parker has pushed through an emergency buyout of National Express West Midlands, the region's dominant operator, paying around £102 million for a business that runs 94% of local bus journeys, 1,300 vehicles, nine depots and employs roughly 4,800 staff. Parker moved after the operator signalled it would walk away from the network.

"I wasn't prepared to stand by and watch hundreds of buses disappear from our roads because a private company decided to walk away," Parker said. "They are a public service and I believe they should be run in the public interest. So we stepped in."

Burnham, visiting Wolverhampton hours after the deal closed, called the purchase "absolutely massive" — an odd verb for a transaction roughly the size of a single Championship football club's annual wage bill, but a telling one. The West Midlands buyout is the most aggressive test yet of the municipal-ownership model Burnham himself pioneered in Manchester, where franchising has already reshaped fares and routes. If it holds, other combined authorities eyeing their own bus markets — several are watching closely — will have a second live case study, not just Manchester's, to point to.

The common thread

What ties the three together is not economics — the Treasury connection here is thin, a few hundred million pounds of committed spend against a government that is otherwise absorbed by gilt yields and a widening fiscal gap. It's an argument about what government is for. Burnham built his political career on retail, visible interventions — Housing First, free bus passes, municipal ownership — delivered at the city-region level, and he is now trying to run the same playbook at national scale, at national cost, on a national clock.

The test is not whether the announcements land well this week. It's whether "everyone in for Christmas" produces a rough sleeping count that's visibly lower in the January statistics, whether the West Midlands can run a franchised network without the funding gaps that have dogged Manchester's, and whether a prime minister who made his name outrunning Whitehall from the outside can now deliver at Whitehall's pace from within it. The next rough sleeping count, expected in the new year, is the first hard number against which any of this can be judged.
