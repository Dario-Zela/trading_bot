Vinted sellers complain of bans over minor listing disputes

Vinted's marketplace has grown to more than 16 million UK users, intensifying scrutiny of how it enforces seller rules. — Vinted / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Vinted sellers say accounts are being permanently banned over trivial disputes — including one over £2 sandals — with little clear path to human review.

A pair of £2 flip-flops does not sound like the sort of thing that ends an online business. But according to sellers who spoke to BBC News, that is roughly the scale of dispute that has been enough to get an account permanently shut on Vinted, the Lithuania-founded resale marketplace now valued at roughly €8bn ($9bn).

The complaints follow a pattern: a buyer flags a problem with a minor, low-value listing, the platform's enforcement system suspends the seller's account, and no clear route to a human reviewer follows before the ban is made permanent. Sellers describe losing not just a single sale but years of accumulated reviews, wallet balances and — for those running Vinted as a side income — their entire secondhand business, over infractions that would barely register on a platform moving hundreds of millions of listings a year.

A pattern, not an anecdote

The sandals case is not an outlier. A grassroots campaign called Sad Vinted Faces, started in June by a seller named Catherine Warrilow after her own account was restricted, has collected testimony from more than 3,700 users. A survey of 1,909 respondents found 59% had been banned at some point, a third of those permanently, and 40% said they had lost money as a result — a self-reported total of roughly £95,000-£109,000 across the group. Nearly two-thirds said Vinted's support simply ignored them; three-quarters described the responses they did get as irrelevant, copy-pasted templates.

Other cases catalogued by the campaign edge from frustrating into absurd: mainstream high-street basics from Primark, M&S and Tesco flagged as counterfeit; a stainless-steel bowl removed as a "used cosmetic" item; Dolce Gusto coffee pods treated as a prohibited beverage. One professional reseller told the Sunday Times the disruption to her account cost her business roughly £80,000. Vinted's own transparency figures show it processed 134,801 complaints from Pro sellers in 2025, including more than 23,000 formal appeals under the EU's Digital Services Act, with an average turnaround of 2.8 days — evidence, sellers argue, that the volume of disputed enforcement is not trivial even if any single case looks like it.

Why the system moves so fast

Vinted has leaned hard on automated moderation to police a marketplace that has grown from 2.5 million UK users in 2023 to more than 16 million last year, screening listings for counterfeits, prohibited items and signs of undeclared commercial selling. Speed is the point: a system built to catch fraud in seconds cannot easily pause to weigh whether a disputed £2 listing merits the same response as a serious violation. Sellers describe an in-app appeal that can be used only once within six months, often answered by another automated message, with a proper human review reachable only by explicitly demanding one — a step many say they didn't know existed.

Vinted has not detailed publicly how much of the enforcement pipeline is automated versus human-reviewed, so the sellers' characterisation of it as "AI-driven" is their own, not a confirmed fact. What is confirmed is the DSA appeal volume, which places Vinted's dispute-handling squarely inside the EU's content-moderation oversight regime — the same framework regulators have used to press larger platforms on transparency.

What's next

Vinted, cash-positive and freshly valued at €8bn after an April secondary share sale, faces no immediate pressure to list publicly, and executives have declined to set an IPO timeline. But governance of exactly this kind — how a platform adjudicates disputes between millions of individual buyers and sellers — is precisely what tends to surface in listing prospectuses and regulatory reviews once a private marketplace edges toward public markets. The next marker to watch is whether the DSA appeal data, or renewed BBC and Sunday Times scrutiny, prompts Vinted to publish more detail on how enforcement decisions are actually made.
