Demand for degree apprenticeships in England quadruples in three years

Graduates outside the University of Nottingham's Sports Centre — the traditional path apprenticeships are increasingly rivalling. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Degree-apprenticeship applications have surged in England, with one major employer's per-role applicant count trebling in three years to 165.

The email lands in the inbox of a 17-year-old in Cambridge, and it isn't a university offer. It's a rejection from an apprenticeship scheme — one of roughly 165 people who applied for a single role. Three years ago, that same employer, the energy firm E.ON, was fielding around 45 applicants per place. The maths on getting a degree apprenticeship, it turns out, is starting to look a lot like the maths on getting into a good university — except the students still get paid.

Degree apprenticeships let school leavers work full-time for an employer while studying part-time toward a bachelor's or master's-equivalent qualification, with tuition paid by the employer and the government's apprenticeship levy rather than by the student. Interest in them has been climbing for years. What's new is the slope of the curve: England's Department for Education recorded 60,350 higher and degree apprenticeship starts in 2024/25, up from 50,110 the year before, and Level 6 degree-apprenticeship starts specifically have more than quadrupled over seven years, from roughly 6,400 to 26,800. UCAS says 40% of its undergraduate applicant pool — about 430,000 young people — now expresses interest in an apprenticeship route alongside or instead of a conventional degree, a figure it expects to pass half a million before the decade is out.

Why school leavers are switching

The reasons are less mysterious than the growth rate. A Vocational Attitudes Survey published this year by the skills charity Enginuity found nine in ten young people would now consider an apprenticeship over a traditional degree, and 71% of parents said they'd actively encourage it — up from 67% in 2025. Among 16- to 18-year-olds surveyed separately for E.ON, 68% believed apprenticeships built workplace skills faster than a university course, and 67% cited student debt as a driving factor. That's not a fringe view anymore; it's closer to consensus among the cohort actually making the choice.

"With changes to the student loan system, I see a university degree as long-term debt," says Louis Turpin, 19, who chose an apprenticeship route from Cambridge rather than apply through the standard UCAS cycle.

Clare Marchant, UCAS's former chief executive, has argued that nearly half of all UCAS users are now apprenticeship-curious, and that showing apprenticeship vacancies alongside degree courses on the same platform would give the two routes "real parity" for the first time. Jennifer Coupland, formerly of the Institute for Apprenticeships and Technical Education, frames it as a correction: "Over the past 20 years, learners have viewed higher education as the only entry into high-skilled work." That assumption is the one now under strain.

What employers are signalling

The pull isn't only coming from students. Employers — Deloitte, BMW and the BBC among the roughly 100 universities' worth of programmes now on offer — are underwriting the shift by funding places instead of merely hiring graduates afterwards. Helen Bradbury, E.ON UK's chief people officer, put it plainly: "The choices young people make after school are changing. More are questioning whether university is the only route into a rewarding career." That's a company executive, not an education campaigner, saying the traditional degree pipeline is no longer the default assumption behind hiring plans.

There are caveats worth keeping in view. Degree apprenticeships still make up a small fraction of overall post-18 destinations, application numbers at a single employer aren't a national census, and from January 2026 government funding for Level 7 (master's-level) apprenticeships in England narrows to under-22s, which will squeeze the market from the top end just as demand accelerates from below. Universities, for their part, saw total acceptances tick up too — 564,940 in 2024 versus 554,465 in 2023 — though the softness is concentrated in humanities and creative arts, the subjects apprenticeships don't really compete with.

The next marker is the autumn UCAS cycle, when 2026 entry figures will show whether apprenticeship interest keeps compounding at the same rate against a full-price, full-length degree — and whether that pressure starts showing up in how employers weight the two qualifications when they're deciding who to hire.
