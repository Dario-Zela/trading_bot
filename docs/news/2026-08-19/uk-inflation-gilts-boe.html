UK INFLATION · By M. Halloway · 2026-08-19 · 5 min read

UK inflation jumps to 2.9% as gilt yields hit fresh highs and BoE holds nerve

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, London, where the Monetary Policy Committee next meets on September 17. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UK inflation jumped to 2.9% in July, hardening the case for the BoE to hold rates as 30-year gilt yields hit their highest since 1998.

UK inflation snapped back above the Bank of England's comfort zone on Tuesday, with the Office for National Statistics reporting that consumer prices rose 2.9% year-on-year in July, up from 2.6% in June. It is the fifth straight month the headline rate has run above the BoE's 2% target, and the acceleration — after three months of gentle cooling — arrives at the worst possible moment for a government already fighting to keep its borrowing costs under control.

The reading forces a collision between two things markets had started to take for granted: that the BoE was done raising rates, and that gilt investors could look past Britain's fiscal position until the autumn budget. Both assumptions look shakier this morning.

Inflation reaccelerates, and not just on energy

June's dip in headline CPI was largely a fuel story — cheaper diesel and petrol dragging the top line down while the underlying pressure held firm. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, was already stuck at 2.6% in June, and services inflation — the measure the BoE watches most closely because it reflects domestic wage and pricing pressure rather than imported costs — was running at 3.6%, down only a few tenths from 4.4% at the start of the year. July's jump to 2.9% suggests that stickiness has now reasserted itself at the headline level too, undoing three months of gradual disinflation in a single print.

For the Monetary Policy Committee, that is an awkward set of numbers to reconcile with a policy stance built around gradually easing borrowing costs. It also hands ammunition to the committee's hawkish wing, who have spent the summer arguing that the BoE cut too far, too early.

A split committee finds itself splitting further

At its July 30 meeting, the MPC voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, with Megan Greene, Huw Pill and Catherine Mann dissenting in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise to 4.00% — up from two dissenters at the previous meeting. That three-way split, unusually wide for a central bank that spent much of the past two years presenting a united front, now looks prescient given Tuesday's inflation print.

A Reuters poll taken before the July data showed 56 of 64 economists expecting the Bank to hold rates at 3.75% through the end of the year, up from 83% consensus a month earlier — in other words, the market had been moving toward more confidence in a prolonged hold, not less. The hot CPI number cuts against that drift. Traders will now be watching to see whether Tuesday's data pulls more MPC members toward the hawks' camp, or whether the committee majority holds its nerve on the view that a single month's re-acceleration doesn't yet constitute a trend. The next rate decision lands September 17 — a meeting that carries no fresh Monetary Policy Report, meaning the committee will have to make its call largely off data like this one rather than an updated forecast round. The next full projections, and the more consequential decision, come November 5.

Gilts already pricing in the bad news

While the MPC weighs its options, the bond market has stopped waiting. The 30-year gilt yield touched 5.83% this week, its highest level since 1998, and the 10-year yield has now spent longer above 5% than at any point in nearly two decades. Britain is not alone in this — Japan's 10-year yield has climbed to levels unseen since 1996, German Bunds are at their highest since 2011, and the US 30-year Treasury yield pushed above 5.3% this week, a 19-year high of its own. But UK borrowing costs are rising faster than most of that peer group, a sign that investors are pricing UK-specific risk on top of the global repricing of long-dated debt.

That showed up plainly on Monday, when the Debt Management Office sold £4 billion of 2036-dated gilts at a yield of 5.156% — a level that would have seemed extraordinary even eighteen months ago. Demand was solid, with bids covering the sale 3.65 times over, so this was not a failed auction. But paying above 5% to borrow for a decade, on top of a 30-year rate approaching 6%, tightens the arithmetic on Britain's debt-servicing bill regardless of how eager buyers are to show up.

The politics behind the yield curve

The timing compounds the pressure on John Healey, the new Chancellor installed by Prime Minister Andy Burnham after a leadership change in July. Healey and Burnham have already committed £850 million in 2026-27 to scrapping VAT on household electricity bills, cutting it from 5% to zero from October — a popular but costly move that widens the fiscal gap he will need to close. The government has set October 28 as the date for its first Autumn Budget under the new administration, and Tuesday's inflation print, alongside a gilt market already flashing warning signs, narrows Healey's room for manoeuvre before he has even delivered his opening remarks to the Commons.

Sterling's initial reaction to the data was muted — traders have learned this year that a single hot CPI print rarely holds its currency impact for long when gilt yields are being driven as much by fiscal anxiety as by rate expectations. But the broader signal is consistent: with inflation re-accelerating, a divided MPC, and a government about to test investor patience with a budget under real fiscal strain, the path of least resistance for gilt yields remains upward.

What to watch

The next data point that matters is the September 17 MPC decision, where the vote split will show whether Tuesday's inflation number has pulled more members toward the hawkish dissenters or been dismissed as noise. Behind that sits the bigger test: Healey's October 28 Budget, delivered into a gilt market already charging the government multi-decade-high rates to borrow, and a Bank of England that has just been handed a reason to stay restrictive for longer than either markets or the Treasury would like.
