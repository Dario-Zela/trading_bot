AstraZeneca ends lung-cancer bispecific antibody trial over unlikely success

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters and R&D centre. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca halted a Phase III trial of its bispecific antibody volrustomig in lung cancer after interim data showed it was unlikely to beat Merck's Keytruda.

AstraZeneca has stopped a late-stage lung cancer trial after an independent review concluded the experimental drug was unlikely to beat standard treatment — the latest in a run of clinical setbacks for the British drugmaker this year.

The discontinued study, eVOLVE-Lung02, was testing volrustomig, a bispecific antibody that blocks two immune checkpoints — PD-1 and CTLA-4 — at once, in combination with chemotherapy. The Phase III trial enrolled 895 patients with metastatic non-small cell lung cancer whose tumours express relatively low levels of PD-L1, pitting the AstraZeneca regimen against Merck's Keytruda plus chemotherapy as first-line treatment. An Independent Data Monitoring Committee's scheduled interim look at the data found the volrustomig arm unlikely to hit either of the trial's dual primary endpoints — progression-free survival or overall survival — against the entrenched Keytruda standard.

AstraZeneca said the safety profile of the combination was consistent with the known effects of its component drugs and that no new safety signals emerged — this was a failure of efficacy, not tolerability. Susan Galbraith, the company's oncology R&D chief, said AstraZeneca remained "determined to continue pioneering new medicines" despite the result. Three other Phase III volrustomig trials — in mesothelioma, cervical cancer and head-and-neck squamous cell carcinoma — continue as planned, so the drug itself isn't shelved, only its bid in this particular lung cancer setting.

A pattern, not an isolated miss

The lung trial halt is the latest in a string of 2026 disappointments for AstraZeneca. Earlier this year the company and partner Ionis reported that Wainua failed to beat placebo on cardiovascular mortality in the CARDIO-TTRansform study; the FDA rejected breast cancer drug camizestrant over trial-design issues; rare-disease therapy Ultomiris missed its primary endpoint in a Phase III trial; and sonesitatug vedotin fell short on progression-free survival in gastric cancer, even as it improved overall survival. Shares moved only modestly on the volrustomig news — AZN edged roughly 1% higher, with the setback largely absorbed into otherwise solid second-quarter growth — but the cumulative effect has left the stock down about 7% over three months even as chief executive Pascal Soriot reiterates the company's target of $80 billion in annual revenue by 2030.

The episode is also a reminder that "bispecific antibody" describes a family, not a single bet. The subclass drawing the most competitive heat right now targets PD-1 and VEGF together — Summit Therapeutics and Akeso's ivonescimab, under FDA review with a decision expected in November, alongside rival programmes from Merck, Pfizer/3SBio and a Bristol Myers Squibb–BioNTech partnership. Volrustomig's PD-1/CTLA-4 combination sits in a different lane, and its stumble doesn't directly undercut those rivals' prospects. But it does reinforce a broader lesson the sector has relearned repeatedly this year: pairing two immune targets in one molecule is scientifically elegant and clinically unforgiving, with plenty of ways for a combination to fall short of a single well-established drug like Keytruda.

The next checkpoint for the class comes with Summit's FDA decision on ivonescimab in November, and with maturing overall-survival data from BMS/BioNTech's pumitamig and Pfizer's SSGJ-707 trials in squamous NSCLC — results that will show whether the PD-1/VEGF approach fares better than AstraZeneca's did.
