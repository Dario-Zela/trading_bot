Premier League chief says FIFA 'self-destructed' over World Cup expansion plan

FIFA president Gianni Infantino, whose scrapped World Cup investment plan has drawn criticism from the Premier League and UEFA. — UNIS Vienna / Nikoleta Haffar, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Premier League chief Richard Masters says FIFA "pressed the self-destruct button" over its scrapped $20bn World Cup investment plan, backing a challenge to Infantino.

Richard Masters does not often reach for language this pointed. But asked about FIFA's aborted plan to sell a stake in the World Cup to private investors, the Premier League chief executive told the BBC that Gianni Infantino's organisation had "pressed the self-destruct button."

"This is a problem of FIFA's making," Masters said. "This is a self-inflicted wound."

The plan that blew up

The scheme, which FIFA scrapped in late July after less than a fortnight in the open, would have created a new commercial entity valued at roughly $20 billion to run World Cup broadcasting and ticketing rights. FIFA planned to sell around a fifth of it — raising an estimated $4.2 billion — to a group of outside investors reported to include the Kushner family, in-laws of US President Donald Trump. FIFA is legally a not-for-profit.

The reaction was immediate and, for football's normally fractious governing bodies, remarkably unified. UEFA's 55 member associations voted to boycott the World Cup and all other FIFA competitions if the sale went ahead, with the confederation declaring that "some things are simply too important to sell." England's FA withdrew its backing for Infantino. Kevin Lamour, FIFA's own chief operating officer, broke ranks to criticise the plan publicly last month and left his post on Monday.

Infantino folded within days, saying the project had "created divisions of a nature that ... are no longer in the interest of the objective set out in the first place." But the retreat has not repaired the damage. UEFA said over the weekend that it and "other members of the football family" had lost confidence in FIFA's leadership, pointing to what it called "shabby, back room, opaque" dealing.

A trust, not an asset

Masters's intervention matters because the Premier League sits closer to FIFA's money than almost any other domestic body, and has generally avoided picking fights with Zurich. Not this time. "I don't think FIFA should ever be contemplating selling off a stake in the World Cup," he said. "They're a not-for-profit organisation. They should really be holding that competition in trust for the future of football."

He went further, backing the FA's public break with Infantino and calling for a challenger at next year's presidential election. "I think it's a bad idea, and the FA have taken the right position regards to Gianni," Masters said. "Obviously another candidate has to emerge, a unity candidate" — one whose platform, he suggested, should centre on reforming FIFA's governance rather than expanding its commercial reach.

The episode fits a wider pattern this year of club and confederation power pushing back against Infantino's centralising instincts, from the enlarged Club World Cup to fixture-calendar disputes with leagues across Europe. This time, though, it was FIFA's own finances — and the question of who gets to own a piece of the World Cup — that forced the retreat.

Infantino faces re-election next year, and Masters's comments suggest the Premier League will not simply wait to see who runs. Whether a credible "unity candidate" actually emerges before FIFA's Congress convenes is the next thing worth watching.
