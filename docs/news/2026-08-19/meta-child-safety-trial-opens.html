Meta shares wobble as landmark child-safety trial opens in Oakland

In one sentence
Meta shares fell as an Oakland trial opened over claims it knowingly designed Facebook and Instagram to addict children, with damages sought up to $1.4 trillion.

Meta shares slid for a second straight session on Tuesday as opening statements began in Oakland federal court in the most consequential legal test the company has faced: a trial in which four state attorneys general are seeking damages that, by Meta's own reckoning, could reach $1.4 trillion — a figure roughly equal to the entire market value of the company.

Meta stock fell around 4% Tuesday to about $548, adding to a 4% drop the day before, as the case opened before U.S. District Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers. The declines shaved tens of billions of dollars off Meta's market capitalisation, though the sell-off was compounded by separate worries about the company's spending: second-quarter costs rose 55% against 28% revenue growth, and free cash flow collapsed to $784 million from $8.55 billion a year earlier as AI infrastructure spending accelerated.

Hook, hold, harvest, hide

The case, brought by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey, alleges Meta knowingly built addictive features into Facebook and Instagram, collected data on children under 13 without parental consent in violation of federal law, and misled the public about the harm it knew its products caused. California Deputy Attorney General Megan O'Neill distilled the state's theory into a single line in her opening statement: "Meta's business model can be summed up in four simple words: 'hook' the users, 'hold' them for as long as they can, 'harvest' their data, and then 'hide' the truth from the public when making public statements." She added that Meta had publicly claimed to prioritise safety over profits while, internally, "time and again profits won."

The suit rests on three categories of state and federal law — a child-privacy statute, a false-advertising statute and unfair-competition statutes — rather than a single unified claim, which is part of why the potential penalty is so large: violations can be counted per user, per feature, per state. Meta has called the $1.4 trillion figure a theoretical ceiling rather than a realistic outcome; legal analysts largely agree, noting an award anywhere near that size would put the company into bankruptcy and that courts typically settle well below such maximums. Judge Gonzalez Rogers has empanelled an eight-person advisory jury, but as a bench trial, the ultimate findings and any penalty rest with her. The proceedings are expected to run six to eight weeks.

Meta's defence, previewed in court filings and in earlier related trials, is that "social media addiction" is not a recognised medical or psychiatric condition, and that the company has not been shown to have made knowingly false statements about its platforms' safety. The company points to years of investment in parental controls, teen accounts with default restrictions, and age-verification tools as evidence it has taken the issue seriously rather than concealed it.

Not the first loss

Oakland is not Meta's first courtroom defeat on these questions this year, which is part of why investors are treating the case as more than theatre. In March, a New Mexico jury found Meta liable under the state's consumer-protection law over its handling of child exploitation on its platforms, awarding $375 million in civil penalties at the maximum statutory rate of $5,000 per violation — the first verdict of its kind against a major tech company. A second phase of that case in May added a further $567 million into a state abatement fund, on top of other penalties, pushing New Mexico's total recovery close to $1 billion. Separately, Meta CEO Mark Zuckerberg spent nearly eight hours on the stand in February in a related Los Angeles Superior Court trial, where he was pressed on internal warnings — from 18 of the company's own experts — that beauty filters mimicking plastic surgery could worsen body dysmorphia in teenage girls, and on the decision to reinstate those filters after a brief ban. That case is one of roughly 1,600 pending lawsuits from families and school districts nationwide that could be shaped by how Meta fares in front of juries and judges this year.

The Oakland trial is also only the first of what is coming: 29 states sued Meta in 2023 over the same core allegations, and the 25 states not part of this trial are expected to bring their own cases later, using whatever legal and factual groundwork is laid here.

What a loss would actually cost

Even setting aside the headline $1.4 trillion figure, the remedies on the table extend beyond a cheque. A New Mexico judge already ordered product changes — time limits for minors and restrictions on AI chatbot interactions — as part of that state's penalty, and California's attorneys general are seeking comparable structural changes to how Facebook and Instagram operate, not just financial damages. For a company whose advertising business depends on maximising time spent in-app, a court-ordered redesign of engagement mechanics would cut closer to the bone than any fine, however large.

That is the mechanism markets are pricing, however imprecisely: not the probability of a trillion-dollar verdict, which most lawyers treat as remote, but the accumulating legal and reputational cost of an engagement-optimised business model now under simultaneous attack in California, New Mexico and Los Angeles courtrooms, with two dozen more states waiting their turn. The next marker to watch is the pace of testimony in Oakland over the coming weeks — Meta executives are expected to take the stand, as Zuckerberg did in Los Angeles — and any early rulings from Judge Gonzalez Rogers on the scope of remedies she is willing to consider, which will tell investors more about tail risk than the $1.4 trillion number ever will.
