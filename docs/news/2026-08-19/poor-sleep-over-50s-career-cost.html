Poor sleep could cost over-50s nine months of career, study finds

A digital clock reads 4:12am — the kind of broken night the Turku study links to shorter working lives. — Benjamin Watson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
A Finnish study of 76,000 workers ties severe sleep trouble after 50 to roughly nine fewer months of working life, though it can't rule out reverse causation.

A run of bad nights in your fifties, it turns out, is not just a quality-of-life problem. It may shorten your working life by the best part of a year.

Researchers at the University of Turku tracked 76,410 Finnish adults — 70,339 public-sector employees and a further 6,071 from a separate cohort — from the point each turned 50 until age 68, matching self-reported sleep patterns against pension registry data on when people actually stopped working. The paper, published this week in Occupational & Environmental Medicine, is one of the largest efforts yet to put a number on something employers have long suspected but rarely quantified: that poor sleep drains careers, not just afternoons.

The headline figure is stark in relative terms, modest in absolute ones. Workers with no sleep disturbances could expect 13 years and 5 months of employment between 50 and 68. Those with moderate disturbances — trouble falling or staying asleep, waking too early, or waking unrefreshed, on two to four nights a week — lost roughly five months. Severe disturbances, five or more nights a week, cost eight months. A separate finding, sleeping nine or more hours a night, was associated with retiring around nine months early — the figure behind the headline, and one that combines the worst outcomes across both measures.

Who bears the gap

The spread was not evenly distributed. Professional women with untroubled sleep had the longest working-life expectancy in the sample, at nearly 14 years. Men in routine, lower-skilled jobs with severe sleep disturbance had the shortest, at just under 12 years and 6 months — an 18-month gap between the best- and worst-off groups, doing broadly comparable amounts of paid work over the same age range.

"Short sleep duration has been previously linked in some studies to, for example, earlier retirement," said Saana Myllyntausta of the University of Turku, one of the study's authors.

The mechanism is the study's soft spot. It cannot distinguish between three quite different stories: sleep-deprived workers becoming measurably less productive and being managed out; poor sleep acting as an early symptom of the same underlying illness that later forces someone onto disability benefits; or workers simply choosing to retire sooner because broken sleep makes the job harder to bear. The authors are candid about this — they could not adjust for physical or mental health conditions, health behaviours, or shift-work exposure, any of which could independently explain both the insomnia and the early exit. Reverse causation is the obvious challenge to any headline number here.

The employer question

That caveat matters more to workplace-policy watchers than to the workers losing the months. If poor sleep is mostly a marker of ill health rather than a cause of reduced output, then corporate sleep-coaching apps and wellness stipends — a genre of spending that has grown steadily as employers look for cheap fixes to costly retention problems — are targeting a symptom rather than a cause. If it is closer to a direct productivity and attendance drag, the case for treating sleep as an occupational health issue, alongside ergonomics and stress, gets considerably stronger, and with it any argument that employers carry some liability for shift patterns or workloads that wreck sleep in the first place.

Neither the Turku team nor this single paper settles that question. What it does is give policymakers and pension actuaries a concrete number to argue over, in a period when most rich economies are already trying to coax older workers into staying employed for longer. The next test will be whether follow-up studies, controlling more tightly for prior health, shrink the nine-month figure or hold it steady — that finding, more than this one, will tell employers whether sleep belongs on the same balance sheet as sick leave.
