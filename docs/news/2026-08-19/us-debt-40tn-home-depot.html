US debt nears $40 trillion as Home Depot beats earnings estimates

A Home Depot store at sunrise in Eagan, Minnesota. — Tony Webster / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Home Depot beat earnings as US debt neared $40tn — two separate stories that share a tariff-refund footnote, not a causal link.

The federal government is on pace to cross $40 trillion in outstanding debt within days, according to Treasury data — a milestone forecasters had not expected to arrive until later this year. On the same day the figure dominated Washington headlines, Home Depot told investors that American shoppers are still spending, posting adjusted earnings of $4.92 a share against a Wall Street forecast of $4.73, on revenue of $47.86 billion versus an expected $47.23 billion.

The two facts sat side by side in Tuesday's news cycle, and it is tempting to read one into the other: consumers keep buying paint and lumber even as the government's balance sheet deteriorates, so perhaps the debt story is overblown, or perhaps the earnings beat is itself evidence of an economy running on borrowed time. Neither reading survives close inspection. They are separate stories that happen to share a dateline.

What Home Depot actually reported

Comparable sales rose 1.7% company-wide, the retailer's best gain since the third quarter of 2022, with online sales up 11%. Chief Financial Officer Richard McPhail described the operating environment as "frozen housing market conditions" — elevated mortgage rates and high home prices have kept big-ticket renovation and moving-driven purchases subdued, while smaller repair and maintenance jobs have carried the quarter. Home Depot said it was taking market share in a home-improvement sector that, overall, is not growing much. Management reaffirmed its full-year sales and profit guidance rather than raising it.

One detail complicates the tidy "resilient consumer" narrative: roughly $685 million of the quarter's results came from tariff refunds, not from stronger underlying demand. That refund is itself a byproduct of February's Supreme Court ruling that Trump's IEEPA tariffs exceeded presidential authority — the same ruling now blowing a hole in federal revenue projections. It is the one place these two stories genuinely touch, and it cuts against, not for, the read-through: Home Depot's beat is flattered by the same legal decision that is making the debt problem worse.

The debt side of the ledger

Treasury figures put gross federal debt at roughly $39.9 trillion in mid-August, up from $39 trillion in March — a pace of accumulation the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget has pegged at $14 billion a day. Net interest costs are now tracking toward $1 trillion for the fiscal year, a sum that exceeds what Washington spends on national defense. The Congressional Budget Office has revised its fiscal 2026 deficit estimate up to roughly $2.1 trillion, about $200 billion worse than its February projection, with much of the gap traced to the same tariff ruling: IEEPA duties had been expected to raise $1.4 trillion over the coming decade, and the government is now refunding more in tariffs some months than it collects.

None of that is a story about household spending. It is a story about a fiscal trajectory that predates this earnings season and will outlast it — driven by structural deficits near 6% of GDP, an aging population, and interest payments that, unlike the low fixed-rate debt of the postwar decades, now reprice at market rates and compound.

Why the distinction matters to positioning

For traders reading macro headlines as a single signal, the risk is treating "consumer still spending" and "debt is unsustainable" as two data points on the same line when they measure different things entirely — one a snapshot of household balance sheets, the other a projection of sovereign ones. A portfolio built on the read that resilient earnings validate a higher-for-longer rate path is vulnerable to a very different narrative taking hold: that an unsustainable debt load eventually forces the Federal Reserve's hand regardless of how home-improvement spending is trending. Those two narratives point at opposite trades, and nothing in Tuesday's data resolves which one wins. Home Depot's numbers describe household finances performing about as expected in a soft housing market; the debt clock describes a separate arithmetic problem in Washington. Conflating them substitutes a headline for an analysis.

The next real test of the fiscal story is the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement and the pace toward the $41.1 trillion statutory debt ceiling, which lawmakers set only last year and could approach by early 2026 — forcing another suspend-or-raise vote. For Home Depot, the next data point is the holiday-quarter read on whether small-project spending holds up once tariff refunds stop flattering the numbers.
