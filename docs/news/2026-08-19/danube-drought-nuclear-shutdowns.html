Danube low water levels force Hungary and Romania to shut nuclear reactors

Hungary's Paks Nuclear Power Plant, whose four reactors normally supply up to half the country's electricity, was reduced to a single turbine in early August as Danube levels hit record lows. — IAEA Imagebank / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Record-low Danube levels have knocked most of Hungary's and Romania's nuclear capacity offline this month, sending regional power prices above €500/MWh.

The Danube usually carries enough water to cool eight nuclear reactors across three countries without anyone giving it a second thought. This summer it stopped doing that job reliably, and both Hungary and Romania have spent August taking reactors offline, one after another, because the river feeding their cooling systems is no longer deep enough or cold enough to do the work.

At Budapest, the river fell to 31 centimetres in early August — below the previous record low of 33cm set in October 2018, according to Hungary's General Directorate of Water Management. Upstream at the Paks plant, the gauge read minus 134cm on August 1st, a level low enough that plant engineers began a phased, controlled shutdown of three of the facility's four reactors rather than risk running cooling pumps against an inadequate flow.

Reactors go dark on both banks

Paks is not a marginal player in Hungary's energy mix — its four Soviet-era VVER-440 units, each upgraded to roughly 500 megawatts, together supply somewhere between 40 and 50% of the country's electricity depending on the year. With units 1, 3 and 4 shut down, only unit 2 stayed connected, and for a stretch in early August it was running a single turbine putting out just 240MW against a plant nameplate capacity of 2,000MW.

Romania's response followed a similar arc but with less redundancy to fall back on. The state-owned Cernavodă plant, the country's only nuclear station, took its first CANDU reactor offline on July 28th. On August 13th, Nuclearelectrica began a controlled shutdown of the second unit too — disconnection was complete by 10:53am local time — leaving both 650-megawatt reactors dark simultaneously for the first time. Together they normally supply about a fifth of Romania's electricity. Plant director Romeo Urjan told Digi24 the unit would not be considered for restart inside ten days; that window closes around August 23rd.

"Depending on how the forecasts for the Danube's water level evolve, CNE Cernavodă specialists will decide when to restart the nuclear units," Nuclearelectrica chief executive Cosmin Ghiță said. Transelectrica, the grid operator, has said the system remains under "high operational stress" but insists there is no risk of a major breakdown — a distinction that depends heavily on continued imports from neighbouring grids and the availability of fossil-fuel reserve capacity.

Prices move first, before the headlines do

Energy traders had a preview of this weeks before the reactors actually went offline. On June 28th, before the worst of the drought set in, day-ahead prices in Romania hit €224 per megawatt-hour and Hungary €223/MWh — the highest in Europe that day, against roughly €140/MWh in Germany and €125/MWh in France. By early August, with Paks curtailed by roughly 90% and Cernavodă's first unit already down, regional prices spiked past €500/MWh, with some hours reportedly touching €700/MWh, according to reporting compiled by Balkan Green Energy News and Oilprice.com.

The mechanics are straightforward: consumption rose sharply as households ran air conditioning through a renewed heatwave pushing daytime highs past 38°C, just as several gigawatts of low-carbon baseload dropped off the grid. Wind was weak. Serbia's Đerđap hydro dams on the same river were running at 20-30% of normal output. Solar, which might otherwise have offset midday demand, does nothing for the evening peak when air-conditioning load is highest. Romania has warned it may curb industrial consumption by 10-30% and suspend electricity exports if conditions worsen further.

Rock, barges and a race against the calendar

Both governments have resorted to civil engineering rather than waiting on rain. Hungary is building what it calls a submerged river barrier near Paks — an underwater rock dam intended to use 35,000 cubic metres of stone initially, followed by another 110,000 cubic metres, to stop the river falling below minus 90cm at the plant's cooling-water intake. Prime Minister Péter Magyar said barges were being sunk to help raise the local water level, and by mid-August the tactic had worked well enough to bring a second Paks turbine back online, lifting output from 240MW to about 500MW.

Romania went further, deploying naval demolition teams to blast a rock formation restricting flow near Cernavodă and sinking four barges loaded with stone into the channel, at a budgeted cost of more than €2 million. Nuclearelectrica has invoked force majeure on its electricity sales contracts, a legal mechanism that suspends normal delivery obligations while the plant is unable to generate.

None of it addresses the underlying cause. Forecasters see no significant rainfall over the Danube's Austrian and German catchments in the coming weeks, and longer-range models point to more high pressure rather than the drought-breaking storms the region needs. "We have to plan our power system so that this might be our new reality," said Zsuzsanna Pató of the Regulatory Assistance Project, a framing that grid operators across Central Europe now have to take literally rather than rhetorically.

The next data points worth watching are mechanical, not meteorological: whether Cernavodă's second unit restarts around August 23rd as Urjan indicated, and whether Paks's remaining offline units can follow unit 2 back onto the grid before the autumn drawdown in demand arrives. If the river stays this low into the September and October maintenance-outage season — when reactors are normally taken offline on a planned basis — Hungary and Romania could enter winter heating season with less spare nuclear capacity in reserve than in any year on record.
