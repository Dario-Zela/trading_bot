Suspended Reform UK member vows to fight to remain in party

Zia Yusuf addressing a Reform UK rally at Birmingham's NEC — the party figure at the centre of Tim Montgomerie's suspension. — Z979 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Reform UK board member Tim Montgomerie was suspended after criticising Zia Yusuf on a podcast, and says he will fight to stay in the party.

Tim Montgomerie has spent four decades inside the conservative movement's engine room without ever quite running it — founding ConservativeHome, advising Boris Johnson, co-founding a think tank with Iain Duncan Smith. On 18 August, Reform UK suspended him from the party he joined only in December 2024, and he has no intention of going quietly.

Montgomerie, a Reform UK board member, was suspended after recording an episode of the Spectator's Coffee House Shots podcast in which he said the party's home affairs spokesperson, Zia Yusuf, had "consistently freelanced on Twitter" over major policy positions and suggested Nigel Farage had "fallen out of love with Zia." The party moved against him after the episode was recorded but before it aired — suspending, by Montgomerie's account, a member in line with its rules rather than for what had yet been broadcast.

A dispute months in the making

Montgomerie says the friction was not sudden. He told the podcast that Yusuf "did not talk to me" for three months after learning Montgomerie had helped former Conservative frontbencher Robert Jenrick defect to Reform, and that "a lot of people are on not-speaking terms with Zia and it's nearly always Zia who initiates it." He called Yusuf "a man of huge talents" but argued his conduct online was undermining the party's efforts to court tactical voters, framing the real question as "whether he brings Zia under control." Montgomerie says he raised these concerns privately and repeatedly with both Yusuf and Farage before going public.

Montgomerie's response

Rather than accept the suspension, Montgomerie has said he intends to fight it. "I have no regrets over what I've said or done," he said, adding he would make the case that "my contribution, my constructive criticism, is not only what I said would characterise my membership, but is a healthy part of any political party." In a video statement he noted, pointedly, that "Nigel Farage did not know about my suspension" — an implication that the decision came from elsewhere in the party's machinery — before adding that Reform's grassroots "deserve better than the skirmishes of this kind."

This is not Reform's first brush with public discipline trouble. The party withdrew the whip from MP Rupert Lowe last year following an investigation into threats made against Yusuf, with deputy leader Richard Tice defending the move as correct if "unfortunate" and "difficult." The pattern — a senior figure clashes with Yusuf, then finds themselves outside the tent — is now familiar enough that commentators have started calling it Reform's civil war rather than a one-off spat.

Why it matters beyond Westminster

Reform UK has spent the past two years converting poll strength into the appearance of a government-in-waiting, and its pitch to voters rests partly on discipline the older parties are seen to lack. Public infighting cuts against that image at an inconvenient moment: Labour edged ahead of Reform in a poll for the first time in over a year this week, giving critics fresh grounds to argue the party's momentum was never as solid as its numbers suggested. For a movement whose entire proposition is competence and unity where Westminster has neither, a board member publicly vowing to fight his own suspension is not a footnote.

What happens next depends on Reform's internal process, which Montgomerie has not detailed publicly. Whether Farage intervenes — and what, if anything, he says about Yusuf's conduct — will likely determine whether this settles quietly or becomes the next chapter in a dispute that has already claimed one MP's whip.
