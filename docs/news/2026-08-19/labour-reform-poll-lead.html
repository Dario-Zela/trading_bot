Labour edges ahead of Reform UK in first poll lead in over a year

Andy Burnham's official portrait as prime minister, taken July 2026. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Labour has retaken a poll lead over Reform UK under new PM Andy Burnham, but his fiscal signalling has already moved gilts more than his popularity has moved voters.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for exactly a month, and for the first time in eighteen months, a poll has put Labour back ahead of Reform UK. Opinium's fieldwork from 5-8 August puts Labour on 27%, Reform on 25% — narrow, but the first lead of its kind since early 2025, and enough to have Westminster talking about a "Burnham bounce."

In one sentence: Labour has retaken a poll lead over Reform UK under new PM Andy Burnham, but his fiscal signalling has already moved gilts more than his popularity has moved voters.

The numbers are consistent across pollsters. Ipsos, polling for LBC over roughly the same window, put Labour on 28% to Reform's 25%, with the Conservatives trailing on 18% and the Greens on 12%. Burnham himself scores a net approval of plus 16 in Opinium's tracker — the only party leader in positive territory — and 36% of respondents in the Ipsos poll named him the most capable of running the country, against 15% for Nigel Farage and 13% for Kemi Badenoch.

The bounce has a ceiling

What undercuts the tidy "new leader fixes it" narrative is the comparison to Starmer. Ipsos found 29% of people satisfied with Burnham's performance as PM — eight points behind the 37% satisfaction Starmer had at the equivalent stage of his own premiership, before that number collapsed toward an eventual average of around -46% net by the time he quit in June. Burnham's ratings are respectable next to Starmer's endpoint; they are middling next to Starmer's start, and well short of the 65% Tony Blair or 54% Theresa May recorded at similar points in office, per the same polling series.

That gap matters because it suggests the party's recovery is not really about Burnham's personal appeal. He remains the most popular figure tested against Farage and Badenoch, but by a smaller margin than his own favourables imply. What has moved is more likely relief — a change of leader after a chaotic June, in which more than 95 Labour MPs had publicly called for Starmer to go before he resigned on 22 June and Burnham was acclaimed unopposed on 17 July.

What markets have actually priced

The polling has not been the market-moving event. Burnham's words have. On his first day in office, 20 July, he told reporters his government would "make full use of any flexibility" within the UK's fiscal rules — a single line that sent the 10-year gilt yield up 8 basis points to 5.04%, at the time the highest in the G7, and the 30-year yield up 9 points to 5.75%. Sterling slipped against the dollar the same afternoon. Traders read it as code for more borrowing on top of debt already near 100% of GDP, and gilt yields have stayed elevated since — sitting around 5.07% on the 10-year this week, a level tied up with the inflation print and Bank of England hold covered elsewhere in this edition.

The fiscal question has not gone away. Burnham faces pressure from the TUC and campaign groups such as Positive Money for a windfall levy on bank profits — one estimate puts a targeted bank tax at roughly £19bn from the big four lenders — while bank executives lobby hard against it, much as they did against Rachel Reeves before him. Markets are pricing roughly two quarter-point Bank of England rate rises by year-end on the assumption that fiscal policy loosens rather than tightens under the new government.

What to watch

The poll lead is the first hard evidence that Labour's post-Starmer reset is working with voters, but it says nothing yet about which way Burnham will jump on the bank levy, spending commitments or the fiscal rules themselves. That decision — expected to sharpen around the autumn budget cycle — is what will actually move gilts and sterling, not the next round of voting-intention numbers.
