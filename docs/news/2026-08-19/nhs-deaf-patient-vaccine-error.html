Deaf patient given wrong vaccine dose due to sign language interpretation error

A video relay interpreting session, of the kind that lets deaf patients communicate with hearing staff via a remote BSL interpreter. — SignVideo / Significan't SignVideo Services, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
An NHS ombudsman inquiry found a deaf patient was given a Covid jab instead of her booked flu vaccine after staff skipped a BSL interpreter and explainer video.

A patient identified only as Samantha, who is deaf and communicates in British Sign Language, went to her GP surgery in Northamptonshire for a flu jab. She left having been given a Covid-19 vaccine instead — and an NHS ombudsman investigation has now confirmed the mix-up happened because staff never gave her the information, in a format she could understand, to catch the error before the needle went in.[^1]

According to the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman (PHSO), Samantha had booked her flu and Covid vaccinations for separate weeks and showed the nurse a note on her phone stating she was there for the "flu jab only."[^1] The practice nonetheless proceeded with the Covid vaccine, telling investigators it believed consent had been established through Samantha's grandmother — despite the grandmother living with early-stage dementia and not being present in the treatment room.[^1] Staff did not provide a BSL interpreter, nor did they show Samantha the practice's own BSL explainer video, which the Ombudsman found would likely have flagged the discrepancy before the dose was given.[^1]

An error, not an isolated one

The PHSO upheld Samantha's complaint and recommended the surgery apologise and pay her £450 in recognition of the distress caused.[^1] NHS Northamptonshire Integrated Care Board said it was "very sorry" for her experience and that it expected practices to make "reasonable adjustments to support effective communication" — the legal standard set out in the Equality Act 2010, which obliges health providers to remove communication barriers for disabled patients, deaf people included.[^2] The practice has since signed a contract with a sign language interpreting company, according to the report.[^1]

The case did not surface in isolation. The Ombudsman's office says that since April 2020 it has concluded 623 investigations touching on reasonable adjustments for disabled patients, upholding or partly upholding 496 of them — a proportion that points less to a single practice's failing than to a pattern across the health service.[^1] Research from the deaf charity RNID, cited in a related PHSO report published earlier this year, found seven in ten deaf people and people with hearing loss have never been asked about their communication needs when accessing NHS care at all.[^3]

A wider pattern of interpreter gaps

That earlier case, decided in February, involved Alan Graham, a deaf cancer patient treated at University Hospitals Birmingham NHS Foundation Trust. Over eleven weeks in hospital, professional interpreters were provided on only three occasions; on other occasions staff relied on his grandchildren, aged 16 and 12, to relay serious updates about his prognosis, including that he might not survive the night.[^3] PHSO chief executive Rebecca Hilsenrath said at the time that "public services must be accessible to everyone for the system to be fair and equitable," and that the trust's inconsistent provision of interpreters had caused the family unnecessary distress.[^3] The Department of Health and Social Care has called such accounts "deeply upsetting" and "completely unacceptable," and said NHS and social care organisations have a legal duty to make services accessible.[^1]

Taken together, the two cases describe the same gap seen at opposite ends of clinical severity — a routine vaccination and an end-of-life diagnosis — with the same root cause: no professional interpreter in the room, and no standard fallback when one isn't available. Individual trusts and practices can commission interpreting services, as Samantha's surgery has now done, but there is no NHS-wide mandate requiring it, leaving provision to vary practice by practice.

What happens next rests largely with commissioners rather than legislators. NHS integrated care boards, which fund GP practices, are the bodies with power to require interpreter contracts as a condition of service — Northamptonshire's has said it now expects this of its practices, but has not said whether it will apply that standard system-wide. Whether other ICBs follow, or whether the DHSC moves beyond statements of concern to a mandatory standard, is the next thing worth watching.
