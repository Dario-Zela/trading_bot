AI tool developed to help planes avoid climate-warming contrails

A persistent contrail streaks across the sky — the ice-crystal clouds these trails form are estimated to account for roughly a third of aviation's total climate impact. — Alexander Davronov / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A UK-Google trial will nudge Atlantic flights by 2,000ft to dodge contrails, but proof it works at airspace scale is still months away.

A trail of ice crystals stretching across the sky looks harmless enough — condensation, nothing more. But so-called persistent contrails are now understood to be responsible for roughly a third of aviation's total climate impact, by some estimates more than half, rivalling the warming effect of the industry's carbon dioxide emissions. Unlike CO2, the fix does not require new engines, new fuel or new infrastructure — just better information about where, and when, not to fly.

That is the premise behind Operation Blue Skies, a UK government-backed trial that started this week and will run across the winters of 2026-27 and 2027-28. Google is supplying AI-driven forecasts, built on historical contrail observations layered against Met Office weather data, to predict where cold, humid air at cruising altitude will turn engine soot into persistent, heat-trapping cirrus. NATS, which controls UK airspace, will feed those forecasts to air traffic controllers, who can then nudge aircraft up or down by around 2,000 feet — the kind of routine adjustment already used to dodge turbulence — to avoid the worst zones. Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge will independently verify whether it actually works.

The trial covers Shanwick, the eastern half of the North Atlantic oceanic corridor and one of the world's busiest transatlantic routes, which alone accounts for an estimated 5% of global contrail warming, concentrated in winter months. It is backed by £5 million, with the Department for Transport contributing £2.65 million through its Aerospace Technology Institute programme. "Contrail avoidance could drastically reduce the global climate impact of aviation, at minimal cost to passengers and companies," said Cambridge's Professor Steven Barrett, one of the trial's scientific leads.

Why airlines are already interested

The appeal is economic as much as environmental. A separate Google trial with American Airlines, involving roughly 2,400 flights, found AI-guided altitude changes cut contrail formation by around 62%, while raising fuel burn by only about 0.3% per rerouted flight — Google's modelling puts the climate benefit at some 20 times greater than the warming cost of that extra fuel. For an industry that has struggled to make deep, affordable cuts to its carbon footprint, a routing tweak that costs cents rather than billions is an unusually cheap lever to pull.

What is still unproven

What Operation Blue Skies has not yet demonstrated is scale. Every result so far — the American Airlines pilot, earlier single-airline trials citing 60-70% reductions — has come from voluntary programmes on individual carriers, not from an entire piece of shared oceanic airspace under live air traffic control. Google's Kemal Armada put it plainly: "The question is no longer whether we can mitigate contrails, but whether we can make that mitigation work at the scale of the global aviation system." No commercial rollout timeline has been set, and the DfT's own framing treats this as a feasibility test — of forecast accuracy, controller workload and whether climate benefit reliably outweighs extra fuel burn — not a deployment.

Results from the first 20 to 40 test days this winter, independently checked by Cambridge and Imperial, will be the first real signal of whether contrail avoidance can move from research promise to routine air traffic management.
