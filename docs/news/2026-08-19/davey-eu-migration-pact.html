BEYOND THE TAPE · UK POLITICS · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-19 · 3 min read

Ed Davey urges UK to join EU migration pact to tackle small boats

Sir Ed Davey, Liberal Democrat leader, in his 2024 official Commons portrait. — © House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Ed Davey repeated his long-standing call for the UK to join the EU's migration pact to curb Channel crossings — no new policy, no government response.

Sir Ed Davey has, once again, told the government to do the thing he has been telling it to do for the better part of two years: join the European Union's Migration and Asylum Pact.

The Liberal Democrat leader's argument runs like this. Britain's exit from the bloc left it without a working returns agreement — no mechanism to send migrants who cross the Channel by small boat back to the first EU country they set foot in. Under the EU's pact, which member states are due to implement from June 2026, asylum seekers who arrive via one country and try their luck in another can, in principle, be returned. Davey wants the UK to plug into that system, arguing traffickers are selling passage to Britain partly because there is no watertight deal to send anyone back.

"Being outside the pact just makes us the easiest market for the gangs to sell," Davey has argued, pointing to research suggesting the absence of a returns agreement is a factor migrants weigh before choosing the UK.

An old argument, restated

None of this is new. Davey has pushed closer EU alignment since taking the Lib Dem leadership — on trade, on the single market, and repeatedly on migration — and the party's official policy already commits to joining a Dublin-style returns arrangement. What has changed is the backdrop: Channel crossings are running at record pace this year, with more than 21,000 arrivals by the summer, up over 50% on the same point in 2024, even as monthly totals dipped in July. That gives Davey's well-worn line a sharper edge, but not a new one.

The government's own approach has leaned on bilateral deals with France rather than EU-wide machinery — reflecting both the political toxicity of anything resembling rejoining EU structures and the practical reality that Brussels has shown limited appetite to fold a non-member into a pact built for its own 27 states. There is no indication ministers are about to reverse that stance, and Davey, as opposition leader, has no lever to make them.

What it means, if anything

For a story about actual policy movement, this is a non-event: a familiar critique from a familiar critic, timed to a grim run of crossing figures. It matters more as a marker of where the pre-election dividing lines on Europe and migration are being drawn than as a signal of anything likely to happen in Whitehall this week.

The next real test comes when the EU pact itself takes effect among member states in mid-2026 and analysts get a first read on whether it actually reduces onward movement — the kind of data point that would either bolster or undercut Davey's case with something more than rhetoric.
