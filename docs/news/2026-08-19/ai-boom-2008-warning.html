JPMorgan's Bill Eigen warns of 2008-style risk building in AI-driven market boom

In one sentence
A JPMorgan fixed-income veteran likened the AI boom to the pre-2008 real estate cycle on air, but isn't shorting AI assets or citing any fund-level allocation change.

JPMorgan's Bill Eigen has a habit of showing up on financial television to say the market isn't worried enough. This week he did it again — only this time the comparison he reached for was 2008.

Eigen, chief investment officer of absolute return and opportunistic fixed income at J.P. Morgan Asset Management, told CNBC's Squawk Box on August 17 that the AI investment boom looks less like a technology cycle and more like the pre-crisis real estate cycle: long-lived assets financed with long-dated debt, built on the assumption that demand will keep pace indefinitely.

"What I'm terrified of is when that starts to slow," Eigen said, referring to the pace of AI capital spending and private-market valuations — both of which he says are already showing signs of deceleration even as public equity prices hold near records.

The mismatch he's watching

Eigen's specific worry is a duration mismatch. Data centers are increasingly financed with bonds running 20 to 30 years, while the GPUs and chips inside them typically depreciate over three to six years. That gap, he argues, echoes the mortgage-era habit of financing short-lived economics with long-dated paper — fine as long as growth and refinancing conditions cooperate, less fine once they don't.

He also pointed to credit default swaps on AI-linked names, including Nvidia, which he said have begun widening even though headline credit spreads across public markets remain near historic lows. In his reading, that's a crack showing up in the derivatives market before it reaches the valuations everyone is watching. It's the kind of divergence — quiet in the index, loud underneath — that eventually became a defining detail of 2007 and early 2008, when subprime-linked CDS spreads moved well before broader credit markets did.

Talk without a trade behind it

What Eigen has not done is act on the thesis. He told CNBC he isn't shorting AI-related assets — timing a top is, in his words, effectively impossible — but he isn't buying long-dated data-center debt at current prices either. It's a straddle: flag the risk publicly, take no position that would prove the conviction wrong if he's early. J.P. Morgan Asset Management's broader published research, meanwhile, has generally argued the AI capex build is durable and increasingly profitable, and the firm has not signalled any AI-sector allocation shift tied to Eigen's remarks.

That gap matters. Eigen made a near-identical point in November, telling CNBC he didn't see much fear in markets at all — a complacency call that, nine months on, has not been followed by a JPMorgan portfolio move of any documented size. A warning repeated without a position behind it is closer to color commentary than a market signal, however credentialed the source.

What it's worth to traders

For algorithmic and systematic strategies now leaning heavily on AI-sector momentum, a call like this only matters if it changes flows — fund allocations, credit spreads actually widening past AI names, or a slowdown in hyperscaler capex growth that has, per Dallas Fed research, pushed data-center-related spending toward roughly 3% of GDP, more than double the telecom-boom peak. Absent that, Eigen's remarks are a data point about sentiment among veteran fixed-income managers, not evidence of a positioning shift. The next concrete tell will be hyperscaler capex guidance in third-quarter earnings, due from Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta in late October, and any further move in AI-linked CDS spreads between now and then.
