SEC proposes new 'Regulation Crypto Assets' framework for token offerings

SEC Chair Paul Atkins, who has pushed the agency's crypto framework as part of his broader 'Project Crypto' initiative. — U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (official portrait) / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The SEC proposed two new crypto-specific fundraising exemptions — up to $5m and $75m — with a 60-day comment window and federal preemption of state securities law.

The Securities and Exchange Commission has spent the better part of a decade telling crypto entrepreneurs, in effect, to fit their token sales into rules written for corporate bonds and mining company stock in the 1930s. On Tuesday it proposed something different: rules written for tokens.

The agency published a proposal called Regulation Crypto Assets, which would create two new registration exemptions sized specifically for digital token offerings — one capped at $5 million raised over four years, the other allowing up to $75 million in any 12-month period. A public comment period runs 60 days from publication in the Federal Register, after which the commission can revise and finalize the rule, or let it stall, as such proposals sometimes do.

Two doors, one exit ramp

The smaller of the two exemptions functions like a one-time allowance for early-stage projects: up to $5 million total, spread across as long as four years, in exchange for what the proposal calls "principles-based narrative disclosures" — closer to a detailed whitepaper than a prospectus. The larger exemption, up to $75 million every 12 months, asks for more: audited financial statements and semiannual reporting to the SEC, a structure that mirrors Regulation A+, the existing mid-size capital-raising exemption used by traditional startups.

Both exemptions come with a third piece that has drawn less attention than the dollar figures but may matter more to issuers: a proposed safe harbor from the "investment contract" test that has, since a 1946 Supreme Court case called SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., decided whether a token counts as a security at all. Under the proposal, a project that shows its founders have "permanently ceased all essential managerial efforts" and that its network operates without a central controlling party could exit securities classification entirely — a formal, criteria-based off-ramp rather than the case-by-case litigation that has defined crypto enforcement for years.

That idea is not new. SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce, nicknamed "Crypto Mom" in the industry press, has pitched versions of a token safe harbor since 2020, updating it in 2021 without the commission ever taking it up formally. Tuesday's proposal is the first time anything resembling it has moved from a commissioner's pet project to an actual rulemaking text.

The proposal also asserts federal supremacy over the point: it would preempt state securities registration and qualification requirements for offerings made under either exemption, and for certain secondary-market resales. States have historically run their own "blue sky" registration regimes alongside federal law, and crypto issuers have had to navigate both. A single federal standard, if it survives comment and any court challenges, would remove one layer of that compliance work.

Why the SEC moved without Congress

The timing traces back to a bill, not a market event. The Digital Asset Market Clarity Act — legislation meant to divide jurisdiction over crypto assets between the SEC and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission — passed the House 294-134 in July 2025 and cleared the Senate Banking Committee 15-9 in May. It then stalled. Senate Majority Leader John Thune said the chamber lacked time for debate and the 60 votes needed for cloture before the August recess, and an unresolved ethics provision — capping how much crypto federal officials could personally hold — became the sticking point, with the White House rejecting Democrats' preferred language.

Chair Paul Atkins, who has pushed a broader deregulatory push he calls "Project Crypto" since taking over the agency last year, had already signaled the SEC would write its own rules if Congress could not finish the job. "Today, we are charting a new course with a package of exemptions that would facilitate capital formation and allow crypto asset innovation to flourish in the United States in the years ahead," Atkins said in a statement accompanying the proposal. He was candid about the limits of that approach, too: "Legislation remains indispensable to enacting 'future-proofed' rules of the road that are durable enough to protect the work we are undertaking today from being unwound by a future rogue regulator." A rule, unlike a statute, can be rewritten by the next commission with a simple vote.

The three-member commission that issued the proposal — Atkins plus fellow Republicans Hester Peirce and Mark Uyeda — is currently all-Republican; Caroline Crenshaw, the SEC's last Democratic commissioner and its most consistent crypto skeptic, departed the agency earlier this year. Her absence is part of why the proposal moved as a unanimous staff release rather than a contested vote: a meeting originally set for August 14 to formally approve publication was cancelled, and the commission issued the text days later without the usual open session.

Reaction, and the objections that remain

Industry analysts framed the move as removing a binary choice that has shaped crypto fundraising for years — full securities registration on one side, unregistered offerings and the litigation risk that comes with them on the other. TD Cowen called it "a pivotal rulemaking." Not everyone agrees the trade-off is a good one. Senator Elizabeth Warren, a longtime critic of lighter-touch crypto regulation, warned the exemptions could "undermine decades of investor protections," a version of the objection Crenshaw made from inside the commission until her departure — that carving crypto out of standard disclosure requirements trades investor safeguards for capital-formation convenience.

Both exemptions retain federal antifraud and antimanipulation liability regardless of the lighter disclosure burden, which is the SEC's answer to that criticism: issuers can skip registration paperwork, but they cannot skip the consequences of lying to investors. Whether that balance holds up will be tested in the coming comment period, when consumer advocates, state securities regulators who stand to lose enforcement authority, and crypto issuers eager for the exemptions will all file competing arguments.

What comes next

The 60-day comment window is the immediate checkpoint — public feedback closes roughly mid-October, after which the commission can finalize, amend, or shelve the rule. State securities regulators, who would lose registration authority over exempt offerings under the preemption provision, are likely to be among the more vocal objectors, alongside investor-advocacy groups pointing to the lighter disclosure standard on the $5 million tier. Congress, meanwhile, has not abandoned the Clarity Act; if the ethics-provision standoff resolves and the bill eventually passes, it would sit alongside — or potentially override — whatever the SEC finalizes on its own. For now, Regulation Crypto Assets is the first concrete answer, in eight years of on-and-off debate, to a question crypto issuers have been asking since well before the SEC started regulating them mostly through enforcement lawsuits: what would it actually take to raise money in tokens and stay on the right side of federal law.
