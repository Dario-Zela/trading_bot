Europe's summer heat toll passes 25,000 deaths as wildfires displace hundreds of thousands

A cemetery burns in Piedralaves, Ávila province, as wildfires swept central Spain in late July 2026. — Acohenc / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Heat has killed 25,000-plus across Europe and wildfires have burned 1.2 million acres, straining grids and forcing insurers to reprice climate risk.

Europe's summer of 2026 has produced a death toll that puts it among the deadliest on record for heat, and a wildfire season that has burned an area larger than Luxembourg twice over. More than 25,000 people have died from heat-related causes across the continent since April, according to figures compiled by government agencies and research institutes in Germany, Spain, France, Britain, Switzerland and the Netherlands — and that count predates the heatwave that struck in early August, meaning the final summer tally will run higher still.

Germany has borne the heaviest toll, with the Robert Koch Institute attributing roughly 11,900 excess deaths to heat since the spring. Four distinct heatwaves have rolled across Western Europe since May, each pushing temperatures past 40°C in France and Spain and unsettling a continent that the World Weather Attribution service says is now warming nearly twice as fast as the global average. A rapid-response study by the same group concluded that the June heat spell would have been "virtually impossible" fifty years ago, and that the sweltering overnight temperatures recorded this year are roughly 100 times more likely than they were during the deadly 2003 heatwave.

Fire fronts and forced displacement

The wildfires that accompanied the heat have been unusual less for any single blaze than for their persistence. France has burned through roughly 115,000 hectares this year, a national record, with the Gironde fire alone accounting for some 42,000 hectares. In Spain, fires west of Madrid, in Ávila and Toledo, merged into a single front that had consumed about 80,000 hectares by late July — the worst blaze in the Madrid region's history — while the Ávila fire on its own, at roughly 50,000 hectares, is now the largest in Spain's recorded history. Portugal has lost close to 30,000 hectares. Add Greece, Italy and Turkey and the continental total climbs past 1.2 million acres, roughly the combined area of Los Angeles and Chicago.

The human displacement has been on a similar scale. Authorities evacuated around 220,000 people in France at the peak of the fires, and NASA satellite tracking put the combined displacement across France and Spain at over 300,000 as smoke plumes from the two countries merged into a single visible mass stretching across the aerosol maps. Spain's civil protection agency and NGOs including World Central Kitchen have run sustained relief operations in Ávila province, where fire crews have battled the blaze for weeks rather than days.

The grid buckles

Heat of this duration does not just threaten lives and property — it strains the machinery that keeps the lights on. France's fleet of river-cooled nuclear reactors, which normally supplies around 70% of the country's electricity, has taken the brunt of it. In June, state utility EDF cut about 6.2 gigawatts — nearly 10% of nuclear output — as river temperatures on the Rhône, Seine and Garonne approached the regulatory ceiling of roughly 28°C at which reactors must throttle back or shut down to avoid overheating the waterways they discharge into. By August 12, more than a fifth of French nuclear capacity sat idle simultaneously — the largest environmental-linked shortfall since EDF began publishing the data in 2015, compounded in places by jellyfish clogging cooling intakes.

The effects showed up quickly in wholesale markets. Italian spot power prices and German front-month contracts both touched their highest levels in three-and-a-half years on the same day in early August, and several southeastern European countries moved to formal electricity rationing as demand for cooling collided with reduced nuclear, hydro and even wind output — heatwaves tend to coincide with the still, high-pressure conditions that also becalm turbines.

Repricing the tail risk

For insurers and reinsurers, the summer has been less a single catastrophe than confirmation of a trend they had already begun pricing for. Swiss Re's institute has described the pattern bluntly as Europe's "chronic heat risk," warning that sustained heat reduces agricultural productivity, strains water and energy systems, damages infrastructure and lowers labour output all at once. "Europe's recent wildfires highlight how hotter and drier conditions are making large wildfires more likely," said Balz Grollimund, the firm's head of catastrophe perils, and more costly given how much infrastructure now sits inside wildland-urban interface zones.

The numbers underline the exposure gap. Swiss Re puts total economic losses from this summer's French, Spanish and Greek wildfires at roughly €19.1 billion, but historically only a fraction of such damage is covered — Spain's 2025 wildfire season produced close to €5 billion in economic losses, of which barely €1 billion was insured. Across the EU, weather and climate losses have totalled some €822 billion since 1980, with a quarter of that damage concentrated in just the last four years, and only about a quarter of the total is insured — dropping below 5% in some member states. That gap increasingly lands on public balance sheets: Germany allocated €30 billion in public funds after the 2021 floods. Fitch's Federico Barriga-Salazar summed up the fiscal problem for stretched governments already running eurozone-average deficits near 3% of GDP: "The problem is that they're becoming more recurrent," shifting what used to be treated as one-off shocks into a recurring budget line.

What's next

With wildfire season not yet over in the Mediterranean and reinsurance renewal negotiations for January 2027 already underway, the next data points to watch are the treaty pricing that emerges from those talks and whether first-half 2026's comparatively low $42 billion in global insured catastrophe losses — which Swiss Re has cautioned not to read as a trend reversal — holds through year-end. Portugal's move toward mandatory home insurance backed by a state disaster fund, announced after this year's floods, may be an early signal of the policy direction other governments follow as the protection gap becomes harder to ignore.
