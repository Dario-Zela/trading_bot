Oil holds near $85 as Strait of Hormuz standoff drags into sixth month

The Strait of Hormuz and Gulf of Oman, photographed at night from the International Space Station. — NASA / Tim Kopra (Expedition 46, ISS) — public domain
In one sentence
Iran's demands for sanctions relief and reparations keep the Strait of Hormuz effectively shut after six months, with WTI near $85 pricing in the stalemate.

Six months into the closure of one of the world's most important shipping chokepoints, the basic standoff hasn't moved. Iran wants sanctions lifted, frozen assets released, American troops out of the region and reparations paid before it will let commercial traffic resume normal flow through the Strait of Hormuz. Washington wants Iran to accept the blockade is over and get back to shipping. Neither side is budging, and crude is sitting where a market prices that kind of stalemate — elevated, but not panicked.

WTI settled near $85 a barrel this week, up for a third straight session, while Brent held around $91. That's roughly where both benchmarks have hovered since Iran's Supreme National Security Council spelled out its conditions in full earlier this month: an end to the U.S. naval blockade, sanctions relief, asset releases, a war-damages bill, and American withdrawal. Iranian security official Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr put it plainly — the strait stays shut "until America corrects its behavior."

Traffic tells the real story

President Trump has claimed the U.S. Navy has swept the strait clear of mines and holds "100% control" of the waterway. The shipping data says otherwise. Maritime intelligence firm Windward counted just 10 vessels crossing the strait on a recent Monday; MarineTraffic logged between eight and 15 transits across three separate days in early August. Pre-war, roughly 130 ships a day made the passage, carrying about a fifth of global oil supply. That gap — control claimed versus control exercised — is the crux of why oil hasn't fully priced out the risk premium: buyers can see the tankers aren't moving, whatever Washington says.

Trump has also floated turning Iran's reparations demand back on itself, suggesting Tehran instead compensate the U.S. and Iranian protesters for decades of alleged regime violence — a counter-proposal that, predictably, has gone nowhere. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent had said in early August he expected a deal restoring freedom of movement for commercial vessels; Iran's list of conditions days later undercut that optimism.

A narrow bright spot, and its limits

The one piece of actual progress is procedural rather than political: Iran says it's close to finalizing a new shipping-lane arrangement with Oman — a "middle corridor" through the strait that would sit outside the direct U.S.-Iran negotiation entirely. It's a workaround, not a resolution, and Tehran has been explicit that its broader demands on Washington still stand regardless of what gets agreed with Muscat.

For a market that has now lived with this closure for six months, the current setup functions like a standing tax on crude — a premium that isn't going anywhere without a catalyst, but isn't growing either absent one. That's the mechanical read on why $85 WTI has held rather than climbed: geopolitical risk that's fully anticipated stops being new information. The next real move will come from either side blinking — a sanctions concession, a reparations formula, a fresh attack — not from the passage of another calendar week. Watch the Oman shipping-lane announcement, if it lands, for a signal on whether traffic actually starts moving again, transit counts still say no.
