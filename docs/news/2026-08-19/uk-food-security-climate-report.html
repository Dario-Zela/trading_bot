Report: UK relies on heat-stressed countries for fruit and veg it could grow itself

Strawberries growing under polytunnels on a British farm — one of the crops the report says the UK could grow far more of domestically. — Geograph.org.uk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A new report warns Britain's food security is exposed to climate stress abroad because it imports produce that could be domestically grown.

The strawberries on a British supermarket shelf in February almost certainly did not come from Kent. They came from Huelva, in southern Spain, or from Morocco — places that grow soft fruit through the winter precisely because Britain's climate won't allow it. But increasingly, the same is true even in high summer, for produce Britain's own fields and glasshouses are perfectly capable of growing. That dependency, according to a new report from the Food Foundation, is becoming a liability.

The charity's analysis, published Wednesday, found that 18 of the UK's top 20 sources of fruit and vegetables — 90% — are rated moderately to severely vulnerable to climate breakdown. Britain imports 40% of its food overall, including 80% of its fruit and 47% of its vegetables, one of the highest import shares in Europe. A large share of that produce arrives from countries already absorbing more frequent heatwaves, drought and erratic rainfall — conditions that hit crop yields directly.

"There is a risk [the impacts of climate breakdown] will drive up prices in the future, putting nutritious foods further out of reach," said Anna Taylor, the Food Foundation's executive director.

A gap between what Britain buys and what it could grow

The report's central finding is not that the UK lacks the land or climate to reduce this exposure — it's that it has largely chosen not to. Of the nine fruits Britons buy most, five — apples, strawberries, blueberries, raspberries and pears — grow well domestically, yet the UK produces under 40% of what it consumes for most of them. Among vegetables, only carrots reach a genuinely high self-sufficiency rate, at 94%. Tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers, cucumbers, onions, broccoli and green beans, all popular and all growable at scale in Britain, sit below 50%.

That gap has widened rather than closed. Imports of fresh produce from Spain, South Africa, Morocco and Peru all rose sharply between 2019 and 2024 — Moroccan imports alone climbed by roughly 289% — while domestic production's share of the UK's fruit and vegetable supply fell from 42% in 1987 to about 22% by 2013. Spain, the single largest source, is projected to go from nine days a year above 35C to twenty by mid-century. Cold snaps in Spain and Morocco already caused shortages of lettuce and tomatoes in UK supermarkets in spring 2023 — a preview, the report argues, of more frequent disruption to come.

Why growers haven't filled the gap

The report is less an argument that British soil can't do the job than a catalogue of why it hasn't been asked to. Ali Capper, an apple and hops grower in Worcestershire who chairs the trade body British Apples and Pears, put the barrier list plainly: growers need "certainty on continued and increased access to the seasonal workers scheme; water for food; an agile planning regulation; competitively priced energy."

That last point bites hardest in glasshouse horticulture, where UK energy costs run higher than in competing European producers, squeezing the economics of growing tomatoes and peppers under glass. Add labour shortages since Brexit ended free movement, slow planning processes for reservoirs and glasshouse expansion, and post-Brexit subsidy changes that have left farm incomes less predictable, and the incentives tilt toward buying from Almería rather than investing in Kent or the Vale of Evesham.

The Food Foundation puts a number on the upside of closing even part of that gap: expanding UK horticulture to replace half of current fruit and vegetable imports would lift national food self-sufficiency to 66%, its highest level since 2000, while adding an estimated £1.9bn to the economy and creating more than 20,000 jobs.

What happens next

The report lands as UK ministers face pressure on cost of living and food-price inflation, and as this year's domestic drought has already strained farm output at home — underscoring that resilience abroad and resilience at home are not separate problems. The Food Foundation is calling on government to use the forthcoming farming roadmap to tie support explicitly to horticulture expansion, rather than leaving glasshouse and field-vegetable growers to compete for funding designed with livestock and arable farms in mind. Whether that translates into water infrastructure, seasonal labour guarantees or energy relief for growers will be the test of whether this becomes policy rather than another report on a shelf.
