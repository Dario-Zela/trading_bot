STANDING WATCHES · IRAN-HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-06-22 · 3 min read

Iran-Hormuz: oil holds near three-month lows as supply expectations embed

The Strait of Hormuz as seen by NASA's MODIS satellite — the 21-mile-wide chokepoint through which roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil passes. — NASA / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A US-Iran framework deal sent WTI below $80 for the first time since March, cooling the energy-driven inflation that has kept the Fed on a hiking footing.

The Strait of Hormuz is moving oil again.

A framework agreement between the Trump administration and Iran — mediated by Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif and signed into effect on Wednesday, June 18 — committed Tehran to reopening the strait and Washington to lifting its naval blockade. The deal is interim: a 60-day window remains for a permanent accord. But markets have moved as though the supply shock that haunted 2026 inflation is already in retreat.

WTI crude slipped below $80 a barrel, its lowest mark since early March and down roughly 22% from its one-month peak. Brent settled near $77.73. The national average gasoline price has fallen below $4 a gallon — a roughly 30% decline over two months. On Friday evening, Vice-President JD Vance put a specific number on the reopening: "Last night, 12.5 million barrels of oil went to the Strait of Hormuz — that is a high since the beginning of the conflict."

The Fed arithmetic

The implications for monetary policy are direct. Energy has been the most visible source of inflation's stickiness through 2026. The Iran war — which the International Energy Agency described as the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market — pushed core PCE from 3.0% in December 2025 to 3.3% in April, and the Fed's own year-end PCE forecast was revised up to 3.6% from 2.7%.

At his first FOMC meeting on June 17, new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh held the federal funds rate at 3.50%-3.75% and mentioned "price stability" twelve times. Nine of eighteen committee members pencilled in at least one rate hike before year-end. Rate-cut expectations, once the dominant Wall Street thesis entering 2026, had been substantially repriced.

A structurally lower energy price would reshape those projections. Jason Katz, a senior portfolio manager who discussed the deal on Friday's edition of "Mornings with Maria" on Fox Business, put the transmission mechanism plainly: "If we get this deal, oil should continue to abate. If that happens, the Fed will be empowered. Every dollar not spent at the pump is a dollar the consumer spends elsewhere." The 10-year Treasury yield has already edged down to 4.49%, off roughly 12 basis points over the past month.

The caveat is built into the timeline. The memorandum of understanding is a framework, not a settlement. Oxford Economics noted that markets "welcomed that both sides signed sooner than expected," while BIMCO's Fabien Yip cautioned that relief is "largely priced in" given the logistical complexity: more than 500 vessels await passage, and shipping companies still lack clarity on safe-transit procedures.

The first hard data point will be the PCE print for May, due later this month — the earliest read that could capture any energy-price relief feeding through to consumer prices. If the numbers cooperate, the rate-cut trade will have its strongest argument since the war began.
