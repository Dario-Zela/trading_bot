PCE data forecast to show faster inflation, reinforcing rate-hike consensus

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the U.S. central bank — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
May PCE data due Thursday is forecast to show accelerating inflation, but markets have already absorbed the Fed's hawkish shift — the data beat is non-news.

The numbers should look worse when the Bureau of Economic Analysis releases May personal consumption expenditure data on Thursday. Economists expect headline PCE to accelerate to roughly 4.1% year-on-year, up from April's 3.8%, with core PCE — which strips out food and energy — rising to around 3.4% from 3.3%. War-related oil price shocks flowing from Middle East conflict have pushed gasoline costs higher, and those increases are now spreading into broader price categories.

But at trading desks, the reaction is likely muted. The Federal Reserve — or at least a decisive plurality of it — has already reckoned with hotter inflation. At the June 17 FOMC meeting, the first chaired by Kevin Warsh, officials held the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75% for a fourth consecutive meeting. The more telling signal came from the dot plot: nine of nineteen committee members now project at least one quarter-point rate hike this year, six expecting two or more. The median end-2026 rate forecast moved to 3.8%, up from 3.4% in March. The committee's own PCE inflation projection was revised sharply upward, to 3.6% from 2.7%.

The Surprise Is Already Priced

Bloomberg Economics was direct after the June meeting: "The June FOMC meeting, with half of the committee leaning toward a tighter policy path, sent a hawkish jolt through markets." That jolt has since settled into rate expectations. A PCE print that simply confirms the committee's revised inflation view adds no new directional information. The asymmetry runs the other way — only a dramatic downside miss would reopen uncertainty, and forecasters aren't anticipating one.

There is an overlay from trade policy. Dallas Fed research published last month estimated that Trump administration import duties are adding approximately 0.8 percentage points to core PCE annually, meaning underlying inflation absent tariff effects would run closer to 2.3%. That distinction matters for the committee's read on duration: if tariff pressures are eventually unwound by negotiation or a growth slowdown, the rate-hike case weakens. If they embed, the inflation path is stickier — and Thursday's number will look more structural than cyclical.

Warsh himself adds a layer of ambiguity. He declined to submit his own rate forecast at the June meeting, citing concerns that the dot plot constrains policy flexibility, and signalled an end to forward guidance. Both moves make the Fed harder to read than under Jerome Powell — but the internal arithmetic is clear enough for now. A majority of officials lean toward higher rates, and an accelerating PCE print, priced-in or not, gives them no cause to reconsider.

Three officials speak before the data lands: New York Fed President John Williams and Chicago's Austan Goolsbee appear Wednesday; Minneapolis's Neel Kashkari follows Thursday. Their remarks will be the last public read on the committee's posture before the number hits.
