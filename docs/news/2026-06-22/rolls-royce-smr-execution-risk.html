Rolls-Royce's SMR programme: the gap between political promise and execution risk

Aerial view of Wylfa nuclear power station on Anglesey, the site chosen for the UK's first three Small Modular Reactors — UK Government / Open Government Licence v3.0 via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rolls-Royce holds contracts in three countries and political backing at home, but the UK SMR programme won't reach a Final Investment Decision until 2029 — and its 70% British content target is already fraying.

When Rolls-Royce signed a contract in April 2026 to build Britain's first small modular reactors, the ceremony had the texture of a political set-piece. Ed Miliband, the Energy Secretary, called it "a major milestone for Britain's energy security." Rachel Reeves declared the UK "back where it belongs." Apprentices in Rolls-Royce SMR overalls were on hand for photographs. The programme — three 470-megawatt pressurised-water reactors at Wylfa on the island of Anglesey — was presented as an act of industrial restoration, the clearest expression yet of Labour's ambition, in its own words, to "revive Britain's industrial heartlands."

The £599 million National Wealth Fund commitment announced that day is not a construction contract. It funds site-specific design work, regulatory engagement, and preparatory activity before a Final Investment Decision expected around 2029. Between the ceremony and any electrons reaching the grid lies a decade of execution, a contested supply chain, and a cost trajectory that has already moved in one direction.

Signed, but not quite sealed

The April 13 contract formalised an agreement between Great British Energy – Nuclear (GBE-N), the government vehicle created to drive the programme, and Rolls-Royce SMR. The headline figure sits within a broader £2.6 billion Spending Review allocation. The catch is that the money unlocks pre-construction activity, not the first pour of concrete.

A Final Investment Decision — the moment at which public and private capital must formally commit — is targeted for around 2029. At that point, the cost of each unit, currently estimated at £2–3 billion, will be tested against live procurement numbers. The original target, set in 2019, was £1.8 billion for the fifth unit off a production line that doesn't yet exist. Every major nuclear project of recent memory — Hinkley Point C most visibly — has seen estimates expand substantially between a headline announcement and an FID. Chris Cholerton, CEO of Rolls-Royce SMR, said the contract "unlocks the delivery" of the first three units. It does not yet fund them.

The regulatory picture adds a further variable. Rolls-Royce SMR's Generic Design Assessment (GDA) — the Office for Nuclear Regulation's evaluation of the reactor design itself — is expected to conclude around August 2026. That milestone matters: GDA completion removes one significant uncertainty. It does not authorise the construction of a specific plant. Planning applications, site licences, and a series of further regulatory approvals follow before a shovel enters the ground. The government's stated goal — Wylfa supplying power to the grid from the mid-2030s — requires that entire sequence to proceed without the kind of regulatory or procurement friction that has become almost routine in large nuclear programmes.

Wylfa carries its own historical freight. A previous project at the site, Wylfa Newydd, was planned as a large-scale conventional plant and scrapped in 2021 when Hitachi withdrew after years of development work. The community has been living under the shadow of a cancelled nuclear future once already.

The 70% question

Ministers framed the contract in the language of industrial revival: 3,000 jobs at peak construction around Wylfa, 5,000 more across the national supply chain, and a government target of 70% British content across the reactor fleet. These are the numbers Labour has staked its industrial strategy pitch on.

That 70% figure is under early pressure. In May 2026, Rolls-Royce SMR awarded pre-production work on nuclear island components — including reactor pressure vessels and volume compensators — to Škoda JS of the Czech Republic and Doosan Enerbility of South Korea. Reactor pressure vessels are among the most technically demanding and highest-value items in any nuclear build. The decision prompted Liam Byrne, chair of Parliament's Business and Trade Select Committee, to write to ministers demanding an explanation of how the outsourcing was compatible with the domestic content target.

The episode exposed a gap in the company's positioning. Before winning the government's selection competition in June 2025, Rolls-Royce SMR representatives told MPs the company was "unashamedly Team UK" and that up to 78% of a reactor could be manufactured in Britain. Asked recently for a current UK content figure, the company declined to provide one, stating instead that 88% of its spending since 2021 had gone to UK-based businesses — a different metric that covers a different time period and a different scope.

The trajectory has precedent within the programme itself. In April 2024, plans to build a dedicated pressure vessel manufacturing factory — once touted as a visible symbol of the UK's nuclear renaissance — were quietly abandoned, a casualty of delays in the government's SMR design competition. Rolls-Royce now intends to source pressure vessels from external suppliers, with Sheffield Forgemasters as one UK candidate. The Deeside factory in Wales, once envisaged as a regional anchor, has been scaled back. The dual-supplier model adopted for Wylfa reduces delivery risk; whether it is consistent with the political arithmetic is a different calculation.

What traders should price in

For Rolls-Royce the parent, the SMR programme is still a pre-revenue optionality position atop a core engine business that has driven the stock up more than 1,000% in five years. The group entered 2026 with net cash of £1.08 billion and liquidity of £8.5 billion — enough headroom to sustain dividends, buybacks, and SMR development concurrently. Rolls-Royce SMR itself posted a £78 million loss in October 2024, a figure analysts treat as a development-stage cost rather than a structural signal.

The export momentum is the clearest near-term positive. In June 2026 Rolls-Royce SMR won a multibillion-pound contract from Videberg Kraft — a Vattenfall subsidiary — to supply three SMRs on Sweden's west coast, beating GE Vernova Hitachi after a four-year competition. The company now holds programme positions in the UK, Czech Republic, and Sweden, and has won every competitively tendered SMR selection in Europe so far. The IEA puts the global SMR market at nearly £500 billion by 2050.

That international pipeline is the bull case. The execution risk is the domestic one: a programme that politicians have priced into regional employment expectations at a stage when the FID is three years out, the cost per unit is moving upward, and the supply chain commitments are already being reinterpreted. Government expectations are baked in; the delivery conditions are not.

The next concrete milestone is the GDA conclusion, expected by end of 2026. The FID in 2029 will be more revealing still — it will produce the first hard cost figure for the Wylfa units under real market conditions, and will test whether the 70% British content target survives contact with a global nuclear components market that has no particular reason to observe it.
