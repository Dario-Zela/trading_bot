UK watchdog warns AI poses catastrophic risk to telecoms resilience

The BT Tower in central London, once the heart of Britain's microwave telecoms network — now a symbol of an industry grappling with automation risk — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ofcom warns AI-driven network automation risks 'catastrophic' telecoms failures while the same technology rapidly becomes a weapon for hostile actors targeting critical infrastructure.

Britain's telecoms networks — the pipes carrying emergency calls, mobile data, broadband connections — are being handed over to AI management systems at a pace that Ofcom thinks nobody has properly stress-tested. The UK communications watchdog has warned operators including BT, Vodafone and Virgin Media O2 that this process risks catastrophic outages if it goes wrong.

The word "catastrophic" comes directly from an Ofcom document, not from press-release embellishment. The regulator's AI strategy for 2026/27, published earlier this month, states that "network automation carries risk, potentially of catastrophic network failure, so it is crucial that network automation is very carefully considered in every aspect." That sentence names the risk and then stops short of specifying what careful consideration actually requires.

There are two distinct problems here, and Ofcom is worried about both simultaneously. The first is operational: AI systems managing network routing, fault detection and load-balancing could fail in ways that cascade faster than human operators can intervene. The second is adversarial: AI models have become capable enough to be weaponised by hostile actors against the same infrastructure they are supposed to optimise.

Expert-level attacks, faster than anyone planned for

In April 2026, Natalie Black — Ofcom's head of infrastructure and connectivity — wrote to UK communications providers warning of frontier AI's emerging cyber capabilities. The letter cited UK AI Security Institute research showing that leading AI models moved from "apprentice-level" cyber skills in 2023 to "expert-level" capabilities by 2025, a two-year trajectory that puts systems in reach of tasks that would previously have required more than a decade of specialist experience.

The letter specifically referenced the institute's assessment of Anthropic's then-current frontier model, which it described as demonstrating "incrementally more capable" exploitation abilities compared to previously evaluated systems — a "clear escalation." Identical warnings went to operators of other essential services, including power and water utilities. Ofcom and the Department for Science, Innovation and Technology are treating AI cyber capability as a live threat requiring immediate assessment, not a future risk to log and revisit.

The two threats compound each other in an uncomfortable way. Telecoms operators are adopting AI to detect faults and manage traffic more efficiently — legitimate and sensible. But the same automation that speeds up benign maintenance also compresses the window between an attacker gaining access and causing damage. An AI-directed intrusion can probe and exploit faster than a human-supervised network can notice and respond.

Ofcom's awkward double bind

What makes this harder to navigate is that Ofcom is simultaneously running a review into whether its own security rules are making it harder for operators to adopt AI-driven defences. The Telecommunications (Security) Act 2021 requires providers to take "appropriate and proportionate" security measures — language that cuts both ways. A tool that helps an attacker can also help a defender. Overly cautious interpretation of the rules could push operators toward older, slower response systems just as their adversaries are upgrading.

That tension is not hypothetical. Operators regulated under the TSA 2021 are being asked for input on how they balance AI's defensive benefits against oversight obligations. The outcome of that review could materially shape how UK telecoms secures itself across the rest of the decade.

The stakes of getting this wrong are already visible in the enforcement record. Ofcom fined BT £17.5m in 2024 for a 10.5-hour blackout of the 999 emergency call network in June 2023, during which nearly 14,000 calls to police, fire, ambulance and coastguard services failed to connect. BT lacked adequate warning systems and disaster recovery capacity. That was a conventional software fault. The question Ofcom is now asking, implicitly, is whether AI-automated systems introduce failure modes that are faster to trigger, harder to diagnose mid-incident, and wider in their reach.

Ofcom's current posture is technology-neutral: operators can deploy whatever they choose, provided they meet security obligations. There are no mandatory standards yet for how much human oversight an AI network-management system must retain, or at what threshold automated decisions should escalate to a human engineer. Breaches of general security duties can attract penalties of up to £100,000 per day — meaningful pressure, but only after something goes wrong.

What to watch

Ofcom's ongoing study into how AI is actually being used in UK telecoms network management is the critical deliverable. Its findings are intended to inform future policy on network resilience. If the study identifies significant gaps between current practice and what the regulator considers adequate, the likely next step is binding conditions — either under the General Conditions of Entitlement or through new guidance under the TSA.

Two other pressure points matter. First, how quickly the AI Security Institute's capability assessments continue to move: if models go from expert to something beyond in another two years, the threat landscape will look different again before any new rules have bedded in. Second, whether a significant network incident occurs before Ofcom formalises its guidance — which would accelerate the shift from voluntary caution to enforceable mandate considerably.

So far, the regulator has moved from study to warning. The distance between warning and mandate is where the compliance risk for BT, Vodafone and their peers currently sits.
