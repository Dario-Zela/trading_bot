RBC analyst says SpaceX's industrial ambition is 'unlike anything' he's seen

SpaceX's Starship during a test flight — the vehicle at the center of the company's industrial buildout. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
RBC's Ken Herbert calls SpaceX's industrial buildout unprecedented, but the specifics point to a straightforward, checkable bet on Starship manufacturing scale.

SpaceX has been a public company for barely a month, and Wall Street is already struggling to describe the scale of what it's trying to build. Ken Herbert, RBC Capital Markets' longtime aerospace and defense analyst, put it this way in a note flagged by Barron's: "The amount of ambition around its industrialization is unlike anything I've ever witnessed." Herbert has covered rocket and plane makers for two decades. That kind of superlative, from him, is unusual — and worth unpacking rather than just repeating.

What Herbert means by "industrialization" isn't abstract. SpaceX is in the middle of building Starfactory, a million-square-foot Starship assembly plant at its Starbase site in South Texas, with a stated goal of eventually turning out a finished Starship every day — roughly 1,000 a year at full tilt. Next door, it's raising Gigabay, a $250 million, 700,000-square-foot production hall due to finish by the end of this year. That's less a rocket company than a heavy-industry buildout on the scale of an auto plant, aimed at making orbital launch as routine as a cargo flight.

Why the price target moved

Herbert initiated RBC's coverage with an Outperform rating and a $225 price target, roughly 40% above where the stock was trading — SpaceX priced its June IPO at $135 a share and opened around $150, valuing the company near $1.78 trillion. He's not alone in reaching for big numbers: Cantor Fitzgerald's Colin Canfield set a $246 target, calling SpaceX's ownership of rockets, satellites, ground stations and now orbital compute "vertical integration at civilization scale," implying about 64% upside. RBC's own math leans on roughly 2,440 Starship launches by 2030 and a nearly $2 trillion addressable market by 2035 spanning satellite connectivity, orbital data centers and eventually lunar logistics.

The first-quarter numbers show why the ambition needs financing at industrial scale. SpaceX's rocket segment posted a $662 million operating loss on $619 million of revenue, while Starlink — the satellite internet business — threw off $1.19 billion in operating income on $3.26 billion in revenue. In effect, a profitable satellite business is subsidizing a capital-intensive bet on manufacturing rockets like cars.

That's the real story behind Herbert's line: not a mysterious new insight, but a specific, checkable claim about capital deployment that happens to be dressed in dramatic language. The two catalysts RBC is actually watching are less rhetorical — a second-half 2026 Starship flight-test campaign and the start of a commercial launch cadence in 2027. If Starfactory hits its build rate and Starship starts flying on schedule, the industrialization talk will have been early and correct. If it slips, as Starship's timeline has before, the "unlike anything" framing will look like the analyst commentary it started as.
