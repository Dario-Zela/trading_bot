Majors circle Uruguay's untested Atlantic waters, betting on a Namibia-style discovery

A drillship of the type being contracted for Uruguay's first deepwater exploration well in nearly a decade. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Six oil majors now hold all seven of Uruguay's offshore blocks, betting its Atlantic geology mirrors Namibia's billion-barrel discoveries — with the first test well due late 2026.

Seven blocks of open Atlantic seabed, six of the world's largest oil companies, and a bet that geology doesn't lie: Uruguay's offshore acreage has gone from an afterthought of Latin American energy to one of the most closely watched exploration plays on the planet. Shell, Chevron, Eni, APA Corporation, QatarEnergy and Argentina's YPF now hold stakes across all seven of the blocks Uruguay has put up for licence, and the first deepwater well in roughly a decade is due to spud in the second half of 2026.

The wager is simple to state and expensive to test. Before the South Atlantic existed, Uruguay's continental margin sat pressed against what is now Namibia — the two coastlines were once a single landmass, later split apart as Africa and South America pulled away from each other some 140 million years ago. Namibia's Orange Basin has since produced a string of major finds: TotalEnergies' Venus discovery in 2022, Shell's Graff and Jonker wells the same year, and Portugal's Galp announcing an estimated 10 billion barrels at its Mopane field in 2024. Geologists argue the same Aptian-age source rock that filled those reservoirs should, in theory, continue across the ocean onto Uruguay's shelf. No one has drilled deep enough to confirm it.

"This could be much bigger than Vaca Muerta, it could yield millions and millions of barrels of production," YPF chairman and chief executive Horacio Marín told reporters, while cautioning the figures remain projections, not confirmed reserves.

Six majors, seven blocks, one seismic season

Uruguay's state hydrocarbons agency, ANCAP, finished handing out the last of the seven Atlantic blocks — labelled OFF-1 through OFF-7 — in March 2026, when QatarEnergy formalised its entry alongside Chevron and Shell. The resulting map is a lattice of joint ventures: Chevron operates OFF-1 with a 60% stake alongside junior partner Sintana Energy; Shell operates OFF-2 with QatarEnergy as a 30% partner; APA operates OFF-4 with Shell and, more recently, QatarEnergy; Shell also operates OFF-7 alongside QatarEnergy and Chevron; APA holds OFF-6 outright; and YPF controls OFF-5 through its MIWEN venture, with Italy's Eni negotiating to take a 50% stake and operatorship pending Uruguayan government sign-off. Sintana holds the remaining OFF-3 outright and is running its own farm-out process there.

Between them, the companies have committed more than $230 million to the current work programme — mostly 3D seismic surveys rather than drilling. APA's Sintana-linked seismic campaign on OFF-1 alone had covered 564 square kilometres by late March. The company is now moving to the next stage: a deepwater exploration well more than 200 kilometres offshore, in water over two kilometres deep, using a contracted drillship and four support vessels over an estimated 110 days. YPF's OFF-5 well, covering a 17,000-square-kilometre block in water as deep as 4,100 metres, is not expected until late 2027 or early 2028, pending seismic reprocessing and the Eni farm-in.

Bigger than Vaca Muerta — maybe

Marín's comparison is not idle marketing. Vaca Muerta, the Neuquén Basin shale formation that has turned Argentina into a swing exporter, holds an estimated 16 billion barrels of recoverable oil and 308 trillion cubic feet of recoverable gas, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration — the world's fourth-largest shale oil deposit and second-largest shale gas deposit. Preliminary, unverified studies cited by industry analysts put Uruguay's continental shelf potential at roughly 30 billion barrels of oil equivalent, which is why executives keep reaching for the comparison. But Vaca Muerta's number reflects more than a decade of drilling, well data and production history. Uruguay's number reflects seismic modelling and an analogy to a basin across an ocean. The country has drilled exactly three offshore exploration wells in its history — all dry, the most recent finished about ten years ago.

The strategic logic, independent of what the drill bit finds, is straightforward. A confirmed discovery would give South America a source of crude that ships directly onto Atlantic trade routes to Europe and Asia, without transiting the Strait of Hormuz or relying on Venezuelan or Middle Eastern supply. For Uruguay, a country that currently imports essentially all of its oil, even a modest find would be transformative; for Argentina's YPF, a stake in a second basin diversifies a company whose fortunes are otherwise tied entirely to one shale play. That is also why a domestic environmental coalition has already filed suit to halt the exploration campaign, arguing the risk assessment for deepwater drilling in an untested basin has outpaced the regulatory scrutiny behind it.

What comes next

The next real data point is APA's well later this year — the first hard test of whether Uruguay's Aptian-age rock actually holds hydrocarbons rather than merely resembling the formations that do. A dry hole would not kill the play outright, given the size of the acreage still uncertain, but it would sharply cool the majors' appetite ahead of YPF's own well in 2027–28. A discovery, on the other hand, would put Uruguay on a very short list of frontier basins — alongside Namibia, Suriname and Guyana — where a handful of wells reordered a country's economic future within a decade. Until the drillship spuds, everything else is seismic data and a plausible story about what the ocean floor looked like 140 million years ago.
