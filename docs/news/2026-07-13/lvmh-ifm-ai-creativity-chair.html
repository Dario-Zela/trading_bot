LVMH funds research chair with IFM Fashion School to study AI and creativity

In one sentence
LVMH is funding an IFM research chair on how creative minds engage with AI — a sign the luxury giant now treats the technology as a design-workflow risk, not a novelty.

Somewhere between the ateliers stitching haute couture by hand and the servers running LVMH's internal AI assistants sits a question the world's largest luxury group has spent years avoiding: what actually happens to a designer's imagination when a machine can generate a thousand variations of it before lunch. LVMH has now put its name and its money behind trying to answer that, establishing a research chair at the Institut Français de la Mode (IFM) — the Paris fashion school it effectively presides over — dedicated to the study of science and creativity, with a specific brief to examine how creative minds engage with technology.

It is a modest-sounding move dressed up in academic language, and that is precisely the point. For a conglomerate that spent the early years of the generative-AI boom treating the technology as a back-office concern — inventory forecasting, translation, customer service — funding a chair that puts AI's effect on the creative process itself under a microscope is a tacit admission that the disruption has reached the design table.

From skepticism to a seminar room

IFM runs its outside partnerships as formal academic chairs, each typically a multi-year collaboration in which a company funds original research and teaching on a subject core to its business. Première Vision backed one on textiles in 2016; Kering funded a sustainability chair in 2019; the fragrance house Mane endowed one on diversity and inclusion in beauty last year. Chanel and its ateliers arm, le19M, sponsor a chair in fashion savoir-faire, headed by the historian Émilie Hammen, that studies how craft knowledge is preserved and passed on. LVMH's new chair sits in that lineage, but its subject is more unsettled than heritage textiles or supply chains — it is asking what creativity even means once a generative model is part of the workflow.

The institutional logic tracks. Sidney Toledano, who spent decades running Christian Dior and LVMH's fashion division before moving into a senior advisory role at the group, now chairs IFM's board — a link that makes LVMH's underwriting of the school's research agenda less a act of philanthropy than a bet on shaping how the next generation of designers is trained to think about the tools now sitting on their desktops. IFM was also just ranked France's top fashion school by Le Figaro Étudiant, in the publication's inaugural ranking — a reminder that whoever funds its research agenda is funding the training ground for a meaningful share of the industry's future creative directors.

What LVMH has already been testing

The chair formalizes work that was already under way informally. Earlier this year, three IFM master's students — Paula Lessel, Borja Fernandez Garcia and Alexis Lopez Angeles — used LVMH support to work with Limn.ai, a research tool built to explore "latent creative spaces," distorting and hybridizing reference images into new forms. The brief, notably, wasn't speed: IFM framed the exercise as using AI to expand the boundaries of creative thinking rather than to accelerate prototyping, a distinction the school has been careful to draw.

LVMH's data chief, Gonzague de Pirey, ran a parallel experiment pairing IFM students with those from École Polytechnique, and came away with what he's described as three working rules for the technology: the AI explores, but the human decides; it has to remain a tool the creator can hack rather than one that dictates outcomes; and there needs to be a constant back-and-forth between machine and maker rather than a one-shot output. He has also noted, pointedly, that AI "doesn't necessarily save time" — the value is in the exploration, not the shortcut. Internally, creative teams across LVMH's roughly 75 maisons now have access to an in-house generative assistant called LucIA, and the group used its VivaTech stand this year — a "Dream Gallery" walking visitors through the luxury value chain — to stage a public session titled, in French, "creativity and AI in dialogue."

The stakes behind the seminar

LVMH's own language on the subject has stayed carefully hedged — the group has said generative tools carry "remarkable creative potential" while also raising "questions about authenticity, as well as responsibility," and its chief data officer has framed the task as embracing the technology "in the right way that is virtuous for our future." That caution is the tell. A company confident that AI was a footnote to craftsmanship wouldn't need a standing research chair to interrogate the relationship; it would let its ateliers absorb the tools quietly and move on. Instead LVMH is treating the question of what AI does to a designer's hand as a subject worth years of formal study — the kind of hedge a group makes when it suspects a core input to its business, creative labor itself, is genuinely being reshaped rather than merely assisted.

What comes out of the chair — its first published research, any change in IFM's design curriculum, whether Limn.ai-style tools move from a three-student pilot into standard studio practice — will be the next marker of how far LVMH thinks this goes. IFM's academic year resumes in the autumn, and its VivaTech dialogue suggests the group intends to keep the conversation public rather than confine it to a seminar room.
