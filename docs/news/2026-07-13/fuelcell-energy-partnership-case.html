FuelCell Energy leans on a new partnership to make the case for its stock

FuelCell Energy's manufacturing facility in Torrington, Connecticut. — Dannel Malloy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
FuelCell Energy's stock popped on a non-binding Siemens tie-up, but a widening quarterly loss and dilutive raises suggest the rally is ahead of the business.

FuelCell Energy makes hydrogen and molten-carbonate fuel cells — sealed boxes that turn natural gas or hydrogen into electricity without combustion — and for most of the past two years its stock has moved less on what the Danbury, Connecticut company sells than on who it says it might work with. The latest instance came on July 9, when FuelCell Energy and Siemens announced a memorandum of understanding to jointly develop electrical infrastructure for fuel-cell power projects. Shares jumped 11% on the news. They have since given most of that back, trading around $21 — up sharply on the year but well off the 52-week high of $37.88.

The Siemens deal, on paper, targets a real gap. Siemens will design and supply the electrical balance-of-plant systems — the wiring, switchgear and medium-voltage equipment — needed to connect fuel-cell installations above 100 megawatts to the grid or to a customer's own site, the kind of scale data-center operators are now asking for. "This collaboration enables us to deliver what the market has been asking for — bringing generation and electrical infrastructure together into a single solution," said Shankar Achanta, FuelCell Energy's chief product and technology officer. Kevin Brown, who leads sustainability solutions at Siemens Smart Infrastructure USA, framed it as pairing FuelCell's generation technology with Siemens' "scalable, on-site power solutions."

What the announcement does not contain is a dollar figure, a signed project, or a binding commitment. A memorandum of understanding is a statement of intent, not a contract — the two companies have agreed to explore pilot projects and joint engineering work, not to build anything specific yet.

The numbers underneath the announcement

FuelCell Energy's fiscal second quarter, reported in June, showed why investors keep needing a new reason to buy in. Revenue fell 5% year over year to $35.6 million, and the net loss widened to $77.6 million, driven largely by a $42.6 million impairment at the company's Groton, Connecticut project after management decided to upgrade it with newer-generation power blocks. Adjusted EBITDA improved only slightly, to a loss of $17.1 million. Order backlog stood at $1.14 billion as of April 30, down almost 10% from a year earlier, even as the sales pipeline — orders not yet booked — grew 267% sequentially on data-center demand.

The company has funded the gap with equity, not earnings. An at-the-market stock program raised roughly $153 million in net proceeds this year, part of a $225 million offering that priced at $21 a share, pushing cash and restricted cash to $440.9 million. Trailing per-share losses run near $6.20, and with a beta above 2, the stock trades more like a leveraged bet on hydrogen sentiment than a claim on Siemens-grade industrial cash flow. FuelCell Energy has also long depended on federal support — a 30% investment tax credit for fuel cells that ran through 2024 before folding into a technology-neutral clean-energy credit, plus an Export-Import Bank loan and project financing that underwrote earlier builds.

None of that is disqualifying for a company chasing a genuine data-center power shortage. But it means the Siemens collaboration has to do double duty: validate the technology to a skeptical market, and eventually convert into contracts large enough to close a backlog gap that a name-brand MOU, by itself, does not touch. The next test is whether pilot projects under the agreement produce actual purchase orders before FuelCell Energy's fiscal third-quarter results, expected in September, show whether the loss is still widening.
