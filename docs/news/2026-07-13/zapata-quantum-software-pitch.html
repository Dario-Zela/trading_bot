Zapata Quantum pitches software and AI partnerships as quantum computing's next phase

In one sentence
Zapata Quantum CEO Sumit Kapur told an investor conference that quantum computing's value is shifting from hardware to software and AI, as the OTC-listed firm seeks funding and a DOE grant.

Zapata Quantum has never had a hardware problem to sell its way out of — that was always the point. Founded in 2017 as a spinout of Harvard's quantum computing lab, the company builds algorithms and software that are meant to run on whatever quantum machine a customer happens to have, rather than betting on one architecture winning the field. This week its chief executive, Sumit Kapur, used a slot at the Global Technology Virtual Investor Conference — hosted jointly by Virtual Investor Conferences and OTC Markets on July 9 — to argue that the rest of the industry is finally coming around to that view.

Kapur's pitch, delivered to an audience of retail and institutional investors watching a company that trades over the counter under the ticker ZPTA, was that quantum computing is shifting from a phase dominated by hardware announcements — more qubits, longer coherence times — to one where software, algorithms and applications determine which companies actually capture value. "We continue to drive value creation and utility from all of this exciting technology," Kapur told the conference, framing Zapata as a company that sells picks and shovels regardless of which hardware vendor strikes gold.

The case for hardware agnosticism

Enterprises weighing quantum investments face a genuine dilemma: superconducting qubits, trapped ions, neutral atoms and photonic systems are all still competing for dominance, with no consensus winner in sight. Zapata's argument is that committing software development to any single one of those platforms is a bet most corporate R&D budgets shouldn't have to make. Kapur cited the company's intellectual property position — more than 60 foundational patents in quantum software and over 40 peer-reviewed papers — as evidence the approach has substance rather than just marketing behind it.

He pointed to a concrete example: research conducted with Dana-Farber Cancer Institute and St. Jude Children's Research Hospital on the KRAS cancer mutation, which used a hybrid quantum-classical method to identify 15 molecules worth synthesizing. Nature Biotechnology named the resulting paper one of its top ten of 2025. On the corporate side, Kapur listed BMW, BP, BASF and Mitsubishi Chemical as clients, alongside a role leading all three phases of a DARPA quantum benchmarking program — the kind of government and industrial roster that OTC-listed microcaps rarely get to claim.

AI as the second leg

The presentation's other thread was Zapata's collaboration with Nvidia, applying agentic AI to quantum algorithm development and quantum resource estimation — essentially using AI systems to help design and calibrate the quantum software workloads Zapata sells, rather than treating quantum and AI as separate bets. Kapur described the company's strategy in a three-layer framework: the "why" of use cases, the "what" of algorithms and the "how" of hardware, with software sitting across all three and hardware relegated to a variable rather than a foundation.

That framing matters for a company that has spent the past year on balance-sheet repair rather than product launches. Zapata restructured more than $20 million in debt and closed an oversubscribed $15 million strategic financing round, according to the company. It has also applied to the Department of Energy's Genesis Mission — the federal initiative, formalized in June, to build a fault-tolerant, scientifically relevant quantum computer by 2028 through a DOE-run competition and a national quantum supercomputing facility. A successful application would bring both funding and a marquee validation of Zapata's software for use on next-generation hardware it doesn't itself build.

What it means for the stock

ZPTA shares have traded well below their March 2024 all-time high of $13.60, recently changing hands near $1.20 with a market capitalization in the neighborhood of $200 million — a reminder that the investor-conference circuit, including this presentation and one-on-one meetings scheduled for July 10 and July 14, is as much about sustaining a story for a thinly traded stock as it is about scientific substance. Kapur's own list of near-term catalysts reflects that: converting pilot relationships into paying commercial customers, landing government grants such as the Genesis Mission application, and eventually pursuing an uplisting to Nasdaq or the NYSE.

None of those are guaranteed, and the gap between a compelling technical narrative and a share price recovery has swallowed plenty of small-cap technology stories before. What is more testable is the DOE decision on Genesis Mission applicants, expected as the initiative's 2028 fault-tolerant computer target gets built out over the coming two years — the next real signal of whether Zapata's software-first pitch is winning outside a conference hall.
