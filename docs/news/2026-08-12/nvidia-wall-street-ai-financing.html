Nvidia recruits Wall Street titans for $500bn AI infrastructure push

The Wall Street street sign in Lower Manhattan, near the New York Stock Exchange. — JSquish / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Nvidia signed MOUs with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise over $500bn of outside capital for AI data centres, betting compute can be financed like infrastructure.

Nvidia has spent three years selling the chips that power the artificial intelligence boom. Now it wants Wall Street to own the buildings that house them.

On August 10 the company announced memorandums of understanding with six of the world's largest financial institutions — Apollo Global Management, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield Asset Management, Goldman Sachs and KKR — to construct financing platforms capable of mobilising more than $500bn in third-party capital for AI data centres and the Nvidia hardware inside them. Shares in Nvidia rose about 1% on the news, a muted reaction to a number roughly equivalent to Sweden's annual economic output.

The pitch, delivered by chief executive Jensen Huang, is that compute itself — the racks of GPUs, the power contracts, the cooling systems — should be treated as an investable asset class in its own right, alongside commercial property, toll roads and other infrastructure that institutional money has financed for decades. "This is really the first time that technology chips have become an investable asset class," Huang told CNBC. In a statement, he framed the shift in more sweeping terms: the computer, he said, "is now part of the infrastructure, like electricity, like the internet."

Six firms, one thesis

The six partners are not committing $500bn outright — the MOUs are preliminary, and the figure is a mobilisation target rather than a funded pool. What they are doing is agreeing to build the machinery that lets pension funds, insurers and other long-duration capital pour money into AI infrastructure without Nvidia, or its hyperscaler customers, having to carry the debt on their own balance sheets.

Each firm's public language leaned on the same idea from a different angle. Apollo president Jim Zelter called compute "a scarce, mission-critical asset class with compelling investment characteristics." Brookfield chief executive Bruce Flatt said it was "fast becoming the essential layer of infrastructure." BlackRock's Larry Fink pointed to his firm's "ability to connect long-term capital to essential infrastructure," while Blackstone's Jon Gray noted the firm was already "enormous investors globally across the Nvidia ecosystem." Goldman Sachs chief executive David Solomon and KKR co-chiefs Joe Bae and Scott Nuttall struck similar notes about durable demand and long-duration capital.

The underlying appeal for asset managers is straightforward: fee income and yield at a moment when private credit funds are already the dominant lenders behind the data-centre boom. Blackstone, Apollo, BlackRock and peers such as Blue Owl and Pimco already originate most such debt, frequently through off-balance-sheet vehicles that keep the borrowing away from public company accounts.

The obsolescence problem

The difficulty with treating GPUs like toll roads is that toll roads don't get replaced by a faster model every 18 months. A data centre financed over 10 or 15 years assumes the asset inside it holds value for most of that span; Nvidia's own product cycle argues otherwise. Amazon shortened the depreciation schedule on its servers from six years to five in January 2025, citing the "increased pace of technology development" in AI — a change that added roughly $1.4bn to its annual depreciation charge. Huang has said Nvidia could offer "residual-value support for up to 25% of an opportunity" on some deals, a form of backstop that acknowledges the risk without fully answering who absorbs a loss if a chip generation ages out of usefulness before the debt backing it is repaid.

That question sits on top of a credit market already flagged as vulnerable. Global AI-related debt issuance is on track to reach roughly $570bn this year, and a Bank of America survey of 210 fund managers earlier in July found 48% now view AI hyperscaler capital spending as the most likely source of a future systemic credit event — more than double the share who said so in April. A further 34% pointed specifically to private credit, the segment doing most of the lending. Morgan Stanley has estimated private credit alone could supply an additional $800bn toward data-centre financing over the next two years.

What it signals

Seen against that backdrop, the Nvidia platforms look less like a novelty and more like an attempt to formalise and legitimise a financing pattern that was already underway, informally, through opaque private-credit structures. Bringing BlackRock, Goldman Sachs and Blackstone into named, public partnerships gives the arrangement the appearance of institutional rigour — standardised structures, presumably clearer disclosure — even though the MOUs themselves commit nothing yet. It is also a hedge for Nvidia: capital raised by outside investors to buy Nvidia hardware supports chip demand without appearing as Nvidia's own liability, and without requiring its hyperscaler customers to stretch their balance sheets further than markets have already grown nervous about.

Whether $500bn of institutional capital actually materialises depends on whether these MOUs convert into signed, funded structures — and on whether lenders can get comfortable with financing an asset whose useful life is set by Nvidia's own roadmap. Nvidia reports fiscal second-quarter earnings on August 26, the first checkpoint where analysts will press Huang on how many of the six platforms have moved from handshake to contract, and how much of the $500bn is more than a headline number.
