Labour opens poll lead over Reform UK for first time in over a year

Andy Burnham's official portrait as Prime Minister, taken at 10 Downing Street on 22 July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
An Ipsos poll gives Labour its first lead over Reform UK in a year, but the 'Burnham bounce' says nothing yet about fiscal policy direction.

Labour has opened its first opinion poll lead over Reform UK in more than a year, a fresh sign that the party's fortunes have turned since Andy Burnham replaced Keir Starmer as prime minister three weeks ago.

An Ipsos poll of 1,033 British adults, fielded 30 July to 4 August, put Labour on 28% — up four points since June — against 25% for Reform UK, whose support slipped a single point over the same period. The Conservatives held steady on 18%, with the Greens on 12% and the Liberal Democrats on 9%. Ipsos flags the usual caveat for a single survey: the nine-in-ten confidence interval spans four points either way, wide enough that the parties could in principle be level.

"Labour benefits from changing leadership, achieving their best position in over a year," said Ipsos's Gideon Skinner. "However, underlying issues persist — seven in ten remain dissatisfied with governance." Burnham himself scores narrowly negative on satisfaction — 29% against 30% — with 41% still undecided about the man barely a month into the job. A separate Ipsos/LBC survey found 50% would prefer Burnham as prime minister to Kemi Badenoch's 33%, well ahead of his personal approval numbers, suggesting the shift owes more to relief at Starmer's departure than to enthusiasm for what has replaced him.

A bounce built on low expectations

Burnham took office on 20 July pledging to be "a circuit-breaker" for a country on its seventh prime minister in a decade, and a 10-year plan to match. The bounce has proved double-edged. On his first full day in the job he told reporters his government would "make full use of any flexibility" within the UK's fiscal rules — a phrase that rattled gilt markets within hours, pushing the 10-year yield up 8 basis points to 5.04%, then the highest in the G7, and the 30-year yield toward 5.75%, territory unseen in nearly three decades. Traders drew immediate comparisons to Liz Truss's unfunded 2022 mini-budget, though Burnham has since avoided repeating the phrase and no fiscal statement has followed.

What the poll doesn't tell you

A three-point voting-intention swing, on its own, says nothing about whether Burnham intends to loosen the purse strings, defend the current fiscal rules, or lean on the Bank of England for support — the questions gilt investors actually care about. That test arrives at the autumn Budget, where Chancellor Rachel Reeves is expected to face pressure to raise taxes to keep the deficit rules intact rather than lean further on borrowing. Until then, the polling bounce is a story about political survival, not monetary direction — worth watching for what it buys Burnham in room to manoeuvre, not for any signal it sends on its own.
