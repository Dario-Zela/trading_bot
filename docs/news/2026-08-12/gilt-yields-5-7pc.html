30-year gilt yield pushes toward 5.7% amid fresh fiscal worries

HM Treasury's headquarters on Horse Guards Road, London. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 30-year gilt yield's push toward 5.70% reflects a global long-bond selloff, but a thinning buyer base leaves the UK end especially exposed ahead of Chancellor Healey's November budget.

The yield on Britain's 30-year gilt pushed to 5.70% this week, within a whisker of the 27-year highs touched earlier this year, as a fresh bout of selling swept through long-dated government bonds worldwide. The move sharpens an uncomfortable question for the Treasury: how does a new Chancellor find room to spend when the market is charging record prices for the privilege of lending to him.

This is not a UK-only story. The 30-year US Treasury yield has been pinned near multi-decade highs, and Japan's 30-year bond has traded at levels unseen since the tenor was first issued in 1999. Oil above $100 a barrel — inflated in part by the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz — has kept inflation expectations elevated on both sides of the Atlantic, and traders have been paring back bets on rate cuts accordingly. When three major bond markets sell off in tandem, it typically means investors are repricing the cost of holding long-duration debt everywhere, not punishing any single government.

A structural problem at the long end

What makes gilts more exposed than most is the buyer base. Defined-benefit pension schemes, once the natural anchor bid for 30-year paper, are closing to new members, ageing, and increasingly running off their gilt holdings to pay pensioners rather than adding to them. Insurers have stepped in, but not in sufficient volume to fully replace that demand. The Debt Management Office has responded by skewing its issuance programme away from the long end — it cancelled a scheduled long-conventional gilt auction late last year — precisely to avoid locking in borrowing at these levels. That the yield keeps climbing anyway says something about how thin the marginal buyer pool has become.

Healey's narrowing room before November

The political backdrop has shifted since Rachel Reeves last framed a budget. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July after Keir Starmer's resignation, and named John Healey, previously his defence secretary, as Chancellor. Markets took the appointment calmly — the FTSE 100 edged up and gilts steadied on the news, with Healey read as a continuity pick rather than a risk. But the underlying arithmetic he inherits has not improved. Debt interest is forecast at roughly £109bn for the current fiscal year, already the fourth-largest item in the government's budget, ahead of defence and just behind education. Every basis point added to the 30-year yield compounds that bill over time, even if most existing debt is issued at lower fixed rates.

Healey also arrives with commitments already stacked up — NATO's 3% of GDP defence target, a council-housing programme, a promised cap on bus fares, cuts to electricity VAT — against a Labour pledge not to raise income tax, VAT or National Insurance, and a growth forecast trimmed to around 1% this year, partly a casualty of the Iran war's effect on energy costs. The Office for Budget Responsibility has already flagged that funding earmarked for one of those pledges may not be genuinely new money. None of that forces a crisis on its own. It does mean the autumn budget, expected in November, is being watched by gilt investors less for its headline tax decisions than for whether Healey's fiscal rules survive contact with his own spending list. The DMO's next long-gilt auction, and any signal on issuance skew, will be an early read on how markets are pricing that risk.
