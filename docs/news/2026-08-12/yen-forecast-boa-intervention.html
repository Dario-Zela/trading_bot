Yen forecast to jump 6% by year-end after joint US-Japan intervention

The Bank of Japan's Tokyo headquarters — its September rate decision is the next test of the intervention's credibility. — Fg2 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
BofA forecasts the yen at 149/dollar by year-end after joint US-Japan intervention, but the currency has already drifted back near 158.

Tokyo and Washington did something they had not done since the earthquake and tsunami of 2011: they intervened together to stop the yen falling. That was ten days ago. The yen has since drifted back to almost exactly where it started, and Bank of America thinks that is beside the point.

The bank's FX strategist Shusuke Yamada now expects the yen to strengthen roughly 6% by the end of 2026, reaching around 149 per dollar from about 158 today — a downgrade to BofA's dollar-yen forecast for the third time this year, from 157 to 152 and now to 149. The reasoning has less to do with the mechanics of the intervention itself than with what it signals: that Washington and Tokyo have decided disorderly yen weakness is now a shared problem, not just a Japanese one.

What actually happened

The yen had slid to a 40-year low near ¥164 in late July, squeezing Japanese households through higher import costs and denting Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's approval ratings. On August 3, Japan's Ministry of Finance began buying yen with US backing — estimates of the spend vary by session and source, but Bank of Japan account data point to tens of billions of dollars committed over the week. The currency jumped as much as 3.8% in the following days, touching ¥155.20 before settling back into the ¥157–159 range it occupies now.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called it coordinated action against "disorderly yen movements" and pledged the US "will not hesitate to participate in further joint intervention." President Trump, characteristically less technical, put it as: "They have a weakening yen, and they wanted a little bit of help. And we're always there for Japan." Japan's finance minister, Satsuki Katayama, framed it domestically as a defence of price stability.

The gap intervention alone can't close

BofA's own note is candid about the limits of what just happened: intervention "raises the bar for what counts as a successful defence of the yen going forward," but it doesn't remove the underlying driver of yen weakness, which is the interest-rate gap. The Federal Reserve's policy range sits 250 to 275 basis points above the Bank of Japan's 1%. That differential is what has kept yen-funded carry trades — borrowing cheaply in yen to buy higher-yielding assets elsewhere — profitable even after a G7-grade show of official force. Bloomberg reported this week that euro-funded carry positions in the Brazilian real, Colombian peso and Turkish lira are up roughly 19% year-to-date, the best pace since 2005, with strategists at Morgan Stanley noting that intervention risk is pushing traders to fund those trades from the euro and Swiss franc instead — a rotation around Tokyo's line in the sand rather than a retreat from it.

That is why BofA's forecast hinges less on the intervention than on what the Bank of Japan does next. The bank held rates at 1% on July 31 but signalled openness to a move, and Governor Kazuo Ueda's tone afterward was read as mildly hawkish. BofA's note singles out a September hike, rather than October or December, as the credibility test: a move that early would show the BOJ is "staying ahead of upside inflation risk, reinforcing the credibility of the broader intervention effort." A delay would leave the August intervention looking like a one-off, with the rate gap doing the talking again.

Complicating that timeline is Takaichi's own coalition, which is fractured over proposals to cut the consumption tax — hardly the backdrop for a central bank eager to tighten into a domestic cost-of-living fight. The next data point is the BOJ's September policy meeting; until then, the yen's drift back above ¥158 is a reminder that official resolve and market positioning are still pulling in opposite directions.
