BEYOND THE TAPE · AGRI-SCIENCE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-12 · 4 min read

All-British chocolate bar made using Reading-grown cocoa unveiled

Cacao pods on the tree — the fruit that Reading researchers coaxed to ripeness inside a heated Berkshire greenhouse. — Fran Hogan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
UK researchers have grown cocoa to maturity in a Reading greenhouse and turned it into what is thought to be Britain's first domestically-grown chocolate bar.

A cocoa pod does not, by any reasonable expectation, belong in Berkshire. The tree that produces it, Theobroma cacao, wants year-round heat above 20C, thick humidity and shelter from wind — the climate of the West African rainforest belt, not the Thames Valley. Yet researchers working with the University of Reading's cocoa programme have grown cocoa pods to full ripeness in a greenhouse near the town and turned the beans inside them into a chocolate bar, understood to be the first made entirely from cocoa cultivated on British soil.

In one sentence: UK researchers have grown cocoa to maturity in a Reading greenhouse and turned it into what is thought to be Britain's first domestically-grown chocolate bar.

It is a novelty, and everyone involved would concede as much — a heated greenhouse is not a cocoa plantation, and nobody is proposing that Somerset take on Côte d'Ivoire. But the proof of concept lands at a pointed moment for an industry watching its main growing regions come under sustained strain.

A greenhouse built for quarantine, not chocolate bars

The cocoa work behind the bar traces back to the International Cocoa Quarantine Centre, a facility the University of Reading has run since 1985 in Arborfield, on the edge of Reading. Its 1,000 square metre greenhouse is kept at a tropical 23C and holds several hundred cocoa varieties, sourced mainly from genebanks in Trinidad and Costa Rica. The centre's actual job is unglamorous but consequential: it screens roughly 98% of all cocoa plant material moved internationally for breeding programmes, holding cuttings in quarantine for two years and checking them for disease before they are grafted and shipped onward — because Britain, unlike the tropics, has none of the pests or pathogens that plague cocoa where it is normally grown.

Dr Andrew Daymond, who oversees the centre, has spent years studying how cocoa responds to heat, drought and rising CO2 rather than trying to make it fruit as a crop in its own right. Getting pods to ripen and yield beans fit for a chocolate bar was, by the researchers' own account, more demonstration than agronomy — a way of showing that cocoa's biology is more flexible than its geography suggests, given enough artificial tropics.

Why the timing matters

Cocoa production is heavily concentrated: Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana together supply roughly 60% of the world's beans. Both have had brutal few years. Unusually heavy rains in December 2023 triggered outbreaks of black pod disease, followed by drought that hammered yields further. Cocoa swollen shoot virus has since spread across an estimated 590,000 hectares of Ghana's 1.38m hectares of plantation — trees typically die within three to four years of infection, and the only real remedy is felling them. Futures prices, which spent most of the 2010s below $3,000 a tonne, spiked past $12,000 in 2024 and were still trading above $5,700 in July 2026, with forecasters flagging an 82% chance of El Niño conditions returning through the rest of the year.

Against that backdrop, a UK-grown chocolate bar reads less as whimsy and more as a data point in a wider argument about whether cocoa supply chains can adapt to a warmer, wetter, more disease-prone world — through breeding hardier varieties, shifting cultivation zones, or, per a separate and unrelated track of research, growing cocoa butter from cultured cells in a bioreactor rather than a field at all. Reading's greenhouse experiment sits closer to the first camp: not replacing tropical cultivation, but using controlled environments to understand what cocoa needs and how far its tolerances can be pushed.

What it doesn't solve

None of this scales. Heating and lighting a greenhouse to tropical specification is expensive, and Britain will not be exporting beans. The value of the exercise is scientific rather than commercial: a working proof that cocoa's genetics can tolerate cultivation well outside its native range, which feeds directly into breeding programmes trying to develop varieties more resistant to the droughts and diseases now ravaging West African farms. Reading's germplasm bank already exists for exactly that purpose.

The next marker to watch is less a laboratory demonstration than a market one — how the 2026/27 West African harvest holds up through the remainder of the El Niño risk window, and whether swollen shoot virus containment in Ghana's Western North region slows the disease's spread before it reaches more of the country's 1.38m hectares under cultivation.
