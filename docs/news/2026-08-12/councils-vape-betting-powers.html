BEYOND THE TAPE · HIGH STREET · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-12 · 4 min read

Councils win new powers to block vape shops and betting parlours

Andy Burnham, whose government is scrapping the 'aim to permit' rule for betting shops and adding planning controls on vape retailers. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham scraps the 'aim to permit' rule for betting shops and adds planning permission for vape shops, after BBC investigations tied some outlets to organised crime.

Walk down most British high streets these days and the pattern repeats: a shuttered bank, a charity shop, then — improbably often — a vape shop or a bookmaker's, sometimes two of each within a hundred yards. Prime Minister Andy Burnham wants councils to be able to say no to the next one.

On Tuesday, Burnham unveiled reforms that scrap the 'aim to permit' rule in the Gambling Act 2005, which has effectively forced local authorities to wave through new betting shops and 24-hour adult gaming centres as long as licensing boxes were ticked. In its place: standard planning permission, meaning councils can reject an application on the same grounds they'd use to block, say, an unwanted takeaway. Vape shops — previously able to open with no planning oversight at all — will be brought into the same regime, with the formal definition of a vape retailer tightened so operators can't dodge the rules by rebranding as a 'convenience store'.

"High streets are the beating heart of local communities, but for too long Westminster has stood by" while decline set in, Burnham said.

The irony is not lost on Westminster-watchers: the 'aim to permit' rule dates to Tony Blair's 2005 liberalisation of gambling law, in a government Burnham himself served under as a minister. As Greater Manchester mayor, he was among nearly 300 politicians and campaigners who wrote to Number 10 in January calling for the rule's abolition — a campaign that has now become government policy from the top.

The crime angle

The timing traces back to roughly eighteen months of BBC investigations linking a slice of high-street businesses — vape shops, mini-marts, some barbershops and nail salons — to organised crime: illegal cigarette and vape sales, drug dealing, immigration offences, shell companies fronting shopfronts, and in the West Midlands, evidence of premises used for child sexual exploitation. Reporters worked alongside National Crime Agency raids and, in one case, traced a tunnel supplying illegal cigarettes to shops in Hull.

Alongside the planning changes, the package extends closure orders against premises suspected of criminal links from six months to twelve, giving police and councils more time to build a case — examining ownership, financing and supply chains — without a shop reopening mid-investigation.

The numbers behind the decline

Burnham's team is citing a stark trade-off on the high street ledger: Britain has lost nearly 1,800 pubs and bars since 2016, even as vape and tobacco shop numbers rose to roughly 2,200 over the same stretch. Betting shops have moved the other way — down by more than a third since 2019, a fall the Betting & Gaming Council put at close to 3,000 closures and 15,000 jobs, largely a product of 2019 stake limits on fixed-odds terminals and the shift to online wagering. The government's bet, so to speak, is that fewer new gambling and vape outlets will free up units for the pubs, services and community spaces that survey after survey says residents actually want back.

The Vape Trade Association's response was notably unsentimental about the rogue end of its own sector: "Rogue vape traders on the high street have been a blight on the legitimate sector." The Betting & Gaming Council, for its part, mostly stuck to restating the scale of the industry's contraction rather than contesting the reform outright.

What it means for markets

Direct trading relevance is thin. Listed bookmakers have already pivoted most of their growth to online and app-based channels, and the betting-shop estate has been shrinking on its own for years regardless of planning law — this mainly raises the bar for reopening or relocating shops, rather than threatening existing high-street footprints. Vape retail is dominated by independents and small chains with little public-market exposure. The more relevant reader here is a local authority finance officer or a high-street landlord than a portfolio manager, though gaming-sector equities with UK expansion plans in adult gaming centres may want to note the added friction.

A fast-tracked six-week consultation is expected to open shortly, with ministers targeting the start of 2027 for the new rules to take effect. Watch for how many councils move quickly once the powers land, and whether landlords start marketing vacant units differently in anticipation.
