BBC confirms up to 2,000 job cuts as licence-fee revenue crisis deepens

BBC Broadcasting House in Portland Place, London, the corporation's headquarters. — Oxyman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The BBC will cut up to 2,000 jobs and £500m from its budget after losing £1.1bn to licence-fee evasion and cancellations.

The BBC has confirmed it will cut up to 2,000 jobs and strip £500m from its annual budget — roughly a tenth of total spending — as licence-fee income buckles under evasion, cancellations and a decade-long drift away from live television.

The corporation lost £1.1bn last year alone to non-payment and cancelled licences, according to its own accounts, even after two million doorstep enforcement visits. Evasion now stands at 12.5%, the highest in years. The number of households declaring they no longer need a licence — because they watch no BBC content at all — has risen from 2.4 million in 2021 to 3.6 million this year, an erosion worth £617m on its own. Paying households fell to about 23.3 million in 2025-26, the steepest annual drop since the pandemic, even as the £180 annual fee itself rose with inflation. Real-terms licence income is down £1.3bn over the past decade.

Where the axe falls

Director-General Matt Brittin, the former Google executive who took over in May, told staff "tough choices are unavoidable as we make savings." Of the roughly 2,000 roles going, 550 sit in News, television and radio, with a further 700 corporate and support positions cut — a signal that the reduction is not confined to back-office efficiency. News alone faces cuts of around 15%, well above the 10% applied corporation-wide, with 200 posts and £25m in savings targeted there specifically. Radio 4's The World Tonight is being taken off air after 56 years; BBC Breakfast loses its Sunday edition from September; the Newsnight and Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg production teams are merging.

A funding model built for a different era

The BBC is not a listed company, and none of this moves a ticker. What it does is quantify, in hard numbers, a problem every legacy broadcaster with a compulsory or subscription-anchored funding base is quietly living with: a household-fee model calibrated for linear viewing is shrinking faster than cost bases can be trimmed to match. More than 90% of Britons still consume BBC content monthly, but increasingly through platforms the licence fee was never designed to capture. That gap between reach and revenue is the same one squeezing print subscriptions and cable bundles elsewhere, and it is arriving at the BBC just as its Royal Charter — and the licence fee's future itself — comes up for renewal at the end of 2027. Ministers have floated extending the charge to streaming-only households; they have not committed to it.

The next marker is the Charter renewal negotiation with government, where the shape of BBC funding beyond 2027 gets decided. Until then, the cuts announced this week are the corporation trying to shrink its cost base to fit a revenue line that keeps moving in one direction.
