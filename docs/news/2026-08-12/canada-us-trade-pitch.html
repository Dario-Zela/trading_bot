Canada and US map out trade deal to pitch Trump next week

U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, who has met Canadian counterpart Dominic LeBlanc three times in three weeks as an August 19 tariff deadline nears. — Office of the United States Trade Representative / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Canadian and U.S. negotiators, meeting for a third time in three weeks, aim to hand Trump a trade package before his August 19 tariff deadline hits.

Canadian and American trade negotiators have now sat down three times in three weeks, and the pace tells its own story: both sides are racing to hand President Trump a finished proposal before he can act on his own.

Dominic LeBlanc, Canada's trade minister, met U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer at USTR headquarters in Washington on Tuesday for roughly an hour — the third such session since talks resumed in earnest on August 7. Canada's chief trade negotiator, Janice Charette, had already spent Monday with American officials laying groundwork. The plan, according to people briefed on the talks, is to put a completed package in front of Trump as early as next Monday, giving him a day's cushion before his own deadline arrives.

That deadline is real and it is close. Trump signed an order on July 20 imposing a fresh 50% tariff on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods spanning 554 tariff lines — everything from wine and cement to honey and hockey sticks — set to take effect August 19 if no deal materializes. Energy, potash, fish and critical minerals are carved out, but the list still hits a wide swath of Canadian exporters already absorbing existing U.S. duties on steel, aluminum, lumber and autos.

What's on the table

The emerging package reportedly trades concessions on both sides: Canada would drop its retaliatory tariffs on U.S. autos and the provincial bans several provinces have placed on American alcohol, while Washington would revisit how dairy quotas are administered — a perennial irritant dating back to the original USMCA talks. Ottawa is also pushing for relief on steel and aluminum and hopes the broader negotiation extends into a renewal of the Canada-U.S.-Mexico Agreement itself, which comes up for review next year.

The tone has not been friendly. Prime Minister Mark Carney described the negotiations as "nasty" after Trump publicly criticized Canada's leadership last week, though Carney said Ottawa remains at the table and framed the fight as one over Canadian jobs. He has also made clear Canada isn't negotiating from a position of pure hope: "everything's on the table," he said, if the 50% tariffs do land on schedule, describing "a full range of things that we can do" in response.

Why the rush matters

The accelerated cadence — three meetings in three weeks, after a stretch of comparative quiet — reads as an attempt to box in a decision that has historically gone Trump's way at the last minute. LeBlanc struck an upbeat note heading into this week's session, saying Canada would "continue to engage at the negotiation table to firmly advance and defend Canadian interests." Former Canadian ambassador David MacNaughton was more circumspect, noting that only Trump can actually close a deal of this size: "You never know with the president."

That uncertainty is the whole ballgame for markets exposed to North American supply chains — autos, lumber, dairy processors, and the Canadian dollar all sit on one side of a binary outcome. A negotiated package, even an imperfect one, removes tail risk that a unilateral tariff order would not. The next checkpoint is firm: August 19, when the 50% rate either takes hold or gets shelved by whatever Trump decides after seeing what LeBlanc, Greer and Charette bring him.
