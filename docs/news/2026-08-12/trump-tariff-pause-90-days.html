Trump pauses threatened 125% tariff hike for another 90 days

Trump displays a chart of proposed reciprocal tariff rates at a White House event during the opening round of the tariff campaign. — The White House / Daniel Torok (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump deferred a threatened 125% tariff hike for 90 more days even as 25 states sue over a separate tariff round already in effect, leaving trade policy unresolved on two fronts at once.

The tariff that traders had been bracing for did not land Tuesday. For the second time in as many extensions, President Trump signed off on a 90-day pause of a threatened 125% duty — this time holding it back from a broader slate of import levies rather than China alone — even as his administration presses ahead with a separate tariff regime that 25 states are now suing to unwind. For a market that has spent eighteen months pricing and re-pricing trade risk, the message was familiar: the number stays large, and the clock simply resets.

The pause keeps in place the lower baseline rates that have applied since the last truce — a mix of reciprocal and fentanyl-linked duties that traders have already built into supply-chain cost models — while deferring the jump to 125% that would have applied had talks collapsed. Treasury and USTR officials framed it as recognition of continued negotiating progress; markets mostly read it as the path of least resistance, the same one taken in August 2025 and several times since.

A truce that keeps not ending

This is now a familiar rhythm. Reciprocal tariffs were paused for 90 days in April 2025, extended again that August, folded into a broader deal after the October 2025 Trump-Xi summit in Busan, and revisited at a further summit in Beijing this past May — where Trump told reporters he hadn't even discussed extending the truce with Xi Jinping, only for negotiators to keep the underlying arrangement alive anyway. Each round produces the same headline: a triple-digit tariff rate is threatened, then deferred, with the working rate — currently around 30% on Chinese imports into the US and 10% on American exports to China — left untouched.

For companies that source from Asia, the practical effect of another deferral is relief without resolution. Import planning still has to account for the possibility that the 125% rate arrives on 90 days' notice, which means hedging books, forward contracts and inventory buffers get built for the worst case and only unwound if the truce holds. That asymmetry is expensive to carry even when the tariff itself never materializes.

The states suing over what already exists

While the White House buys time on the threatened rate, it is defending the tariffs it has already imposed. On August 3, a coalition of 25 states — led by California Attorney General Rob Bonta and joined by New York, Illinois, Michigan, Pennsylvania and 21 others — sued in the U.S. Court of International Trade over Section 301 duties of 10% to 12.5% that took effect July 24 on goods from roughly 60 economies, imposed on forced-labor grounds. Bonta called it "President Trump's third attempt to illegally impose tariffs that would make life more expensive for American families and small businesses." A White House spokesman countered that the administration was exercising "lawful authority to obtain the elimination of unreasonable acts, policies and practices."

The suit leans heavily on precedent the states have already won. In February, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 in Learning Resources v. Trump and Trump v. V.O.S. Selections that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs at all — tariffs, the Court held, are "a branch of the taxing power" that belongs to Congress. The administration terminated its IEEPA-based tariffs within days, and Customs and Border Protection stopped collecting them on February 24, a reversal projected to generate up to $175 billion in refunds. The states now argue the Section 301 program is a rushed, overly broad attempt to recreate the same tariffs the Court just struck down, this time dressed in different statutory clothing.

No court has ruled on the new suit, and importers who filed a parallel challenge in July are still waiting too. But the pattern is consistent: every time one legal avenue for tariffs closes, the administration has found another, and every time, someone sues.

What uncertainty costs

For markets, an unresolved 125% threat and an unresolved lawsuit amount to the same thing — a policy variable that cannot be priced with any confidence. The S&P 500 has spent the summer consolidating in a band roughly between 7,200 and 7,600, propped up by unusually strong second-quarter earnings growth even as tariff, oil and Treasury-yield headlines periodically knock it around. Options desks have grown accustomed to volatility spikes clustering around 90-day deadlines, then fading once a pause is announced — a pattern regular enough that some traders now treat the extensions as close to a foregone conclusion, which paradoxically makes an eventual surprise more dangerous, not less.

The next hard date is November 10, when the current China truce framework is due to lapse unless extended again. Trump has already signaled he isn't treating that deadline as urgent, telling reporters after the May summit that extension wasn't even on the agenda in Beijing. Whether the Court of International Trade moves on the states' Section 301 challenge before then — and whether Washington reaches for a fourth legal justification if it loses — will do more to settle the shape of import costs into 2027 than any single 90-day reprieve.
