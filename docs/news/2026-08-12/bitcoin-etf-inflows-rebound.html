Bitcoin ETFs post best week since April on $854m of inflows

BlackRock's Manhattan headquarters, home to the iShares Bitcoin Trust that captured 81% of last week's ETF inflows — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bitcoin ETFs drew $854m in their best week since April, led by BlackRock's IBIT, but a record 78-day negative Coinbase premium shows spot buyers still aren't convinced.

Bitcoin exchange-traded funds pulled in $853.5 million over five trading days in early August — the strongest week for the category since mid-April — reversing a run of outflows and putting BlackRock firmly back in the driver's seat of institutional crypto demand. But strip away the fund-flow headline and the picture underneath is far less convinced: spot trading volumes are soft, Bitcoin has spent days stuck below $65,000, and a widely watched gauge of US buying pressure has been negative for longer than at any point on record.

That gap — real money moving into regulated wrappers while the underlying market shrugs — is the story analysts are now trying to parse.

The flows, in detail

Every session between August 3 and August 7 closed green for US spot Bitcoin ETFs: $170.1 million on Monday, $211.5 million Tuesday, a peak of $244.4 million Wednesday, then $128.7 million and $98.9 million to close the week. BlackRock's iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) alone accounted for $693.5 million of the total — better than 80 cents of every dollar that went into the category. Fidelity's FBTC added $116.5 million and Ark 21Shares' ARKB brought in $50.8 million, with Bitwise and Morgan Stanley's products picking up smaller allocations behind them.

It was a sharp reversal from the week before, when Bitcoin funds shed $61.5 million and snapped a three-week inflow streak. Ether ETFs rode the same wave, adding roughly $245 million, with BlackRock again dominant at close to 87% of that category's flow. The rebound coincided with softening rate-hike expectations after a run of mixed US data — July payrolls fell 23,000 even as headline inflation held at an elevated 3.7% year-on-year, muddying the Federal Reserve's next move and, in the process, nudging risk appetite back toward crypto wrappers.

Money in, conviction absent

The trouble for anyone reading the flows as a bullish spot signal is what they're not doing to the price. Bitcoin drew roughly $626 million in ETF inflows over the first three trading days of August and still could not clear resistance at $64,000; even after the full week's haul, it has spent most of the period consolidating in the $62,600–$65,000 band, capped by what several trading desks describe as sell walls stacked between $65,000 and $67,000. Twenty-four-hour spot volume fell nearly 18% to about $18.1 billion over the same stretch — thin turnover for a market absorbing nine figures of daily fund buying.

The more telling number sits away from the ETF tape entirely. The Coinbase Bitcoin Premium Index — which tracks the price gap between Coinbase and international exchanges as a proxy for US buying pressure — has been negative for 78 straight days, the longest such streak since the index began, easily eclipsing the 40-day record set in January and February. Markus Thielen of 10x Research put it bluntly: selling activity by American financial institutions remains stronger than purchasing interest, meaning the same institutional cohort funnelling money into IBIT is, by this measure, still a net seller in the spot market.

That split points to two distinct pools of institutional behaviour rather than one coherent view: allocators re-entering through the regulated, custodied wrapper — where compliance and balance-sheet mandates favour an ETF over direct custody — while trading desks and more tactical holders continue to lighten spot positions into whatever bid shows up. On-chain data from Glassnode backs the same read, describing resilient long-term holders but broadly weak transactional demand beneath the surface.

Why the distinction matters

For a trading desk, an ETF inflow number and a spot conviction signal are not interchangeable, and treating them as one is where the mistake creeps in. Flows into IBIT say something about allocator mandates, fee structures and where large pools of capital are permitted to sit — they say comparatively little about whether the people who can trade the coin directly, without a custodian in between, actually want more of it at current prices. When both signals point the same way, that is confirmation. When they diverge, as they have for most of the past eleven weeks, the ETF number is closer to a symptom of financial plumbing than a verdict on the asset.

The next test comes from whether the divergence closes or widens. A break above the $65,000–$67,000 sell-wall zone on rising spot volume, or a Coinbase Premium reading that finally turns positive, would suggest direct buyers are following the fund money in. A repeat of last week's pattern — strong IBIT prints against soft on-exchange turnover — would instead argue that this rebound, like the one in July, is a rotation of institutional capital between vehicles rather than into the asset itself. The next weekly ETF flow report and the Fed's September rate decision, still contested after this month's split data, will be the first places that answer shows up.
