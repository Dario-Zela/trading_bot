CPI cools to 2.5% but Fed speak stays split ahead of September

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Headline CPI fell to 2.5%, its lowest since February 2021, but a sticky 0.3% core reading pushed Fed bets from a 50bp to a 25bp September cut.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics' July inflation report gave the Federal Reserve something it has wanted for more than four years: a headline number starting with a 2. Consumer prices rose 2.5% from a year earlier, the lowest annual rate since February 2021 and squarely on the Fed's own target. Wall Street took the number as license to keep buying.

But a closer read of the report explains why the Fed itself sounded far less celebratory than the index suggested. Core CPI — the measure that strips out food and energy and is generally treated as the better guide to underlying price pressure — rose 0.3% on the month, a pace that, annualized, sits well above where the central bank wants to be. That gap between a flattering headline and a stickier core is now the central fact shaping the Fed's September 16 decision, and it explains why a 50-basis-point cut, which traders had been pricing as a live possibility, is largely off the table in favor of a smaller, more conventional quarter-point move.

The number and the reaction

Futures markets moved fast. According to CME Group's FedWatch tool, the probability assigned to a 25-basis-point September cut jumped to roughly 90% in the hours after the release, up from a market that had been split closer to even money between a quarter-point and a half-point move earlier in the week. A larger cut hasn't been ruled out for the rest of the year — FedWatch pricing still assigns better-than-even odds to a cumulative 50 basis points of easing by October — but the July print pushed the near-term bet decisively toward caution.

Equities, already running hot, added to a strong run. The S&P 500 had closed at a record 7,757.64 on August 8, capping its best week since April with a 3.58% gain, while the Nasdaq Composite climbed more than 5% over the same stretch and the Dow set its own high above 54,000. The CPI print gave that rally another leg, reinforcing a trade that has held for months: soft-enough inflation plus a Fed inching toward cuts equals higher multiples, even with core prices still running above target.

The Fed's own mixed signals

If the market read the report as good news, Fed officials themselves split along familiar lines in the days around the release — and their disagreement says as much about the path ahead as the data itself.

Chicago Fed president Austan Goolsbee, a voting FOMC member this year, used an interview with Wired days ahead of the meeting to make the case for cutting. "The biggest problem facing our economy right now is not the collapse of industry and the collapse of jobs; it's that the prices have been rising too fast, we got an inflation problem and people hate inflation," he said — but he paired that with a notable downgrade of his own prior language on employment, describing the labor market as "stable, without being good." That's a deliberate softening from the Fed's earlier framing of a strong jobs market, and it matters mechanically: if employment is merely stable rather than tight, it removes one of the traditional arguments against cutting rates.

Cheryl Venable, running the Atlanta Fed, struck a different note. She told reporters inflation remains too high, citing business contacts across the Southeast who are still reporting elevated price pressure on the ground — a reminder that national aggregates can mask regional stickiness. Venable also flagged the Middle East conflict as a live risk to the inflation outlook, noting that a resolution would likely ease energy costs and supply pressures while continued fighting would do the opposite. Her comments underscore that the path back to target isn't guaranteed to be smooth, and that geopolitics, not just domestic demand, now sits on the Fed's inflation dashboard.

Housing adds another data point

A third data point released this week complicates the picture further. The National Association of Realtors reported existing-home sales fell 1.7% in July to a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 4.06 million, a steeper drop than forecasters had penciled in, even as sales were still up 0.7% from a year earlier. The median sales price rose to $434,100, up 2.0% year-over-year and the 37th straight month of annual price gains — evidence that affordability, not demand, is the binding constraint, with elevated mortgage rates keeping would-be buyers on the sidelines even as inventory tightens.

Housing data feeds directly into how the Fed reads the broader economy, and a market this constrained by rates argues for cutting — but not so fast that it reignites the price pressure Venable is describing in the Southeast.

What the box constraint means

Taken together, the three threads point to the same conclusion: the Fed has room to ease, but not much. Headline inflation has reached target, employment is cooling enough that Goolsbee feels comfortable calling it merely stable, and a housing market starved of transactions is quietly lobbying for lower rates. Set against that is a core inflation print still running hot enough to worry a regional president, and a Middle East conflict that could reverse the energy-price tailwind at any point.

That combination argues for exactly the kind of cut markets are now pricing — a conventional quarter point in September, not the more aggressive half-point some had bet on a week ago — with the committee keeping optionality for October and December rather than front-loading easing. For portfolios built around a steeper cutting path, the recalibration is real: carry trades and rate-sensitive positioning that assumed 50-basis-point moves now have to work with a slower glide path. The next major test comes with August's jobs report and the September 16 FOMC decision itself, when the committee will have to reconcile a target-level headline number with a core reading that hasn't yet cooperated.
