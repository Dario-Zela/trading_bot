BEYOND THE TAPE · ECLIPSE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-12 · 3 min read

UK and Europe brace for deepest solar eclipse since 1999

The solar corona visible during totality — the phenomenon millions across Iceland and northern Spain will witness this evening. — Brucewaters / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
A total eclipse crossing Iceland and northern Spain will darken 90–96% of the UK sun this evening, forcing grid operators to manage Europe's biggest coordinated solar drop since 1999.

There is a particular kind of quiet that falls over a solar farm during an eclipse — panels that were humming along at full tilt an hour earlier suddenly delivering a fraction of the current, then clawing it back just as fast once the Moon slides clear. This evening, that quiet arrives across an entire continent at once.

A total solar eclipse cuts a path from Siberia across the Arctic, over Greenland, and down through western Iceland before curving into northern Spain — Reykjavík, A Coruña, Oviedo, Bilbao, Valencia — and out towards the Balearic Islands, with up to two minutes eighteen seconds of totality visible from Iceland. It is the first total eclipse to touch mainland Europe since 11 August 1999, when the shadow last crossed Cornwall and northern France. Twenty-seven years is a long wait, though Europe won't have to wait as long again: a second total eclipse crosses southern Spain and Gibraltar next August, before the path goes quiet until 2081.

The UK misses totality — that won't return to British shores until 2090 — but it gets close. The Royal Observatory Greenwich puts coverage at around 82% in Edinburgh and Glasgow, rising to 90% over London, Bristol and Cardiff, and reaching roughly 95% in Cornwall, with the Isles of Scilly seeing as much as 96.5% of the Sun blotted out. First contact in London comes at 6:17pm, maximum eclipse at 7:12pm, and the Moon clears the Sun by around 8:06pm — a window that, conveniently for grid engineers, falls as the evening sun is already sliding towards the horizon.

A stress test with 27 years' notice

For astronomers this is spectacle; for the people who keep Europe's lights on, it is a scheduled drill. The National Energy System Operator expects Great Britain to lose around 700 megawatts of solar generation at the eclipse's peak, with historical modelling suggesting the figure could run as high as 1.3 gigawatts — enough, NESO says, to power roughly half a million homes — against a maximum ramp rate approaching 4,400 MW an hour as output first collapses and then rebounds. Across the wider European grid, transmission body ENTSO-E has pencilled in a dip of up to 9.7 gigawatts of photovoltaic capacity, a number that has grid operators from Germany to the Netherlands lining up gas-fired backup capacity to smooth the swing.

Unlike a total blackout of demand, an eclipse announces its arrival to the minute, years in advance, which is precisely why operators treat it less as a threat than as a rehearsal for the faster, less predictable ramps that cloud cover throws at increasingly solar-heavy grids. The timing helps too: because the eclipse falls in early evening rather than at midday, when panels are already easing off for the day, the shortfall is gentler than the raw wattage figures suggest.

Octopus Energy is leaning into the moment rather than just absorbing it, offering customers who dial down consumption during the eclipse window up to 16 kWh of free electricity through its rewards scheme. "When the sun goes off grid," the supplier told customers, "we want you to do the same." Whether enough households take up the offer to matter is beside the point — it is a small, cheerful piece of demand-side flexibility dressed up as a sky-watching event.

The next comparable test comes on 2 August 2027, when totality returns to southern Spain and Gibraltar. Between now and then, the more immediate marker is simpler: whether NESO's ramp-rate forecasts hold up against what actually happens on Britain's grid this evening.
