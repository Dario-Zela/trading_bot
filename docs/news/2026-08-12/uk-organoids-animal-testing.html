UK scientists to grow mini human organs from NHS patient cells to cut animal testing

Kidney organoids grown from human stem cells in test chambers, colour-coded by kidney segment. — University of Washington / Freedman Lab, via National Center for Advancing Translational Sciences (NIH, public domain)
In one sentence
Cambridge and Oxford are scaling up lab-grown organs from NHS patient cells, betting standardised organoids can predict drug responses better than mice and cut animal testing.

A mouse, no matter how carefully bred, cannot tell a researcher which drug will work in a particular patient's gut, liver or brain. That mismatch — between the animal in the cage and the human in the clinic — is the reason more than 90% of drugs that clear animal trials still fail when they reach people. It is also the reason Cambridge and Oxford are now racing to grow tiny, working versions of human organs from NHS patients' own tissue.

The Cambridge effort is anchored by a new £20m research hub funded by the Medical Research Council, based at the Cambridge Stem Cell Institute. Its job is not just to grow organoids — clusters of cells, sometimes just a few millimetres across, that replicate the architecture and function of a real organ — but to standardise them into a library that academic labs and pharmaceutical companies can actually use. That distinction matters. Organoid science has existed for over a decade; what has been missing is a version reliable enough for industry to bet a drug programme on.

A library of guts, tumours and brains

Matthias Zilbauer, a clinical professor at the Cambridge Stem Cell Institute, put the logic bluntly: "A mouse cannot tell us what treatments work," he said, adding that the hub should have "a major impact on the numbers of animals used." Teams are starting with organoids modelling inflammatory bowel disease — ulcerative colitis and Crohn's — because those conditions vary enormously between patients and animal models have historically been poor predictors of which drug helps which person. Other groups are growing tumour organoids, or "tumoroids," to test cancer drugs against a patient's own cancer before it is given to them, work pioneered at Cambridge by Meritxell Huch. Further down the pipeline sit beating heart tissue and electrically active brain organoids for neurological disease.

A smaller, commercial strand of the same push comes from VivoSphere, whose chief technology officer Yuan Tian described the appeal to drug developers in risk terms: when a candidate compound fails on human tissue in a dish, "lower risk for animals and patients" follows, because it never proceeds to the next, more expensive and more ethically fraught stage of testing.

Oxford's parallel bet

Oxford is building its own version. The Oxford Organoid Hub, housed in the university's Sherrington Building and co-directed by Christopher Toepfer and Filipa Simões, is assembling a patient-derived stem cell biobank linked to real clinical data and outcomes — the idea being that an organoid is only as useful as the medical history attached to the tissue it came from. Where Cambridge's hub leans toward gut and liver disease, Oxford's is being built to support multi-organ systems that model how organs interact, which matters for testing drug safety as much as efficacy.

Neither project claims organoids can fully replace animals yet. A government-commissioned review in February found "a gap in maturity of in vitro pre-clinical models developed in academic settings compared to the translational readiness required for a model to be used in industry" — polite science-speak for: promising in a university lab, not yet proven enough for a regulator to sign off a drug on that evidence alone. Many organoids still lack blood vessels, capping how large and realistic they can grow, and they miss immune cells that shape how a real organ responds to a drug.

The numbers behind the push

The UK used 2.54 million animals in scientific procedures in 2025, a 3.8% fall on the year before and the lowest total since the law governing animal research took effect in 1987. Mice and rats still account for 95% of that figure. In November, science minister Lord Vallance set out a roadmap to accelerate the decline further: animal testing for skin and eye irritation is due to be phased out by the end of this year, with mouse-based botulinum toxin testing following in 2027. That roadmap carries £60m to help alternative methods clear the regulatory bar, on top of £15.9m from the Medical Research Council, Innovate UK and Wellcome for human tissue models, and a separate £2m from Innovate UK specifically aimed at cutting the use of dogs and monkeys in safety testing. The US Food and Drug Administration announced a parallel shift last year, saying animal testing should become "the exception rather than the norm" for monoclonal antibody safety data within three to five years.

What this threatens — and what it doesn't yet

The economic stakes are large enough to explain why both universities and government are moving now rather than waiting for the science to fully mature. Pharmaceutical companies collectively spend close to $300bn a year on research and development, and getting a single drug to market costs upward of $1.3bn once failures are priced in — failures that organoid advocates argue happen too late and too expensively under the current animal-testing-first model. The organoid market itself is forecast to grow at roughly 22% a year through 2030, reaching over $6.5bn, and industry estimates suggest organ-chip and organoid methods could save the sector several billion dollars annually simply by killing bad drug candidates earlier. That is also, implicitly, a threat to the contract research organisations that have built businesses around animal toxicology testing — several of the larger ones, aware of the direction of travel, are already investing in organoid platforms of their own rather than waiting to be undercut.

Dr Juliet Dukes of the charity Replacing Animals in Research called the approach one with "real potential to deliver truly personalised medicine" — a claim that is easier to make than to prove at scale. The honest state of play, per the February review, is that organoids work convincingly in individual academic papers but have not yet been standardised enough for a regulator or a pharma safety board to trust them wholesale. The next marker to watch is whether Cambridge's library produces organoid batches consistent enough for industry partners to actually adopt, rather than merely admire — a test that will start to be answered as the hub's first standardised models reach outside labs over the coming year.
