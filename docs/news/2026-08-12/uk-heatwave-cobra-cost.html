Burnham chairs Cobra as UK heatwave costs economy £4.4bn this year

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who chaired Wednesday's emergency Cobra meeting on heatwaves, wildfires and drought. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Burnham chaired a Cobra meeting as UK heatwaves — now costing £4.4bn in lost output — fuelled record wildfires and drought across most of England and Wales.

Andy Burnham convened an emergency Cobra meeting on Wednesday, pulling in the Environment Agency, the Home Office, Defra and the Department of Health to confront a summer that has, by the government's own reckoning, moved from uncomfortable to unmanageable. Temperatures reached 33°C on the day of the meeting and were forecast to hit 38°C by Thursday, arriving on top of a wildfire season fire services describe as the worst on record and a drought now gripping most of England and Wales. It was the second such meeting this year — the first came in June, when a separate heat spike forced the same set of ministers into the same room.

What distinguishes this round is the arrival of a number. Analysis from the green thinktank Verdant put the cost of Britain's 2026 heatwaves at £4.4bn in lost economic output through July, up from an initial £2.36bn estimate for June alone. For a government that has spent the summer managing wildfires and standpipe logistics, that figure turns a weather story into a growth story — and gives the Treasury a line item it did not have before.

A wildfire season without precedent

The scale is not in dispute. Fire services across England and Wales have tackled 966 wildfires so far in 2026, including 458 in July alone — already the busiest month on record — and a further 185 in the first ten days of August. A blaze that broke out near Ringwood in Hampshire, ignited by a vehicle fire on the A31, spread into New Forest heathland home to rare and protected species, one of several incidents straining crews this month.

Steve Wright, general secretary of the Fire Brigades Union, described the situation as a "national emergency" already under way, arguing that crews "do not have the new modern equipment to deal with these" fires — a capacity complaint that sits awkwardly next to a government insisting it is responding with urgency. Health Secretary Yvette Cooper's public message was more cautious in tone, urging people simply to heed heat warnings, underscoring a gap between what the union says is needed on the ground and what ministers are offering by way of resource.

Drought spreads faster than the response

Behind the fires sits a drier problem still. The Environment Agency now classifies 71.3% of England as in "flash drought" — conditions that developed rapidly on the back of the driest July on record — with almost three-quarters of the country affected once prolonged dry-weather zones are included. Seven of the agency's operational areas, from East Anglia to Devon and Cornwall, have moved into formal drought status. Roughly 45 million people live in the affected areas, more than 27 million are under water-use restrictions, and around 20 million are subject to hosepipe bans.

The practical consequences reach beyond gardens. Farmers face restricted irrigation and, in places, early harvests on light land in the south and east, though this year's crop looks likely to be uneven rather catastrophic — reasonable yields on heavier, wetter soils offsetting real losses elsewhere. Downing Street has acknowledged as much: a spokesperson said the government understood that firefighters, farmers and NHS staff were all facing challenges connected to the weather, a rare instance of a government naming the stress across three separate frontline services in a single sentence.

What Cobra actually buys

A Cobra meeting is coordination machinery, not policy in itself, and Wednesday's session produced no announced package — no emergency fund for fire services, no compensation scheme for farmers, no water infrastructure spending. That absence is where Verdant's economic analysis becomes more than background colour. The thinktank's research points to 30°C as a threshold beyond which labour productivity drops sharply, hitting construction and agriculture hardest because those sectors cannot simply move indoors. It is calling on the government to set a legal maximum working temperature and to be ready to compensate workers whose hours are cut by heat — recommendations that, if adopted, would convert an ad hoc emergency response into a standing policy framework. Left unaddressed, Verdant projects cumulative heatwave losses reaching £25bn by the end of 2030.

That is the throughline connecting the wildfires, the drought and the balance sheet: each Cobra meeting treats the current heat spike as a discrete event, while the underlying pattern — repeated, escalating, increasingly costly — behaves like a structural feature of the UK economy rather than a one-off shock. The test of Wednesday's meeting will not be whether it happened, but whether it produces the kind of standing measures, on fire service resourcing, water infrastructure or workplace heat rules, that a second annual Cobra convening this year suggests are overdue. The next marker is the Environment Agency's drought status update, expected within weeks, and any formal government response to Verdant's policy recommendations.
