Burnham says wealthier areas must house more asylum seekers after village revolt

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government wants to place 1,250 asylum seekers at a former MoD site bordering Piddington, Oxfordshire. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham told wealthier areas to house more asylum seekers after a 400-person Oxfordshire village revolted over a 1,250-bed MoD site plan.

Andy Burnham used his first major intervention on asylum policy since entering Downing Street to tell wealthier parts of England they can no longer opt out of housing asylum seekers, after residents of a 400-person Oxfordshire village staged a mock independence vote against a Home Office plan to place 1,250 men on their doorstep.

"More affluent areas" would have to take their share, the prime minister said, days after more than 900 people crossed the Channel in small boats over a single three-day stretch and a record 230 migrants arrived on one inflatable boat alone — the largest single crossing on record. The remark was aimed squarely at Piddington, a hamlet near Bicester where average house prices run above £583,000, more than double the national figure, and where children wrote letters to Burnham begging him to abandon the scheme.

A village of 400, a camp for 1,250

The Home Office wants to convert MoD Bicester — a disused garrison site wedged between Piddington and Upper Arncott — into accommodation for up to 1,250 single adult male asylum seekers, part of a wider push to end the use of hotels that has cost the department billions since the small-boats crisis began. It is one of three former military sites earmarked to hold roughly 3,700 people between them.

For Piddington, population around 400, the arithmetic is stark: parish chairman Tim McNally likened the influx to "adding 32 million people into London overnight." Residents say they learned of the plan with no consultation from the Home Office and no warning from Cherwell District Council, whose leader, Leslie McLean, called it "an arbitrary decision from the Home Office taken yet again without any consultation with the council, residents or locally elected members."

The backlash escalated on 4 July, timed to the 250th anniversary of American independence, when residents held a symbolic referendum on seceding from the United Kingdom. Ninety-six per cent backed holding the vote. "We're taking a hit on behalf of the United Kingdom," McNally said. Farmer Mervyn Wynne raised a more practical worry: "We don't even have a police station. So if there's no police in our area, how long are we going to wait for a response?" Callum Miller, the Liberal Democrat MP for Bicester and Woodstock, has called the site selection "wrong for residents and wrong for people seeking asylum," while stopping short of opposing the asylum system itself.

Burnham's reply

Burnham's response was less an apology than a challenge to the rest of the country. Dispersal of asylum seekers has long skewed toward Britain's poorer boroughs — more than 180 local authorities currently house none at all — a pattern Burnham complained about for years as Mayor of Greater Manchester, when he warned his own region was carrying a disproportionate load. As prime minister, he is now applying that argument in reverse, to a village that is anything but deprived.

The political calculation is not subtle. With small-boat arrivals still running at roughly 14,500 for the year — down sharply on 2025 but running through record-sized single crossings — hotel costs remain one of the most politically toxic line items in the Home Office budget, and Burnham has little room to simply cancel schemes wherever they meet resistance. Piddington is a test case for whether that logic holds when the objectors are not in a struggling ex-mill town but in the Oxfordshire commuter belt.

What's next

No final decision has been taken on the Bicester site; the Home Office has filed an Urgent Crown Development application with the housing ministry, a route that bypasses ordinary local planning permission, and due diligence work is continuing with a target of opening before the end of 2026. Piddington's independence vote carries no legal force, but the row has already drawn cross-party attention in Westminster, and further Home Office site announcements — expected at other former military bases — will show whether Burnham's "wealthier areas" line becomes policy or stays a slogan aimed at one aggrieved village.
