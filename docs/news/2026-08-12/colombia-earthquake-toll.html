Colombia earthquake death toll passes 250 as rescuers search for survivors

Cali, Colombia's third-largest city, was among the hardest hit by Monday's magnitude-7.4 earthquake. — Johannes Bud / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Colombia's strongest earthquake in decades has killed 254 and paused coffee exports through its main Pacific port, while oil infrastructure stayed largely intact.

The earthquake gave Cali and Pereira almost no warning. At 7:34am on Monday, as commuters were pouring coffee and opening shop shutters, the ground beneath Colombia's Pacific coast and central Andes convulsed for nearly two minutes. By Tuesday morning the death toll stood at 254, with Pereira — 101 dead — and Cali — 95 dead — bearing the worst of it. As of Wednesday, search teams were still working through collapsed buildings in four cities, and the toll was continuing to rise.

The US Geological Survey put the magnitude at 7.4, with an epicentre some 20 kilometres from San José del Palmar in Chocó department and a depth of 110 kilometres — deep enough that the shaking radiated outward across a wide swathe of the country rather than concentrating destruction narrowly. Colombia's national geological service called it the strongest quake to hit the country so far this century, eclipsing anything recorded since a magnitude-7.2 event struck near Tumaco in 1979. Tremors reached Bogotá, 400 kilometres east, and were felt as far as Ecuador and Panama.

A widening search

Gloria Giraldo, a 38-year-old housewife in one of the neighbourhoods that came down around her, put it plainly: "Since yesterday we've been wondering what will become of our lives." In Cali, 22-year-old journalism student Luis Saenz was drinking his morning coffee when the shaking started. "For me, the earthquake felt eternal," he said.

Official counts put more than 2,500 people injured and, by some tallies, over 4,000 still missing — a figure aid agencies caution will fall as communications are restored and rise as more bodies are recovered from rubble. Rescue workers have been listening for signs of life in the wreckage; near one collapsed building in Cali, volunteer Andrés Felipe Mejía described a rescuer asking anyone trapped to whistle. "Someone whistled back," he said. Aid groups regard the 48-to-72-hour window after a quake as the most critical for finding survivors — a window that, five days on, has largely closed, shifting the operation from rescue toward recovery.

The destruction is concentrated in the cities strung along Colombia's central mountain corridor. In Cali, the country's third-largest city, more than 5,000 buildings were damaged and at least 40 collapsed, among them three floors of the Hospital Universitario del Valle, forcing the evacuation of over 600 patients. Pereira lost part of its airport terminal and dozens of structures, including a law-school building at Universidad Libre. In Manizales, a tower of the city's basilica cathedral came down. President Abelardo de la Espriella, touring the wreckage, said it "broke my heart going through the city centre and seeing all these businesses destroyed." He has declared a state of national disaster and deployed more than 1,000 soldiers, while offers of help have arrived from Ecuador, the United States — which pledged $15.5 million — El Salvador, Venezuela, Mexico and half a dozen other governments.

The coffee axis goes quiet

The geography of the disaster is what gives it a second life beyond the humanitarian story. Pereira and Manizales sit at the heart of Colombia's so-called coffee axis — the departments of Caldas, Risaralda and Quindío that together grow roughly a quarter of the country's arabica crop. Colombia is the world's second-largest producer of premium arabica beans, behind only Brazil, and the quake struck during a period when global stockpiles certified for delivery on the ICE exchange were already thin.

The more immediate problem for exporters is logistics rather than lost trees. Maersk said terminal operations at Buenaventura — the Pacific port that handles the bulk of Colombia's coffee shipments — were temporarily suspended, and Bloomberg reported that exports were largely paused as landslides and debris closed key highways linking the growing region to the coast. Arabica futures in New York whipsawed on the news, touching a three-week high before giving back some of the gain as traders weighed a disrupted supply chain against production that, so far, appears structurally intact.

Oil and gas infrastructure, by contrast, seems to have been spared the worst. Ecopetrol's refineries at Barrancabermeja and Cartagena continued operating, and the pipeline network feeding the Coveñas export terminal on the Caribbean coast reported no interruption — a reminder that Colombia's energy infrastructure sits mostly outside the earthquake zone, even as its agricultural heartland does not.

An economy on emergency footing

Colombia's government moved this week to declare an economic emergency, a legal mechanism that would let de la Espriella issue tax and spending decrees without waiting on Congress. Finance Minister Miguel Gómez told lawmakers the declaration could come within days, framing the moment as "the worst economic crisis in Colombian history" while pledging not to raise taxes to pay for reconstruction. Perhaps counterintuitively, the peso has firmed for five consecutive trading sessions since the quake — a sign, for now, that currency markets are pricing the disaster as a fiscal and humanitarian shock rather than a threat to the country's export earnings.

Whether that calm holds depends on what happens at Buenaventura in the coming days. If the port and the mountain highways reopen quickly, this becomes a short, sharp disruption that coffee traders absorb within weeks. If roadblocks persist through the September harvest window, the story shifts from rescue to supply, and arabica prices — already sensitive to any hint of tightness — become the mechanism by which the earthquake is felt far beyond Colombia's coffee axis.
