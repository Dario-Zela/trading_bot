Shell extends Ormen Lange gas outage to February 2027

The Nyhamna plant processes gas from Ormen Lange before it is piped to Britain and the continent. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell has extended a 40% output cut at Norway's Ormen Lange gas field to February 2027, spanning the entire winter heating season.

Shell has pushed back the timeline for fixing a broken subsea compressor at Ormen Lange, Norway's second-largest gas field, meaning the site will run at roughly 60% of capacity through the entire European winter. The field, which normally sends gas to Britain and the continent via the Nyhamna processing plant and the Langeled pipeline, has been curtailed by about 8.9 million cubic metres a day — a 40% cut from its 22.9 mcm daily capacity — since July 13. Shell had initially told the market the outage would be resolved by October. It now says February 1, 2027.

That single date change matters more than it looks. October would have meant a short, awkward gap before winter demand ramped up. February means the shortfall runs straight through the coldest months, when European heating demand is least able to shrug off a supply hit.

A young compression system, already faltering

The fault sits with one of Ormen Lange's two subsea compressor stations, forced offline by what Shell's Norway operation describes only as "technical challenges we have had on some of the compressors." The company says it is still investigating the underlying cause.

The timing is awkward. The subsea compression system now malfunctioning was commissioned barely a year ago, part of a project designed to offset the field's declining reservoir pressure — Ormen Lange has been producing since 2007 and pressure naturally falls as a reservoir depletes — and lift its ultimate recovery rate from 75% to 85%, unlocking an estimated 30 to 50 billion cubic metres of additional gas over the field's life. Instead of adding supply, the new equipment has, for now, subtracted it. The field sits 120 kilometres off Norway's coast, with wellheads and compression units on the seabed at depths of 800 to 1,100 metres — engineering that is hard to service quickly even when everyone knows exactly what's wrong.

Ownership of the field is split five ways: Petoro holds the largest stake at 36.5%, Equinor 25.3%, Shell 17.8%, Orlen Upstream Norway 14% and Vår Energi 6.3%. Shell operates it on the group's behalf.

Why a Norwegian field problem is a European problem

Norway overtook Russia as Europe's largest gas supplier in 2022 and has held that position since. In 2025 its exports were equivalent to more than 30% of total gas consumption across the EU and UK combined — a scale that makes any Norwegian outage, and not just headline ones like this, a continental event rather than a national inconvenience.

Ormen Lange's trouble is also not isolated. Maintenance at the Troll field, Norway's largest, has run longer than scheduled this month, and combined Norwegian flows to Europe have fallen below 240 million cubic metres a day, down from around 340 mcm in early August. Layered on top of that is the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz, which has curbed LNG cargoes reaching Europe and is on course to produce the first contraction in global LNG trade in over a decade this year, according to Shell's own market outlook. Europe ended last winter with storage at just 28% full, against a five-year average of 41%, leaving less cushion than usual heading into the refill season.

The market's response

Traders have not needed convincing that supply is tight. The European benchmark TTF contract climbed to €61.8 per megawatt-hour on August 11, extending gains from the prior session, as hopes faded for a deal that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz to normal tanker traffic. The Ormen Lange extension adds a second, more durable strand to that tightness: Hormuz-linked disruption could, in principle, ease with diplomacy on any given week, but a broken subsea compressor does not fix itself on a negotiator's timetable.

The practical consequence is that Europe will lean more heavily on marginal LNG cargoes to make up the pipeline shortfall — cargoes that are simultaneously being bid for by Asian buyers competing for the same tight global pool. That combination, a structural pipeline gap on one side and a strained LNG market on the other, is the mechanism through which a compressor fault off the Norwegian coast turns into higher heating bills from Berlin to Birmingham.

The date to watch is February 1, 2027, Shell's new target for restoring full output — though the field has already missed one such deadline. Storage refill data through the autumn, and whether Troll's flows normalise, will tell traders whether this winter's Norwegian shortfall stays contained to Ormen Lange or widens further.
