Meta hit with $567m New Mexico fine as child-safety penalties near $1bn

Meta's headquarters complex in Menlo Park, California, where the company is contesting a New Mexico court's child-safety order. — Nokia621 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A New Mexico judge, comparing Meta to a polluting factory, added a $567m fine and ordered redesigns to like-counts and teen notifications, pushing case penalties near $1bn.

Meta's legal bill for failing to protect children on Facebook and Instagram has climbed toward $1 billion in less than five months — and for the first time, a court isn't just making the company pay. It's making the company change its product.

A judge in Santa Fe, New Mexico, ordered Meta on August 7 to pay $567 million into a state fund for treating and preventing harm to young people, on top of the $375 million a jury already assessed against the company in March. Combined, the two penalties from a single case now total $942 million — and they come bundled with a set of court-mandated changes to how Instagram and Facebook work for anyone under 18.

A factory that pollutes

Judge Bryan Biedscheid, who oversaw the case after New Mexico's jury verdict, didn't reach for the language courts usually use for consumer-protection violations. He reached for an environmental-law metaphor instead.

"The Court considers Meta's platforms to be analogous to the factory, the advertising and other content displayed on those platforms to be what is produced by the factory, and the psychological harm to and sexual exploitation of children to be the pollution that must be abated."

He went further, writing that "just as noxious pollution produced by the factory can harm the common public right to reasonably clean air, the harmful effects of Meta's platforms on children do not stay contained by its platforms" — they "migrate to the internet as a whole and, perhaps most concerning, to the real world."

That framing matters legally as much as rhetorically. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez sued Meta under public-nuisance law, the same legal theory historically used against polluters, opioid manufacturers and gun makers — not the theory typically used against a social network. A jury sided with the state in March, finding Meta had violated consumer-protection law by designing products that hook young users while concealing what it knew about child sexual exploitation happening on its platforms. Biedscheid's abatement order this month decides what the company now has to do about it.

What actually changes for teenagers

The remedies go beyond a check-writing exercise. Under the order, Meta must, for users it identifies as under 18 in New Mexico:

  • Hide like counts by default, restorable only with parental or guardian approval
  • Pause push notifications overnight (10 p.m. to 7 a.m.) and during school hours on academic-year weekdays
  • Cap combined Facebook and Instagram usage at roughly 90 hours a month — about three hours a day
  • Make Instagram accounts private by default and unsearchable except by username
  • Block adults from messaging minors they haven't previously connected with
  • Build a dedicated model to predict under-13 users within two years, request age verification from suspected under-13 accounts, and delete data collected on children found to be under 13

Meta must also file compliance reports twice a year and set up an appeals process for users who believe the company misjudged their age. WhatsApp is exempt.

$420 million of the new fund is earmarked for treatment services for young people harmed by the platforms; the rest goes to prevention, screening and awareness programs over five years, according to the New Mexico Department of Justice.

Rounding error, precedent-setter

Set against Meta's finances, the number is almost trivial: the company reported roughly $60 billion in profit in 2025, making $942 million a fraction of a single quarter's earnings. Meta's stock barely moved, closing down less than half a percent at $589.44 on the day of the ruling. A company spokesperson said Meta "disagrees with the ruling and will appeal," adding that it has been transparent about the challenges of identifying and removing bad actors from its platforms.

What's harder to shrug off is the template. More than 40 states and over 1,300 school districts have filed similar public-nuisance suits against social media companies, and Massachusetts' attorney general has already cleared a court hurdle to pursue Meta on nearly identical grounds. If other judges adopt Biedscheid's factory framing — treating engagement features as the product and psychological harm as an externality to be abated rather than merely penalized — the exposure stops being about fines altogether and starts being about which features companies are legally allowed to ship to minors.

That's the shift traders should note even if the dollar figure looks like noise: like counts, overnight notifications and open-ended scrolling are not incidental to the business model, they're tuned to maximize time-on-platform. A court ordering their removal for a chunk of the user base is a different kind of risk than a fine — one that scales with however many other states decide to follow New Mexico's lead.

Meta's appeal will test whether the remedy survives on review, likely stretching into 2027. Watch for how Massachusetts' case — already cleared to proceed after an April ruling — handles the same public-nuisance theory, and whether other state attorneys general cite Biedscheid's order directly in filings of their own.
