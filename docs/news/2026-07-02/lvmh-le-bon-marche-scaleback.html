LVMH scales back for sixth Le Bon Marché open-doors event

Le Bon Marché's Art Nouveau facade on Paris's Left Bank, the flagship of LVMH's selective retailing business. — Fred Romero / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH is deliberately scaling back the size of its sixth Journées Particulières open-doors event as its fashion and leather goods sales keep sliding.

Every artisan has a story, but LVMH would rather you learn only some of them at a time this year.

The French luxury conglomerate has confirmed the sixth edition of Les Journées Particulières — its group-wide open-doors event, in which normally closed workshops, ateliers and heritage sites across LVMH's roughly 75 maisons throw open their gates to the public — will run October 16 to 18, 2026, marking its return after a four-year hiatus. But this time, the house that owns Louis Vuitton, Dior and Le Bon Marché is deliberately not trying to outdo itself.

"What matters is putting the spotlight on our artisans, ensuring they're happy, and making sure members of the public leave having learned something and having had a unique experience," Antoine Arnault, LVMH's director of image and environment, told WWD.

From record-chasing to right-sizing

The 2022 edition — the last before this year's — pulled in roughly 200,000 visitors across 96 sites in 15 countries, a record for the event since it launched in 2011. Arnault's framing this time is notably different: rather than chase a bigger number, the group is emphasizing curated intimacy over crowd size. New venues for 2026 include Celine's ready-to-wear workshop in Paris, Louis Vuitton's leather-goods atelier in Vendôme, and Loro Piana's knitwear facility in Ghemme, Italy — fewer marquee flourishes, more workshop floor. This year's theme, "Where Dreams Take Root," leans into heritage and craft rather than spectacle.

Le Bon Marché, LVMH's Left Bank department store and the world's oldest, has long been one of the group's proving grounds for this kind of experiential retail — torchlight heritage tours, artist-curated window installations, capsule launches with designers like Tatiana de Nicolay. Journées Particulières is the biggest of these gestures, staged across the entire group rather than one store, and its scaled-back return puts Le Bon Marché's flagship role in a tighter frame this October.

The backdrop: a rough stretch for the tape

The timing is not incidental. LVMH's fashion and leather goods division — its largest and most profitable business, home to Louis Vuitton and Dior — opened 2026 with organic sales down 2% in the first quarter, extending a slump that has run for the better part of two years and was aggravated in the spring by the Middle East conflict's drag on tourism-driven spending. Arnault was careful to insist the open-doors event was never designed as a sales lever in the first place, saying it "stems from a genuine desire to periodically draw attention to these artisans and give them a platform to express themselves" rather than to move product or drive recruitment.

That may be true of the event's original intent. It is harder to ignore that a company trimming the scale of its flagship experiential showcase is doing so at the same moment its core fashion business is under real pressure — and that luxury groups broadly have grown more careful about where they spend on brand theater versus where they let the craftsmanship speak for itself in a smaller room.

What to watch

The test comes in October, when the event's actual footprint — visitor numbers, site count, country spread — can be measured against 2022's high-water mark. LVMH reports first-half results in late July, which will show whether the fashion and leather goods slide has stabilized or deepened, and give a clearer read on whether Les Journées Particulières' more modest staging is a one-off recalibration or the start of a broader retreat from big-ticket experiential retail across the group.
