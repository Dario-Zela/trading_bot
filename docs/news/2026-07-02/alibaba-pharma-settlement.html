Alibaba and AUS Merchant Services to pay $600m over illegal pharmacy sales

Alibaba's Xixi campus headquarters in Hangzhou, China. — Thomas Lombard / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Alibaba and its U.S. payments affiliate will pay $600 million after admitting roughly 80,000 illegal pharmaceutical sales reached American buyers over nine years.

Alibaba Group and its U.S. payments affiliate, AUS Merchant Services — formerly known as Alipay US — have agreed to pay $600 million to resolve federal allegations that they let illegal pharmaceuticals and drug-manufacturing equipment move through their platforms and into the American market for nearly a decade.

The deal, announced by the Justice Department, is structured as a non-prosecution agreement rather than a criminal indictment. It is, according to the U.S. Attorney's office in Rhode Island, the largest monetary settlement in that district's history — notable given the case was built there rather than in a more obvious venue like the Southern District of New York.

What investigators found

According to the DOJ, Alibaba.com and AliExpress.com failed to stop merchants from selling and shipping prohibited pharmaceuticals, controlled substances, listed chemicals and pill presses — equipment used to manufacture counterfeit pills, including fentanyl-laced products — to U.S. buyers between January 2016 and December 2024. Federal agents ran more than 40 undercover purchases of drugs and equipment that were illegal to import, and Alibaba ultimately admitted that roughly 80,000 transactions violating the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act and related statutes slipped through, with a combined gross merchandise value exceeding $200 million.

AUS Merchant Services, the Ant Group-affiliated processor that handles payments and offshore transfers for Alibaba's U.S. customers, is named as a separate party to the settlement. Prosecutors said its transaction-monitoring system did not fully incorporate wire-transfer data and often missed payments tied to high-risk jurisdictions or multiple payors funneled through a single invoice — the kind of red flags compliance systems are built to catch.

"This resolution reflects the Department of Justice's commitment to holding companies accountable when their platforms are used to facilitate the unlawful sale of illegal pharmaceuticals, related pharmaceutical equipment and other prohibited products in the United States," said Charles C. Calenda, First Assistant U.S. Attorney for the District of Rhode Island. He added that the settlement "achieves meaningful accountability while securing significant compliance measures designed to strengthen oversight, prevent future violations, and better protect American consumers."

Alibaba has agreed to keep building out its compliance infrastructure and third-party seller monitoring as part of the deal, though the company has not issued substantive public comment beyond acknowledging the terms.

The broader pattern

The case lands alongside a run of enforcement actions targeting how marketplace operators police what happens on their own platforms — payment processors and logistics partners increasingly treated as liable parties, not passive intermediaries. For Alibaba, already navigating a depressed U.S.-listed share price and years of regulatory friction in Beijing, $600 million is a manageable one-off cost against a balance sheet of that scale. The more durable exposure is structural: platforms with large third-party marketplaces now face a higher bar for proving their controls actually work, not just that policies exist on paper. Whether the DOJ pursues similar non-prosecution deals with other e-commerce operators — Amazon and Temu both run comparable third-party marketplaces with U.S. exposure — is the next thing worth watching.
