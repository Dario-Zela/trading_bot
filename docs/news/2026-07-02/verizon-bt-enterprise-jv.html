Verizon and BT form $4bn venture for global enterprise clients

Verizon's Manhattan headquarters; the carrier is folding its international enterprise wireline arm into the new venture with BT. — Bjoertvedt / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Verizon and BT are merging their international enterprise units into a 50:50, $4bn-revenue joint venture, betting scale can offset hyperscaler pressure on long-haul networking.

Verizon and BT Group have agreed to fold their international enterprise networking businesses into a single 50:50 joint venture worth roughly $4 billion in combined annual revenue, betting that multinational corporations will pay for one company to manage their global connectivity rather than stitching it together from regional providers.

The venture, announced June 29, will serve more than 3,000 customers in over 180 countries once it closes — expected sometime in 2027, pending regulatory clearance. It will be incorporated in Jersey but headquartered and tax-resident in the UK. Both parents will hold equal voting rights. As part of the balancing act, Verizon will pay BT $625 million in cash to equalize the value of the assets each side is contributing, since Verizon's international wireline unit and BT International aren't identically sized or profitable.

Two carriers, one drag on earnings

The logic is less about growth than about offloading a problem neither company particularly wanted to solve alone. BT's international arm has been a persistent weight on group margins — its adjusted EBITDA margin runs around 5.9%, against roughly 47% in BT's core UK business, according to Megabuyte analyst Tom Oughton, who called the international unit "a drag on BT Group" that management has now essentially decided to stop hauling by itself. BT's international business is forecast to bring in £1.82 billion in revenue this year against just £108 million in adjusted EBITDA — thin enough that the case for combining it with a similarly-sized rival, rather than continuing to run it as a standalone cost center, was not a hard one to make internally.

Verizon's calculus is the mirror image. Its international enterprise wireline business is, in the company's own framing, immaterial next to its US wireless and fiber operations, but it still requires capital and management attention to keep running credibly for multinational clients who expect coverage in dozens of jurisdictions. Combining it with BT's footprint lets Verizon claim scale in international enterprise services without having to build or buy it outright, and lets both companies redirect focus toward their home markets — Britain for BT, the US for Verizon.

Allison Kirkby, BT's chief executive, described the aim as building "a stronger, scaled connectivity partner — one that has the reach, innovation and investment to succeed." Verizon's Dan Schulman framed it around AI-readiness and security: a "cutting-edge, AI-ready and secure platform run by a single global organization." Strip the marketing language and the more durable rationale, per Oughton, is straightforward consolidation — combining duplicative infrastructure and back-office operations to extract scale that neither firm could achieve solo.

The hyperscaler problem underneath it

The venture's more interesting fight is not with other telecom carriers, though it will compete against Orange Business, AT&T, NTT, Vodafone, Tata Communications, Telefónica and Colt for the same corporate contracts. It's with the hyperscalers. Google, Meta, Microsoft and Amazon have spent the past decade quietly buying and building their own transoceanic cable capacity, and now carry the bulk of transoceanic data traffic themselves — a shift that has steadily eroded the pricing power legacy carriers once held over long-haul bandwidth. Telecom analyst Jitesh Bhayani put it plainly: the hyperscalers "have been quietly winning the long-haul infrastructure battle for a decade," leaving traditional carriers to compete increasingly on managed services and compliance rather than raw pipe.

That is where the new venture is trying to plant its flag. Its most cited strategic asset is BT's Global Fabric network-as-a-service platform, which gives the combined company a modern, software-defined offering Verizon's international arm reportedly lacked on its own. The pitch to clients centers on data sovereignty and regulatory compliance — infrastructure built to keep data routing and storage within specific jurisdictions, a feature increasingly demanded by European enterprises navigating tightening data-residency rules. Whether that is enough to hold ground against hyperscaler-backed alternatives, or merely slows an already-visible decline in carrier bargaining power, is the open question Bhayani flagged: the combined entity is "a more defensible position than either could sustain independently," but defensibility and growth are not the same thing.

Markets read it as a cost, not yet a win

Investors treated the announcement as a near-term expense rather than a strategic coup. Verizon shares fell 5.3% on the day, closing at $44.08 — a decline compounded by the company's separate removal from the Dow Jones Industrial Average that same session, but driven in large part by management's disclosure that the transaction will trigger $700 million to $800 million in restructuring charges. Verizon has said it expects the venture to be accretive to EBITDA once integration is complete, and sell-side coverage has largely stayed constructive — JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley carry price targets of $52 and $50 respectively against Wall Street's consensus target near $50.68 — but the charge disclosure is a reminder that consolidation plays extract their cost up front, with the payoff, if it comes, arriving years later.

Martijn Blanken, a telecom and infrastructure veteran of nearly three decades at firms including Telstra, EXA Infrastructure and KPN, has been named chief executive-designate of the new venture and takes up the role September 1, ahead of the deal's expected 2027 close. Clive Selley, BT International's current chief, stays on through the transition. The gap between announcement and completion — roughly eighteen months — leaves ample room for the regulatory review process across multiple jurisdictions to reshape the venture's terms, and for competitors to react before the combined platform is actually live. The next marker to watch is the regulatory filing process itself, which will show how competition authorities in the UK, US and elsewhere weigh a tie-up between two of the last independent-scale carriers in a market hyperscalers are already reshaping from underneath.
