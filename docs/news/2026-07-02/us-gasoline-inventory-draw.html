US gasoline stockpiles post bigger-than-expected draw

A gas station in the United States; national average pump prices stood at $3.85 a gallon on July 1. — Harrison Keely / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
US gasoline stocks fell 2.33 million barrels last week, more than double forecasts, as crude stocks also dropped further below average.

US gasoline stockpiles fell by 2.33 million barrels last week, more than double the drawdown Wall Street had penciled in, according to Energy Information Administration data released Wednesday. Economists surveyed ahead of the report had forecast a 950,000-barrel decline; the actual figure landed at roughly two-and-a-half times that, reversing a 2.06-million-barrel build the week before.

Crude stocks moved the same direction. Commercial crude inventories dropped 3.775 million barrels for the week ended June 26, versus a forecast decline of 2.9 million, leaving stockpiles at 408.4 million barrels — 7% below the five-year seasonal average. It was the second straight week of an outsized draw, following a 6.1-million-barrel drop the week prior.

Refiners are already running flat out

The gasoline number is harder to wave off as noise given what refiners are doing to meet it. US plants processed crude at 96.1% of operable capacity in the most recent complete week on record, churning out gasoline at close to 10 million barrels a day — near the top of the range for this time of year. Four-week average gasoline demand stood at 9.0 million barrels a day, and total petroleum products supplied, the EIA's proxy for nationwide fuel demand, averaged 20.6 million barrels a day, up 1.7% from the same period last year. Stockpiles fell even with refiners at nearly full tilt, which points to consumption running ahead of what a single week's build-up the prior week had suggested.

A market already on edge

The timing matters. Gasoline and crude inventories are being drawn down against a global market still absorbing the fallout from the Strait of Hormuz crisis that began in late February, when tanker traffic through the chokepoint collapsed after the US and Israel struck Iranian targets. The International Energy Agency has called the resulting supply loss the largest disruption to oil markets in decades. Retail gasoline has followed: AAA's national average stood at $3.847 a gallon on July 1, up nearly a dollar over the past month, even as futures pulled back modestly — the gasoline benchmark tracked by Trading Economics eased 6.7% over 30 days before ticking up 1.4% on July 1 alone, the day the inventory data landed.

None of that means this week's draw is the start of a durable trend rather than one data point arriving into a jumpy market. But it does mean the number carries more weight than a routine weekly print would in calmer conditions, landing just as the Fourth of July holiday pushes driving demand toward its seasonal peak.

The next EIA report, covering the holiday travel week itself, will show whether the draw compounds or whether refiners' output catches up with demand. A repeat drawdown of this size, layered on top of stockpiles already running below their five-year average, would be the clearer signal that this is supply tightness rather than a single week's blip.
