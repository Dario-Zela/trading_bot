Fed's Warsh calls inflation 'too high' as July hike odds slide

Kevin Warsh delivers remarks after being sworn in as Federal Reserve chairman at the White House, May 22, 2026. — Daniel Torok / The White House
In one sentence
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh called inflation 'too high' at Sintra, but futures markets still peg July hike odds at roughly 30% or less.

Kevin Warsh has been Federal Reserve chairman for six weeks, and on Wednesday he used a stage in Portugal to say, in effect, that the inflation fight he inherited from Jerome Powell is not over. Markets heard him — and then priced in less chance of a July rate increase anyway.

Speaking on a panel at the European Central Bank's annual forum in Sintra, Warsh told CNBC's Sara Eisen that whatever open-mindedness central bankers had shown about artificial intelligence and productivity, "we've all looked around, and we've seen that prices are too high." He added a line clearly aimed at investors betting the Fed will tolerate a warmer inflation target under new management: "If there were people in the household or the business sector and the financial markets who thought that this central bank was going to be comfortable with an inflation objective above 2%, well, I guess they'd be disappointed. We're going to deliver price stability in the US."

"A good family fight"

What Warsh did not do was tip his hand on the July 28–29 meeting. Asked directly, he demurred, then corrected his own timeline with a bit of dry humor: "I said I'm not going to give forward guidance because we're meeting in six weeks, but I have an update for you, we're meeting in four weeks. I want us to have a good family fight … When we get into that room and shut the door, we're going to have a good debate, but I don't have much more for you than that." He also batted away a question about White House pressure on the central bank, saying the Fed had "been an independent central bank for a very long time … you're going to see no changes on that."

The rhetorical package was consistent with the hawkish turn Warsh set at his first press conference on June 17, when the Fed held its policy rate at 3.50%–3.75% for a fourth straight meeting and stripped language suggesting a bias toward cuts. Nine of eighteen FOMC participants now pencil in at least one hike this year, versus a committee that spent most of 2025 debating when to start cutting.

The market's math doesn't move much

And yet CME's FedWatch tool, which derives odds from fed funds futures, showed roughly a 70%–73% probability of no change in July after Warsh spoke — little different from where it sat before his remarks, and still a firm bet against near-term tightening. The same pricing implies about a 65% chance of a quarter-point hike by the September meeting, meaning traders are willing to entertain Warsh's inflation concern, just not yet, and not without more data. Treasury yields did tick higher on the comments and equities wobbled modestly — the S&P 500 slipped 0.22%, the Nasdaq 0.66% — but the moves were consistent with a hawkish reminder, not a repricing of the July meeting itself.

If anything nudged hike odds up in the run-up to Sintra, it was labor data rather than rhetoric. May JOLTS job openings rose to 7.594 million, ahead of the 7.296 million forecast and the second straight monthly increase, a signal of labor-market resilience that briefly pushed implied July hike odds toward one-in-three. The yen's slide past ¥162 to the dollar, a 40-year low, added to the same "policy stays tighter for longer" narrative. Warsh's own words, delivered the same week, did less to move the futures curve than a jobs report did.

Rhetoric versus reality

That gap has a name among Fed watchers: some read Warsh's hawkishness as theater rather than a policy signal. Robin Brooks at the Brookings Institution has argued the tough talk is "largely performative," intended to draw a visible line between the new chairman and a White House that pushed hard for his appointment and for lower rates. Citi's Andrew Hollenhorst makes the substantive case for skepticism: oil markets have swung from shortage to surplus, removing an upside inflation risk; first-quarter GDP growth excluding AI-related capital spending was roughly 0.5%; and core CPI is projected to cool below 2.5% by August from 2.9% in May. On that view, a Fed chair talking tough in Sintra costs nothing and buys credibility, while the incoming data does the actual work of deciding policy.

Warsh's defenders would counter that a new chairman confirmed by the narrowest Senate margin in the Fed's history — 54 to 45 — has extra reason to sound tough on inflation regardless of what he intends to do with rates. Either explanation leaves traders in roughly the same position: pricing a Fed that talks like 2022 and is expected, for now, to act like a committee on hold.

What comes next

The next real test of whose read is correct lands on July 14, when the Bureau of Labor Statistics releases the June CPI report — the last major inflation print before the July FOMC meeting. A hot number would validate Warsh's framing and could push hike odds meaningfully higher; a soft one, as some economists expect, would strengthen the case that his Sintra remarks were exactly what critics called them. Between now and the meeting, Warsh has also promised five task forces reviewing Fed operations, with outside members to be named in the coming week — a structural overhaul running in parallel with the rate debate, and one more reason the "family fight" behind closed doors in late July may look different from the one being staged in public.
