Shell sees steady LNG trade even as fossil-fuel strategy weighs on stock

Shell Centre, the company's London headquarters, on the South Bank of the Thames. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell says 2026 LNG trade could roughly match 2025 despite Hormuz disruption, but the forecast is conditional and its own stock is lagging the market.

Shell expects worldwide LNG trade to run roughly flat this year against 2025's 422 million tonnes — not the growth the market had banked on, but not the collapse that seven months of Middle East shipping disruption might have produced either. The company's LNG Outlook 2026, published this week, pins that forecast on one big condition: that traffic through the Strait of Hormuz gets back to normal by summer.

It has not been normal for a while. Iranian strikes on Qatari export terminals at Ras Laffan and Mesaieed on March 2 forced a complete halt to production at facilities that, between them, handle a meaningful share of the world's LNG. Shell estimates the wider disruption has shut in around one-fifth of global monthly LNG supply since the conflict began, pushing Asian spot prices above $20 per million British thermal units at the peak. Buyers were paying $11-12 per MMBtu in May, against $7-11 before the fighting started in January.

Redundancy, or just less demand?

That the system absorbed a fifth of monthly supply without a bigger price spiral is the closest thing to evidence for the "resilient supply chains" reading of this story. But Shell's own explanation leans less on rerouting and more on substitution: new liquefaction capacity ramping up in North America, better uptime at existing plants, and — tellingly — slower LNG imports into Asia, meaning some of the gap was closed by buyers pulling back rather than sellers finding workarounds. If the current outlook holds, 2026 would mark the first flat-to-negative year for global LNG trade after more than a decade of steady growth. Shell CEO Wael Sawan has called underlying demand "resilient even through major international crises," but the forecast itself is a single company's modelling exercise, built on an assumption — Hormuz reopening on schedule — that is out of Shell's hands.

The stock isn't buying it

The confidence in gas has a mirror-image problem on Shell's own share price. Shares have underperformed the FTSE Global All Cap Index by roughly 6 percentage points over the past year, including a 5% slide in early February after fourth-quarter results missed estimates. Some of that is the oil cycle — Brent has fallen about 18% over the same period — but some of it is strategy. Sawan has cut renewables spending to roughly 8% of the capital budget, moved to sell Shell's offshore wind portfolio, and in April agreed to buy Canadian gas producer ARC Resources, doubling down on the Montney shale basin. Rivals BP and Equinor have made their own retreats from earlier transition targets, but Shell's tilt back toward hydrocarbons is now the more pronounced of the majors.

The two threads meet at the same point: Shell is betting that gas demand outlasts the politics disrupting it, and betting its capital allocation the same way. The company's own numbers say that wager on demand held up, barely, through the worst of the Hormuz shock. Whether investors reward it is a separate question — one that Shell's next earnings update, and any further movement on the strait, will help answer.
