Wall Street closes at record highs as Meta's AI-cloud bet pays off

The Wall Street sign outside the New York Stock Exchange, where the Dow touched a fresh intraday record before industrial stocks pared the gain. — Carlos Delgado (Kadellar) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
Meta jumped nearly 9% on a report it will sell surplus AI computing capacity, pushing the Dow to a record intraday high even as the closing gain evaporated.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average touched an all-time intraday high of 52,742.66 on Wednesday, a level that lasted only a few hours before industrial stocks dragged the index back down. But the number that mattered to traders wasn't the Dow's close — it was Meta Platforms, up as much as 11% at the peak and closing near 9% higher, after Bloomberg reported the company is building a cloud business to sell its surplus AI computing capacity to outside customers.

The move capped a week in which Wall Street had already banked a genuine record: the Dow closed at an all-time high on Tuesday, its second straight record close, as the major indexes wrapped up their best first half since 2020. The Dow gained 8.9% over the first six months of the year, the S&P 500 rose 9.6%, and the Nasdaq Composite climbed 12.8%. Wednesday's session was supposed to be a quieter coda. Instead it turned into a referendum on who actually profits from the trillion-dollar AI buildout.

The pivot

Meta has spent much of the past two years pouring money into data centers and chips — as much as $145 billion in AI infrastructure capital expenditure this year alone, part of an industry-wide outlay that has topped $700 billion in 2026. Until Wednesday, the working assumption on Wall Street was that this spending was defensive: Meta needed the compute to keep its own products — Instagram, WhatsApp, its Llama-successor Muse Spark models — competitive with rivals. What changed is that Meta now looks ready to sell what it doesn't use.

The effort is organized internally as Meta Compute, led by infrastructure chief Santosh Janardhan alongside Meta Superintelligence Labs' Daniel Gross and company president Dina Powell McCormick. Executives are still deciding whether to sell raw GPU cycles — the neocloud model pioneered by CoreWeave — or bundled access to Meta's own models. Mark Zuckerberg previewed the idea on a shareholder call in May, saying it was "definitely on the table" and noting that "almost every week there are different companies that come to us from the outside asking us to both stand up an API service or asking if we have compute that they could buy from us at some premium to what we've bought it at."

That framing is the story in miniature: Meta didn't build data centers to become a landlord, but the market is now pricing it as one. At a market capitalization above $1.5 trillion, the company is effectively being offered a second revenue line — infrastructure-as-a-service — layered on top of its advertising business, without having broken ground on a single new building to get there.

Winners, losers, and a $48 billion problem

The clearest losers were the two companies that had been supplying Meta with the capacity it now intends to resell. CoreWeave, which holds a $21 billion compute agreement with Meta, and Nebius, sitting on a $27 billion contract, both fell more than 10% Wednesday — CoreWeave and fellow neocloud operator IREN dropped alongside Nebius on the same logic: a customer worth tens of billions of dollars in combined contracts is preparing to compete with its own suppliers. Bitcoin miner-turned-AI-infrastructure player IREN slid in sympathy.

The reversal inside the Dow told a related story. Caterpillar, which had ridden the data-center buildout to become one of 2026's best-performing industrials, fell nearly 7% from its opening highs. Honeywell, mid-spinoff, slid 8.1%. Between them the two stocks stripped more than 440 points off the Dow's intraday advance — the mechanical reason the index touched a record high before ending the day essentially flat, down 13.96 points at 52,305.24. The S&P 500 slipped 0.22% to 7,483.23 and the Nasdaq fell 0.66% to 26,040.03, with chip stocks giving back some of a run that had taken many semiconductor names up more than 80% in the first half.

Traders have taken to calling it the Great Rotation: money moving out of the industrial suppliers that sold the shovels for the AI buildout and into the hyperscalers and software names that might actually monetize what got built. It is not a rejection of AI spending — the Dow's own record this week says otherwise — so much as a repricing of where in the chain the profit sits.

Why the market is buying the pivot

The read-through for the rest of Big Tech is direct. Amazon, Google and Microsoft already run public cloud businesses that absorb their AI capex and generate revenue immediately; Meta, historically, did not. If Meta can convert idle capacity into a paying business line, the multi-hundred-billion-dollar spending figures that have worried some investors start to look less like a bet on faith and more like a bet on unit economics — capacity that earns a return whether or not Meta's own apps need it that quarter.

That is also why CoreWeave and Nebius sold off so hard: their pitch to investors has been that they are the picks-and-shovels layer insulated from any single hyperscaler's strategy shifts. Wednesday demonstrated the opposite risk — customer concentration. When roughly a fifth of Nebius's backlog sits with one client, and that client decides to compete rather than keep buying, the stock reprices in hours, not quarters.

None of this resolves the underlying question hanging over the AI infrastructure trade: whether the more than $700 billion in collective hyperscaler spending this year will generate returns commensurate with the outlay. What Wednesday did was hand Wall Street its first concrete answer from one of the largest spenders — Meta thinks its excess capacity is worth monetizing directly, rather than absorbing quietly into its own product margins. Whether that logic holds will show up in Meta's next earnings report, when investors get their first look at how much revenue Meta Compute is actually generating, and in whatever CoreWeave and Nebius say when they report on how much of their backlog with Meta survives the transition.
