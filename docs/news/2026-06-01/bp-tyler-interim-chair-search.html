Ian Tyler named BP interim chair as board opens search for Manifold's permanent successor

BP's global headquarters at 1 St James's Square, Westminster, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP removed chairman Albert Manifold after eight months over conduct and governance concerns, installing Ian Tyler as interim chair while a permanent search begins.

When BP's board voted unanimously to remove Albert Manifold as chairman on 26 May, the decision landed like a surprise to almost everyone outside the boardroom. Manifold had been in the role for eight months. He had run CRH, the Irish building materials company, for a decade and presided over a roughly 400% rise in its share price. At BP, he did not last through a full calendar year.

Ian Tyler, a non-executive director who joined BP's board in April 2025 and had been chairing its remuneration committee, was appointed interim chairman with immediate effect. A search for a permanent replacement is underway.

BP's London-listed shares fell as much as 9% on the news before paring losses to close around 4% lower at £5.29 per share.

Eight months in the chair

Manifold took over from Helge Lund in October 2025, arriving as arguably BP's first genuine industry outsider to hold the role. He came with a mandate for cost discipline and strategic focus at a company already carrying considerable leadership scar tissue: former CEO Bernard Looney had resigned in 2023, and Manifold's own tenure began alongside BP's public acknowledgement, under then-CEO Murray Auchincloss, that the company had moved "too far and too fast" on its green energy pivot.

The board cited "serious concerns related to important governance standards, oversight and conduct." It did not specify. What anonymous colleagues told reporters was more granular: Manifold was described as "shouty," prone to speaking down to senior staff in individual and group settings, and exerting a degree of executive control uncommon — and constitutionally inappropriate — in a non-executive chair. Senior independent director Amanda Blanc said the board had "been surprised and disappointed to learn of governance oversight and conduct issues it deems unacceptable."

The vote was unanimous.

It was not the first signal of disquiet. At BP's AGM in April — just weeks before the removal — proxy adviser Glass Lewis had urged shareholders to vote against Manifold's re-election. Eighteen per cent of investors did. In the context of major oil company shareholder meetings, that is a pointed rebuke. Whatever the board received in the weeks following, it was apparently sufficient to convert dissatisfaction into decisive action.

A contested exit

Manifold moved quickly to push back. He said he had been removed "without warning and without explanation," and that he "dispute[s] entirely the characterisation of my conduct and will not allow a false narrative to go unchallenged." He acknowledged pushing hard and challenging people directly during his tenure, and suggested he had been physically present at BP's London headquarters on roughly thirteen days in 2026 — an implicit rebuttal of claims about excessive executive involvement.

BP's response was terse. A spokesperson said the company had "a duty of care to all our employees, particularly those impacted by his behavior," and referred back to the board's original statement.

The divergence is now a matter of public record, and neither side has shown any inclination to elaborate while the governance and potential legal implications remain live. What is clear is that the board acted fast, acted unanimously, and chose not to manage Manifold out quietly — a choice that itself tells you something about what they believed they had on their hands.

Governance at the worst possible moment

BP has now cycled through three chairmen in roughly eight months. That figure concentrates the mind of institutional shareholders who rely on a functioning, stable board to make credible judgements on capital allocation, executive pay, and long-term strategy.

The timing is particularly awkward. Meg O'Neill, who stepped into the CEO role in April 2026 following Auchincloss's December 2025 departure, is in the early stages of a significant restructuring — dismantling BP's gas and low-carbon energy division, refocusing on two core units (upstream and downstream), and raising the structural cost reduction target to $6.5–7.5 billion by 2027, some $1 billion above prior guidance. That kind of transformation requires consistent, functional board oversight. A permanent chair in transition mode is not the same thing.

Analysts reacted accordingly. Citi's Alastair Syme noted that Manifold had become "unusually important to BP's investment case in a way rarely seen among large international oil companies" — a description that, intended or not, carries its own warning about how fragile that case had become. Barclays' Lydia Rainforth was more direct, questioning how BP's selection process produced Manifold in the first place. RBC's Biraj Borkhataria offered the day's most qualified silver lining: absent evidence of financial misconduct, prolonged share-price weakness could make the company attractive to potential acquirers.

Ian Tyler brings a deliberately different profile. His executive career was built at Balfour Beatty, the infrastructure group, where as CEO from 2005 to 2013 he grew revenue from roughly £3.5 billion to over £9 billion. His subsequent non-executive experience spans BAE Systems, Anglo American, Cairn Energy, and Vistry Group. He is, in the board's evident view, a governance specialist with real operational weight — not an energy executive arriving with a strategic agenda of his own, but someone capable of steadying the institution while O'Neill executes hers.

The permanent search is now underway. How quickly BP can attract a credible, durable candidate — and whether the selection process this time produces fewer downstream surprises — will serve as the first test of whether the board has extracted the obvious lesson from the Manifold episode. A new chair will inherit a CEO with a clear mandate, a company in mid-restructuring, and a shareholder base whose patience for governance missteps has been eroding for the better part of three years. The next AGM will clarify how far that erosion has run.
