Glencore maps copper growth and buybacks at AGM as safety and labour scrutiny intensifies

Glencore's headquarters in Baar, Switzerland, where shareholders approved all 17 AGM resolutions on 28 May 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Glencore's 2026 AGM cleared all 17 resolutions as management mapped a path to 1.6Mt copper by 2035 and cited $27bn in cumulative returns, while unions and NGOs pressed on recent fatalities, labour hire and tailings dam safety.

Glencore's shareholders gathered in Baar on 28 May for an annual meeting that was, by shareholder-vote standards, orderly. All 17 resolutions passed, backed by roughly 78% of issued capital. The stage was set for management to walk investors through a copper buildout spanning nearly a decade — and for a quieter cast of voices, union officials and a Brazilian community group among them, to press on the less comfortable parts of the company's record.

The copper runway, and what backs it up

Chief executive Gary Nagle framed copper as central to Glencore's identity, describing it as "the commodity of the future" amid demand from data centres, AI infrastructure and the energy transition. The company's stated path runs from an annualised output of just over 1 million tonnes by the end of 2026 to 1.6 million tonnes by 2035 — a figure that would place Glencore among the world's largest producers in that metal alone.

The strategy leans heavily on brownfield expansions rather than greenfield bets. Glencore's own description — "low-risk, low-capital intensity" — captures the logic: existing infrastructure, known geology, shorter permitting timelines. The bulk of the pipeline sits in South America, with some projects in Africa.

A specific data point anchoring the near-term ramp: the Alumbrera mine restart in Argentina's Catamarca Province. Management expects mining to resume in the fourth quarter of 2026, with first copper production in the first half of 2028. At full run-rate, Alumbrera is projected to deliver roughly 75,000 tonnes of copper annually alongside 317,000 ounces of gold and 1,000 tonnes of molybdenum across a four-year mine life.

The longer-term ambition does not come without near-term friction. Glencore trimmed its 2026 copper guidance to 810,000–870,000 tonnes, down from an earlier 930,000-tonne target, after operational setbacks at Collahuasi, the large open-pit operation in northern Chile it co-owns with Anglo American. The cuts underscore that the runway to 1.6 million tonnes is built on project execution, not just stated ambition.

The marketing division, often underappreciated by investors who treat Glencore primarily as a miner, featured prominently in management's presentation. The business generated approximately $2.9 billion in adjusted EBIT for 2025, which Nagle positioned as sitting around the middle of an updated long-term guidance range of $2.3 billion to $3.5 billion. For a segment that operates largely off-balance-sheet to outside observers, the floor matters: it implies the trading book can sustain distributions even when commodity prices compress industrial margins.

Returns framework: buybacks at a discount, distributions on schedule

Glencore reported adjusted EBITDA of $13.5 billion for 2025, with industrial assets contributing roughly $10 billion of that figure. Net debt stayed below one times EBITDA. The company generated more than $10 billion in cash and returned $2 billion to shareholders: a $0.17-per-share distribution plus $1.8 billion in buybacks executed at an average of $4.40 per share.

With the stock trading around $7.70, that buyback average looks notably well-timed. Chair Kalidas Madhavpeddi cited cumulative returns of more than $27 billion since 2021 as evidence of a durable capital-return framework rather than opportunistic one-off payments. Shareholders appeared persuaded: the resolution authorising the capital reduction and repayment passed with 99.92% approval.

Safety, labour and the tailings question

The morning's less scripted moments came from outside the boardroom hierarchy. Nagle opened with safety, acknowledging two fatalities in the prior year and three deaths at a Kazakhstan operation "just a month ago." He described zero harm as the company's objective and said Glencore's recordable injury rate now sits below the International Council on Mining and Metals average. The metrics are moving in the right direction; the deaths remain a material fact about the nature of the business.

Emmanuel Ajayi Dansou, representing IndustriALL Global Union, raised concerns about the planned closure of the Cerrejón coal mine in Colombia — currently expected in 2034 — arguing that workers had not been adequately consulted in transition planning. He also cited subcontracting practices and the integration of gender-mainstreaming policies as outstanding issues. Australian union delegates separately asked about pay structures and protections for injured workers. Nagle's response on labour law compliance was direct: "Absolutely no intention to skirt the laws."

Anna Leissing of the non-profit Voices organisation raised a different class of concern: the safety of tailings dams at Mineração Rio do Norte in Brazil, where Glencore holds a 45% stake. Local communities, she said, were worried about the risk of dam failure. Madhavpeddi noted that Glencore publicly discloses information on more than 140 tailings facilities globally and pointed out that MRN is operated by its own management, not directly by Glencore. The response was factually accurate; it did not satisfy the question of what influence a 45% shareholder exercises over dam-safety standards at an operation it does not directly manage.

That gap — between disclosure and accountability — is likely to keep resurfacing at successive meetings, particularly as the company's copper growth plans bring more assets and communities into the frame. The next concrete milestone to watch is first production from the Alumbrera restart, expected in the first half of 2028, and the copper output figure Glencore reports at its half-year update this August.
