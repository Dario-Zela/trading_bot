Xi Jinping to make state visit to Washington this month

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping during Trump's state visit to Beijing in May 2026, ahead of Xi's reciprocal Washington trip this month. — The White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Xi Jinping is set to arrive in Washington around September 24 for a summit with Trump, where trade truce extensions and Iran-sanctions tensions collide with looming US midterms.

Xi Jinping is due to land in Washington around September 24, the first state visit by a Chinese leader to the United States since 2015 and the first ever hosted by Donald Trump. The trip closes a loop that opened in May, when Trump travelled to Beijing and was received with a red-carpet welcome at the Great Hall of the People. Over a state banquet toast that evening, Trump turned to Xi and extended the invitation on the spot: "Tonight, it is my honor to extend an invitation to you, Madam Peng, to visit us at the White House this September 24."

Four months on, the reciprocal visit arrives in a far more combustible environment than the one that produced that toast. A shooting war between the United States, Israel and Iran is still disrupting tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz. Washington has spent the summer sanctioning the Chinese "teapot" refineries that buy the bulk of Iran's oil. And the summit itself falls roughly six weeks before Americans vote in midterm elections that will decide control of Congress — a timeline that has turned an already complicated bilateral relationship into something closer to a high-wire act.

A truce that still needs tending

The immediate business of the summit is mundane by the standards of the relationship: keeping a fragile trade truce from unravelling. The two sides are working from a one-year agreement struck in Busan last October, and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has said Washington and Beijing expect to make "some announcements on agriculture and non-tariff barriers" when the leaders meet.

The agriculture file has become the most closely watched barometer of goodwill. China had already met a commitment to buy 12 million metric tonnes of US soybeans for the 2025 season and is, according to US officials, "on track" to hit an agreed target of 25 million tonnes this year — a package that, combined with other farm goods, would bring purchases to roughly $30 billion. American processors are pushing for something more: relief from a 10 percent Chinese tariff on US agricultural imports that has squeezed margins on both sides of the Pacific. Chinese executives may travel with Xi's delegation, a signal, officials briefed on the planning told the South China Morning Post, that an extension of the trade truce is "almost certain."

That modest, transactional framing is deliberate. Neither government wants a repeat of the tariff shocks of Trump's first term, and both have concluded, in the words of one analysis of the run-up to the summit, that they are locked in a posture closer to mutually assured economic destruction than open conflict — each side holds levers (export controls, rare earths, tariffs) capable of doing real damage, but neither wants to pull them.

Iran's shadow over the talks

What could upend that careful choreography is Iran. China buys more than 80 percent of Iran's oil exports, according to 2025 trade data, making it the single largest financial lifeline keeping Tehran's economy afloat during the war. The Treasury Department has already sanctioned Hengli Petrochemical's Dalian refinery — one of Iran's largest crude customers — along with four other teapot refineries and roughly 40 shipping companies and vessels tied to Iran's so-called shadow fleet. Beijing has retaliated by blocking some of those sanctions from taking effect domestically and has publicly demanded Washington stop what it calls the "abuse" of unilateral sanctions against Chinese firms.

So far, the US has held back from targeting the Chinese banks that clear payments for that oil trade — the step that would do the most economic damage and invite the sharpest retaliation. Whether that restraint survives the summit is an open question. Trump faces his own domestic pressure on the war: his approval rating has slid to around 33 percent as fuel costs and inflation climb, giving him incentive to extract something concrete from Xi on Iran, even as Beijing calculates it has more leverage the longer the war drags on and the more Washington needs Chinese cooperation to keep global energy markets from spiraling further.

Both leaders have found some common ground on paper — agreeing in a recent call that the Strait of Hormuz must stay open and that Iran should not acquire a nuclear weapon — but analysts who track the relationship note that Iran played a smaller role than expected in the Beijing summit in May, and may do so again in Washington, overshadowed by the more tractable trade agenda.

Politics, protocol and what's at stake

The midterm calendar cuts against a breakthrough. Anti-China rhetoric on Capitol Hill tends to intensify, not soften, in the run-up to an election, as candidates in both parties look for a low-cost way to look tough on Beijing, and Trump's recent public accusation that China meddled in the 2020 election — which Beijing has flatly denied — has already added friction ahead of Xi's arrival. Chinese officials, for their part, are said to believe they can extract more from Washington closer to the vote, betting that Trump will want a marquee deal to announce as Election Day nears rather than settling for less now.

Even the choreography carries risk. Trump received a state banquet and red-carpet treatment in Beijing and promised Xi's Washington visit would be "reciprocal"; how the White House stages the return leg — full state visit trappings versus a scaled-down working meeting — will be read in Beijing as a signal of respect or its absence, in a relationship where protocol often substitutes for substance. The summit is also being described by regional diplomats as a stabilizing checkpoint ahead of two more encounters already on the calendar: the APEC summit in China in November and the G20 in Florida in December. A visit that goes badly in Washington would complicate both.

For markets, the summit is a binary-risk event layered onto an already jumpy macro backdrop — oil prices elevated by the Iran war, the Federal Reserve weighing its own rate path, and a US election that will shape trade policy for years regardless of what is signed this month. A calm, transactional outcome — an extended truce, an agriculture announcement, no new tariff escalation — would remove one source of volatility. A rupture over sanctions enforcement or a protocol slight would do the opposite, and do it in a week when investors already have plenty else to price.

The next concrete marker is the date itself: September 24. Whether Xi's delegation includes the business leaders now being discussed, and whether Washington and Beijing emerge with an announcement on tariffs and farm purchases, will be the first real test of whether the fragile calm from Beijing in May can survive contact with an American election season.
