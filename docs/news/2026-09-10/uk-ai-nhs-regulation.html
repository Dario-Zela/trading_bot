UK watchdog urges new laws for AI in the NHS

A UK hospital: NHS trusts are already using AI scribes and triage tools, prompting calls for new regulation. — Rod Ward / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A UK commission says AI that keeps learning after approval breaks static device-licensing rules, and is urging Parliament to legislate rather than patch the system.

Britain's medicines regulator has concluded that the rulebook built for hip replacements and stethoscopes cannot cope with software that keeps learning after it leaves the factory — and is asking Parliament to write a new one.

The verdict comes from the National Commission on the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, an independent panel convened last year to advise the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA). In a report published this week after consulting more than 12,000 patients, clinicians and developers, the commission set out 44 recommendations and concluded that the existing medical-device framework needs primary legislation, not another round of guidance notes.

"The medical devices regulatory framework predominantly dates from a period where we were thinking about things like hip replacements and knee replacements, or smaller things like stethoscopes and plasters," MHRA chief executive Lawrence Tallon told the BBC. Static rules, he said, still work reasonably well for simple AI tools trained once to spot a known pattern on a scan. They break down for the more complex systems now entering clinics. "Unlike most of the medical products we're used to regulating, these products continue to change after the point of authorisation — as new data gets fed in, they learn, they adapt, they drift."

Why the old rules don't fit

UK and EU device law, like its US counterpart, is built around a single approval moment: a company submits evidence, a regulator signs off, and the product is fixed at that specification until someone applies to change it. A continuously updating algorithm — one that retrains on fresh clinical data, or adjusts its own weightings as it encounters new cases — never stops changing, which makes the concept of a fixed, approved version increasingly meaningless.

The commission's answer is a staged model rather than a one-off stamp of approval. Its recommendation 14 proposes what MHRA officials have taken to calling an "L-plate" system: new AI models would launch under close supervision and real-world monitoring, graduating to full authorisation only once they demonstrate they are safe and effective in routine use — and losing that status automatically if performance slips. Other recommendations would give regulators explicit power to withdraw a product that malfunctions or "drifts" away from its validated behaviour, hand developers financial penalties for non-compliance, and guarantee patients the right to be told when AI has played a role in their care.

Already in the room

The urgency is less about the future than the present. AI scribes — ambient listening tools that transcribe a consultation and draft clinical notes for a doctor to check — are already used by roughly 40% of GPs in England, according to the commission's own figures. Other tools are helping flag symptoms on scans or assist with administrative triage, generally under clinician oversight rather than making decisions unsupervised.

That rollout has outpaced public comfort. A poll cited alongside the report found 69% of patients want a human professional to check AI-generated notes before they go in the record, and 58% said they would feel better if doctors explicitly told them an AI tool was involved. Professor Henrietta Hughes, the patient safety commissioner and the commission's deputy chair, framed it as a matter of choice as much as safety: "Some say, 'I don't want to talk to a robot,' and that is also fine." The commission's chair, Professor Alastair Denniston, was more bullish about the technology itself, comparing its potential to antibiotics and MRI scanning — while stressing that public trust depends on the guardrails being visible, not assumed.

What happens next

Some of the changes — tighter monitoring protocols, clearer disclosure rules — the MHRA can pursue through existing regulatory powers. But the commission is explicit that the core shift, from static device approval to continuous, adaptive oversight with statutory teeth, needs an act of Parliament. The MHRA has said it intends to begin operationalising a revised framework this year, with legislative elements pencilled in for 2027 where they require lawmakers' sign-off — a timeline that assumes parliamentary time is found for a health-tech bill amid a crowded legislative calendar. Whether that slot materialises, and how closely any eventual statute tracks the commission's 44 recommendations, is the thing to watch as the government prepares its formal response.
