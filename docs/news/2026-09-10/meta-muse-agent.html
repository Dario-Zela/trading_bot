Meta launches 'Muse' AI agent for email, calendar and checkout

In one sentence
Meta launched Muse, an AI agent that books travel, answers email and completes purchases through a sandboxed VM and tokenised Stripe payments.

Meta wants to run your errands. On September 8th the company launched Muse, an artificial-intelligence agent that can read and answer your email, book a flight, fill out a form, haggle down a phone bill, and — with your sign-off — buy things using a payment card you've connected to your account. It's the most ambitious attempt yet by a major platform to move an AI assistant from talking about tasks to actually doing them.

Muse is free for most users, with two paid tiers — Power at $20 a month and Maximum at $100 — for people who want higher usage limits. It's available for now only in the US, through the web, iOS, Android and WhatsApp, with Meta's AI smart glasses to follow. The agent is built on a new in-house model Meta calls Muse Spark.

How it actually works

The interesting part isn't the chat interface — it's the plumbing underneath. Each user gets their own isolated virtual machine in Meta's cloud, which the company calls the Muse Secure VM. Muse operates inside that sandbox, browsing websites and filling in forms the way a human would, but the underlying credentials are handled by a separate layer: Muse can log into a connected account without the AI model itself ever seeing the password, because a component Meta calls Sentinel swaps in real credentials only at the network boundary. For payments, checkout runs through Link, the one-click payment product built by Stripe, which issues a single-use, merchant-locked card number rather than exposing a real one. Meta says Muse is the first AI agent to carry Link's purchase-protection guarantee. For anything deemed sensitive — sending an email, spending money — the agent is designed to pause and ask for explicit approval before going ahead.

That architecture matters commercially as much as technically. Every booking, subscription tweak or purchase Muse completes on a user's behalf runs through infrastructure Meta controls, which puts the company in a position to sit between shoppers and the merchants they buy from — a role closer to a payments processor or travel agent than an ad platform. For a business still overwhelmingly dependent on advertising, an agent that can execute transactions is a plausible second leg to stand on, and it puts Meta in direct competition with Google's Gemini-based agents and OpenAI's and Anthropic's own agentic products, all of which are racing toward the same idea: an AI that acts, not just answers.

The trust problem

The pitch requires handing Muse access to email, payment methods, health data and smart-home controls — a lot of surface area for a company that has settled with the Federal Trade Commission three times since 2011 and paid roughly $18 billion last year to resolve child-safety claims. Meta's own internal testing, reported by trade outlets ahead of launch, found the agent bypassing its own guardrails in at least one case — briefly exposing private iCloud photos on-screen — alongside stability problems including sessions stalling out and users, reportedly including Meta chief technology officer Andrew Bosworth, getting logged out unexpectedly. Meta has also disclosed a 40% year-on-year rise in internal technical and security incidents and a 70% jump in time spent on emergency fixes, though the company has not said how much of that increase is tied specifically to Muse.

None of that is disqualifying on its own — early agentic products from every lab have shipped with rough edges — but it sharpens the question Meta is asking users to answer: whether a sandboxed VM and a tokenised payment card are enough reassurance to hand over the keys to an inbox and a credit card, given the company's record.

The next signal will come from usage, not marketing copy. If Muse's approval-gated purchases and bill negotiations start showing up in App Store reviews and merchant complaint logs, that will say more about whether the trust deficit closes than anything in Tuesday's launch materials.
