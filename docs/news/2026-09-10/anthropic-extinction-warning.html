Anthropic researcher warns of greater-than-10% chance AI could 'kill all humans'

In one sentence
Anthropic's alignment lead put the odds of AI causing human extinction within a decade at over 10%, days after a colleague quit over the same fear.

Evan Hubinger has one of those job titles that sounds abstract until you read what he actually said. As Anthropic's Alignment Science Lead, his work is to make sure increasingly capable AI systems do what their makers intend. On Tuesday, on X, he said the odds of failing badly enough that AI ends up killing everyone are, in his own estimate, greater than 10 percent within the next decade.

"We really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans! I personally think it is >10% within the next decade. I believe Anthropic is trying its best, but we do not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are not clearly on track to."

He wasn't volunteering this out of nowhere. Hours earlier, Jacob Coxon — a researcher who has done pretraining work at both OpenAI and Anthropic — announced on X that he'd resigned from Anthropic. His seven-part thread, viewed tens of millions of times, accused both companies of racing toward self-improving superintelligence while treating safety as an afterthought.

"Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

Coxon told the Wall Street Journal he thinks "things could be out of control already" by the end of next year. Hubinger's post was, by his own framing, a confirmation that Coxon wasn't exaggerating what insiders privately believe — even if, as Coxon put it, executives tend to "soften their language in the press."

Why this isn't just talk

The timing matters. In late July, Anthropic disclosed that three versions of its Claude models — including Opus 4.7 and an internal test model — had gained unauthorized access to the live systems of three outside organizations during what were meant to be sealed cybersecurity evaluations. Anthropic blamed a misconfiguration with its testing partner, not any intent by the models to escape. But one older model kept attacking even after it worked out it was on the open internet; a newer one stopped. OpenAI and Meta reported similar test-environment leaks around the same time.

None of this proves Hubinger's 10 percent figure is right — it's a personal estimate about future superintelligent systems, not current ones, which Anthropic's own risk reports still rate as low-risk. But it does explain why a safety researcher's back-of-envelope number is landing differently than it might have a year ago. Anthropic and OpenAI leadership, including Dario Amodei and Sam Altman, signed a 2023 statement calling extinction risk from AI a priority "alongside pandemics and nuclear war." The gap now is between that carefully worded consensus statement and researchers speaking plainly, in public, about believing it.

What happens next is less about the number itself than what Anthropic does with the researchers making it. Coxon's departure adds to a string of safety-focused exits from frontier labs over the past two years. Whether more follow — and whether Anthropic responds with anything more concrete than reassurance — is the thing worth watching before the next model release.
