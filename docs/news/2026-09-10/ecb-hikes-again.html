ECB set to hike rates again as Iran war fuels inflation

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where the Governing Council voted to raise the deposit rate to 2.5%. — Daniel Vorndran (DXR) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
The ECB hiked its deposit rate 25bp to 2.5%, its second increase since the Iran war, even as eurozone inflation sits at 3.3% and growth forecasts get cut.

The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate by a quarter point to 2.5% on Thursday, its second increase this year and a clear signal that policymakers in Frankfurt are prepared to keep tightening even as the war between the United States, Israel and Iran drags on the eurozone economy.

The move was almost fully priced in — markets had assigned it close to even odds of certainty for weeks — but it still marks a notable moment in the ECB's post-pandemic history. Christine Lagarde's Governing Council has now raised rates twice in three months after nearly three years of holding steady or cutting, reversing course as energy costs tied to the conflict push inflation further from target.

A hike delayed, then delivered

The path here has not been a straight line. The ECB lifted its deposit rate from 2% to 2.25% in June, its first increase since 2023, after the reignited Hormuz-area conflict sent Brent crude and European gas benchmarks sharply higher. In July, with no fresh economic projections due and Lagarde's council wary of overreacting to a fast-moving war, the bank held rates unchanged and repeated its now-familiar refrain: decisions would be made "meeting by meeting," with no pre-commitment to a particular path.

That caution gave way on Thursday. Brent crude broke back above $100 a barrel for the first time since May after strikes on energy infrastructure widened the conflict's economic reach, and eurozone headline inflation hit 3.3% in August — well above the ECB's 2% target, though still short of the peaks seen during the 2022 energy shock. Core inflation, which strips out volatile food and energy prices, eased slightly to 2.4%, evidence that the immediate pressure is concentrated in energy rather than broadening wildly through wages and services — for now.

Growth versus prices

The dilemma facing Lagarde's council is the classic stagflationary bind: an external shock is pushing prices higher while simultaneously threatening the growth that would normally argue for lower rates. The IMF has cut its 2026 eurozone growth forecast to 1.1%, down from an earlier 1.4% estimate, citing the war as the principal drag, with Germany and Italy among the member states revising their own outlooks downward. Lagarde described the difficulty in April in stark terms, noting that the "stop-start nature of the conflict" makes it exceptionally hard to gauge how deep or lasting the economic consequences will be.

Despite that drag, the eurozone economy has held up better than many economists expected earlier this year, which analysts say gave the Governing Council room to prioritise price stability over growth support at this meeting. The ECB is expected to nudge up its own growth projections for 2026 alongside Thursday's decision, while likely pushing back its forecast for when inflation returns to target — previously pencilled in for summer 2027.

What the hike signals — and what comes next

The more important message from Thursday may be less about the 25 basis points themselves than about what they signal for the ECB's appetite to keep going. Markets are pricing in the possibility of one further hike before year-end, with one or two additional moves conceivable in 2027 if the conflict keeps energy prices elevated. Several economists, however, argue Thursday's increase could be the last for a while, on the view that financing conditions across the currency bloc are already tight enough to start doing the disinflationary work on their own.

That divide will be tested at the ECB's next policy meeting, when fresh staff projections should clarify how much of the energy shock the bank expects to persist into 2027. Until then, the relevant variable is not in Frankfurt but in the Gulf: further disruption to Hormuz-area shipping or energy infrastructure would keep the case for tightening alive, while any de-escalation would hand Lagarde's council the disinflation it needs to plausibly call this hike the last one.
