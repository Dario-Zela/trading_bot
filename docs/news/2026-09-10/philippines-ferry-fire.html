BEYOND THE TAPE · DISASTER · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-10 · 3 min read

Philippines ferry fire: five dead, dozens missing off Palawan

A Philippine Coast Guard vessel; PCG ships have led the search off Coron since the June Aster caught fire. — Philippine Coast Guard / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A Manila-to-Coron ferry carrying 134 people caught fire off Palawan; five are confirmed dead and 87 remain missing as the search continues.

The MV June Aster left Manila on Wednesday evening bound for Coron, the limestone-ringed gateway to Palawan's diving country, with 134 people aboard. It never made it. Around 6:45pm local time, a fire broke out on the passenger ferry roughly a mile and a quarter off Barangay Marcilla, near Coron, and by Thursday the Philippine Coast Guard was reporting five confirmed dead and 87 people still unaccounted for.

Forty-two people — 38 passengers and four crew — have been pulled from the water so far, out of 117 passengers and 17 crew who were on the manifest. Coast Guard spokesperson Noemie Cayabyab cautioned that the numbers were "live and could change as search and rescue operations continued," the kind of hedge that tends to accompany disasters where the full toll is still being assembled boat by boat.

The ferry's distress call initially placed it about four nautical miles off Turda village; by the time rescue vessels reached the scene, strong currents had dragged the burning hull more than seven and a half nautical miles away, complicating an already difficult operation in rough seas. Coast Guard cutters, local fishing boats and municipal responders have been working through the night and into Thursday, though officials have not said what sparked the fire or whether the vessel's safety equipment — life vests, extinguishers, evacuation routes — functioned as required.

A familiar pattern

That question sits atop a long, uncomfortable history. The Philippines' inter-island ferry network moves millions of people a year through waters prone to typhoons and sudden squalls, using a fleet that skews old and unevenly maintained. The regulator, the Maritime Industry Authority, inspects vessels for seaworthiness roughly once annually — a cadence safety advocates have long argued is too thin to catch problems with fire suppression systems or overcrowding between checks. A ferry sinking in the country's south earlier this year, which killed dozens, was later blamed in part on overloading and vehicles that were never weighed before boarding, despite that being a legal requirement.

The June Aster's operator, Atienza Interisland Ferries, has not detailed the vessel's inspection history or the results of any onboard fire drills. The Coast Guard says it is coordinating with the company and maritime authorities as the search continues, but a formal cause won't emerge until investigators can board the wreck.

For now, the story is a human one rather than a market one: families gathered at Manila and Coron ports waiting for names, and a death toll that officials expect to climb once the missing list is reconciled against final headcounts. The next marker is whether MARINA orders a fleet-wide safety review — the kind of regulatory response that followed past disasters — and whether it holds this time.
