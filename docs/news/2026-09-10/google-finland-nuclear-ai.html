Google commits €13bn to Finnish AI infrastructure, backs nuclear plant extension

Fortum's Loviisa nuclear plant, seen from the air, supplies about 10% of Finland's electricity and is now central to Google's Nordic AI buildout. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Google locked in a 22-year Finnish nuclear power deal alongside €13bn in data-centre spending, showing energy supply — not capital — now limits AI infrastructure growth.

Google will spend roughly €13bn building and expanding data centres across four Finnish municipalities over the next two years — its largest single infrastructure commitment in Europe. But the more telling number in Tuesday's announcement wasn't the capital figure. It was 22, as in years: the length of the power purchase agreement Google signed with Finnish utility Fortum to keep the Loviisa nuclear plant running into 2050.

In an industry where capital has become almost boringly abundant — Alphabet alone is guiding to $195bn–$205bn in group-wide capital expenditure this year — a two-decade wager on a specific nuclear plant's output says more about what's actually scarce than the headline euro figure does. Money can be raised in a quarter. Grid capacity cannot.

The mechanics of the deal

The agreement covers Fortum's Loviisa plant, two Soviet-era VVER-440 reactors on Finland's southern coast that have been running since 1977 and 1981 and currently supply about 10% of the country's electricity. Fortum's operating licence for the plant already runs to 2050, but keeping ageing reactors alive for another quarter-century requires money — Fortum has flagged roughly €1bn of investment, with some €700m of that still awaiting final sign-off.

Google's PPA supplies the revenue certainty to unlock it. Under the terms, Google will take up to 50% of Loviisa's output from 2030 through 2049, with a smaller volume starting in 2028 tied to a 38-megawatt turbine uprate already under way. Fortum CEO Markus Rauramo called it a foundation for "the continued development and reliable operation of our Loviisa power plant for decades to come" — language that reads less like a marketing quote and more like a utility relieved to have found a counterparty willing to underwrite a multi-decade capital programme in a market where power-price forecasting that far out is close to guesswork.

Markets took the point immediately: Fortum shares rose as much as 13.6% over two sessions following the announcement, with analysts citing an estimated 1.4 percentage-point lift to the company's return on net assets from the contracted volumes alone.

Why energy, not money, is the constraint

The Loviisa deal sits inside a broader package. Google's €13bn will fund at least three new data-centre sites — in Kajaani, Muhos and Vaala — plus an expansion of its existing Hamina facility, a converted paper mill that has anchored Google's Nordic operations since 2009. Ruth Porat, Alphabet's president and chief investment officer, framed the spending as pairing infrastructure growth with "new energy capacity, grid enhancements, and energy affordability initiatives" — three phrases that, taken together, describe a company negotiating for electrons as carefully as it negotiates for chips.

That shift in emphasis fits a pattern across the industry. Microsoft is funding the restart of the Three Mile Island reactor in Pennsylvania. Amazon has put more than $20bn into converting the Susquehanna nuclear plant's site into an AI campus. Meta has issued a request for proposals covering up to 4 gigawatts of new nuclear capacity. Across the four largest hyperscalers, more than a dozen separate nuclear agreements now total close to 10 gigawatts of contracted capacity — and Goldman Sachs estimates the industry will need 85–90 gigawatts of new nuclear supply by 2030 to meet data-centre demand growth, against well under 10% of that likely to be built in time.

The arithmetic explains the urgency. Google's own disclosures show electricity demand from its operations rose 37% last year. Global data-centre power consumption is on a path to roughly triple by 2035. GPUs can be ordered, financed and delivered in quarters; a nuclear life-extension, a new wind farm, or a grid interconnection takes years, sometimes a decade, to permit and build. Compute has effectively become a commodity that money can buy quickly. Reliable, carbon-light, baseload power has not.

Finland's pitch, and the read for investors

Finland's appeal to Google is a mix of cold climate — useful for cooling — cheap and largely nuclear-and-wind-based electricity, and a EU member state's regulatory predictability. Google is also signing new onshore wind agreements with developer Valorem, lifting its total contracted Finnish renewable capacity to 629 megawatts, and adding a 94-megawatt battery system near the Kajaani site due online in late 2027. Google projects the two-year build will add €3.6bn annually to Finnish GDP and support more than 37,000 jobs, about 16,000 of them in construction.

For traders parsing the announcement, the signal runs two ways. It reinforces the case for European utilities and grid operators with spare low-carbon capacity — Fortum's stock move is the clearest evidence yet that a hyperscaler PPA can reprice a nuclear asset almost overnight. And it argues for treating tech capex forecasts less as a single number and more as two separate curves: one for compute, still rising steeply and still largely a function of chip supply and balance-sheet capacity; another for power, increasingly the binding constraint that will determine which regions actually capture the next wave of AI infrastructure spending, and which utilities get to charge for the privilege of enabling it.

What's next

Construction at the Finnish sites begins in 2027, with the bulk of the €13bn committed through 2028. Alphabet's next earnings call will be the first opportunity to see whether the Loviisa-style approach — locking in decades of power alongside the data centre itself — becomes the template for its other European sites, several of which face similar grid-capacity constraints in Germany, the Netherlands and Ireland. Fortum, meanwhile, still needs to finalise investment decisions on roughly €700m of the Loviisa life-extension programme; how quickly that clears will be the next test of whether the Google contract actually de-risks the plant's economics or merely improves the odds.
