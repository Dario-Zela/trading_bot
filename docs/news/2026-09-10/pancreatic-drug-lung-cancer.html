Pancreatic-cancer drug shows early promise against lung cancer

A 3D CT volume rendering showing a tumor in the left lung — the kind of advanced non-small-cell lung cancer targeted in the trial. — Lange123, German Wikipedia / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Revolution Medicines' pancreatic-cancer pill daraxonrasib shrank tumors in a third of advanced lung-cancer patients — but its $44bn stock barely moved, having priced the news in already.

A pill approved three weeks ago to treat pancreatic cancer is now showing signs it could work against a far bigger target: lung cancer, the world's leading cause of cancer death. In results published last week in the New England Journal of Medicine, the drug shrank tumors in roughly a third of patients with advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) whose disease had already progressed on chemotherapy and immunotherapy.[^1]

The drug, daraxonrasib, is made by Revolution Medicines, a Redwood City, California biotech that won FDA approval for it on August 26th under the brand name Rasonque, for metastatic pancreatic cancer that has failed prior treatment.[^2] It works by blocking RAS proteins — a family of cellular switches that, when mutated, drive roughly 30% of NSCLC cases and are notoriously difficult to target with drugs.[^1] Crucially, the trial excluded the one RAS mutation, G12C, already addressed by rival drugs from Amgen and Bristol Myers Squibb; daraxonrasib is aimed at the mutations those medicines don't touch.

The Phase 1/2 trial, run under the name RMC-6236-001, enrolled 136 previously treated NSCLC patients on a once-daily pill. Among 38 patients who had not yet had docetaxel — standard second-line chemotherapy, which typically shrinks tumors in only 9–14% of similar patients — the response rate rose to 42%, with a disease-control rate of 89% and median progression-free survival of 8.3 months.[^3] Side effects were common: about a quarter of patients on the higher dose range had a grade-3 treatment-related event, mostly rash or diarrhea, though none were graded 4 or 5.[^3]

A number the market had already priced in

This is the part of the story that gets lost in the medical-journal framing: Revolution Medicines is not an obscure name to traders. Its stock had already climbed 166% in 2026 before the pancreatic-cancer approval, on the strength of Phase 3 data presented at oncology conferences earlier in the year, pushing its market capitalization to roughly $44 billion.[^4] When the NEJM lung-cancer data landed on September 2nd, shares moved only about 2.6% — a muted reaction compared with the double-digit swings that have followed past clinical readouts from the company, evidence the lung-cancer opportunity was largely anticipated rather than a surprise.[^4]

Oncologists outside the company have been more circumspect than investors. UNC pharmacologist Channing Der called the results "a significant improvement over docetaxel" but said larger comparative studies are needed to confirm patients live longer, not just that scans look better.[^5] That larger test is already underway: Revolution Medicines is running a randomized Phase 3 trial, RASolve 301, pitting daraxonrasib directly against docetaxel in previously treated RAS-mutant NSCLC, with initial data expected in 2027 — the event that will determine whether this becomes a second approved indication or a promising signal that didn't hold up.[^3]
