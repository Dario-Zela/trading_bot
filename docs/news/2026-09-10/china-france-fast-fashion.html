China threatens France with retaliation over Shein and Temu levy

Shein packaging bearing the retailer's branding — the kind of low-cost, high-volume shipment now facing France's per-item levy. — Danielll10 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China threatened 'necessary measures' against France after Paris imposed per-item levies of up to €20 on Shein and Temu products.

France's new anti-fast-fashion law is a week old. China's response arrived almost as fast: stop it, or face consequences left deliberately unspecified.

The law, which took effect on 1 September, imposes a sliding per-item levy on ultra-fast fashion products — the cheap, high-turnover clothing that has made Shein and Temu into two of the most-downloaded shopping apps in France. Underwear is fined 50 cents; a T-shirt €2; jeans €9; a jacket €12. The scale is designed to climb: by 2030 the top levy reaches €20 per item, capped at half the item's pre-tax price. The two criteria used to define "ultra-fast fashion" — how much volume a retailer pushes through the market, and how the cost of repairing a garment compares with the cost of just buying a new one — were written with Shein and Temu specifically in mind, though AliExpress is caught as well.

Beijing did not wait long to object. On 3 September, commerce ministry spokesperson Huang Ling told reporters China was "strongly dissatisfied" and urged France to "immediately halt the implementation of the anti-ultra-fast-fashion law." The line that will matter more to markets than to diplomats came next: "If France insists on proceeding, China will take necessary measures to safeguard the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese enterprises," Huang said, adding that "France will bear all consequences arising from this." She did not say what those measures would be.

The WTO argument

China's formal objection is that the levy is discriminatory dressed up as environmental policy — a law that singles out foreign platforms under the banner of sustainability while leaving comparable European retailers largely untouched, in violation of the World Trade Organization's non-discrimination principles. It is not a frivolous claim on its face: the levy schedule was built around the business model Shein and Temu run — thousands of new items a week, priced too low to be worth mending — rather than around a fabric type or a production process that any retailer might use. Paris has not offered a WTO-facing defense yet, and neither the French government nor the European Commission has responded publicly to Huang's remarks.

The French law also does not stand alone. It follows an EU-wide agreement, reached in the Council in 2026, to impose a flat €3 customs duty on parcels under €150 entering the bloc from outside — a stopgap replacing the old duty-free threshold that Shein and Temu's direct-from-factory shipping model was built to exploit. That measure takes effect from July 2028 as a fixed fee before reverting to ordinary customs duties. France's per-item levy stacks on top of it, and is the more aggressive instrument of the two because it is calibrated by garment type rather than parcel value.

Why the vagueness is the point

"Necessary measures" is a stock phrase in Chinese trade diplomacy, deployed before it is decided what the measures actually are. It has preceded, in other disputes, everything from anti-dumping probes to informal customs slowdowns to outright import bans, and its function is to signal displeasure while preserving room to calibrate the response — or drop it — depending on how the target country reacts. For a market watching French-Chinese trade flows, the near-term risk is not a specific tariff line but the uncertainty itself: French exporters with China exposure, from luxury goods to agri-food, now sit under a threat with no defined scope or timeline.

The bigger issue is precedent. If an environmental levy aimed at platform business models can be recast by its target as covert protectionism, the same argument becomes available to any government wanting to tax imports under a health, safety or sustainability rationale rather than a straightforward tariff — the kind of framing WTO rules treat far more permissively. Brussels has watched the same dynamic play out over EU carbon border adjustments and Chinese electric-vehicle tariffs; a France-specific skirmish over t-shirt levies is a smaller stage for the same fight over who gets to define "environmental policy" versus "trade barrier."

The next marker is whether Beijing moves from language to a named target — an anti-dumping probe, a customs delay, a sector singled out for scrutiny — which is roughly the sequence its past disputes with the EU over solar panels and brandy have followed. Until then, this stays a war of statements, not tariffs.
