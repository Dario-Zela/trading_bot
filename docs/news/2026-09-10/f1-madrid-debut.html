F1 heads to Madrid for historic Madring circuit debut

The 5.416km, 22-turn Madring layout, built around Madrid's IFEMA exhibition centre in Barajas. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
F1 returns to Madrid for the first time since 1981 this weekend, backed by an €83m circuit and an unaudited promise of €450m a year in economic impact.

Formula 1 has not raced in the Spanish capital since Gilles Villeneuve nursed a fragile Ferrari to victory at the Jarama circuit in June 1981. That drought ends this weekend, when the sport's twenty cars line up on a brand-new 5.416km, 22-turn street-and-permanent hybrid track built around the IFEMA exhibition centre in Barajas. Practice starts Friday, the race is Sunday, and roughly 90,000 tickets have already gone for race day alone — a sellout that owes as much to novelty as to any driver on the grid.

The layout itself is the pitch. Twelve of its corners are conventional street-circuit tarmac threaded between exhibition halls; the rest opens onto a purpose-built, banked section anchored by Turn 12, a corner nicknamed La Monumental that organisers compare to Indianapolis's old oval banking. Carlos Sainz, the Madrid-born Williams driver serving as the event's ambassador, has called it a track that could become "the best circuit in the world" — the kind of quote a home race is built to generate, and worth discounting accordingly.

What the numbers actually say

Strip away the driver enthusiasm and the case for Madring is a municipal-finance story. Contractors Acciona and Eiffage won the build contract in April 2025 for €83.2 million, undercutting an original €111 million tender. IFEMA, the state-linked exhibition operator bankrolling the project, is putting in €47.5 million of its own money and has told Spanish media it does not expect to recover that outlay for thirteen years. In exchange, race organisers are reportedly paying Formula One around €48 million a year to host — nearly double what Barcelona's Circuit de Catalunya paid for the same slot on the calendar, which Madrid has now taken through 2035.

The promised return is a claimed €450 million in annual economic activity and more than 8,200 direct and indirect jobs, figures that originate with the organisers and Madrid's regional government rather than independent audit. Host cities have a long, well-documented habit of overstating mega-event windfalls before the fact and underdelivering after it; nothing here yet distinguishes Madring from that pattern. The number worth tracking is hotel occupancy and hospitality tax receipts for greater Madrid once the weekend closes — that is the test IFEMA's thirteen-year payback assumption actually has to pass.

Cracks before the chequered flag

The run-in has not been clean. Roughly 300 metres of cable were stolen from the circuit's tunnel section after an August Formula 3 test, prompting a police investigation. Separately, a residents' group called Stop F1 Madrid has taken legal action over noise, after F3 running measured an average of 102.7 decibels against a 55-decibel residential limit — organisers are installing acoustic panelling before Sunday, with the effect untested. The FIA dismissed earlier delay concerns in May, and more than 90% of the asphalt was reportedly down by the time F3 cars turned their first laps.

None of that will stop the race happening. What it does is underline the gap between this weekend's coverage — a historic return, a hyped new track, a hometown driver playing salesman — and the harder question of whether an €83 million circuit and a public-money bet on tourism actually pays for itself. That verdict won't arrive Sunday night. It arrives, if it arrives at all, somewhere in the thirteen years IFEMA has given itself to find out.
