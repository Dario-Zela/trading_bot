BoE's Bailey faces MPs as markets price hikes, not cuts

Andrew Bailey, Governor of the Bank of England, testified to the Treasury Committee on the BoE's July Monetary Policy Report. — Office of the U.S. Ambassador to the UK / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bailey told MPs a BoE hike isn't preordained, but markets are pricing one by December anyway as inflation keeps outrunning the Bank's script.

Andrew Bailey spent Tuesday telling MPs what the Bank of England's rate-setters are not doing, which is following a script. Testifying to the Treasury Committee on the July Monetary Policy Report, the Governor pushed back on the idea that a rate hike is now simply a matter of timing. "What I want to dispel is the idea that we've really got a secret plan, we know where we're going to go to and it's unconditional," he told MPs.

Markets are not entirely convinced. Sterling swap pricing now points to one quarter-point rise in Bank Rate by the end of 2026, with a further two hikes priced in through 2027 — a sharp reversal from the cutting cycle traders had pencilled in as recently as the spring. Bailey, characteristically, tried to talk the curve down rather than confirm it, telling the committee that what markets are pricing is "essentially a risk premium" rather than a Bank commitment.

In one sentence: Bailey told MPs a rate hike isn't a done deal, but traders are pricing one in by December anyway — and his testimony did nothing to change their minds.

A committee that's already split

The MPC held Bank Rate at 3.75% on 29 July, but the vote — 6-3 — undersells how much ground has shifted. In June the hold carried 7-2; a month later, Catherine Mann crossed over to join Megan Greene and chief economist Huw Pill in voting for an immediate move to 4.00%. That is three dissents in favour of tightening from a committee that was cutting rates as recently as last year.

The dissenters and the majority are not disagreeing about the direction of travel so much as its urgency. Deputy Governor Dave Ramsden told the committee that domestically generated inflation looks "relatively benign," the case for patience. External member Alan Taylor framed holding rates restrictive as "insurance" against risks arriving from outside the UK rather than a signal of intent to tighten further. Greene, by contrast, warned that a prolonged run of oil-price shocks risks doing more lasting damage — bedding inflation expectations in rather than passing through as a one-off spike.

Inflation isn't cooperating

That argument has data behind it. UK CPI ran at 2.9% in July, up from 2.6% in June, and the next print — due 16 September, a day before the MPC's own decision — will show whether August extended the climb. Energy markets are doing the MPC no favours: Bailey pointed to events in the Middle East as a reason the near-term inflation path is unusually hard to call, a nod to the same oil-price volatility rattling markets after strikes on US assets in the region pushed crude above $100 a barrel.

Investors see only a slim chance the Bank actually moves at its 17 September meeting. The December pricing is the more telling signal: it says traders expect the current run of upside inflation surprises to force the MPC's hand before year-end, testimony or no testimony.

What Bailey's testimony didn't do

The point of Tuesday's session was, nominally, to explain the July report. What it demonstrated instead is that the Bank's communication is no longer the thing setting the market's expectations — the data is. Bailey's insistence that no hike is preordained landed alongside a swap curve that has already made up its mind, and gilt and sterling desks treated the testimony as confirmation of a hawkish drift rather than a reason to unwind it.

That matters beyond the UK. The Bank joins a widening list of central banks facing the same test — the Fed's odds of a September hike have firmed past 50% since Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks, and the ECB is contending with its own Middle East-driven inflation pressure. For the BoE specifically, the next checkpoints are close together: the August CPI reading on 16 September, the rate decision the following day, and the minutes that will show whether the 6-3 split has narrowed further.

What's next

Watch the 16 September CPI print for whether inflation extends its climb past 2.9%, and the MPC decision on 17 September for any change in the vote split. A move to 5-4 or narrower would all but confirm the market's December call.
