Google patches Chrome's seventh actively-exploited zero-day of 2026

Google's current Chrome logo, adopted in 2022. — Google / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Google patched Chrome's seventh actively exploited zero-day of 2026, a V8 memory bug, just six days after fixing another.

Google shipped an emergency fix for Chrome this week after learning that criminals were already using a previously unknown flaw to break into the browser — the seventh time this has happened in 2026, a pace that puts Chrome on track for its worst year yet for in-the-wild attacks.

The bug, catalogued as CVE-2026-87491, sits in V8, the engine that runs JavaScript inside Chrome. It's an "out-of-bounds write" — a class of memory bug where a program is tricked into writing data past the boundary of the space it's supposed to use, which an attacker can exploit to run their own code. Google's advisory describes it plainly: a specially crafted web page could let a remote attacker "execute arbitrary code inside the sandbox." The sandbox is the containment layer meant to stop a compromised tab from touching the rest of the machine — significant, but not a guarantee the intrusion stops there, especially when chained with a second flaw to escape it.

The flaw was reported on August 6 by Jihyeon Jeong, a researcher at Compsec Lab, Seoul National University, who was paid a $2,500 bounty. Google pushed the fix to the stable channel within roughly a month of the report, landing in Chrome 153 (version 153.0.8010.36 on Windows, macOS and Linux) released September 9. The update patches 230 vulnerabilities in total, five of them rated critical, though this was the one already being used against real targets.

Seven and counting

What makes this notice worth reading past the headline number is what came just before it. Google fixed a different actively exploited V8 bug, CVE-2026-85046 — a type-confusion flaw rated 8.8 out of 10 on the CVSS severity scale — only six days earlier, on September 3. That one earned a federal patching deadline of September 18 after the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added it to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. Four of this year's seven zero-days, including both of September's, trace back to V8 specifically, rather than to Chrome's rendering engine or its network stack.

That clustering matters. V8 is one of the most heavily audited pieces of open-source code in existence, and it takes real technical sophistication to find bugs in it faster than Google's own engineers can. The recurrence suggests dedicated groups — likely well-resourced, whether state-linked or commercial exploit brokers — have made V8 a standing target rather than a one-off score, treating each patch as a puzzle to solve for the next one.

For anyone whose trading access, credentials or session tokens run through a browser tab — which is most people, most of the time — the practical exposure is the gap between when an exploit starts circulating and when a machine actually installs the patch. Chrome's silent auto-update usually closes that gap within days, but it still requires the browser to be relaunched to take effect, and enterprise fleets on managed update schedules can lag further behind.

Google has withheld technical detail on the bug, as is standard practice, until update adoption is further along. The next test of whether this is a genuine escalation or a busy year will come with Chrome's next scheduled release, expected in early October — and with whatever number comes after seven.
