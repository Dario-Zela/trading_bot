AstraZeneca posts trial wins in lung cancer and COPD

In one sentence
AstraZeneca posted Phase III wins in two separate lung diseases this week — survival gains with Amgen in small cell lung cancer, and sharply fewer flare-ups in COPD.

AstraZeneca had a good week in respiratory and thoracic medicine, and it had it twice over. On September 8th, at the European Respiratory Society Congress in Barcelona, the company presented full Phase III data showing its experimental antibody tozorakimab cut moderate-to-severe COPD flare-ups by up to 34 percent. Two days later, AstraZeneca and its partner Amgen disclosed that a combination of their cancer drugs Imfinzi and Imdelltra had met its main goal of extending survival in a notoriously lethal form of lung cancer. Different diseases, different drugs, same message: AstraZeneca's bet on combining existing medicines rather than chasing single new blockbusters is paying out in two of the toughest corners of lung disease.

A survival line in a cancer that rarely gives one

Extensive-stage small cell lung cancer (ES-SCLC) is the uglier cousin of the more common non-small cell disease. It is fast-growing, usually diagnosed after it has already spread, and for decades has been treated with a grim set of options: chemotherapy, radiation, modest gains, then relapse. The Phase III trial, named DeLLphi-305, tested whether adding Amgen's Imdelltra (tarlatamab) — a bispecific antibody that drags a patient's own T-cells toward tumor cells carrying the DLL3 protein — to maintenance treatment with AstraZeneca's Imfinzi (durvalumab) could do better than Imfinzi alone, in patients whose disease had not progressed after initial chemotherapy.^1

It did. At a planned interim analysis, the combination showed a "statistically significant and clinically meaningful" improvement in overall survival, AstraZeneca and Amgen said, along with improvements in progression-free survival and objective response rate.[^1][^3] Amgen's head of research and development, Jay Bradner, called it a result that would "further revolutionize the standard for survival earlier in the treatment journey" — the kind of language drugmakers reach for when a trial clears its bar, though neither company disclosed the actual median survival figures or hazard ratio in the initial release.^2 Those numbers, plus the trial's statistical confidence interval, are due at a future medical conference and will matter more to how the market ultimately prices the result than the topline alone does.

That omission is not unusual for an interim readout, but it does limit what can be said with confidence today. For comparison, a rival approach — Jazz Pharmaceuticals' Zepzelca paired with Roche's Tecentriq — produced a 27 percent improvement in median overall survival (13.2 months versus 10.6) in a similar first-line setting, giving some sense of the bar DeLLphi-305 needs to clear to be considered a genuine advance rather than an incremental one.^2 Shares in both AstraZeneca and Amgen moved only modestly on the news, which analysts read as investors withholding judgment on commercial size until the magnitude of benefit is known.[^4]

A second win, in a much bigger market

The COPD data arrived with more specificity attached. Tozorakimab, which blocks the inflammatory protein IL-33, reduced moderate-to-severe exacerbations by 29 percent in the OBERON trial and 34 percent in TITANIA among former smokers, and by roughly 30 percent in each trial across the broader population of current and former smokers, compared with placebo added to standard inhaled therapy.[^5] The two trials — simultaneously published in the New England Journal of Medicine — enrolled 2,306 patients combined, deliberately including people across all lung-function severities and blood eosinophil counts, a population wider than existing biologics are licensed to treat.[^5][^6]

That breadth is the commercial thesis. COPD affects roughly 30 million people in the major markets AstraZeneca tracks, and the biologics segment of that market is currently dominated by Sanofi and Regeneron's Dupixent, which is restricted to patients with elevated eosinophil counts — a marker of a specific inflammatory subtype.[^7] Jefferies analyst Michael Leuchten estimated that tozorakimab, because it does not depend on eosinophil status, "could more than double the eligible biologics population to nearly 80 percent," while still competing with Dupixent head-on in the eosinophilic subgroup.^7 AstraZeneca has pointed to peak annual sales of $3 billion to $5 billion if the drug reaches the market, a figure that would make tozorakimab one of the larger products in its respiratory portfolio.^8

What still has to happen

Neither result is a approval, and neither is without a caveat worth footnoting. Tozorakimab has no disclosed regulatory filing date yet, and a drug's ability to unseat an entrenched incumbent like Dupixent — which has years of prescriber familiarity and payer contracts behind it — depends as much on reimbursement terms and label breadth as on trial percentages. The DeLLphi-305 survival benefit, similarly, is a topline without the underlying months-and-confidence-interval detail that oncologists will want before changing first-line maintenance practice; that data is expected at an upcoming cancer conference, alongside submission to regulators.[^1][^3]

What ties the two stories together is less about any single drug and more about AstraZeneca's pattern: pairing an established immunotherapy backbone with a newer, more targeted mechanism — a bispecific antibody in lung cancer, a cytokine-blocking antibody in COPD — and testing that combination in a broad, unselected patient population rather than a narrow biomarker-defined slice. If the fuller data sets due later this year confirm the interim signals, both companies — and Amgen in particular, which is leaning on Imdelltra as a growth product amid Enbrel's decline — have reason to expect the commercial follow-through the topline headlines have only gestured at so far.
