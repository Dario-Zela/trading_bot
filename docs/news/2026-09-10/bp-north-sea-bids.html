BP's North Sea business draws bids from rival-backed consortiums

Jacket foundations for BP's Clair Ridge platform, part of the North Sea portfolio now up for sale, awaiting topsides installation west of Shetland. — Grahame C Anderson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BP's UK North Sea assets, on sale since July, have drawn competing bids from an Equinor-Shell venture and a TotalEnergies-Repsol group.

BP has spent 118 years in the oil business and roughly 60 of them in the North Sea. It is now trying to leave, and the queue forming at the door says something about where the rest of the industry still wants to be.

The company confirmed on July 31 that it was marketing its UK North Sea upstream business — five production hubs, Andrew and ETAP in the central North Sea plus Glen Lyon, Clair and Clair Ridge west of Shetland — for potential sale. Chief executive Meg O'Neill framed it as capital discipline rather than retreat from the basin outright: "As we focus our portfolio and direct capital to our highest-value opportunities, we believe our North Sea business will be better positioned as part of another company."

In one sentence: BP's UK North Sea business, up for sale since July, has drawn competing bids from an Equinor-Shell venture and a TotalEnergies-Repsol group, suggesting the basin still has buyers even as BP exits.

Two consortiums, one basin

Bloomberg reported this week that two groups have emerged as the lead contenders: Adura, the joint venture Equinor and Shell formed by combining their own North Sea assets, and NEO Next+, backed by TotalEnergies, Repsol and the private equity firm HitecVision. Both already operate in UK waters — Adura holds stakes in the Jackdaw gas field and the contested Rosebank oil project — which makes BP's hubs a bolt-on rather than an entry bet. A longer list of smaller suitors, including EnQuest, Harbour Energy, Ithaca Energy, Serica Energy and Norway's Var Energi, is also said to be circling. No binding offers have landed yet, and BP has given no timeline for a decision.

The business produced about 117,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day in 2025 and employs roughly 1,100 people. Rystad Energy puts the value at around $2.6 billion; other estimates, including one cited by the Telegraph, put the package closer to £2.5 billion. Either way, it is a modest sum against BP's roughly $80 billion market capitalisation — the point of the sale is portfolio focus, not a windfall.

Why buyers still want aging fields

The interest is notable precisely because these are not new discoveries. Andrew and ETAP have produced since the 1990s; Clair, west of Shetland, is one of the largest oil fields in UK waters but geologically difficult, which is why Clair Ridge — the second-phase platform pictured above — took until 2018 to come online. Mature, fee-heavy North Sea assets of this vintage are usually associated with decommissioning liabilities, not competitive bidding.

What's changed is the ownership structure of the basin itself. BP is the last of the supermajors to hold onto a standalone UK North Sea unit; Shell and Equinor merged theirs into Adura, and TotalEnergies folded its assets into NEO NEXT to create NEO Next+, in which the French major holds a 47.5% stake. Both vehicles were built for scale — spreading fixed costs like platform maintenance and decommissioning provisions across a bigger production base — and BP's hubs slot neatly into that logic. For Adura and NEO Next+, adding barrels to an existing operating footprint is cheaper than it would be for a standalone buyer starting from scratch.

What it says about capital allocation

That two rival-backed consortiums are competing for BP's leftovers, rather than the assets going unbid, is itself a data point for energy investors watching how majors are allocating capital through the transition. It suggests boardrooms still see cash-generative value in extending the life of existing North Sea infrastructure, even as the same companies talk up renewables and low-carbon spending elsewhere. BP's own move fits the same pattern in reverse: shedding a business that no longer meets its return hurdles so it can redirect capital toward what it now calls higher-value opportunities, a phrase O'Neill has used to justify both the North Sea exit and a broader push back toward oil and gas project economics since taking over from Bernard Looney's successor.

The next marker to watch is whether BP moves from soft interest to a formal bid deadline — sale processes in the basin have typically taken six to nine months from launch to signed deal. A firm number from either consortium, rather than valuations from Rystad or unnamed sources, will be the real test of what six decades of North Sea production are worth to the companies staying behind.
