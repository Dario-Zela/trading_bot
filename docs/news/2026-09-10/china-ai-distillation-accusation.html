US agencies accuse Chinese AI firms of mass model-distillation attacks

The seal of the National Security Agency, one of three US agencies behind the joint advisory on Chinese AI distillation campaigns. — U.S. Government / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
NSA, CISA and FBI accuse DeepSeek, Alibaba and four other Chinese AI firms of industrial-scale campaigns to distill Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok since 2024.

Three US security agencies have put a name and a paper trail to something the American AI industry has grumbled about privately for two years: that Chinese labs are building fast, cheap models by systematically siphoning the outputs of the expensive American ones.

In a joint advisory published this week, the National Security Agency, the FBI and the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency accused six China-based AI companies — DeepSeek, Alibaba, Moonshot AI, MiniMax, StepFun and Z.AI — of running "industrial-scale" campaigns to distill proprietary capabilities out of Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok. The document, designated AA26-251A, says the campaigns date to at least late 2024 and describes distillation not as a side activity but as "the core — not merely a supplement" of these companies' development strategy.

In one sentence: A joint NSA-CISA-FBI advisory accuses six Chinese AI firms of running years-long, industrial-scale campaigns to copy US frontier models — a claim with real export-control teeth.

What distillation actually is, and why it's contentious

Distillation is not exotic. It's a standard technique in which a smaller model is trained to mimic the outputs of a larger, more capable one, and it's used routinely inside US labs too — it's often the cheapest way to make a fast, affordable model without retraining from scratch. Done with permission, it's unremarkable. Done against a rival's paid API, at scale, without consent, it edges into something closer to industrial espionage — though because nothing physical is stolen and no code is copied line for line, it's also far harder to prove or police than a conventional data breach.

That gray zone is what the advisory tries to close. It describes methods rather than just accusations: "transfer stations" — gray-market API proxies that route requests around geographic and usage restrictions; fraudulent accounts that obscure who's really asking; bulk-purchased subscriptions shared across development teams; and prompt-injection techniques used to extract hidden chain-of-thought reasoning that providers try to keep behind the curtain. The agencies say the operations pulled billions of tokens across millions of queries.

The specifics per company are pointed. DeepSeek, the advisory says, ran organized campaigns since late 2024 targeting Claude, GPT-4 variants, Gemini 2.5 and Grok, focused on legal reasoning and agentic tasks — and it notes, pointedly, that DeepSeek's widely publicized $5.6 million training cost "does not include the true cost of the data acquired through extensive malicious distillation." Alibaba is accused of the largest single campaign: extracting from Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet and GPT-5 in late 2025 to sharpen coding, customer-service dialogue and image-generation features. StepFun allegedly targeted Claude Opus 4.1/4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5 and multiple GPT-5 variants between late 2025 and early 2026 to improve its Step 4 model's coding and agentic skills.

This didn't come out of nowhere

Anthropic laid the groundwork months before Washington acted. In June, the company said DeepSeek, Moonshot AI and MiniMax had used roughly 24,000 fraudulent accounts to generate more than 16 million exchanges extracting Claude's capabilities, and separately accused Alibaba-affiliated operators of the largest known distillation attack against it — 28.8 million exchanges across 25,000 accounts between late April and early June. By September, Anthropic said the fight had moved to the dark web, where marketplaces trade access to compromised or bulk-purchased API credentials specifically to run distillation at scale.

The Trump administration had already shown it was willing to use export controls as leverage in this fight. In the spring it briefly ordered Anthropic to cut off access to its Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models for foreign nationals — including the company's own foreign-national employees — before the Commerce Department reversed course in June following negotiations led by Anthropic co-founder Tom Brown. That episode showed the administration treats model access as a control point it can turn on and off. This advisory reads as the government reasserting that leverage from the other direction, no longer relying on a company's own telemetry but putting its own intelligence agencies' names on the allegations.

Why it lands now

The timing is not incidental. The advisory arrives days ahead of Xi Jinping's planned state visit to Washington this month, giving US negotiators a fresh grievance to place on the table alongside existing disputes over chip export limits. China's foreign ministry has already dismissed the accusations as groundless, with spokesperson Mao Ning instead calling for deeper US-China AI cooperation and framing Chinese progress as self-reliant.

That framing matters strategically. If distillation genuinely lets Chinese labs close the capability gap using a fraction of the compute — cheap API queries rather than racks of restricted Nvidia chips — then Washington's chip-export regime, its primary lever for slowing Chinese AI progress, becomes less decisive than it was designed to be. That is likely why the agencies chose to publish now: not just to shame six named companies, but to argue that a policy built around controlling hardware needs a companion policy for controlling access to models themselves.

What to watch

The advisory recommends AI providers deploy behavioral monitoring for anomalous usage patterns and even "response alteration" — quietly degrading outputs for suspected distillation attempts rather than tipping off the operators. Whether that becomes formal policy, and whether Washington follows this advisory with actual restrictions on model API access for Chinese entities, will likely surface around the Xi visit. Beijing's rejection of the claims, paired with its own complaints about US export-control overreach, suggests this becomes a standing item in the bilateral relationship rather than a one-off dispute.
