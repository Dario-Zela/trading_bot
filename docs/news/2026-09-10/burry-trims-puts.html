Michael Burry trims Nvidia and Palantir puts, stays structurally bearish

Nvidia's Santa Clara headquarters — one of two AI-trade bellwethers Burry is still betting against, just with less time pressure. — Coolcaesar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Burry sold his December 2026 Nvidia and Palantir puts to dodge time decay, but his longer-dated 2027 bearish bets are untouched.

Michael Burry closed out his short-dated bearish bets against Nvidia and Palantir this month, selling all of his December 2026-dated put options on both stocks. The move, disclosed by Burry himself rather than in a regulatory filing, trims the portion of his book most exposed to time decay — the steady erosion in an option's value as its expiration date approaches — without touching the longer-horizon positions that reflect his actual thesis.

That distinction matters. Burry still holds December 2027 puts on Palantir and on the Invesco QQQ Trust, the Nasdaq-100 tracking fund heavy with the same AI-adjacent names he has spent much of the past year criticizing. Even stripped of the December 2026 contracts, his short stock positions account for more than a fifth of his portfolio, according to his own disclosure. Nothing about the underlying wager — that the AI trade has run ahead of the fundamentals — has changed.

A trim, not a retreat

Burry framed the sale as portfolio hygiene. In a post on his Substack newsletter, Cassandra Unchained, he said he had "pulled in risk" during September and was content to hold the resulting cash rather than redeploy it immediately. "This will be an interesting market this fall," he wrote — the kind of hedged, watch-and-wait line that has become his signature since he began publishing there.

Options with a near-dated expiry are a blunt instrument for a slow-moving thesis. If a stock doesn't move sharply before the contract expires, the position bleeds value every day regardless of whether the eventual call turns out right — a cost Burry has referenced before when explaining why he prefers longer-dated structures for directional bets he intends to hold. Rolling out of the December 2026 contracts without replacing them removes that clock without abandoning the position it was attached to.

Why the disclosure looks different this time

The circumstances around this update are worth noting, because they've changed since Burry's last headline-grabbing Nvidia and Palantir trade. That earlier position — roughly $1.1 billion in notional exposure across the two names — surfaced in a 13F filing covering the third quarter of 2025, when Scion Asset Management was still a registered investment adviser filing with the SEC on a quarterly lag.

Scion no longer exists in that form. Burry deregistered the firm in November 2025, handing his remaining outside capital and the Scion name to a successor, Phil Clifton, who now runs a separate firm called Pomerium Capital. Burry kept trading on his own account and started Cassandra Unchained, a paid newsletter that has since become his primary channel for disclosing positions — on his own timetable, voluntarily, with none of the quarterly-lag transparency a 13F requires. The September update on the Nvidia and Palantir puts came through that channel, not a regulatory one.

What it signals

For traders who treat Burry's positioning as a sentiment gauge, the takeaway is narrower than the headlines suggest. This wasn't a reduction in conviction — it was a reduction in the cost of holding that conviction. The 2027 Palantir and QQQ puts, the ones actually built to pay off if the AI trade unwinds over a longer horizon, are untouched.

The next real test of that thesis isn't another Substack post — it's whether Nvidia and Palantir's earnings through the rest of 2026 give the market a reason to reprice the AI-spending story Burry has been betting against. Until then, his book reads less like a retreat and more like someone tightening the position ahead of a wait he expects to be long.
