UK sells 30-year debt at highest yield since 1998, stoking Budget tax fears

HM Treasury's Horse Guards Road headquarters, where Chancellor Healey is preparing his first Budget under mounting fiscal pressure. — Wutthichai Charoenburi / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Britain sold £4.25bn of 30-year gilts at 5.8168%, the highest yield since 1998, halving Chancellor Healey's fiscal headroom before the October 28 Budget.

Britain's government paid the highest price to borrow for thirty years since modern records began, after Tuesday's gilt sale priced at a yield of 5.8168% — a level not seen since the Debt Management Office started keeping comparable records in 1998, and above the previous high of 5.79% set at auction that same year.

The sale itself went smoothly enough. The DMO placed £4.25bn ($5.75bn) of a new 2056-maturity gilt, carrying a 5.375% coupon, via syndication with Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, Santander and UBS running the books. Orders came in above £85bn — nearly twenty times the amount on offer — which is the kind of demand that lets a debt manager say, accurately, that appetite for UK paper is intact. "Today's syndication shows demand for gilts at these yields remains in good health," said Matthew Amis of Aberdeen Investments.

That is the reassuring read. The less reassuring one is the yield itself, and what it costs the Treasury to keep clearing at that price.

Why long gilts are repricing

Thirty-year borrowing costs have been grinding higher across most of the developed world this year, and Britain has not been spared. Intraday trading pushed the 30-year gilt yield as high as 5.89% before it settled back to around 5.85% — still the highest level since early 1998. Among major advanced economies, only Australia now pays more to borrow long-term than the UK does.

Part of the move is imported. The spike in Middle East tensions since Iran's strike on the US base in Jordan has kept a bid under oil and a premium in inflation expectations globally, and gilts have moved with US Treasuries and eurozone bonds rather than against them. But UK paper has moved further than most of its peers, which points to a UK-specific problem sitting on top of the global one: a fiscal position that markets are pricing with increasing scepticism ahead of the Budget.

The Office for Budget Responsibility already expects debt interest payments to hit £109bn this year — 8.4% of all public spending, more than the education budget. Every basis point added to long-dated yields compounds that bill for years, since long gilts are precisely the instrument the government uses to lock in financing for decades at a stretch. Long-dated issuance is a small slice of the annual programme — under 10% of the £246bn the DMO plans to raise this year — but it sets the marginal price at which Britain borrows for the long run, and it is the number investors watch as a verdict on fiscal credibility.

The Budget arithmetic tightens

Chancellor John Healey took the job in July with, by most estimates, something like £23-26bn of fiscal headroom against his self-imposed borrowing rules — already a thinner margin than his predecessor Rachel Reeves worked with in recent years. The bond sell-off has cut that cushion hard. Analysts at Pantheon Macroeconomics now put Healey's remaining headroom at roughly £13bn, close to half of where it started, purely as a function of what the gilt market has done since his appointment.

That leaves Healey with an unforgiving equation on October 28. Higher debt-servicing costs eat directly into the money available for anything else — spending commitments, investment pledges, the usual pre-Budget wishlist from departments — while the market's own pricing behaviour makes clear that further slippage on the fiscal rules would likely be punished with even higher yields, not tolerated as harmless. Economists who track the OBR's methodology describe the position as close to mechanical: without new revenue, the sums do not balance, which is why speculation has shifted from whether Healey raises taxes to which ones and by how much.

What the market is actually pricing

It is worth separating two things the gilt sale tells investors. One is that liquidity and appetite for UK government debt remain fine — the order book size settles that question. The other is that the price of that liquidity has moved permanently higher, and that a 27-year high in the cost of thirty-year money is not a technical blip but a read on Britain's debt trajectory relative to its growth prospects. Rating agencies and bond desks alike will be watching the October 28 Budget less for headline tax rates than for whether Healey's numbers hold up under the OBR's own forecasting, since another downgrade to fiscal headroom this close to the wire would give the market little reason to price UK risk any more generously than it already has.

The next test comes well before the Budget itself: the OBR's pre-measures forecast, expected in the weeks ahead, will show how much of that £13bn cushion survives contact with updated growth and borrowing numbers. If it shrinks further, the pressure on Healey to reach for revenue-raising measures — rather than spending restraint alone — becomes harder to avoid.
