Burnham's approval holds up as he leaves door open on Scottish independence vote

Andy Burnham, whose comments on a Scottish independence referendum drew a rebuke from his own Downing Street press office within hours. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham said he'd consider a Scottish independence referendum given a 'clear consensus' in Scotland, but left the threshold undefined and his own office disputed any policy shift.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for less than two months, and he has already done something none of his predecessors managed after the 2014 referendum: made "never again" sound less than absolute.

At Prime Minister's Questions on Tuesday, responding to SNP MP Chris Law, Burnham reached for the language of Northern Ireland's Good Friday Agreement to describe how he'd handle a second vote on Scottish independence. A border poll, he noted, gets "considered by the Northern Ireland Secretary when there is a clear consensus in the country, when public opinion has changed to the point where the call has to be considered." Then the line that made headlines: "I would say exactly the same situation in Scotland."

From "off limits" to a formula

The shift matters because of where Burnham started. Scotland's First Minister John Swinney says the prime minister had previously told him privately that a second referendum was "off limits" — full stop, no conditions attached. Tuesday's comments replaced that with a test, however vague. Swinney called it exactly that: "To go from 'off limits' to this is an acceptance of the reality that the people of Scotland will decide their own future — not the UK Government."

It is worth being precise about what changed and what didn't. Burnham did not announce a referendum, set a date, or even commit to one in principle. He immediately hedged his own opening: "I am not aware that there is a majority of public support for another referendum, and until that changes there won't be one." Downing Street's press office went further within hours, insisting there had been no change in policy at all and pointing back to Labour's manifesto opposition to independence and to holding another vote.

That leaves "clear consensus" doing an enormous amount of unexplained work. Would it mean sustained polling above 50%? A pro-independence majority at Holyrood? A specific election result? Burnham did not say, and nobody in Westminster has forced him to. Scotland last voted on the question in September 2014, rejecting independence 55–45 on a turnout above 84%. Polling since has been closer to a coin flip than a mandate in either direction — recent surveys this year have put Yes support anywhere from 48% to 52% excluding don't-knows, with age and Brexit-vote history doing most of the explaining: support runs as high as 64% among 18-34-year-olds and as low as roughly 40% among the over-65s. A number that unsettled would need Downing Street to define the finish line before anyone could say whether Scotland had crossed it.

The approval Burnham can spend

The referendum comments landed against a backdrop that helps explain why Burnham felt he had room to make them. Opinium's most recent tracker puts his net approval at +16 — 37% approve of his handling of the job against 21% who disapprove — making him the only major UK party leader in positive territory. Kemi Badenoch sits at -5, Nigel Farage at -25. Burnham entered office in July with a 20-point improvement on his ratings from before he became prime minister, and he has held that advantage since.

That is an unusual position for a UK government to be in less than two months into a term, and it gives Burnham latitude that a weaker prime minister would not have to float a conditional opening on independence without it reading as capitulation. But the same data point cuts the other way: with every other national figure underwater, Burnham's standing is effectively a one-man show. There is no deep bench of popular government figures to fall back on if his numbers slip, which makes a comment like Tuesday's — read generously by nationalists, walked back hours later by his own press office — a higher-stakes gamble than it might look on paper.

What to watch

The test of whether this was a genuine opening or a rhetorical release valve will come from Holyrood, not Westminster. Scotland's Parliament election in May returned a pro-independence majority, per pre-election polling, and how Swinney's government chooses to press the "clear consensus" question — whether through a formal request for a Section 30 order, a fresh polling push, or simply repeated public pressure — will determine whether Burnham's phrase becomes a live political mechanism or a line that Downing Street quietly lets die. Until the government defines its own threshold, the story is less about Scottish independence than about a prime minister choosing his words carefully enough to keep every option open.
