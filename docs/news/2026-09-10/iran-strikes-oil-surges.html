MIDDLE EAST ESCALATION · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-10 · 5 min read

Oil surges past $100 and stocks slide as Iran strikes US base in Jordan

A supertanker underway — the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of global oil consumption transits, is at the center of the latest US-Iran exchange. — Photographer's Mate 1st Class Kevin H. Tierney / US Navy (public domain)
In one sentence
Brent crude topped $100 a barrel and European stocks sank after the US destroyed five Iranian tankers and Iran struck a US-linked airbase in Jordan.

Brent crude settled above $100 a barrel on Wednesday for the first time since late July, and European equities sold off in tandem, after the United States destroyed five Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf and Iran answered with a ballistic-missile barrage against a US-linked airbase in Jordan. The exchange marks the sharpest escalation yet in a maritime and military standoff that has simmered, on and off, for more than six months.

The moves were not subtle. Brent touched $101.40 intraday, up 3.6% on the day, while West Texas Intermediate gained 3.8% to $96.59. In London, the FTSE 100 fell 1.3% to a seven-week low of 10,669.32. Frankfurt's DAX dropped 1.7% and Paris's CAC 40 lost 1.9%. On Wall Street, the Dow Jones Industrial Average shed 405 points, or 0.77%, to close at 52,380.66 — its third straight losing session — while the S&P 500 fell 0.48% and the Nasdaq 0.64%.

Tankers, warships, and a base in the desert

The immediate trigger was a naval exchange in the Gulf of Oman and the Strait of Hormuz. According to US Central Command, Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps targeted a US Navy warship with ballistic missiles on two separate occasions over the preceding two days; the ship evaded both attempts. The US military responded on Tuesday by striking and destroying five vessels it identified as IRGC-linked oil tankers — the Kivik, Charminar, Horizon 1 and Riesco in the Gulf of Oman, and the Derya near Kharg Island inside the strait itself.

Iran's retaliation came hours later, several hundred miles away. The IRGC said it launched a combined missile-and-drone operation it called "Punishment of the Aggressor" against the Muwaffaq Salti air base — also known as Al-Azraq — in Jordan's Zarqa governorate, which hosts US aircraft and personnel. Tehran claimed the strikes hit maintenance and deployment sites for F-35, F-16 and F-15 jets and inflicted "heavy damage." Jordan's military gave a different account: it said it intercepted 18 of 20 incoming ballistic missiles, with the remaining two landing in unpopulated areas, and a Trump administration official said the barrage caused no US casualties. Satellite imagery reviewed by independent outlets has shown some damage at the base, though the scale remains disputed between Tehran's claims and the official US and Jordanian line.

It was the most direct Iranian strike on a US-linked target since Tehran first hit Al-Azraq in March, part of a pattern of more than ten claimed strikes on the base since spring. Whether Wednesday's attack settles as another round in that cycle or marks a genuine widening of the conflict is now the question hanging over every desk pricing Gulf risk.

Oil as the transmission mechanism

For traders, the tanker strikes matter more than the missiles over Jordan, because they threaten the one thing markets cannot substitute around: physical flow through Hormuz. Roughly a fifth of global oil consumption transits the strait, and Iranian officials this week warned that any further US or allied action would be met with strikes on energy infrastructure across the Gulf, explicitly including American oil and gas assets in the region.

That threat is doing real work on the futures curve. Brent's break above $100 is its highest close since the Islamabad memorandum of understanding collapsed in July and the two sides last traded fire in the strait. Analysts now flag $120 a barrel as a plausible level if strikes on tankers continue or if Hormuz traffic is further disrupted — a scenario that would ripple beyond pump prices into freight costs, insurance premiums (several war-risk underwriters have already pulled coverage from the strait) and, eventually, headline inflation prints across importing economies. Ryanair, among the more oil-sensitive corporates in Europe, has already trimmed its 2027 passenger targets and flagged higher short-haul fares, citing unhedged fuel costs.

Equities catch the same current as Treasuries

The read-through into equities has been just as mechanical. Wednesday's Dow decline was its third in a row, and it did not happen in isolation from the bond market: the 10-year Treasury yield climbed to 4.857%, its highest since November 2023, after the Treasury Department said it would triple its emergency buyback of longer-dated debt to $6 billion in an attempt to shore up demand. Rising oil and rising real rates are, for equity investors, the same story told twice — both point toward inflation proving stickier than the market had priced, and both raise the odds the Federal Reserve leans hawkish at its September 15–16 meeting.

In Europe the transmission was more direct: energy-import-dependent economies simply cost more to run at $100 oil, and the FTSE, DAX and CAC all sold off broadly rather than rotating into energy names, a sign traders are treating this as a net macro drag rather than a sector-specific windfall.

What connects the threads

The pattern across commodities, rates and equities this week is not really about Iran and Jordan in isolation — it is about what happens when a geopolitical shock lands on a market that was already nervous about inflation. Oil at $100 does two jobs simultaneously: it is a safe-haven bid on war risk, and it is a direct input into the next CPI print. That print lands Friday, September 11, the last major inflation data before the Fed's meeting the following week. Consensus expects headline CPI to hold near 3.4% year-on-year with core cooling slightly to about 2.4%; a hot number, arriving days after a fresh oil spike, would leave the Fed little room to look through the noise. Until Hormuz traffic either stabilizes or the tanker exchanges escalate further, oil, yields and equities are likely to keep moving as one trade rather than three separate ones.
