Trump claims US control of 65bn barrels of Venezuelan oil

The Puerto La Cruz refinery complex in Anzoátegui state, part of the aging infrastructure at the center of the US-Venezuela oil deal. — Daniel Zambrano / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Trump says the US now controls 55% of a new venture holding 65bn barrels of Venezuelan oil, but crude prices fell on the news amid doubts over contract terms and years of needed investment.

President Trump announced on Friday that the United States has secured majority control of 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan oil reserves — a claim he made on Truth Social in capital letters, calling it "THE BIGGEST OIL DEAL IN WORLD HISTORY." The agreement, negotiated by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth with Venezuela's interim president Delcy Rodríguez, covers 17 fields and arrives eight months after a US military operation captured Nicolás Maduro and flew him to New York to face narco-terrorism charges.

The number is real enough to matter, and murky enough to argue about. Trump says the deal "more than doubles" American oil reserves — a claim that checks out arithmetically, since the US Energy Information Administration puts proven domestic reserves at roughly 46 billion barrels. What it does not settle is whether 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan crude, most of it heavy and buried in a country whose oil industry has spent two decades in decline, behaves anything like reserves the US actually controls.

A stake, not a purchase

The mechanics matter more than the headline figure. Rather than nationalizing anything, Washington and Caracas have created a new private joint venture in which the US government holds an effective 55 percent of output — an ownership stake, plus the right to buy Venezuelan crude at cost. It would be, by some measures, the second-largest corporate holder of proven oil reserves in the world after Saudi Aramco, and the first time the US federal government has held a majority position in an oil company of any kind. Washington has never run a national oil champion the way Riyadh, Moscow or Brasília do; this deal edges it toward one, at least on paper.

Caracas's side of the ledger is also specific. Rodríguez's government is promised roughly $100 billion in private investment and more than $209 billion in tax revenue over the life of the arrangement, which Trump's team has pitched as a 100-year concession. Rodríguez called it "historic." Rubio framed it as an America First win that would also rebuild Venezuela's economy. Notably absent from the announcement: Exxon Mobil, ConocoPhillips or Chevron, none of which — according to people familiar with the process — was consulted by the administration before or after Maduro's capture. Trump, asked about the process, said plainly that he didn't consult anybody.

Why crude fell on the news

If this were the supply shock Trump described, oil markets would have moved accordingly. They did not: Brent crude, which has traded around $80 a barrel through the Iran war and speculation over a Strait of Hormuz reopening, slipped rather than spiked on the announcement. Traders read the timing correctly. Any material increase in Venezuelan output — analysts cite figures of one to two million additional barrels a day as the eventual ceiling — sits somewhere in the late 2020s to early 2030s, not next quarter.

Rory Johnston, an oil market researcher, was blunter, telling reporters the 65-billion-barrel figure "is a red herring and will have little relevance to any actual deals." The skepticism has a track record behind it. Venezuela's production has collapsed from a peak of roughly 3.5 million barrels a day to about 1.1 million today, a function of nationalizations under Hugo Chávez, sanctions, and chronic underinvestment. Oil executives estimate turning the sector around will cost on the order of $10 billion a year in fresh capital — money that has to arrive before any of the 65 billion barrels reaches a tanker. Chevron, the only major US producer that stayed through the sanctions years, currently pumps about 150,000 barrels a day there under license; scaling that up by even a fraction requires years of drilling, pipeline repair and refinery work that heavy Orinoco crude specifically demands.

What 65 billion barrels doesn't buy

Even at face value, the figure needs context most coverage of the announcement skipped. Venezuela's total proven reserves — the largest of any country on earth — stand at roughly 303 billion barrels, or about 17 percent of the global total. The 65 billion barrels under this deal is a fifth of that, and closer to 4 percent of world reserves than the 6-to-7 percent figure that circulated in early reporting. That distinction is not pedantry: a genuine US claim on 4 percent of global proven supply is still consequential for pricing power and for reducing reliance on Gulf producers, but it is not the reordering of world energy markets the White House's language implies.

There is a geopolitical layer too. Russia's Rosneft had held stakes in Venezuelan joint ventures for years, a foothold Moscow has effectively ceded as part of the broader realignment following Maduro's fall — one more marker of Russian influence receding from the Western Hemisphere as its own war economy strains under sanctions. Dean Baker, senior economist at the Center for Economic and Policy Research, offered the rejoinder that will likely define the political fight over this deal's durability: "if Venezuela ever gets anything resembling a democratic government, the very first thing it will do is flush Trump's deal down the toilet." A concession negotiated with an unelected interim president installed after a foreign military operation carries exactly that risk — it is only as durable as Rodríguez's government, which has yet to face a vote.

What comes next

The full contract terms have not been published, and lawmakers in both parties say they have not seen them either — Senator Bernie Moreno backed the deal as a rebuff to Chinese buyers of discounted Venezuelan crude, while Democratic critics argue the capture of Maduro was orchestrated with this outcome in mind. Watch for whether Congress demands the underlying documents, whether Exxon and ConocoPhillips seek their own arrangements now that Chevron no longer has the field to itself, and whether US gasoline prices — which spent all of August above $4 a gallon, the most expensive month at the pump on record — show any movement before November's midterms. None of that turns on the 65-billion-barrel number. It turns on capital, pipelines, and a government in Caracas that has not yet faced an electorate.
