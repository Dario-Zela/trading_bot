Greens demand UK data-centre moratorium; government says no

Zack Polanski, leader of the Green Party of England and Wales, who called for a moratorium on new UK data centres. — Rob Browne / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Labour rejected the Green Party's call to strip new UK data centres of critical-infrastructure status, saying a pause would cost jobs and investment.

Zack Polanski wants the UK to stop treating data centres like hospitals. The government isn't having it.

The Green Party leader used a visit to the site of a proposed data centre in east London this week to call for what he termed a moratorium on new builds — "basically a moratorium, other than local-scale data centres for the local community," as he put it. His core objection isn't to data centres existing; it's to their 2024 designation as "critical national infrastructure," a status that puts them alongside power stations and water networks and exempts them from restrictions that apply to everyone else — including, this summer, hosepipe bans.

"To most people, critical infrastructure means hospitals, water, electricity," Polanski said. "It doesn't mean artificial intelligence." He called it "outrageous" that new server farms are shielded from scrutiny while three-quarters of England sits in drought.

The numbers he's pointing at are real enough. Some London data centre proposals have sought consent to draw up to three million litres of water a day for cooling, and official forecasts of the country's future water needs have, until recently, simply left data centres out of the calculation. On the power side, data centres drew 1,442 megawatts at the end of 2024; projects already under construction or in planning would take that past 3,600MW. Their share of the UK's electricity supply, currently around 2.5%, is expected to roughly quadruple by 2030.

Labour's answer: jobs, not water

The government's rejection was blunt. A spokesperson told the BBC that a pause "would simply send investment and good jobs abroad, while leaving Britain dependent on overseas computing," and separately dismissed the Greens' proposal as "a disaster for jobs and national security." That's consistent with the framing Labour has used since granting data centres critical-infrastructure status in 2024 — as a growth bet, not just a technical classification, tied to attracting the hyperscaler capital pouring into AI compute.

What's missing from that answer is any commitment to address the water and grid strain directly, short of a pause. Parliament's own researchers flagged the gap earlier this year, and NESO — the grid operator — is already managing a tighter balancing act this summer with a fifth of France's nuclear fleet offline for drought-related reasons, cutting into the interconnector capacity Britain leans on.

For now, the practical upshot is that nothing changes: developers keep their fast-track planning status, and campaigners keep making the water-and-power case without a parliamentary vote in sight. The next pressure point is likely to come from local planning fights and drought-order renewals rather than Westminster, unless Ofwat or the Environment Agency moves to bring data centre water use into statutory reporting — something MPs have been pushing for since spring.
