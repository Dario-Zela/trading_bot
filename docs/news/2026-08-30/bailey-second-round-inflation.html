Bailey downplays second-round inflation risk before September call

Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey — Office of the U.S. Ambassador to the U.K. / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Bailey told Bloomberg the UK isn't seeing significant second-round inflation effects, pushing back against BoE hawks ahead of the September 17 rate call.

Andrew Bailey picked an odd venue — a gathering of central bankers in the Wyoming mountains dedicated largely to Kevin Warsh's inflation warnings — to make the case that Britain's price problem isn't spiralling. In a Bloomberg Television interview at the Jackson Hole symposium on Friday, the Bank of England governor said the UK is "not yet seeing significant second-round inflation effects," three weeks before the Bank's next rate call.

"We're seeing quite subdued second-round effects. I think we've seen a softening labour market for some time now."

Second-round effects are the mechanism economists worry about most after a price shock: workers demand higher wages to cover the cost of living, firms pass those wages into prices, and the cycle repeats. Bailey's argument is that this loop isn't turning — because the labour market has been cooling for long enough to cap wage demands before they feed back into inflation.

A hold, not a pivot

The comment lands at an awkward moment for that reading. UK inflation rose to 2.9% in July, its highest in four months, driven largely by a 13% jump in Ofgem's energy price cap and the sharpest monthly rise in gas prices since October 2022. The Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% at its July meeting for a fifth straight time — but the vote split, 6-3, was more hawkish than it looks: three members wanted a quarter-point hike, up from two the meeting before. Gilt markets, meanwhile, have been marking down UK creditworthiness on fiscal grounds rather than growth grounds — 30-year yields hit their highest since 1998 this year as investors priced in tax rises or spending cuts ahead of November's budget.

Into that setting, Bailey's "subdued second-round effects" line reads less like a dovish pivot than a rebuttal aimed at the three hawks on his own committee. If wage-price spirals aren't building, the argument for a hike weakens even as the headline number ticks up. He was careful not to overplay it, adding that the Bank remains "not pre-committed" to any rate path and will judge each meeting on its own data.

"I cannot promise that muted second-round effects will continue."

What it means for September 17

That hedge matters. Economists surveyed by Reuters mostly expect the Bank to hold again on 17 September, but financial markets have been leaning the other way, pricing some probability of a hike given the inflation and fiscal backdrop. Bailey's remarks give the doves on the committee cover to argue that the labour-market slack is doing the disinflationary work that a rate rise would otherwise have to do — without him committing to that outcome in public.

The next hard data point is the August CPI release, due in the fortnight before the decision, alongside updated labour-market figures. If wage growth or services inflation surprise to the upside, Bailey's Jackson Hole comfort will look premature; if the labour market keeps softening as he describes, it becomes the Bank's justification for standing pat while gilt markets continue to price the UK's fiscal troubles on their own terms.
