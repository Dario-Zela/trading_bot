Nikkei nears record highs even as Japan's GDP disappoints

The Tokyo Stock Exchange, where the Nikkei 225 has spent August climbing back toward its June record high. — EXECUTOR / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The Nikkei is closing in on its June record on AI chip demand and a 40-year-low yen, even as Q2 GDP growth of 0.3% badly missed forecasts.

Tokyo's stock market spent the summer proving that a war, a wobbling currency and a disappointing set of growth numbers are no match for enthusiasm about artificial intelligence. The Nikkei 225 closed at 66,405 on Friday, roughly 8% below the all-time record of 72,353.96 it set on June 22 — a level that erased the index's losses from the Iran-Israel war and marked the first time in the benchmark's 77-year history it had crossed 72,000. After a summer pullback driven by worries over "AI overinvestment" and a bout of Middle East-driven risk aversion, the index has spent August clawing its way back, lifted again this week by a strong Nvidia sales forecast and gains in chip-testing equipment maker Advantest and SoftBank Group, the index's two most heavily weighted stocks.

That rally is unfolding in spite of, not because of, what Japan's domestic economy is actually doing. Data released this month showed gross domestic product grew just 0.3% quarter-on-quarter in the April-to-June period, well short of the 0.5% economists had forecast, and equivalent to an annualized pace of 1.1% against expectations near 2%. It was Japan's third straight quarter of expansion, but a thin one — and the composition of the miss is the more telling part.

A growth number that flatters exporters, not households

Private consumption was flat in real terms, missing forecasts for a 0.5% rise. Capital expenditure fell 1.2% on the quarter — a 4.6% annualized decline — as firms turned more cautious about big spending amid tariff uncertainty and rising energy costs. Net exports were the one bright spot, adding 0.5 percentage points to growth as exports rose and imports fell, while domestic demand alone subtracted 0.2 points.

Norihiro Yamaguchi, an economist at Oxford Economics, put the dynamic plainly: AI-related exports should stay robust in the near term, but "sluggish non-AI-related global economic activities will limit overall export gains," and he expects growth to remain weak through the second half of the year as companies pass rising energy costs on to consumers. That is not the profile of an economy that would, on its own, justify a market flirting with record highs. It is the profile of an economy being carried by one export category — AI-linked chips and equipment — while everything else treads water.

The yen did the rest of the work

The other engine behind the rally has been currency, not commerce. The yen fell to roughly 163.7 per dollar in late July, its weakest level since 1986 — a genuine 40-year low — driven by a widening gap between US and Japanese interest rates, elevated oil prices tied to the Iran war, and doubts that the Bank of Japan was moving fast enough to close that gap. A weak yen mechanically inflates exporters' repatriated earnings and makes Japanese equities cheaper for foreign buyers, which is a meaningful part of why the Nikkei's advance has run alongside, rather than despite, currency weakness.

Tokyo and Washington judged the move disorderly enough to act. On August 1, Japan's Ministry of Finance and the US Treasury confirmed a coordinated intervention — the first joint action of its kind since 1998 — buying yen after it touched those 40-year lows. The currency snapped back to around 157.6 per dollar, but the relief proved partial: by August 11 it had drifted back out to roughly 159, giving back about half its post-intervention gain. Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama said Tokyo would not hesitate to intervene again.

A central bank caught between two signals

The currency question feeds directly into the Bank of Japan's next move, and that is where the growth-versus-markets divergence becomes a live trading risk rather than an academic curiosity. The BOJ lifted its policy rate to 1.0% in June — a 31-year high — then held in July, and Governor Kazuo Ueda has since faced pressure, including reportedly from Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's camp, to push it to 1.25% as soon as the September 18 meeting. A weak GDP print complicates that timeline in both directions: raising rates into soft domestic demand risks choking off the consumption recovery the BOJ has been waiting for, while holding rates risks leaving the yen exposed to renewed declines and another round of the volatility that forced August's intervention.

For positions built around the current setup, the read-through is straightforward. The Nikkei's push toward its June record rests on two supports — AI-driven chip demand and yen weakness — that have little to do with how Japanese households and businesses are actually faring. Both supports are also things that can move quickly and independently of Japan itself: a wobble in US AI capital-expenditure plans, of the kind that briefly knocked the Nikkei off its June highs, or a BOJ rate hike that firms the yen, would remove one leg of the trade without any change in the domestic growth picture. Crowded trades built on a narrow set of external supports tend to unwind fast when one of those supports gives way, and a market sitting a few percent from a record with underlying growth this soft is a market where the gap between price and fundamentals has room to widen further — or close abruptly.

What comes next

The next test arrives September 18, when the Bank of Japan meets against a backdrop of the softest growth data since the current expansion began. A hike would test whether the yen-driven leg of the rally holds up as the currency firms; a hold would test whether markets keep betting the AI trade can carry the index the rest of the way to a new record on its own. Either way, Japan's Q3 GDP figures, due in mid-November, will show whether August's yen intervention and September's rate decision did anything to revive the domestic demand that this summer's numbers found wanting.
