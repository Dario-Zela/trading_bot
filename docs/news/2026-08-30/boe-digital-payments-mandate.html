Treasury hands BoE a new digital-payments innovation remit

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which will gain a new secondary objective on payments innovation. — Acediscovery / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Treasury will give the Bank of England a secondary objective to back payments innovation, subordinate to financial stability — a mandate the Bank's own stablecoin-cap climbdown this year shows will be contested in practice.

The Treasury wants the Bank of England to start caring, formally, about fintech. On Thursday HM Treasury said it intends to give the Bank a new secondary objective — to support innovation in payment systems and emerging forms of digital money, including stablecoins — sitting alongside, but beneath, its core job of keeping the financial system stable.

City Minister Lucy Rigby framed it as keeping pace with the plumbing of finance. "Developments in digital payments technology, including tokenisation and DLT, have the potential to transform financial markets across the globe," she said, adding that the objective "will support the Bank to continue to drive innovation in payments and digital finance, ensuring that the UK remains a global leader in financial services." The Bank's Deputy Governor for Financial Stability, Sarah Breeden, welcomed the move, saying it would "further boost our work to support innovation in financial services without compromising on financial stability."

Mechanically, this is not a new invention. The Bank has held a comparable secondary innovation objective for central counterparties and central securities depositories since the Financial Services and Markets Act 2023. Thursday's proposal extends the same template to systemic payment systems, including those built on digital settlement assets. It arrives via amendment to the Financial Services and Markets Bill, due for House of Lords debate on 7 and 9 September, and comes with a new accountability hook: the Bank will have to report to Parliament each year on how it has advanced the objective.

A mandate already being tested

The subordination clause — the Bank need not support innovation where doing so "would undermine financial stability" — is not abstract drafting. It describes a fight that has already happened once this year. In November the Bank proposed capping individual holdings of systemic sterling stablecoins at £20,000 and business holdings at £10 million, a stability-first guardrail against runs. Issuers and a House of Lords committee argued the caps were unenforceable across wallets and exchanges and incompatible with a usable payment instrument. By its June policy statement the Bank had dropped the individual caps altogether, replacing them with a £40 billion issuance ceiling per stablecoin and a backing regime of 70% short-dated gilts and 30% non-interest-bearing central bank deposits.

That retreat shows the innovation objective will not simply add a friendly gloss to work the Bank was already doing. It will be invoked, by industry and by ministers, whenever a stability-minded rule looks like it is slowing adoption — and the Bank, not the Treasury, will still decide where the line sits. Governor Andrew Bailey has separately warned of transatlantic friction over stablecoin redemption standards, arguing that laxer US rules for dollar tokens could import stress into the UK in a crisis, a reminder that the Bank's caution has roots beyond domestic lobbying.

None of Thursday's announcement specifies what "innovation" will be measured against, or what happens if the Bank's annual report to Parliament shows little progress. That is left to the Bill's passage through the Lords next month, and to the Bank's own rule-writing under the regime it is already building for stablecoins due to go live in 2027.
