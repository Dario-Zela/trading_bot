BEYOND THE TAPE · IRELAND · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-30 · 3 min read

Gordon Brown says a united Ireland is coming

Gordon Brown, pictured at a Policy Network conference, said in a Guardian interview that Irish reunification is inevitable "in the long term." — Policy Network / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Gordon Brown said Irish unity is inevitable "in the long term," complicating PM Andy Burnham's recent claim that a border poll is off the table.

Gordon Brown does not do backseat driving, he insisted this week — right before climbing into the passenger seat and grabbing the wheel.

In an interview with The Guardian's Saturday magazine, timed to the release of his new book, The Future Starts With Us, the former prime minister was asked whether the land border between Northern Ireland and the Republic meant unification simply made sense. His answer was unhurried but unambiguous: "Yes. Well, of course, in the long term, Ireland will be united." He called it "a long process where people have got to come together and find a way of agreeing," and drew a pointed contrast with Scotland, where he has long argued the economic and geographic case for staying in the UK is overwhelming. Northern Ireland, in his telling, is a different kind of case entirely.

Into Burnham's line of fire

The timing was not incidental. Brown's comments landed days after Prime Minister Andy Burnham said a united Ireland referendum was "off the table" — a remark that Downing Street has since been trying to walk back into something less absolute. Brown, who was Tony Blair's chancellor when the Belfast Agreement was signed in 1998 and prime minister in 2010 when policing and justice powers were devolved to Stormont, has more standing than most retired politicians to weigh in on this particular question. That he chose to do so, gently contradicting the sitting prime minister within days of Burnham's own remarks, is the real story here — not the forecast itself, which he offered no timeline, polling or mechanism for.

The reaction has split along familiar lines. Sinn Féin's Mary Lou McDonald said no prime minister has the right to "high-handedly" write the unity referendum provision out of the Good Friday Agreement. The DUP's Gavin Robinson took the opposite reading of Burnham's original remarks, saying they had "put to bed the nonsense" of unity-poll speculation. In Dublin, junior minister Niamh Smyth said she "categorically and fundamentally" disagreed with Burnham. Brown's own comments have simply given each side fresh ammunition rather than resolving anything.

What the agreement actually says

Under the Good Friday Agreement, it falls to the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland — currently Chris Bryant — to call a border poll, and there is a duty to do so if unification looks likely to command majority support. Any actual change would then need separate majorities in both Northern Ireland and the Republic. None of the ingredients for that test exist today: no fresh polling was cited alongside Brown's remarks, no economic modelling, no government working group. What exists is a well-regarded former PM using a book tour to nudge a debate that Burnham had just tried to close down.

The next marker to watch is not a poll or a bill but whether Burnham's government offers a fuller account of what "off the table" actually means in practice — and whether Brown's intervention pulls more Labour figures into a debate Number 10 would clearly rather avoid this side of an election.
