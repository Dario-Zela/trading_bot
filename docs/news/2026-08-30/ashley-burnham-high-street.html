Mike Ashley attacks Burnham's 'populist' High Street plans

Frasers Group founder Mike Ashley, who wrote to Downing Street calling the PM's high street plans populist and unworkable. — Johnvedwards / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Mike Ashley branded Burnham's high street rescue plan 'populist' and 'delusional' ahead of the 28 October Budget, echoing a wider business tax revolt.

Mike Ashley does not do quiet disagreement. The Frasers Group founder, never a man to let a government policy pass without a verdict, has written to Downing Street to tell Andy Burnham that his plan to revive Britain's high streets is populist window-dressing — good for a headline, bad for anyone actually running a shop.

The proposals in Ashley's sights are the centrepiece of the prime minister's retail pitch ahead of Chancellor John Healey's first Budget on 28 October: a review of business rates for pubs and hotels, a new levy on warehouses to fund lower rates for bricks-and-mortar retailers, a crackdown on "fake" discounting, plus the more consumer-facing sweeteners — a £2 bus fare cap and the removal of VAT from household electricity bills.

"Rather than address the real underlying issues... it is easier for you to pick topics which provide good media soundbites and the old salve of yet more regulation."

That is Ashley's assessment of the government's approach in the letter, which brands the warehouse levy "delusional" and warns that shortsighted or populist reactions to genuine business challenges are not the answer. His objection to the warehouse tax is straightforward economics as he sees it: warehouses are where a huge share of modern retail — his own online operations very much included — actually make their margin, and taxing them harder to subsidise the high street risks simply pushing costs onto the shopper the policy claims to protect. He is similarly unimpressed by Burnham's rhetoric on "rip-off Britain" discounting, arguing that markdowns are precisely the mechanism by which retailers make goods cheaper, not a trick played on customers.

Part of a wider grumble

Ashley is not shouting into a void. Earlier the same week, Phones 4u founder John Caudwell and the former Marks & Spencer and Asda chairman Lord Stuart Rose put their names to the "Stop the Creep" campaign, a business-founder coalition pressing the government to reverse the rise in employers' National Insurance, restore entrenched entrepreneurial tax reliefs, and rule out fresh levies on dividends, capital gains and business assets ahead of the Budget. Rose said he had "never been more concerned about the cost of doing business", with tax and regulation now "serious impediments to growth and employment".

Burnham, for his part, has told the Financial Times he intends to tackle the "challenging" cost of doing business and has tried to head off fears that October's Budget will bring another tax raid on firms already smarting from previous increases. That reassurance sits awkwardly next to a warehouse levy explicitly designed to raise money from one part of retail to subsidise another — the kind of redistribution that tends to please whoever isn't paying it and infuriate whoever is.

Noise, not yet a movement

What Ashley's letter amounts to, for now, is one very rich, very online retailer restating a view he has held for the better part of a decade: that business rates and regulatory tinkering are a distraction from the deeper structural problems — footfall, rents, online competition — that have hollowed out Britain's town centres. It is a familiar complaint from a familiar source, and it stops well short of the sector uniting against the government the way pension funds or gilt markets might.

What gives it weight is timing and company. Landing in the same fortnight as the Caudwell-Rose intervention, it reads less like an isolated grievance and more like an opening skirmish in the run-up to the Budget, with business groups trying to shape the narrative before Healey's numbers are even drawn up. Whether that pressure changes anything in the Treasury's plans is the more interesting question, and one that won't be answered until 28 October.

The next marker to watch is whether other major retailers — the ones without Ashley's taste for a public letter — put their names to similar warnings before then, or whether this stays a fight fought by a handful of outspoken billionaires while the rest of the industry keeps its head down.
