Royal Navy's Russian-vessel monitoring up 25% this year

HMS Somerset, the Type 23 frigate that led July's monitoring operations against Russian naval activity in UK waters. — LA(Phot) Abbie Gadd / UK Ministry of Defence, Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Royal Navy activations to monitor Russian warships and sanctioned shadow-fleet tankers rose 25% in the first seven months of 2026.

The Royal Navy activated its warships and patrol vessels 25% more often in the first seven months of 2026 than over the same period last year, as the task of shadowing Russian naval movements and sanctioned oil tankers around the British coast grows steadier and more demanding.

The figures, confirmed by the Ministry of Defence on 7 August and covering January to July, show a fleet increasingly stretched between two related jobs: tracking Russian warships and submarines transiting UK waters, and watching the so-called shadow fleet — ageing, opaquely owned tankers that move sanctioned Russian oil while dodging Western insurance and flag rules.

July alone accounted for 21 days of continuous operations. The frigate HMS Somerset, fresh from a four-month, 24,000-nautical-mile North Atlantic deployment, worked alongside the patrol vessels HMS Tyne, HMS Mersey and HMS Severn, backed by Wildcat and Merlin helicopters, to keep pace with Russian transits through the Channel and North Sea. Commander Matt Millyard, Somerset's commanding officer, said the deployment "demonstrates exactly what the Royal Navy exists to do — protect the UK and allies."

An escort that costs Moscow capacity

Much of the extra tempo traces back to a single Russian frigate, RFN Neustrashimy, which has spent much of 2026 stationed in the Channel and North Sea specifically to escort shadow-fleet tankers through recognised traffic lanes. On 20 July the frigate fired roughly 50 rounds — about 20 from its 100mm main gun and 30 from a 30mm battery — in gunnery drills outside UK and French territorial waters, monitored throughout by HMS Tyne from five nautical miles away. Lieutenant Commander Sam Fields, Tyne's commanding officer, said the ship was "ideally suited to this task" given its sustained presence and cooperation with allies.

That an escort ship is needed at all is the more telling detail. Tankers moving sanctioned Russian crude no longer transit quietly; they require a warship nearby, which soaks up a vessel Moscow might otherwise deploy elsewhere. In June, Royal Marines from 42 Commando and National Crime Agency officers boarded and detained the tanker Smyrtos in the Channel — Britain's first shadow-fleet interdiction — with the vessel's captain now facing trial. The UK has now sanctioned more than 500 vessels tied to the shadow network.

The Ministry of Defence links the pressure to a broader financial squeeze: Russian oil and gas revenue fell 24% year-on-year in 2025. Whether that decline holds through 2026 depends partly on how successfully Moscow can keep rerouting tankers around tightening UK and EU enforcement — and on whether allied navies can sustain a 25%-higher operating tempo without wearing down ships built for a smaller Cold War-era patrol load. The next data point comes with the MoD's autumn update on shadow-fleet interdictions, expected once the Smyrtos trial concludes.
