Burnham to abstain on assisted dying vote, breaking with Starmer

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose office confirmed he will not vote when the assisted dying bill returns to the Commons on 11 September. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Andy Burnham will abstain on the assisted dying bill's 11 September Commons vote, breaking with Keir Starmer, who voted for it twice as PM.

Andy Burnham has decided the one vote he won't cast as Prime Minister is the one that would define him most as a man from Merseyside who has watched his own views shift over two decades in public life.

Downing Street confirmed this week that Burnham will abstain when the Terminally Ill Adults (End of Life) Bill returns to the Commons on 11 September, telling reporters he does not want to "unduly influence the debate" from the despatch box. It is a conscience matter, he said, and Parliament's decision to make — his job is to implement whatever MPs decide, not steer them toward it.

The decision is notable mostly for who it isn't. Keir Starmer, the man Burnham replaced in Downing Street in July after a bruising Labour leadership contest, voted in favour of the bill both times it came before the Commons — at second reading in November 2024 and again at third reading last June, when it passed narrowly, 314 votes to 291. Starmer's yes was itself notable: a sitting Prime Minister putting his name behind a piece of deeply contested private members' legislation rather than staying above it. Burnham, by contrast, is choosing to stay above it entirely.

A bill that keeps coming back

The legislation itself has had an unusually long road. Sponsored originally by backbench Labour MP Kim Leadbeater, the bill would let terminally ill adults in England and Wales with fewer than six months to live apply for an assisted death, subject to sign-off from two doctors and an independent panel. It cleared the Commons last summer only to run aground in the House of Lords, where a small number of peers tabled hundreds of amendments and effectively talked out the session. With time up, the bill fell.

It is now back as a fresh bill for the 2026–27 session, reintroduced by Lauren Edwards MP and due its Commons stage on 11 September. Should it pass again, the Parliament Act means the same handful of peers won't be able to block it a second time — which is precisely why the coming vote matters more than the procedural label of "second reading" suggests.

Cabinet still split

Burnham's abstention doesn't paper over the divide inside his own government. Health Secretary Wes Streeting remains opposed, warning the bill risks diverting "time and money" from a palliative care system he considers under-resourced. Edwards has pushed back, arguing better end-of-life care and the right to choose assisted dying "can and must go hand in hand." Burnham has sat somewhere in between: he said last month that fixing the funding of palliative and social care ought to come before Parliament legislates on assisted dying, even while acknowledging his own view has moved, over the years, toward supporting the principle.

That evolution — mayor of Greater Manchester to backbencher to Prime Minister within a matter of weeks this summer — is part of what makes the abstention read as more than procedural caution. A free vote lets MPs vote their conscience without the whip; a Prime Minister declining to use his own vote is a step further, an explicit renunciation of the informal weight a PM's declared position carries over undecided colleagues.

Why it travels beyond Westminster

For an ostensibly domestic, moral-conscience question, the vote has drawn interest from life-sciences and healthcare investors watching for signals on how assisted-dying regulation might shape palliative care commissioning, hospice funding and the broader end-of-life care market in England and Wales. A government that won't state a preference is, in that narrow sense, a government whose regulatory direction on the issue remains genuinely unsettled — not a strategic ambiguity but an actual one. More broadly, a Prime Minister stepping back from a fight this consequential may embolden backbenchers weighing rebellion on other fronts, at a moment when Burnham's government is already governing without much room for error.

The vote itself lands on 11 September. Whether it passes will turn less on Burnham's absence from the lobby than on the roughly three dozen MPs still undecided since June — the same margin that separated 314 from 291 last time.
