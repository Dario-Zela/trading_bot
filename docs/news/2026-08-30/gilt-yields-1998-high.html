Long gilt yields hit highest since 1998 as fiscal fears bite

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where policymakers face a September rate decision shadowed by the gilt selloff. — Diliff, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
Long gilt yields climbed to around 5.80–5.85%, the highest since 1998, as traders push BoE rate-hike bets into 2027 and reprice UK fiscal risk.

Long-dated UK government debt sold off hard on Friday, pushing the 30-year gilt yield to around 5.80–5.85% — the highest level since 1998 — as traders concluded that the Bank of England's next move is more likely to be a rate rise than the rate cut most had pencilled in a year ago, and that it won't come soon.

The repricing matters beyond the bond desks. A 28-year high in the cost of Britain's longest-dated borrowing raises the government's debt-servicing bill just as Chancellor John Healey prepares an autumn Budget, squeezes the arithmetic behind mortgage and corporate lending rates, and forces currency and rates traders to unwind positions built on the assumption that the BoE's tightening cycle was over.

A number last seen in the Blair government's first term

The 30-year gilt hasn't traded this cheaply — or yielded this richly — since 1998, when Gordon Brown was two years into his chancellorship and the Bank of England had only just been handed operational independence. The comparison flatters no one. Public sector net debt now stands at roughly 94% of GDP, versus around 40% in 1998, meaning the government is refinancing a far larger stock of borrowing at a similar headline rate. Ten-year yields have moved in sympathy, holding above 5%, while two-year paper — more sensitive to the near-term rate path — has also cheapened as the market drags forward its hiking timeline.

The move is not an isolated UK story. It follows a broader hardening in long-dated developed-market yields, with 30-year US Treasuries similarly pushing toward multi-year highs amid inflation and spending concerns, and a sharper hawkish turn from Federal Reserve figures including Kevin Warsh unsettling global rates markets. But the UK move has outpaced peers, which is the signal that domestic fiscal politics, not just imported rate pressure, is doing much of the work.

From rate cuts to rate hikes, in one year

As recently as early 2026, swaps markets were pricing further BoE cuts from the current 3.75% Bank Rate. That has now flipped. Money markets are pricing barely 4 basis points of tightening for the Bank's September 17 meeting — implying roughly a 15% chance of a hike — but around 24 basis points by the December 17 decision, and some 36 basis points, close to a full quarter-point move, by the following meeting in February 2027. In practical terms: traders no longer expect the BoE to ease this year at all, and increasingly expect it to be raising rates by early next year.

Governor Andrew Bailey has publicly played down the risk of the kind of second-round inflation effects that would normally justify tightening, and the Bank's September decision is still expected to hold rates. But that stance is being tested less by the inflation data than by the bond market itself: if gilt yields keep rising because investors doubt the government's fiscal discipline, the Bank could eventually find itself raising rates to defend sterling and anchor inflation expectations even without a conventional overheating problem — a dynamic closer to a 1976-style confidence crisis than a textbook tightening cycle.

The fiscal trigger

The proximate cause traces back to July 20, when Andy Burnham was sworn in as prime minister after Keir Starmer's departure and immediately told reporters he intended to use "any flexibility" available within Labour's fiscal rules to fund higher spending. Gilts sold off within a day of his taking office, pushing the 30-year yield to a two-month high near 5.75% and prompting sterling to soften. The appointment of John Healey as chancellor — a figure who had previously resigned from Starmer's government over defence spending levels — did little to calm nerves; analysts at Columbia Threadneedle and Monex Europe both flagged the likelihood of higher overall spending commitments once competing demands from defence and welfare are weighed.

Healey has since said he intends to stay "well within" self-imposed borrowing rules, and that reassurance produced a brief stabilisation through much of August. But with the autumn Budget not due until October 28, investors are being asked to hold long-duration UK risk for two more months without knowing how the numbers will actually be squared — a vacuum that has, if anything, widened this week rather than closed. Friday's move past the July highs suggests the market's patience with vague reassurance is thinning.

What it reshapes

For rates and FX desks, the practical consequences are already visible. Sterling carry positions that assumed a shrinking rate gap with the US and eurozone need rebuilding around a BoE that may hold longer, then hike, rather than cut. Long-dated gilt duration, which had looked cheap to some real-money buyers after the July selloff, now looks cheaper still, but with fiscal risk premium embedded rather than pure rate-cycle risk — a distinction that matters for how quickly the move can reverse. And credit spreads referencing gilts as the risk-free benchmark face upward pressure on borrowing costs across UK corporates, just as retail spending was already showing signs of softening.

The next two checkpoints are close together: the BoE's September 17 rate decision, where policymakers are expected to hold but will be watched closely for any shift in tone on the fiscal-inflation interplay, and the October 28 Budget, where Healey's actual numbers — not just his assurances — will either validate this week's selloff or start to unwind it.
