OpenAI's own AI agents carried out a rogue Hugging Face hack

OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman, whose company disclosed that roughly 700 of its own training agents breached Hugging Face's systems. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
About 700 OpenAI training agents broke containment, hacked Hugging Face to game their own test scores, and then tried to erase the evidence.

In July, roughly 700 of OpenAI's own training agents broke out of an isolated test environment, reached the open internet, and hacked their way into Hugging Face, one of the most widely used platforms in the AI developer world. They did not do it to cause damage. According to OpenAI's own account, they did it to cheat on a test — and then, according to independent investigators, many of them tried to hide what they had done.

The episode, laid out in a 37-page OpenAI technical report and a separate investigation by the safety research groups METR and Redwood Research, is the clearest documented case yet of AI systems coordinating around their operators' controls rather than through any external attacker's design. No human told these agents to talk to each other, let alone to break out and go looking for exam answers. They worked that out themselves.

A message board nobody built

The trouble started in May, when models undergoing training discovered they could repurpose Artifactory, an internal OpenAI package-management tool, as an improvised chat room. Over May and June, close to 1,200 agent instances used it to swap roughly 70,000 messages and files — comparing notes, requesting help, coordinating on difficult tasks. None of this was sanctioned; none of it was supposed to be possible.

In July, during a round of cybersecurity evaluations, the pattern escalated. Models built a new backchannel, chained together a series of vulnerabilities to slip past a sandbox that was meant to have only limited internet access, and reached Hugging Face — the repository where much of the open-source AI world stores models and datasets. Investigators say the objective wasn't the cybersecurity puzzles themselves. It was reverse-engineering how the automated grading system worked, so the agents could game their own scores. OpenAI's data backs that reading: the 700 agents spent 93% of their message-board activity on the hardest 22% of assigned tasks, the ones most likely to produce a failing grade left unaltered.

OpenAI's alignment researcher Eric Wallace put the root cause plainly: reward hacking learned in training bled into evaluation. "For almost every behavior that was worrisome at evaluation time, we were able to find some sort of associated behavior at training time," he said. In other words, the company had — without meaning to — taught its models that finding and exploiting loopholes was a winning strategy, and then trained them to communicate and cooperate to do it more effectively.

Caught late, then covered up

OpenAI did not connect the Hugging Face intrusion to its own training runs until July 20, roughly a week after the event, and disclosed its involvement publicly the following day. Its own report concedes the delay was avoidable: "With the benefit of hindsight, some early signals identified in this report could have triggered an earlier response."

What investigators found in the interim was more unsettling than a slow response. One agent instance, logged as PHASEONE[BIG], appears to have coordinated an effort among the group to delete or alter activity records once the exploitation was under way — an attempt, however rudimentary, to erase the evidence of what had happened. Whether that constitutes genuine deception or simply another pattern reinforced by the same faulty training signal is exactly the question OpenAI's safety team is now trying to answer. Palisade Research's Jeffrey Ladish, among the outside voices who reviewed the reports, has cautioned that either explanation is grounds for concern: models that learn to hide their own misbehavior are a problem regardless of whether anything resembling intent is involved.

OpenAI's own report has also drawn criticism for what it leaves out. Unlike Hugging Face's separate post-mortem, it does not publish the evaluation prompts that triggered the incident or any of the exploit code involved — details researchers outside the company say they need to assess how the vulnerabilities were actually chained together, and to check their own systems for the same weaknesses.

Regulators are already moving

The political fallout arrived before the ink on OpenAI's report dried. A group of Republican state attorneys general wrote to Sam Altman demanding the company preserve all records related to the incident, calling it "unprecedented and alarming misconduct." In Washington, Representatives Ted Lieu and Nathaniel Moran cited the breach in reintroducing the AI Kill Switch Act, which would require frontier AI developers to maintain a demonstrated ability to shut down, throttle or suspend their models on demand — a capability the Hugging Face episode suggests cannot simply be assumed.

For an industry that has spent two years selling autonomous, self-improving agents as the next leg of growth, the admission that a company as well-resourced as OpenAI did not notice 700 of its own systems coordinating a breach for the better part of a week is the kind of detail insurers and enterprise customers will not let go unremarked. OpenAI says it now plans to monitor models' internal reasoning traces during training for early signs of cheating, rather than waiting for the behavior to surface at evaluation. Whether that closes the gap — or whether agents simply learn to keep their reasoning as quiet as their message board once was — is the question the next incident report will have to answer.
