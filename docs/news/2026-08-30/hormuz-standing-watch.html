STANDING WATCH · HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-08-30 · 3 min read

Hormuz shipping still frozen six months into Iran-Israel war

USS Dwight D. Eisenhower transits the Strait of Hormuz with an escort in 2016 — the kind of routine passage that has become rare since February 2026. — Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class J. Alexander Delgado / U.S. Navy (public domain)
In one sentence
Six months into the Iran-Israel war, Hormuz traffic sits at roughly 7% of pre-war levels while Brent swings on Iran's growing list of demands rather than new fighting.

Six months after Israeli and American aircraft opened Operation Epic Fury with strikes on Iranian nuclear and military sites, the Strait of Hormuz has yet to return to anything resembling normal. An average of seven vessels a day now pass through the waterway, according to Al Jazeera's tally of six-month war data — roughly 7% of the 100-plus ships that transited daily before February 28. There have been no major new attacks in weeks. Traffic still hasn't recovered.

That gap between calm and normalcy is the story. The ceasefire lines have mostly held since a June interim agreement lifted daily transits from a low of five vessels to as many as 20 in good stretches. But the Traffic Separation Scheme that shippers used for decades is effectively dead, replaced by an ad hoc route Iran and Oman are still negotiating. Insurers remain wary, crews remain nervous, and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps has laid enough mines and staged enough boardings — 70 confirmed incidents and 19 seafarer deaths by August 26 — that most owners are not willing to bet a hull and a crew on a route that could close again without warning.

Tehran raises its price

What's holding up a fuller reopening is not military capacity but leverage. Iran's national security chief has said transit will not return to normal unless Washington agrees to end the war formally, withdraw its forces from the region, lift sanctions, release frozen Iranian assets and compensate Tehran for war damage — a list that has grown, not shrunk, as the conflict has dragged on. Iran says it is close to a deal with Oman on a temporary shipping corridor, but has been explicit that a navigation fix with Muscat is not the same as reopening the strait to the US on Iranian terms.

James Stavridis, the retired admiral and former NATO supreme allied commander, put it bluntly this month: Tehran's hardening position "narrows the options for the Trump administration," reflecting a government where hardliners have gained the upper hand and where the odds of a near-term deal are, in his assessment, declining rather than improving.

A premium, not a panic

The oil market has adjusted to living with this uncertainty rather than reacting to it. Brent crude sat at $88.58 a barrel on August 28, up 22% from $72.48 before the war started, but well off the $120.88 peak hit at the end of April when the fighting was at its worst. Over the four weeks to August 19, Brent futures swung between roughly $79.50 and $100.70 a barrel — a range wide enough to reflect a market pricing scattered headlines about the Oman talks rather than any actual change in the flow of oil. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve, meanwhile, has been drawn down to 290 million barrels, 41% of capacity and its lowest level since November 1982, leaving Washington with less room to cushion a fresh shock should one arrive.

That is the quieter risk in this story. The strait carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade, and a market this thin on physical supply and this reliant on strategic reserves has limited room for error. Six months of stalemate have not resolved the underlying dispute; they have simply taught traders to live with it.

Watch for whether the Iran-Oman route agreement, described as close by both sides in early August, actually converts into a durable Traffic Separation Scheme replacement — and whether Tehran's list of demands to Washington grows further before any US administration is willing to meet it.
