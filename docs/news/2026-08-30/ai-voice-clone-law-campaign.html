Actors launch campaign for a UK AI voice-clone law

Hugh Bonneville, one of more than 80 performers backing the Save Our Voices Now campaign. — Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 80 UK performers, including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, want PM Burnham to legislate a statutory right to own one's voice against AI cloning.

More than 80 actors, comedians and audiobook narrators have written to Prime Minister Andy Burnham asking for something that sounds almost too basic to need legislating: the right to own your own voice.

The campaign, called Save Our Voices Now, is backed by Hugh Bonneville, Matt Lucas, Nicola Coughlan, Luke Evans, Jen Brister, Siobhán McSweeney and Pearl Mackie, among others. It wants a statutory right — held by every UK resident, not just working performers — to control whether an AI system can clone their voice. Alongside the open letter, organisers have launched a petition on the government's own petitions site, the standard route for forcing a subject onto the floor of the Commons if it clears 100,000 signatures.

"My voice is unique, it's mine, it's part of my identity. So is yours. That's worth speaking up about." — Hugh Bonneville

Why performers are worried

Voice-cloning tools have got good enough, and cheap enough, that a few seconds of audio is now sufficient to generate a convincing fake. Equity, the performers' union, says it has filed claims on behalf of more than 20 members whose voices it believes were used without permission — enough, the union says, that unauthorised cloning has become one of the main issues it now handles day to day. The problem isn't confined to actors: campaigners cite a figure of 28% of UK adults reporting they've been targeted by a voice-cloning scam, typically fraudsters impersonating a relative in distress.

Audiobook narrator Alice Sockett, one of the campaign's co-founders, has called the practice "an existential threat to our entire industry," pointing to narrators whose voices are used to generate synthetic audiobooks with no fee and no consent.

What the law would actually do

The campaign's ask is narrower than it might sound. Rather than a blanket ban on synthetic voices, it wants a personality right — similar to image rights in some jurisdictions — that requires consent before a voice is cloned and gives people standing to sue or demand takedown when it isn't. Organisers point to Denmark, which recently amended its copyright law to give individuals ownership over their face, body and voice, with the ability to force removal of unauthorised AI-generated content and to claim compensation. So far, the UK government's response has amounted to a promised consultation rather than a bill, leaving the timeline open.

That gap matters commercially. A statutory consent requirement would land directly on voice-synthesis startups and the media companies licensing their tools — forcing provenance checks, consent records and takedown processes that smaller developers may struggle to build quickly. If Westminster follows Denmark's lead, the practical effect is likely to favour better-capitalised players who can absorb compliance costs, while raising the bar for entrants relying on scraped or unlicensed training audio.

The next marker is the government's consultation, still without a published date, and whether the petition clears the 100,000-signature threshold that would guarantee a Commons debate. Until then, the campaign's leverage is star power rather than law.
