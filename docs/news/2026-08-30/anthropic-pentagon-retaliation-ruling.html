Judge rules Trump administration illegally retaliated against Anthropic

The Pentagon, whose supply-chain risk designation against Anthropic a federal judge ruled unlawful retaliation. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A federal judge ruled the Pentagon's 'supply chain risk' label on Anthropic was unlawful retaliation for refusing to drop limits on military surveillance and autonomous weapons use.

A federal judge has told the Pentagon it cannot punish an AI company for refusing to let its chatbot be used for autonomous weapons or domestic surveillance — at least not by branding the firm a national security threat and hoping nobody asks too many questions about why.

In a 59-page ruling issued Thursday night, US District Judge Rita Lin found that the Department of Defense's decision to label Anthropic a "supply chain risk" — a designation Congress built for firms suspected of ties to foreign adversaries — was unlawful retaliation against a company that had simply said no to the military's terms. The order came down hard: the designation was, Lin wrote, "arbitrary and capricious," issued without the constitutional process Anthropic was owed, and driven by motives the government is not allowed to act on.

A contract that ran into a red line

The dispute traces back to last summer, when the Pentagon awarded Anthropic — alongside Google, OpenAI and xAI — a contract worth up to $200 million to speed the military's adoption of AI tools. Anthropic's version came with conditions attached: its acceptable-use policy barred Claude from being deployed in autonomous weapons without a human in the loop, and from mass surveillance of Americans.

That clashed with what the Pentagon actually wanted, which was unrestricted access — "all lawful use cases," in the contract language the two sides fought over for months. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei held the line, telling the administration the company "cannot in good conscience accede" to stripping the safeguards, and arguing the restrictions touched only "high-level usage areas,” not the operational decisions commanders make in the field.

Blacklisted for saying no

The administration's answer came on February 27, when President Trump directed federal agencies to "immediately cease all use" of Anthropic's technology. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth followed by ordering the Pentagon to designate the company a supply-chain risk — a status that, under the Federal Acquisition Supply Chain Security Act, exists to let the government excise contractors and products believed to be conduits for foreign espionage or sabotage. It is not, on its face, a tool for settling policy disagreements with a vendor over how its own software gets used.

Anthropic called it exactly that in lawsuits filed in March in both San Francisco and Washington, describing the move as an "unlawful campaign of retaliation." At a July 30 hearing, Judge Lin signaled where she was headed, telling government lawyers their position looked "at odds ... with the First Amendment" and calling it "really troubling."

"Arrogance is not a legal basis"

Lin's written order lands on a specific, almost blunt finding: the Pentagon's actions, she wrote, "were based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its 'arrogance' in criticizing the government, not based on any articulable basis to believe that Anthropic would actually sabotage its model." Neither the Constitution nor the statute the administration invoked, she found, lets officials "impose sweeping penalties based principally on Anthropic's critique of the Administration's views."

What seems to have undercut the government's case most was its own inconsistency. Lin pointed out that officials were simultaneously trying to have it both ways — floating a plan to classify Anthropic as essential infrastructure under the Defense Production Act, continuing contract talks, and working with the company on cybersecurity projects, all while publicly branding it a threat to national security. A company genuinely suspected of sabotage is not usually also being courted for critical-infrastructure status.

Anthropic's response was measured: "We welcome the court's ruling that this supply chain risk designation was unlawful." The Pentagon is expected to appeal, and a parallel case Anthropic filed in Washington, D.C. is still working its way through the courts.

What the ruling actually settles

The practical effect, for now, is that the supply-chain risk label comes off and contractors barred from working with Anthropic can resume doing so. The larger effect is a marker in the sand: a court has said the government cannot reach for national-security tools to punish a company for exercising editorial control over its own product, even when that product is being sold to the military. Given how many AI firms now hold defense contracts with similar human-in-the-loop and surveillance carve-outs, the ruling gives them a legal argument — not just a moral one — the next time an agency threatens to make an example of a vendor that won't remove its guardrails. Whether it holds depends on the appeal, and on the D.C. case still ahead.
