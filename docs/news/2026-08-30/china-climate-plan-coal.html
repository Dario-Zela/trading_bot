CLIMATE · CHINA · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-30 · 5 min read

China's new climate plan lands alongside a coal-power surge

A coal-fired power plant in Shuozhou, Shanxi province — part of a fleet that added 30GW of new capacity in the first half of 2026 — Kleineolive / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Beijing's 2026-2030 climate plan sets economy-wide targets, even as 30GW of new coal capacity came online in the first half of 2026.

Beijing spent late July unveiling the most comprehensive climate policy document it has produced in over a decade. It spent the first half of the year connecting more new coal-fired power capacity to the grid than it had in the previous ten years combined. Both things are true, both happened under the same government, and together they say more about China's actual energy priorities than either headline does alone.

The five-year plan, released by the Ministry of Ecology and Environment together with 18 other central departments, sets out what officials call the "main policy instrument" for China's climate action through 2030: a target to cut carbon dioxide intensity — emissions per unit of GDP — by 17% over the period, a reaffirmed pledge to peak emissions before 2030, and a new goal to push electricity's share of final energy consumption to 35% by the end of the decade. It also commits to trimming 30 million tonnes of non-CO2 greenhouse gases, mainly methane from coal mines, by 2030.

None of that is new in substance. Analysts at Carbon Brief describe the plan as a consolidation exercise rather than a step up in ambition — it restates commitments Xi Jinping made at the United Nations climate summit last September, when he pledged to cut net greenhouse gas emissions 7-10% below peak levels by 2035 and lift non-fossil fuels to more than 30% of energy consumption. What makes the document notable is its scope: for the first time, a five-year plan builds a single target framework spanning CO2, other greenhouse gases, carbon markets and adaptation, rather than scattering climate goals across separate industrial and energy plans.

The coal counter-narrative

While the plan was being finalised, China's power sector was moving in the opposite direction. New research from the Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air (CREA) and Global Energy Monitor puts new coal-fired capacity commissioned in the first half of 2026 at 30 gigawatts — up 43% on the same period last year — against just 2.7 GW retired. Coal-fired generation itself rose 3.4% year-on-year even as individual plants ran fewer hours on average, evidence that new capacity is arriving faster than the system needs it.

A further 274 GW of coal plants sit in the pipeline, equivalent to 22% of China's existing operating fleet, which at roughly 1,270 GW already accounts for more than half of the world's coal power capacity. Much of what came online this year traces back to a wave of permits issued during the 2021-2025 plan period, when 112.8 GW of coal capacity was approved in 2023 alone — projects now simply reaching completion on schedule, regardless of what the new climate plan says about the years ahead.

"Coal capacity is still growing because of legacy approvals and policy protections, even as plants run fewer hours," said Qi Qin, a CREA China analyst. Christine Shearer of Global Energy Monitor put it more bluntly: new coal plants keep entering operation "supported by market mechanisms that guarantee coal both capacity payments and significant electricity sales."

Paid to sit idle

That reference to capacity payments points to the real mechanism at work. China's National Development and Reform Commission revised its capacity-payment rules this year, raising the fixed-payment share from 30% to 50% of a plant's costs and rebranding the scheme the "reliable capacity compensation mechanism." It now extends to batteries and gas alongside coal, but coal remains its biggest beneficiary — plants are effectively paid to exist as backup generation for a grid managing record but volatile renewable output, whether or not they burn much fuel.

The volatility is real. Wind and solar curtailment — clean electricity generated but discarded because the grid can't absorb it — rose to 360 terawatt-hours in the first half of 2026, up 49% year-on-year, and by itself exceeded the entire increase in power demand over the period. Beijing's stated logic is that firm capacity, chiefly coal, is needed to back up intermittent renewables and cover peak demand during heatwaves and cold snaps until storage and grid infrastructure catch up. Critics, including researchers at CREA, counter that the country is building redundancy it will have to strand: paying to keep plants standing while wasting the very clean power those plants were meant to complement.

Two plans, one government

The tension is not hidden — it's arguably by design. The new climate plan explicitly pledges to "peak coal use" during 2026-2030, an implicit admission that coal use is still rising and needs to be capped rather than cut. That framing lets Beijing claim climate credentials — record renewable build-out, a 17% carbon-intensity cut, an economy-wide emissions ceiling — while its energy planners keep approving coal as insurance against blackouts, grid instability and the political cost of an outage during a heatwave.

For investors, the read-through is less about whether China is abandoning its climate targets — it isn't — and more about pace and sequencing. Chinese utilities and coal-plant operators are locking in another five years of guaranteed capacity revenue, a floor under coal-sector valuations that pure decarbonisation narratives tend to miss. Renewables developers, meanwhile, are absorbing the cost of curtailment: generation that gets built, connected and then discarded doesn't earn. Both dynamics can persist simultaneously, and the new plan does nothing to resolve which one dominates as the decade wears on.

The next test comes at the UN climate conference, where China's follow-through on its 2035 pledge — the 7-10% emissions cut below peak, the 30%-plus non-fossil energy share — will be measured against whatever the coal pipeline data shows for the second half of 2026. CREA and Global Energy Monitor are expected to publish updated fleet figures early next year; a pipeline still running at 274 GW by then would suggest Beijing's "peak coal use" pledge is arriving later, and at a higher plateau, than the plan's language implies.
