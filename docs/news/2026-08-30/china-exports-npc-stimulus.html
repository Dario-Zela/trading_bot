China's exports hold up as NPC weighs fresh stimulus

Container terminals like Yantian have handled the surge in Chinese exports, increasingly driven by AI-linked electronics and semiconductors. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's exports jumped 23.9% in July, but weak retail sales, investment and a PBOC-managed yuan point to a domestic economy the NPC's August 25-28 session may need to address.

China's factories are shipping more than ever. Its households are spending less than forecast. And this week, the country's top legislature sat down in Beijing for a meeting that will tell investors which of those two facts Beijing intends to act on.

Exports rose 23.9% in July from a year earlier, customs data show, beating a Reuters poll forecast of 22.2% and extending a run that has pushed the first seven months of 2026 to roughly $2.52 trillion in outbound goods — up about 18.5% on the same period last year. Total trade, imports included, hit 30.13 trillion yuan ($4.46 trillion), a 17.3% gain. On paper, that is not an economy that needs rescuing.

The export number's fine print

The headline growth rate is real, but it is not evenly spread. High-tech exports climbed 40.7% over the first seven months, with semiconductor shipments nearly doubling in value and industrial robots, 3D printers and electric-vehicle components leading the charge — output tied to the global AI infrastructure build-out rather than broad-based demand for Chinese consumer goods. Some of July's strength also reflects front-loading: exporters rushing containers out before a new 12.5% US tariff took effect late in the month, replacing a temporary 10% levy that had lapsed. Exports to the EU rose 18.5% and to the US 13.9%, but ASEAN (34.6%), South Korea (42.6%) and Taiwan (43.7%) grew far faster — a reminder that China's trade engine increasingly runs through its own regional supply chains rather than directly into Western consumers. The trade surplus with the EU alone widened to a record $32.9 billion in July, intensifying a dispute Brussels has been trying to manage for over a year.

Where the strength runs out

Domestically, the picture published on August 17 was softer than expected on every major line. Retail sales grew just 0.6% in July, against a Bloomberg-polled forecast of 1.5% and down from June's 1%. Industrial output rose 4.5%, missing a 4.8% forecast and slowing from June's 5.3%. Fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% over the first seven months, a deeper contraction than the 5.7% drop recorded through June, and auto sales fell for a tenth straight month. Three typhoons disrupted factory schedules during the month, but analysts point to a more structural problem: Citi noted that daily spending under Beijing's consumer trade-in subsidy scheme dropped to 6.3 billion yuan in July from 9 billion yuan in June, suggesting the boost from that programme is fading faster than households are picking up the slack on their own.

A currency caught between two stories

The yuan sits at the crossing point of these two narratives, and the People's Bank of China has been leaning on it. Having risen roughly 4% against the dollar this year — one of Asia's best performers — the currency touched a three-and-a-half-year high of 6.7192 on August 21. The PBOC responded on August 20 by setting its daily reference rate at its widest weak-side deviation from market estimates in roughly six months, a signal traders read not as an attempt to reverse the yuan's climb but to slow it. The central bank has repeatedly set weaker-than-expected fixings since November 2025. The logic is straightforward: a stronger yuan makes Chinese goods pricier abroad just as export volumes are doing the heaviest lifting for growth, and just as the domestic data confirms the economy needs that lifting more than ever.

What the NPC is actually voting on

This is the backdrop to the National People's Congress Standing Committee's 24th session, running August 25–28 in the Great Hall of the People. The published agenda covers ten legislative bills and five oversight reports, including the State Council's mid-year review of the 2026 development plan, its report on 2026 budget execution, and a separate report on government debt management for 2025. That combination matters to markets because it is precisely the kind of agenda that, in past sessions, has preceded actual changes to bond issuance quotas or incremental fiscal measures — as opposed to sessions confined to personnel appointments or unrelated legislation, which have typically passed without moving rates or commodities. Nothing on the public schedule guarantees a stimulus announcement; investors are reading the agenda's shape, not a promise, and the distinction matters for anyone positioning ahead of the readout.

The committee's mid-year budget report is also an opportunity for Beijing to reveal how much fiscal room remains after a first half in which government debt issuance already ran hot. If the Standing Committee moves on bond quotas or signals additional spending toward consumption support — rather than the export and infrastructure channels that have carried growth so far — it would mark a shift toward the household side of the economy that the July data suggests is overdue.

The session closes August 28, with state media typically publishing a readout within a day. Until then, the trade figures and the yuan fixing are the two data points doing the talking — one flattering, one cautionary — and neither settles the question the NPC now has to answer.
