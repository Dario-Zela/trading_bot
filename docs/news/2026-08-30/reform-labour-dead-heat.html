Reform and Labour locked in a dead heat under PM Burnham

Andy Burnham, six weeks into his premiership, now leads Reform UK by just 1.7 points in the polling average. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Labour's lead over Reform has shrunk to 1.7 points under PM Burnham, and gilt markets — already at 1998-high yields — are pricing the widening range of election outcomes.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for six weeks, and the polling gap that once separated Labour from Reform UK — eight to twelve points at its widest under Keir Starmer — has all but closed. The PollCheck seven-poll moving average puts Labour on 26.3% and Reform on 24.6%, a 1.7-point margin that sits comfortably inside the noise of individual surveys. YouGov, Ipsos and PollCheck all show the same convergence, which rules out the usual excuse — one rogue pollster — and leaves a genuine two-horse race where, eighteen months ago, Reform looked to be running away with it.

For a markets desk, the interesting part isn't the horse race. It's what a viable Reform government, or no government with a workable majority, would mean for gilts, sterling and the regulatory settings that price UK risk.

The numbers

Ipsos's late-July fieldwork had Labour on 28% to Reform's 25%, with the Conservatives a distant third on 18%. YouGov's August 9–10 poll was tighter still — Labour 24%, Reform 22%, Conservatives 21% — a swing that put Labour ahead in that pollster's series for the first time since Reform took the lead in early 2025. Patrick English, YouGov's head of political research, credited Burnham's personal ratings rather than any policy breakthrough: the prime minister "leads the favourability ratings," he said, even as voters "still believe the government is not addressing their concerns over the cost of living and immigration."

PollCheck's seat model, built off the moving average, currently projects Labour as the largest party with 259 seats — 67 short of a majority — against 164 for Reform, 93 for the Conservatives and 65 for the Liberal Democrats. No combination of two of those blocs produces a comfortable working majority. That is the number that matters more than any single vote share: a hung parliament, not a Reform government, is the modal outcome the model is pricing.

Why bond desks are watching a poll

This isn't the first time Burnham has moved gilt prices. His leadership challenge to Starmer in May sent 30-year yields up as much as 20 basis points in a session, to 5.86% — the highest since 1998 — on the view that a Burnham premiership would mean looser fiscal discipline and more borrowing than the market had priced under Starmer. That repricing has only partly unwound; long gilt yields remain near those 1998 highs, a story covered elsewhere in this edition. The mechanism repeats itself now in reverse-gear form: a hung-parliament scenario raises the odds of a minority or coalition government needing Reform's votes, or facing a second election within eighteen months, either of which complicates the ten-year fiscal projections the Office for Budget Responsibility uses to certify the government's spending plans against its own rules.

Sterling has so far been calmer than gilts, trading in a narrower band than the political headlines might suggest — but currency desks tend to lag bond desks in pricing UK political risk, not lead them. The precedent from May and June, when Burnham's rise first became plausible, is that gilts move first and sterling catches up once a scenario looks closer to a plurality outcome than a tail risk.

What a closer race changes

The policy areas most exposed are the ones where Labour and Reform diverge sharply rather than by degree: energy-price regulation, where Reform has proposed unwinding elements of the transition levy; corporate tax, where Reform's platform undercuts Labour's revenue assumptions; and financial oversight, where a minority Burnham government reliant on smaller parties would have less room to legislate than the 172-seat majority Labour won in 2024. Investors pricing UK risk over a five-to-ten-year horizon are, in effect, pricing the probability distribution across those three outcomes — solid Labour majority, hung parliament, Reform-led government — and that distribution has visibly widened since Starmer's resignation on 22 June.

None of this is imminent. There is no election scheduled, and Burnham's own approval rating — 29%, against 37% for Starmer at the same point in his premiership — suggests a governing party still finding its footing rather than one cruising to a mandate. But the repricing in the bond market this year shows that gilt investors are not waiting for a vote to start adjusting. The next data points worth watching are PollCheck's seat model after any further movement in the average, and whether 30-year gilt yields test fresh highs beyond the 1998 level if the polling gap narrows further before the autumn Budget.
