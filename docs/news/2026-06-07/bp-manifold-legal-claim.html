Ousted BP chairman prepares legal claim for millions in unpaid dismissal dues

BP's global headquarters at 1 St James's Square, Westminster, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Albert Manifold has engaged Mishcon de Reya to pursue a legal claim against BP for what he believes are millions of pounds in unpaid dismissal dues after his abrupt May exit.

Albert Manifold, the Irish executive who lasted barely eight months as chairman of BP before being shown the door in May, is preparing a formal legal claim against the oil company for what he contends are millions of pounds owed under his dismissal terms, according to a report in The Daily Telegraph.

Manifold, 63, has retained Mishcon de Reya, the London law firm, to advise him. The chairman's role at BP carries an annual fee of £1 million — and his contract's terms around notice and severance are now the focus of the dispute. He was notified of his removal only hours before BP disclosed the decision to the stock market on 26 May.

A swift and contested exit

BP's board acted unanimously, citing "serious concerns related to important governance standards, oversight and conduct." Reports subsequently pointed to allegations of bullying, a "volcanic" temper and the use of personal email accounts for board correspondence — a breach of company policy, BP said, though people close to Manifold argued the personal-account issue arose from a known fault in BP's own systems and a workaround that BP's company secretary office had routinely facilitated.

Manifold did not take the characterisation lying down. "I dispute entirely the characterisation of my conduct and I will not allow a false narrative to go unchallenged," he said within days of his removal. He added that he would not allow "lies" about his time at BP to go unanswered.

The case now turns on whether BP can invoke misconduct provisions to withhold pay that Manifold believes he is contractually owed. The company has a recent and instructive precedent on its side: when former chief executive Bernard Looney was dismissed in December 2023 after the board concluded he had "knowingly misled" it over personal relationships with colleagues, Looney forfeited up to £32.4 million in unvested awards and deferred bonuses. BP applied discretionary clawback to recover cash already paid.

Manifold's lawyers will likely contest both the conduct characterisation itself and its sufficiency to trigger forfeiture. A chairman's contract is structurally different from a CEO's, with a thinner long-term incentive component, meaning the dispute may turn more directly on notice pay and unpaid fees for the remainder of an agreed term than on unvested equity.

The dismissal landed heavily on BP's share price regardless: the stock fell 3.5 per cent on the day of the announcement, wiping roughly £5 billion from the company's market capitalisation over two sessions — a striking toll for a governance action the board framed as routine house-keeping.

Manifold had been appointed in July 2025 to succeed Helge Lund, bringing a reputation built over 28 years at CRH, the Irish building materials group, where he served as chief executive from 2014 until 2024 and oversaw its New York listing and a decade of acquisitive growth.

The next significant marker will be whether BP and Manifold reach a negotiated settlement before any claim is formally filed — or whether the dispute surfaces in court, bringing the underlying conduct allegations into the public record in ways neither side is likely to relish.
