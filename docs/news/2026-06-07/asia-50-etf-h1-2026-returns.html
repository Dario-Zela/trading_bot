Asia 50 ETF posts 53% gain in five months, leaving developed markets well behind

One of TSMC's fabrication facilities in Taichung's Central Taiwan Science Park — the company alone accounts for more than 22% of the iShares Asia 50 ETF. — Briáxis F. Mendes / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AIA's 53% gain in five months is less an Asia story than a TSMC-and-AI-semiconductor story, with China internet holdings acting as a partial drag.

The numbers are hard to argue with. A $10,000 stake in the iShares Asia 50 ETF (NYSEARCA: AIA) placed on the last trading day of 2025 was worth roughly $15,267 by June 3, 2026 — a 52.67% return in five months, against 10.61% for the S&P 500 tracker SPY over the same stretch. For anyone wondering why Asia has begun to look different in a global equity allocation, this half-year provides the exhibit.

But the return obscures the mechanism. The AIA ETF is not a broad survey of Asian economic activity. It is, in practice, a heavily concentrated bet on semiconductor hardware — one that happened to coincide with the most intense period of AI-driven chip demand the industry has recorded.

A semiconductor story in regional clothing

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSMC) alone accounts for more than 22% of the fund's assets and gained roughly 44% year-to-date. Samsung Electronics (12.7%) and SK Hynix (4.2%) add further weight, meaning a single theme — chipmaking for AI infrastructure — underlies close to 40% of the portfolio before counting MediaTek, Hon Hai, or UMC.

TSMC's first-quarter results stripped away any ambiguity about the driver. Revenue reached NT$1.13 trillion, net income rose 58% year-over-year, and management cited "surging demand for advanced AI and high-performance computing chips" as the operative phrase. Second-quarter guidance of $39–40.2 billion — a step up again — carried the same message. Advanced nodes (7nm and below) now account for 74% of wafer revenue; high-performance computing is 61% of total sales. TSMC is the chokepoint through which the current AI buildout must pass, and its stock price reflects exactly that.

South Korea's market told a parallel story. The Kospi surged more than 80% in 2026, driven largely by SK Hynix, whose AI memory chips are essential to the same data-centre construction sustaining TSMC's order books. Goldman Sachs, reviewing the region's outperformance in May, was blunt: "It's the AI hardware theme that's clearly what is propelling things."

The drag that offsets

The fund's China internet allocations worked against it. Alibaba — a 4.4% holding — fell 13.2% in the period; NetEase shed roughly 10%. Alibaba's most recent quarter showed revenue growth of only 3% and a $123 million operating loss, a reminder that Chinese consumer internet is not the 2026 China story. Financial names partially compensated: HSBC gained 23% and Asian banks, insurers and exchanges collectively added around 12%.

The net effect is an ETF that functions, as one analysis put it, as "a leveraged TSM trade with a Samsung kicker" rather than a diversified Asian index. The top ten holdings account for nearly 68% of the fund — a concentration level that explains both the upside and the potential speed of any reversal.

What to watch

The thesis for continued outperformance rests on three variables that AIA investors cannot diversify away: the pace of AI capital spending by hyperscalers, the Taiwan Strait's continued absence from the headlines, and whether Chinese internet stabilises or deteriorates further. The clearest near-term signal arrives in mid-July, when TSMC reports its second-quarter results. Management's tone on demand visibility into late 2026 — and whether AI chip orders remain supply-constrained or begin to ease — will indicate whether this exceptional half-year is the opening leg of something durable or a momentum trade approaching its limits.
