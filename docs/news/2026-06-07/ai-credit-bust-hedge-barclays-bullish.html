Credit heavyweights bet on an AI bust while equity desks still cry buy

Server infrastructure at CERN, Geneva — the kind of hardware now financed by a record wave of hyperscaler bond issuance — Florian Hirzinger / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
DoubleLine and Oaktree are buying bust-proof credit as AI bond issuance hits $155bn while Barclays calls equities higher but flags the rally as dangerously crowded.

The same technology wave is producing two very different market positions. On equity desks, the dominant posture remains bullish — semiconductor and AI names have carried global indices to fresh highs this year. In credit, a quieter but more pointed conversation is under way: some of the largest fixed-income investors in the world are positioning for the eventual unravelling of exactly that trade.

That divergence crystallised this week when Bloomberg reporting confirmed that DoubleLine Capital LP and Oaktree Capital Management — two of the most closely watched credit managers globally — are actively buying debt designed to hold up when an AI-driven credit cycle eventually turns. At roughly the same moment, Barclays strategists published analysis maintaining a bullish equity call while acknowledging that positioning in artificial intelligence and semiconductor stocks had grown "increasingly stretched."

The juxtaposition is not accidental. It describes a market in which the same facts — torrential capital spending, parabolic stock moves, record bond issuance — are producing genuinely different conclusions depending on which part of the capital structure one is reading.

"100% probability": DoubleLine names the bubble

Robert Cohen, portfolio manager and director of global developed credit at DoubleLine, did not equivocate when asked about an AI debt bubble at the Bloomberg Global Credit Forum in New York on June 3. "What's the probability that we will be in an AI bubble?" he said. "I'll put maybe 100% on that."

Cohen's logic draws on credit history rather than technology prognostication. He defines a credit bubble as the moment investors provide debt financing to companies that need real growth just to service their obligations — a condition he argued will eventually arrive in AI, as it did in railroads and the dot-com build-out. His prescription was structural: seek credits that can survive through protective covenants or strong underlying balance sheets. Debt with decades to maturity is particularly exposed, he noted, because current technology may be obsolete long before the paper matures.

The hedge is already live. DoubleLine has been adding positions expected to outperform in a credit dislocation scenario.

Oaktree co-founder Howard Marks has made a related argument from a different angle, warning that investors may be systematically underestimating disruption risk embedded in AI — and that, for a lender, the asymmetry is unfavourable. Equity holders participate in the upside when an AI-backed company wins; a lender's best outcome is still just the money back at a fixed rate, while the downside is full exposure to a disrupted borrower.

Oaktree's co-portfolio manager in private credit, Christina Lee, described the firm's approach as deliberate selectivity. "We need to be selective because winners and losers remain unclear," she said. The firm carries $1.8 billion in available liquidity as of March 2026 and a leverage ratio of 0.57 times net debt to equity — a conservative posture for a manager of its scale.

The debt machine behind the build-out

The scale of the issuance helps explain the concern. US hyperscalers — the major cloud and AI infrastructure groups — sold more than $155 billion in unsecured bonds during the period examined, a 45% increase over the prior full year. Bloomberg Intelligence projects total AI-related capital expenditure over the next five years at $5 trillion globally. Morgan Stanley estimates data centre spending between 2025 and 2028 alone will reach $3 trillion, with roughly half financed through private credit markets.

The specific risk Cohen and Oaktree flag is not aggregate size but structure and timing. Data centres take years to build. Many projects launched simultaneously during the first phase of the AI investment wave. That combination produces a classic overbuilding scenario: supply arriving in a glut, with no guarantee that demand — or the underlying technology — remains constant over the life of the debt. A great deal of the paper sold to fund this construction will not mature for decades. By then, the infrastructure could be at several generations' remove from the models it was originally built to run.

Equities still climbing — but Barclays sees crowded exits

Barclays equity strategists led by Emmanuel Cau take a different, though not incompatible, view of the same moment. In a note published this week, they maintained a constructive overall stance on global equities — raising their European earnings growth forecast to 10% for 2026 and setting a STOXX 600 "peace target" of 670 — while flagging AI and semiconductor names as visibly overcrowded.

The numbers support the caution. The MSCI World Semiconductors index has risen roughly 50% over two months, the second-highest two-month reading since November 2001. Cau described momentum in AI and semiconductor names as "increasingly stretched" and characterised the sector dynamic broadly as a "gold rush." "The combination of frothy technicals and a catalyst-heavy June suggests that the chances of a tactical pullback, especially in the narrow momentum space, cannot be dismissed," the note read.

To be clear, Barclays is not calling the rally over. The position is temporally specific: the bank sees near-term structural vulnerabilities — heavy hedge fund and CTA positioning, a busy IPO calendar, weak summer trading patterns — that could produce sharp rotational pressure even inside a continuing bull market. Software, aerospace and defence, and consumer discretionary names are among the cited beneficiaries of any rotation out of the concentrated AI trade.

A concrete near-term catalyst Barclays names explicitly: Kevin Warsh's first Federal Open Market Committee meeting as chair, scheduled for June 17. Any surprise on rates or forward guidance would arrive into an equity market already carrying unusually concentrated AI exposure.

What the credit signal says about the equity story

The insight embedded in this week's positioning news concerns the distribution of conviction. Equity markets are pricing continued AI-driven earnings growth. Some of the most experienced credit allocators are pricing the possibility that the debt underpinning that growth will eventually crack.

These two views can coexist — and typically do — for a stretch. During the late dot-com period, equities continued rising even as credit analysts began working through the leverage maths on overextended telecoms borrowers. The eventual convergence was not gradual.

What to watch: the pace of investment-grade AI-related issuance heading into the second half of 2026; the outcome of Warsh's June 17 FOMC debut; and whether the hyperscalers' capex guidance — currently running at combined hundreds of billions for the year — holds firm in upcoming quarterly results. If guidance softens, the credit market's defensive positioning will look prescient. If it holds, the equity bulls will have their ratification. Either way, the gap between the two camps is now formally on the record.
