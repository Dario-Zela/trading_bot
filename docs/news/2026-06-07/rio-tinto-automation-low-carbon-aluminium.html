Rio Tinto pairs autonomous drilling with low-carbon aluminium in twin growth push

Rio Tinto's Complexe Arvida aluminium facilities in Saguenay, Quebec, where a $1.5bn AP60 expansion is being commissioned through 2026. — Khayman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto's $1.5bn Quebec AP60 smelter and a new Sandvik autonomous-drilling pact are parallel bets on premium pricing and lower costs — with capex yet to be justified by disclosed returns.

Rio Tinto Group announced two headline initiatives this week that, taken together, sketch its near-term growth story: a formal partnership with Sandvik to extend autonomous drilling across its open-pit operations, and the commissioning of a $1.5bn expansion at its AP60 low-carbon aluminium smelter in Saguenay, Quebec.

The drilling deal pairs Rio Tinto's Autonomous Drilling System — the ADS™ — with Sandvik's AutoMine® platform and i-series surface rigs. Testing begins at Sandvik's own pit in Finland before trials move to Western Australian operations. Rio Tinto already runs 40 autonomous drills across seven Pilbara mine sites, all monitored from a Perth operations centre roughly 1,500 kilometres away; the new arrangement adds a second hardware platform and multi-rig capability rather than launching the concept from scratch. "Building on AutoMine® and our i-series platform, we are proud to extend our long-standing relationship with Rio Tinto," said Petri Virrankoski, Sandvik's president of surface drilling.

The Quebec project carries the bigger dollar sign. Commissioning of 96 new AP60 reduction cells at Complexe Arvida began in March; all pots are expected operational by year-end. The headline figures: 160,000 additional metric tonnes of primary aluminium per year, lifting total AP60 output at the site to 220,000 tonnes. The older Arvida potrooms — running technology now measured in decades — close this month. Jérôme Pécresse, Rio Tinto's aluminium and lithium chief executive, described the project as "the first major primary aluminum development in the western world in more than a decade" and said the company is "strongly positioned for decades to come."

The premium that has to materialise

The commercial logic rests on a pricing assumption: that buyers will pay a durable spread for aluminium with documented low-carbon credentials. AP60, powered by Quebec's hydroelectric grid, produces roughly 1.6 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per tonne of metal — about half the intensity of the retiring potrooms, and well below the global average. That positions output for the certified procurement programmes that Mercedes-Benz, Apple, and Volvo, among others, have built for supply-chain decarbonisation. Spot premiums for verified low-carbon aluminium have ranged from $20 to $150 per tonne over standard LME price, depending on certification method and buyer. At the upper end of that band, incremental revenue on 160,000 new tonnes approaches $24m a year — real money, but modest against a $1.5bn capital outlay.

Rio Tinto has not disclosed payback assumptions for the Quebec build, nor any capex figure for the Sandvik drilling programme. What it can point to: the AP60 expansion is the first new primary smelter capacity built in the western world in over a decade, a structural gap that lends credibility to the premium thesis if green demand holds.

Watch the AP60 ramp through the second half of 2026. Supply agreements at a material low-carbon premium before year-end would validate the pricing logic. On the automation side, the Finland trial results — expected before any commercial Pilbara deployment — are the first concrete signal of whether the Sandvik interoperability holds under operating conditions.
