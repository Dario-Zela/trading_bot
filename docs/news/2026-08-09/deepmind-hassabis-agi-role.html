DeepMind's Hassabis steps back from day-to-day role to lead Alphabet's AGI push

Demis Hassabis, DeepMind co-founder, pictured after receiving the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for AlphaFold's protein-structure work. — John Sears / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Hassabis steps back from running DeepMind day-to-day to chase AGI as Alphabet's chief scientist; Alphabet shares fell 4% on the news.

Demis Hassabis is giving up the job of running Google DeepMind day to day. The DeepMind co-founder and chief executive is moving to become chair of the unit and chief scientist of Alphabet as a whole, handing daily operations to Koray Kavukcuoglu, DeepMind's chief technology officer of 13 years. The stated reason: Hassabis wants to spend his time on artificial general intelligence — AI that can match or exceed human ability across most cognitive tasks — rather than on running a several-thousand-person research organisation.

"I've decided that now is the right time for me to hand over my day-to-day operational responsibilities at GDM, so that I have the time and space to focus on the big picture," Hassabis wrote in announcing the move. "I've been working towards AGI my whole life and now, like many of you, I feel it is close at hand."

In one sentence: Demis Hassabis becomes DeepMind chair and Alphabet chief scientist to focus on AGI strategy and Isomorphic Labs, handing daily operations to CTO Koray Kavukcuoglu.

A drift that became formal

According to Semafor's reporting, this was not a sudden move — Hassabis had been quietly shifting responsibility for Gemini's development and DeepMind's consumer AI push onto Kavukcuoglu for roughly a year before the title changed. People close to the matter describe someone who never much enjoyed being a conventional tech executive, and who was spending an increasing share of his week on AI governance and safety discussions with governments — Hassabis attended the recent G7 summit in that capacity — rather than product roadmaps.

Kavukcuoglu now becomes senior vice president of Google DeepMind, reporting directly to Alphabet chief executive Sundar Pichai rather than holding a standalone CEO title of his own. His remit covers Gemini model development, frontier AI research, the Gemini consumer app, and Google's AI developer platforms — effectively the entire commercial and technical engine room of the company's AI effort. He joined DeepMind in its early years and has worked alongside Hassabis for more than a decade, leading breakthroughs including the WaveNet speech-generation system and the DQN reinforcement-learning work that helped establish DeepMind's reputation before the Google acquisition.

The reshuffle did not stop there. Jeff Dean, Google's chief scientist and a 27-year veteran of the company, is also leaving — not for a rival, but to launch an independent public-benefit company called Discovery Loop with longtime collaborator Sanjay Ghemawat, focused on applying AI to scientific and engineering problems. Google is joining as a founding investor and cloud partner, and Pichai has characterised the exit as amicable. Even so, two of the most senior technical figures at the company are leaving prominent operating roles in the same week.

Chasing AGI, and a cure for cancer

Hassabis's new brief is explicitly about the long game. As Alphabet's chief scientist he will advise across the company's model and research efforts, but he keeps direct control of one thing: Isomorphic Labs, the London-based drug-discovery venture he founded in 2021 that applies DeepMind's protein-structure work — the AlphaFold research that won Hassabis and colleague John Jumper the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry — to finding new medicines. In his announcement, Hassabis argued that AI "should help finally cure diseases like cancer," framing Isomorphic as the practical, near-term proof point for AGI-adjacent research while the harder questions of general intelligence and its governance play out over a longer horizon.

That framing matters for how Alphabet is telling investors to think about the money it pours into DeepMind. Rather than a chief executive optimising Gemini's roadmap against OpenAI and Anthropic quarter by quarter, the company now has its founding scientist elevated to a role explicitly about strategy at the frontier — AGI readiness, safety, and where the technology goes over years rather than product cycles — while an operator with deep technical credibility runs the machine that ships products and generates revenue.

Markets read it as a risk, not a rupture

Investors did not shrug this off. Alphabet shares fell around 4%, to roughly $362, on the day of the announcement — a reaction that reflects unease at losing two senior AI figures from operating roles simultaneously, at a moment when the company is trying to demonstrate it can keep pace with OpenAI and Anthropic in model capability and enterprise adoption. But the sell-off was measured rather than panicked: Alphabet's cloud and AI-infused ad business continues to grow faster than its largest rivals' comparable segments, giving the market a reason to treat this as a governance and succession question rather than a sign the underlying business is faltering.

The test now shifts to execution. Kavukcuoglu inherits a Gemini roadmap under competitive pressure from OpenAI's and Anthropic's frontier models, plus the day-to-day judgment calls — release timing, compute allocation, safety trade-offs — that Hassabis used to make personally. For a market already pricing Big Tech partly on AI capital-allocation credibility, the next signal to watch is less a headline than a cadence: how Gemini releases and enterprise deals land under the new reporting line, and whether Pichai uses Alphabet's next earnings call to spell out how chief-scientist-level AGI strategy actually translates into near-term product and spending decisions.
