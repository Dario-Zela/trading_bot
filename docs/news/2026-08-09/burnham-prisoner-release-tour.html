Burnham faces early test on prisoner releases as he launches cost-of-living tour

Andy Burnham, who became UK prime minister in July 2026, now faces criticism over his early prisoner-release scheme. — Wikimedia Commons / official portrait
In one sentence
Burnham excluded rapists and child sex offenders from England's early-release scheme but left manslaughter convicts — including PC Andrew Harper's killers — eligible, drawing fire on his first major test.

Six weeks into the job, Andy Burnham has learned what every new prime minister eventually discovers: the small print of a policy can undo the headline. On 3 August his government confirmed that rapists and serious child sex offenders would be excluded from an early-release scheme designed to relieve England and Wales' overcrowded prisons. It was meant to draw a line under weeks of pressure. Instead it drew attention to who was left on the other side of it — including the two people convicted of killing PC Andrew Harper.

A carve-out that satisfies no one

The scheme itself is a response to a genuine crunch: the prison population in England and Wales has passed 87,000 against a certified capacity of around 70,000, and the Public Accounts Committee warned earlier this year of "total gridlock" in the system. From October, most adults serving standard sentences will be released after a third of their term rather than 40% or 50%, cutting roughly 5,000 people's time inside over the scheme's first ten months.

Burnham's fix — excluding around 18,000 offenders convicted of rape, serious child sexual offences and grooming — was announced after a fortnight of mounting criticism. But manslaughter was not added to the exclusion list. Justice Secretary Alex Norris defended the line squarely: "These are decisions no Minister would ever want to take, but it is right these sickening crimes are excluded from these reforms." Extending the carve-out to manslaughter, officials argued, would gut the scheme's purpose of freeing up cells.

That left Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers — convicted of manslaughter over the 2019 death of PC Andrew Harper, who was dragged along a road by a car while attempting to stop a quad-bike theft — still eligible for release this autumn under standard terms. Harper's driver, Henry Long, sentenced separately and more severely, is unaffected. But the distinction between Long and his two accomplices has proven difficult to explain to a public that remembers the case as a single, brutal event.

Lissie Harper, the officer's widow, was blunt: "I am beyond appalled and bristling with anger to hear that Andrew's killers are soon to be free to roam the streets... This is an utter betrayal — and it's one I will not be able to forgive or forget." Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch called it a "partial climbdown" that had left victims facing "weeks of uncertainty and fear" while violent and domestic offenders still walk out in October. Burnham has not disputed the anger. "I fully understand the anger, anxiety and distress that's been caused," he said, promising "much tougher community measures" for offenders released early — a pledge long on tone, short on detail.

The episode matters less for its scale — a few thousand sentences shortened by months — than for what it says about a government still finding its footing. Burnham inherited the prisons crisis rather than created it, and the arithmetic behind the scheme is real. But the sequencing — announce, retreat under pressure, then defend a line that still leaves a household-name case exposed — is the kind of pattern that opposition parties learn to exploit and that erodes a leader's authority to make the next hard call.

Listening, not yet answering

That vulnerability sits awkwardly alongside the other project Burnham launched this week: a nationwide "listening tour" on the cost of living, running through the rest of August and intended to feed into a ten-year plan for the economy. The format echoes the mayoral town-hall style that built his reputation in Greater Manchester, and the government is keen to point to concrete wins already delivered — a £2 cap on single bus fares outside London, modelled on Manchester's Bee Network, and a temporary cut in VAT on household electricity from 5% to zero, due to run from October and expected to trim about £45 off the average annual bill.

What the tour does not yet offer is anything new. No figures, no fresh commitments, just Burnham travelling to hear concerns he and his ministers have heard many times before. Framed as a "cost of living government," the exercise reads as much as reassurance-seeking as policymaking — an attempt to project engagement while the harder decisions, on prisons as much as on bills, are still being worked out in Whitehall.

The two stories share a thread. A prime minister only weeks into office is discovering that competence in opposition, or in city government, does not automatically translate into the discipline of national policy under scrutiny — where every exemption, every carve-out, invites the question of who was left off the list. The bus fare cap and the VAT cut are real, deliverable things; the manslaughter exclusion is a real, difficult gap. How Burnham handles the next version of this test — expected as more sentencing details firm up before the October start date — will say more about his premiership than either announcement alone.
