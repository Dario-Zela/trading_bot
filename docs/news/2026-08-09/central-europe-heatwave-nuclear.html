Central Europe heatwave sets records as Danube hits record lows and a nuclear plant throttles back

Hungary's Paks Nuclear Power Plant on the Danube, which normally supplies close to half the country's electricity. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Slovakia hit a record 42.2°C and Hungary's Paks nuclear plant is running at roughly 10% capacity as record-low Danube levels choke reactor cooling across Central Europe.

Slovakia's national weather service confirmed on Thursday that a village in the country's south, Dolné Plachtince, had reached 42.2°C — a new all-time national record, and the second time in as many days the mark had been broken. A day earlier, Austria recorded 41.2°C at Bad Deutsch-Altenburg. In Budapest, the thermometer never fell below 28°C overnight, the warmest night the Hungarian capital has logged since records began.

The numbers are striking on their own. What makes this heatwave a story for energy markets rather than just the weather page is what has happened to the Danube. The river that cools Central Europe's nuclear fleet has fallen to levels not seen in decades, and the region's power system is now absorbing the consequences in real time.

A river too thin to cool a reactor

Nuclear plants along the Danube don't just need electricity to run — they need a reliable flow of river water to carry away waste heat from the reactor core. Regulators cap how warm that water can be when it's returned to the river, to protect fish and other aquatic life downstream. When the river itself is low and already warm from weeks of heat, plants run out of headroom on both sides of that equation: less water to draw, and less capacity to cool it before it goes back in.

Hungary's Paks plant, 100km south of Budapest on the Danube's bank, has taken the hardest hit. Paks normally supplies close to half of Hungary's electricity from its four reactors. Officials confirmed this week it has been running at roughly 10% of capacity, after water levels at the Budapest gauge fell to around 23 centimetres — well below the previous record low of 33 centimetres set in 2018.

Slovakia's own nuclear station, which draws on the same river system, is faring better but is still constrained, running at around 80% of rated output. Romania's Cernavodă plant came closer to a full shutdown: engineers there sank barges loaded with rock into the river to redirect flow toward the plant's intake after the Danube's discharge fell to roughly a third of its long-term July average — a scramble that, for now, has kept a second reactor online.

The market is already pricing it in

The timing compounds the problem. This is peak air-conditioning season, when demand for power is highest and the room for error in supply is thinnest. Utilities across the region have been forced to lean harder on gas-fired plants and cross-border imports to fill the gap left by derated nuclear capacity, and other thermal generators are hitting the same river-cooling limits — French nuclear output has been cut by roughly 11% amid its own heat-driven reactor derating, with EDF trimming output at Blayais and extending an outage at Gravelines.

The price effect is already visible. Electricity bills in Germany and France rose by an estimated €700 million combined during a June heat spell, well before this month's records were set, and Italian spot prices have pushed toward €500 per megawatt-hour. Every megawatt of nuclear output lost to the heat has to be replaced by something, and in a market still absorbing elevated gas prices tied to the Strait of Hormuz standoff, that something is rarely cheap. The mechanics are straightforward: gas-fired plants tend to set the marginal price in European power markets, so as they're called on more often to cover for constrained nuclear and hydro capacity, wholesale electricity prices ratchet up in step with gas.

What happens if the heat doesn't break

Europe has been here before, though not quite this badly. The 2018 and 2022 droughts also forced river-cooled reactors offline and pushed up power prices, and in both cases the disruption eased once autumn rain returned. What's different this time is the starting point: this heatwave arrives on top of a dry spring, meaning reservoirs and groundwater had less of a cushion to begin with, and Slovakia has now broken its national temperature record twice within six weeks.

Operators are managing rather than resolving the constraint. Hungary has room to raise the permitted discharge temperature at Paks in a supply emergency, with ministerial sign-off, and Romania's barge-and-rock intervention buys time rather than capacity. None of it adds new cooling water to the system — it only rearranges how the shortage is shared.

The next inflection point is meteorological, not regulatory: forecasters are watching for the first sustained rainfall over the Alpine and Carpathian catchments that feed the Danube, which would be the clearest signal that river levels — and with them, reactor output — are on the way back up. Until then, the region's grid operators are effectively trading hydrology for gas, one degree of river temperature at a time.
