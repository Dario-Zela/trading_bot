Payrolls slump narrows Fed's path to further rate hikes

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
July payrolls fell 23,000 and wage growth hit a five-year low, cutting September Fed rate-hike odds even as three regional presidents pushed to tighten.

The Labor Department's July payrolls report landed with a thud on Friday: nonfarm employment fell by 23,000, a sharp reversal from the modest but steady gains that had defined the labor market through the spring. Economists polled by Dow Jones had penciled in a gain of roughly 83,000. Instead, the country lost jobs for the first time in years — and the two months before it were revised down by a combined 103,000, meaning the hiring strength markets had been pricing for most of the summer was largely an illusion.

The reaction was immediate and, at first glance, counterintuitive: stocks rallied. The S&P 500 closed at a record 7,757.64, up 0.6%, and the Nasdaq Composite jumped 1.3%. Bad news for workers was good news for a market betting the Federal Reserve's next move is off the table — for now. The 10-year Treasury yield slipped more than 3 basis points to 4.639%, and the dollar index fell half a percent to 99.4, its weakest level in two months.

A report worse than the headline number

The details reward a closer look, because the headline decline understates the softness. Government payrolls dropped 53,000, with local government education shedding 50,000 of that. Leisure and hospitality lost 40,000 jobs, retail fell 19,000, and financial activities were down 14,000. Healthcare, which has carried job growth almost single-handedly for two years, added just 22,000 — well below its 12-month average of 36,000. Manufacturing was one of the few bright spots, adding 5,000.

The unemployment rate actually ticked down, to 4.1% from 4.2%, but the improvement is not the reassurance it might once have been. Labor force participation slipped to 61.4%, the weakest reading in more than five years, meaning fewer people are working or looking for work — a sign of discouragement rather than strength. "The two-month revision is just staggering," said Steve Sosnick, chief strategist at Interactive Brokers. "It basically wipes out two months of what we thought were healthy job gains." Adam Crisafulli of Vital Knowledge was blunter: "This is a pretty horrendous report." ADP's Nela Richardson and Navy Federal's Heather Long both reached for the same word — "gamechanger."

Wages cool to a five-year low

The wage data may matter more to the Fed than the payroll count itself. Average hourly earnings rose just 2 cents in July, leaving the 12-month rate at 3.2% — the slowest pace since May 2021 and below the 3.5% economists had expected. For a central bank that has spent more than a year treating a tight labor market as its last remaining argument for higher rates, a wage slowdown of this size removes a load-bearing pillar. Slower pay growth means less pressure on unit labor costs, and less of the wage-price dynamic that hawks on the committee have cited as reason to keep tightening even as headline inflation cools only slowly.

Richmond Fed President Tom Barkin, not among the more excitable voices on the committee, offered a measured gloss: the report was "very consistent with how I've been seeing the labor market, which is, it's not loose, it's not tight, it's sort of been a weak balance." That kind of equivocation from a centrist voter is itself a data point — it suggests the report nudges opinion without settling it.

A Fed already split, now split louder

The report landed nine days after a Federal Open Market Committee meeting that exposed just how divided the central bank has become. The FOMC voted 9-3 on July 29 to hold its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75%, but three regional presidents — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Lorie Logan of Dallas and Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis — dissented in favor of an immediate quarter-point hike, the most hawkish dissent tally since 2016. Their argument rests on inflation that has now run above the Fed's 2% target for more than five years, plus a 4.1% unemployment rate and low jobless claims that, on their face, still look like a labor market with little slack.

Presiding over that split is a new chair. Kevin Warsh was sworn in on May 22, replacing Jerome Powell, and one of his first moves was to strip the post-meeting statement of the Fed's customary forward guidance, arguing the tool is "not well suited for the current policy conjuncture." He has said the Fed will "deliver price stability" and left the door open to a September hike if inflation data due later this month run hot. That stance now collides with a jobs report that argues the opposite case. Traders, at least, read Friday's numbers as tilting the balance: futures pricing tracked by CME's FedWatch tool showed the odds of a September hike falling from roughly 57% to the low-40s within hours of the release, with the probability of a move pushed out to October rising above 57%.

What decides it from here

The committee will not meet again until September 15–16, and the single data point most likely to settle the argument arrives five days before that: the August CPI report, due September 11. If inflation holds stubbornly above target, the hawkish dissenters have ammunition to argue that a soft jobs report is a lagging read on an economy that is still overheating on price. If it cools alongside wages, Warsh's committee will have a harder time justifying a hike its own chair once seemed inclined toward. Either way, a report on hiring has, for now, done more to move the rate debate than anything said in a policy statement — which, under a chair who has deliberately stopped writing forward guidance, may increasingly be the way this Fed communicates.
