Cyberattacks hit water facilities in seven US states

The interior of a municipal drinking-water treatment plant, the kind of facility now under scrutiny after coordinated intrusions into plant control systems. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Water utilities in at least seven — now twelve — US states report hacked industrial controls since late July, with Iran the leading suspect.

The Federal Bureau of Investigation is treating it as one campaign, not seven unrelated break-ins. Since July 27, water and wastewater utilities in at least seven states — Minnesota, Michigan, Georgia, New Jersey, South Dakota and others — have reported hackers gaining remote access to the industrial equipment that runs their plants. By the following week, federal officials said the number had grown to at least twelve states. Nobody has been publicly named as responsible, but multiple US intelligence sources point to Iran.

No one has gotten sick. The Minnesota Bureau of Criminal Apprehension and Michigan's environmental agency both say no drinking water has been contaminated, and operators caught the intrusions before anything worse happened. But the attacks did what they needed to do: they knocked operators out of the systems that watch pressure, chlorine levels and pump flow, forcing plants back onto manual controls that most modern utilities haven't relied on in decades.

Thirty plants, one weekend

The clearest picture comes from Minnesota, where more than 30 municipal water systems were hit over the weekend of July 26–27. Attackers got into the plants' programmable logic controllers — the small industrial computers, often made by Rockwell Automation, that physically open and close valves and turn pumps on and off — and then changed the IP addresses and passwords protecting them. That's a specific, low-tech lockout: it doesn't require breaching a corporate network or stealing credentials through phishing. It just requires the controller to be reachable from the open internet in the first place, which a large share of small water systems still are.

CISA's acting director, Nick Andersen, said the agency is "observing a significant increase in cyber threat actors targeting programmable logic controllers at water utilities," adding that the activity "has resulted in boil water notices and sustained manual operations" at some sites. Georgia and Michigan confirmed similar intrusions days later; Michigan's environment department said nine communities reported activity matching the federal warning, though all systems "continued to operate safely."

A familiar signature

The pattern isn't new — it's an escalation of one the water sector has seen before. In November 2023, a group calling itself CyberAv3ngers broke into a booster station at the Municipal Water Authority of Aliquippa, Pennsylvania, by exploiting a Unitronics controller that was still running its factory-default password. The US Treasury later sanctioned CyberAv3ngers as a front for Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps Cyber-Electronic Command. This summer's attacks look like the same playbook run at scale: instead of one controller with a default password, investigators say the intrusions this time exploited a known authentication-bypass flaw in Rockwell Automation Logix controllers, a vulnerability rated 9.8 out of 10 for severity that utilities were supposed to have patched years ago.

That gap — a fix that existed but wasn't applied — is the part that has regulators most frustrated. Water utilities are a famously fragmented sector: roughly 50,000 community water systems across the country, most of them small, publicly owned, and running on budgets that leave little room for a dedicated cybersecurity staffer. CISA has said its scanning still finds water-system control equipment exposed directly to the internet even after months of public warnings.

Regulators scramble to catch up

The response has moved fast for Washington. The EPA, which shares oversight of drinking-water cybersecurity with CISA and the FBI, has pushed out roughly $9 million in fresh grants aimed at midsize and large systems, on top of earlier funding for rural utilities through the Rural Community Assistance Partnership. In the Senate, the Water Resources Development Act of 2026 carries new cybersecurity provisions and would reauthorize a resilience program for midsize and large drinking-water systems, while a separate bipartisan bill from Senators John Boozman and Mark Kelly — the FLOWS Act — would create a targeted grant program for small and rural utilities to modernize aging, internet-exposed controls. The American Water Works Association has backed both efforts, arguing that voluntary guidance has run its course and that funding, not just advisories, is what closes the gap between a known vulnerability and a patched one.

For an industry that has largely escaped the kind of binding cybersecurity mandates already imposed on the electric grid, the timing matters. Water utilities remain governed mostly by state-level rules and EPA sanitary surveys rather than a single federal cybersecurity standard, and insurers who cover municipal utilities have been watching the sector's exposure grow for years without a comparable pricing framework to the one that emerged after repeated grid-focused incidents.

What happens next depends on two things moving in parallel: whether US officials formally attribute the campaign to Iran, which would trigger a different diplomatic and possibly military calculus than a criminal ransomware case, and whether the Senate moves the water-resources bill before the current legislative session runs out. CISA has said it will keep publishing which controller models and configurations are being targeted, giving the clearest real-time signal of whether the campaign is still expanding.
