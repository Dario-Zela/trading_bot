Royal Navy spends 25% more time monitoring Russian vessels in UK waters

A Royal Navy Type 23 frigate of the class tasked with shadowing Russian warships in and around UK waters — LA(Phot) Abbie Gadd / UK MOD, Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Royal Navy sea-days spent shadowing Russian warships near UK waters rose 25% this year, exposing a gap between surveillance and actual interdiction of Moscow's sanctions-busting shadow fleet.

The Royal Navy spent 25% more time this year shadowing Russian warships near British waters than it did over the same period in 2025, according to figures released by the Ministry of Defence this week — the clearest sign yet that London views Russia's maritime activity as a persistent, low-grade threat rather than an occasional nuisance.

In July alone, Royal Navy assets spent 21 days tracking two Russian frigates, the Neustrashimy and the Admiral Grigorovich, as they escorted tankers belonging to Moscow's so-called shadow fleet through the Channel and North Sea. Offshore patrol vessels HMS Tyne, HMS Mersey and HMS Severn took turns on the tasking, backed by Wildcat helicopters from 815 Naval Air Squadron and Merlin submarine-hunters from 814 Squadron. On 20 July, the Neustrashimy fired its guns — 20 rounds from its 100mm mount and 30 from a 30mm battery — in international waters off the UK and French coasts, a live-fire drill the MoD logged rather than escalated.

Escort duty for an evasion network

The frigates' job is less about projecting Russian naval power than protecting it — specifically the ageing, opaquely-owned tankers that move sanctioned oil around the price cap imposed after the invasion of Ukraine. The UK has now sanctioned more than 500 vessels linked to the shadow fleet, part of a squeeze that contributed to a 24% year-on-year fall in Russian oil and gas revenue in 2025. An estimated 300 shadow fleet vessels passed through or near UK waters in the first quarter of this year alone.

But sanctions on paper have not translated cleanly into enforcement at sea. Since March, when Downing Street authorised the armed forces to board sanctioned ships in UK waters, at least 94 shadow fleet transits went unchallenged before the Royal Marines and National Crime Agency finally boarded the tanker Smyrtos in the Channel on 14 June — the first such interdiction. Commander Matt Millyard of HMS Somerset, which logged over 24,000 nautical miles on Russia-watching duties this year, called the mission "exactly what the Royal Navy exists to do, protect the UK and our allies"; critics in Parliament have been less charitable about the gap between presence and interdiction.

Cables, not just convoys

The deeper worry animating the extra sea-days is not the tankers but what else Russian vessels might be doing near them. The intelligence-gathering ship Yantar was tracked off Scotland in November loitering near undersea cable routes, prompting a new joint patrol arrangement with Norway to guard North Atlantic infrastructure. Germany separately said in the past year that several of its warships had been sabotaged, and NATO officials increasingly describe Baltic and North Sea infrastructure — cables, pipelines, wind farms — as a live target set, not a hypothetical one.

None of this marks a break from the pattern set since 2022: Western navies have been leaning into Russian-shadowing duties for four years, and the trend line, not any single incident, is the story. The MoD reshuffled its own leadership on this brief in June, when Dan Jarvis replaced John Healey as defence secretary after a budget dispute — a change worth watching as the department weighs whether stepped-up monitoring can be matched by the political will, and hulls, to actually stop the ships it is watching.
