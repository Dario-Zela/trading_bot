Union calls for 'robust review' of OBR over fears it is holding back growth

Whitehall's government office buildings, home to the fiscal machinery the TUC wants overhauled — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The TUC wants a 'root and branch' review of the OBR, arguing its assumption that public spending crowds out private investment understates UK growth potential.

The Trades Union Congress wants someone to take a hard look at the body that decides how much room the government has to spend money — and it thinks that body has been getting the sums wrong for years.

In a submission ahead of Chancellor John Healey's first Budget on 28 October, the TUC is calling for a "root and branch" review of the Office for Budget Responsibility, the independent watchdog whose forecasts determine whether a chancellor has met the fiscal rules or blown through them. The union's complaint is technical but consequential: it says the OBR's economic models assume that public investment "crowds out" private capital — that is, every pound the state spends on infrastructure or industry is a pound the private sector would otherwise have spent itself. Strip that assumption out, the TUC argues, and the growth case for bolder public spending looks a great deal stronger.

"For too long, the OBR has been a millstone preventing good growth across the country," said Paul Nowak, the TUC's general secretary. "The world has moved on from the self-defeating logic of austerity, but the OBR models still bake in its false assumptions."

A dispute over a contested idea

Crowding out is not a settled question in economics — it is one of the oldest fault lines in the discipline. The classical case holds that government borrowing pushes up interest rates and squeezes out private investment; Keynesians counter that when an economy has spare capacity, public spending can pull in private activity rather than displace it. Where an economy sits on that spectrum matters enormously for a forecaster whose job is to tell the Treasury exactly how much fiscal headroom it has.

Alongside the review, the TUC wants the National Wealth Fund's mandate widened so it can back projects on investment horizons of up to 15 years, rather than being judged against the OBR's shorter forecast window — the union's argument being that big infrastructure and industrial bets rarely pay off within a five-year forecast period, and the OBR's methodology penalises them for it.

The Treasury's response was polite but unmoved. A spokesperson said fiscal discipline "remained the foundation of economic stability and national security," and that the government intended to meet its fiscal rules with a buffer against uncertainty — a formulation that stopped well short of endorsing a shake-up of the forecaster that underwrites those rules.

The OBR was created in 2010 precisely to take forecasting out of the hands of chancellors, after years of complaints that Treasury-produced forecasts were consistently, conveniently optimistic. Its credibility since then has been a large part of why gilt markets take UK fiscal rules seriously at all — which is also why any successful push to loosen its assumptions cuts both ways. Investors who currently price UK debt on the OBR's caution might not thank a government for a more generous forecaster, particularly with gilt yields already sitting at the top of the G7 range on unrelated fiscal-flexibility jitters. Healey's Budget on 28 October is the next point at which this argument, still confined to submissions and select-committee testimony, meets an actual fiscal event.
