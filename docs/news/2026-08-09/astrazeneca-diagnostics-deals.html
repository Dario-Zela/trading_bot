AstraZeneca signs cancer-diagnostics deals with SOPHiA GENETICS and Pathos AI

AstraZeneca's Cambridge headquarters, where much of its oncology R&D is coordinated — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca signed two AI-diagnostics deals this week to speed up matching cancer patients to the right drug, without disclosing financial terms.

AstraZeneca has signed two separate agreements aimed at the same problem: matching cancer patients to the right drug, faster and in more places. The pharma group struck a multi-year companion-diagnostics collaboration with SOPHiA GENETICS on August 5, and a co-exclusive licensing deal with Pathos AI covering an early-stage breast cancer therapy, announced days apart. Neither company disclosed deal size or milestone payments.

The diagnostics side

The SOPHiA GENETICS agreement covers two companion-diagnostic programmes: turning the company's Solid Tumor application into a decentralized companion diagnostic, and validating its Hematological Oncology application to support blood-cancer patients. The point of "decentralized" is specific — rather than shipping samples to a handful of central labs, SOPHiA's cloud-based platform lets local laboratories in more than 75 countries run the AI-powered analysis themselves, plugging into a network the company says spans over 1,000 connected institutions.

Ross Muken, SOPHiA GENETICS' chief executive, framed the logic bluntly: "A breakthrough therapy only matters to the patients we can find in time to treat." The pitch to AstraZeneca is that a diagnostic bottleneck — patients unable to get tested for the right biomarker near where they live — can undercut a drug's launch even after it clears regulators. Getting local labs able to run the test on day one of approval is the goal.

The drug-development side

The Pathos AI deal is a different shape of bet. AstraZeneca is handing early clinical development of AZD4241 — a preclinical PROTAC (proteolysis-targeting chimera) designed to degrade the estrogen receptor in ER-positive, HER2-negative breast cancer — to Pathos, which will run trial design, dosing and patient-matching decisions through its Foundry platform, which it describes as the largest oncology foundation model in the industry. Pathos said "every major asset-level decision" at the company flows through Foundry, meaning AstraZeneca is effectively outsourcing part of its trial-design judgment to an AI system built on pooled clinical, molecular and imaging data.

It is not the companies' first contact: AstraZeneca, Pathos and Tempus AI signed a separate agreement in April 2025 to build a foundational oncology model together. This week's licensing deal builds on that relationship with a concrete clinical asset attached.

Why pharma is doing this now

Precision oncology only works if diagnostics keep pace with the drugs meant to exploit them — a mismatch that has dogged the field for years, with approved targeted therapies sitting idle in some markets because no local lab could run the qualifying test. AstraZeneca's answer is to buy that capability rather than build it: link with a diagnostics network for reach, and with an AI-native biotech for faster, better-targeted trial design. Pathos itself is well capitalized for this role, having closed a $365m Series D round to fund its foundation-model work and a pipeline that includes assets licensed from Novo Nordisk and Prelude.

Neither announcement included a valuation impact for AstraZeneca, whose shares have traded down over the past month despite a 58.5% five-year gain. The more interesting number to watch is not AstraZeneca's stock but SOPHiA GENETICS' and Pathos's next funding or partnership news — if rivals such as Roche, Novartis or Merck respond with comparable diagnostics tie-ups, it would confirm this is a sector-wide land grab for exclusive access to oncology data platforms rather than a one-off AstraZeneca initiative.

What's next

Watch for AstraZeneca's regulatory submissions tied to the SOPHiA companion diagnostics — the first concrete milestone by which the partnership can be judged — and for AZD4241's move from preclinical into first-in-human trials under Pathos's oversight. Either would be the first hard evidence that these AI-diagnostics tie-ups translate into faster drug-to-patient timelines rather than just partnership press releases.
