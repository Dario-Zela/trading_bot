S&P 500 Q2 earnings growth hits 50%, best since 2021, powered by energy

The Deepsea Delta oil drilling rig in the North Sea — energy sector earnings rose 135% in the second quarter as oil prices rebounded. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Blended Q2 earnings growth for the S&P 500 reached 50.4%, led by a 135% jump in energy-sector profits, as Vanguard's VOO becomes the first fund to top $1 trillion in assets.

Corporate America just posted its best quarter of earnings growth since 2021. Blended earnings for the S&P 500 rose 50.4% year-over-year in the second quarter, according to FactSet — a figure that has climbed steadily through reporting season, from an estimated 23.1% at the end of June to 47.4% last week to 50.4% now, with 88% of the index having reported. It is the kind of number that, on its face, suggests the broadest possible strength across corporate America.

It isn't. Pull the number apart and it rests on two supports, and only one of them looks like an earnings story at all.

A commodity rebound, not a productivity story

The most straightforward distortion is energy. The sector's profits jumped 135% year-over-year in the quarter, the largest gain of any of the eleven S&P sectors and by a wide margin — comfortably ahead of the double-digit growth logged by communication services, consumer discretionary, technology and materials. The mechanism is not complicated: West Texas Intermediate crude averaged $92.55 a barrel in the second quarter, up 45% from $63.68 a year earlier, when energy earnings were still depressed by weak 2024–2025 pricing.

The sub-sector detail makes the point sharper. Oil and gas refining and marketing earnings are up 249%. Integrated oil and gas is up 166%. Exploration and production is up 104%. The one laggard is oilfield services, down 16% — a reminder that when the gain is priced off the commodity rather than the well, the companies that sell shovels rather than pump oil don't necessarily benefit. This is a base-effect rebound, not evidence that energy companies are extracting more value per barrel. Whether it persists into the third quarter depends entirely on where oil prices sit, not on anything energy management teams are doing differently.

The Alphabet and Amazon effect

The more consequential distortion, and the one FactSet's own analysts flagged first, sits outside energy altogether. Strip out Alphabet and Amazon and the S&P 500's blended growth rate falls from 50.4% to 32.0%.

Both companies reported second-quarter results that blew past consensus by an unusual margin — Alphabet posted $9.11 in earnings per share against an expected $2.88; Amazon posted $5.75 against an expected $1.82. In each case the gap was not operating income but investment gains. Alphabet booked roughly $98 billion in other income, largely unrealized gains on its equity stakes, including holdings in Anthropic and SpaceX. Amazon booked $53.4 billion in other income, primarily from its own investment in Anthropic. Strip out just those two companies and the S&P 500's earnings surprise — the gap between what analysts expected and what companies delivered — falls from 29.2% to 10.9%, still above historical averages but no longer extraordinary.

Those gains are also not guaranteed to hold. SpaceX's implied valuation has fallen roughly a third since the quarter closed, which would show up as a loss on Alphabet's books in the third quarter if it persists. Mark-to-market gains on private AI stakes are, definitionally, a paper number until someone sells.

So the 50.4% headline is really two separate stories stapled together: an old-economy commodity rebound in energy, and a handful of megacap balance sheets swelling on the value of AI bets that haven't been realized in cash. Neither is what a reader pictures when told corporate earnings are growing at the fastest clip in five years.

Money follows the headline anyway

Investors have not waited for the caveats. Vanguard's S&P 500 ETF, ticker VOO, crossed $1 trillion in assets on June 2 — the first exchange-traded fund ever to do so — pushed over the line by a single day's $1.7 billion inflow. It has pulled in roughly $85 billion so far this year, helped by an expense ratio of 0.03%, a third of what its closest rival, State Street's SPY, charges. SPY has added a comparatively modest $12 billion in new assets in 2026, while State Street's cheaper SPYM fund has taken in more than $52 billion, pushing its own assets above $162 billion. Passive money, in other words, is flowing toward whichever index wrapper is cheapest — and doing so at exactly the moment the index's headline earnings number is least representative of what any individual holding is doing.

That gap between the passive flows and the underlying earnings mix is worth watching into the third quarter. If WTI holds near current levels, energy's growth rate should moderate mechanically as the year-over-year comparison gets harder. If SpaceX's markdown holds, Alphabet's fourth-quarter other-income line could turn from tailwind to drag. The next FactSet earnings update, due as the bulk of the remaining 12% of S&P 500 companies report through late August, will show whether the 50.4% figure was a peak or a floor — and whether the rest of the index, the 32% version, is the number that actually describes the economy underneath the headline.
