France records 420 arrests as wildfire season intensifies

A Canadair water bomber fights a wildfire near Toulon, in the Var — the department at the centre of this year's longest-recorded blaze in the region. — Romain JETUR / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
France has arrested over 420 people this fire season as officials say nine in ten of 14,000+ outbreaks were human-caused and a third suspected arson.

French police have arrested more than 420 people this fire season on suspicion of setting or carelessly starting blazes that have now torn through over 117,000 hectares of French countryside — an area larger than greater London. The figure, updated this week by the interior ministry, has climbed steadily since Laurent Nuñez, the interior minister, first disclosed 184 arrests in late July. By last weekend it had passed 400. Among those held, well over a hundred are minors.

The arrests are the clearest sign yet that France's escalating wildfire crisis is not simply a story about heat and drought. According to the interior ministry, roughly nine in ten of the more than 14,000 fire outbreaks recorded since the season began were started by human activity — carelessness with machinery, discarded cigarettes, campfires — and investigators believe about a third of those were deliberate. That distinction matters: it reframes a summer of fire less as an act of nature and more as a matter of enforcement, land management and, increasingly, criminal investigation.

A season without precedent in the Var

The department bearing the brunt is the Var, on the Mediterranean coast east of Marseille, where a fire in the Gros Bessillon area has burned for more than a fortnight — what Nuñez called "the longest wildfire ever recorded in the Var." At its peak the blaze forced the evacuation of roughly 2,500 people and drew 1,250 firefighters, backed by Canadair water bombers, Dash aircraft and helicopters. Fires that broke out earlier in the season in Gironde, near Bordeaux, and in Aude have since been brought under control, but the Var's mix of dense pine forest, steep terrain and repeated mistral winds has kept crews on the ground since late July.

France's 2026 season is already among its worst on record, exceeded in scale only by 1949, when a series of fires in the Landes killed 82 people. This year's toll on people has been lower — French officials have not attributed direct fire deaths to the current outbreaks — but the area burned, at well over 100,000 hectares nationally, rivals the historic benchmark. Spain has fared worse still, with more than 220,000 hectares burned, making this summer's combined Iberian and French losses among the largest Europe has recorded in a single season.

Enforcement as the policy response

What distinguishes this year is the scale of the criminal response layered on top of the firefighting effort. Arrests have run well ahead of prior seasons: 32 suspects detained in a single sweep across 22 departments in mid-July, doubling to over 300 by month's end, and now beyond 420. Nuñez has framed the arrests as evidence that French authorities are treating arson and reckless fire-starting as a security priority rather than an unfortunate side effect of a hot, dry summer — internal security forces, not just fire brigades, are now central to the response.

That framing carries an implicit admission: if roughly a third of ignitions are deliberate and nine in ten are human in origin, then reducing outbreaks depends as much on policing, prosecution and public behaviour as on rainfall. It is a harder problem to solve than weather, and one without an obvious ceiling — arrests can rise each week the season continues, as they have done since June.

The economic tail

The insurance and reconstruction implications are becoming a story of their own. Analysts estimate France's wildfire losses this year could reach €10–15 billion, with insured losses running into the billions of euros — potentially the country's costliest wildfire season on record. Unlike floods and droughts, wildfire damage in France sits outside the state-backed natural catastrophe compensation scheme, so private insurers are expected to absorb most of the recovery bill; some have already begun covering hotel costs for evacuated policyholders. Restoration spending across the five hardest-hit eurozone countries is estimated at €3.1 billion so far this year, a figure that does not yet include the slower-moving costs to homes, tourism and agriculture.

The longer-run risk map is shifting too. A July analysis by AXA's climate research unit found that the average number of high-wildfire-risk days around French cities could rise by nearly 70% by 2050, and by close to 130% by century's end, as fire-prone conditions spread beyond the traditional Mediterranean hotspots into central and northern France. Combined with a record-arrest season concentrated in the south, the pattern being described by officials and insurers alike is structural rather than cyclical: not a single bad summer, but a widening zone of risk that reconstruction planning, agricultural insurance and regional infrastructure will need to price in going forward.

What comes next

The Var fire's perimeter is largely secured but not yet declared over, and forecasters are watching for another round of mistral winds that could reignite hotspots, as happened in early August. The interior ministry has signalled it will continue to publish arrest tallies through the end of the season, which typically runs into September in southern France — meaning the 420 figure is very unlikely to be the final count. Separately, the scale of insured losses will start to firm up as adjusters complete assessments this autumn, a number that will matter well beyond France's borders for how European insurers price wildfire risk next year.
