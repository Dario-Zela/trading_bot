Klesch becomes Germany's second-largest refiner as BP completes Gelsenkirchen exit

BP's Gelsenkirchen facilities at Stadthafen, part of the refining complex now sold to Klesch Group. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP completed the sale of its 265,000 bpd Gelsenkirchen refinery to Klesch Group, cutting $1bn a year in costs and making Klesch Germany's second-largest refiner.

BP has finished selling its Gelsenkirchen refinery to Klesch Group, the privately held commodities firm controlled by American investor Gary Klesch — a transaction that hands Klesch control of Germany's second-largest refining complex and strips roughly $1bn a year from BP's cost base.

The deal, first announced in March, closed this month. It covers the Horst and Scholven processing sites in Gelsenkirchen plus the Bottrop tank farm — an integrated hub with 265,000 barrels a day of crude distillation capacity that processes around 12 million tonnes of crude annually into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel, heating oil and petrochemical feedstocks. Roughly 1,800 employees have transferred to Klesch along with the assets. Financial terms were not disclosed.

A complex changes hands

For BP, the arithmetic is straightforward: Gelsenkirchen's underlying operating expenditure runs to about $1bn a year, and offloading it lets the company redirect that spending toward higher-return upstream and downstream assets. "This deal strengthens our balance sheet and simplifies our portfolio," said Richard Harding, BP's interim head of downstream, language that has become a refrain across the industry's refining retreat. Patrick Wendeler, who runs BP's German business, put it more plainly: Klesch is "well placed to take Gelsenkirchen into its next chapter," while BP continues serving German drivers through its separate Aral fuel-retailing network.

The sale is the latest marker in a two-decade contraction of BP's refining footprint — from 15 sites in 2006 to 10 by 2016 and now just five: Cherry Point and Whiting in the United States, and Castellón, Lingen and Rotterdam in Europe. Only Whiting and Rotterdam still exceed 400,000 bpd. The company has been steadily paring lower-margin, capital-intensive refining in favour of businesses — trading, lubricants, integrated gas — where it can extract a better return on capital employed.

Klesch's quiet accumulation

Gelsenkirchen is not Klesch's first German refinery. The group bought the Heide plant in Schleswig-Holstein from Shell in 2010 and added the Kalundborg refinery in Denmark from Equinor in 2022. Layering on Gelsenkirchen's 265,000 bpd now makes Klesch Germany's second-largest refiner by capacity, trailing only Shell's Rheinland complex, which processes upward of 15 million tonnes a year.

"Our strategy is built around the long-term stewardship of high-quality refining assets," the group said in a statement announcing the deal's close. "Gelsenkirchen Refinery fits within that vision and provides a strong foundation for sustainable value creation." It is a line built for permanence, though Klesch's history — three decades assembling distressed steel mills, aluminium smelters and chemical plants alongside refineries — has also drawn scrutiny from analysts who note that critical fuel infrastructure is migrating from listed majors with disclosure obligations to privately held vehicles with far less transparency.

Germany's refining sector is already a patchwork of ownership types that illustrates the point. Rheinland sits with Shell; the PCK Schwedt refinery, majority-owned by Russia's Rosneft, has been run under a German government trusteeship since 2022 for energy-security reasons; and now Gelsenkirchen sits with a private investor largely outside public markets. Fuel security for Europe's largest economy increasingly depends on entities answerable to shareholders, a state trustee, or — in Klesch's case — a single controlling owner.

The bigger retrenchment

Gelsenkirchen's exit lands inside a broader recalibration at BP. The company has raised its structural cost-reduction target twice since unveiling a strategic reset in February 2025: first from $4-5bn to $5.5-6.5bn after a review of its Castrol lubricants business, and now to $6.5-7.5bn by 2027, with the Gelsenkirchen savings accounting for roughly $1bn of that increase. The deal was struck under then-chief executive Murray Auchincloss and closed after Meg O'Neill, formerly of Woodside Energy, took over as CEO on April 1. That continuity suggests the refining pullback is now structural rather than tied to any one leadership team's preferences.

For traders, the read-through is less about Gelsenkirchen itself than what it confirms: European refining margins have not been rich enough, for long enough, to hold the interest of integrated majors focused on capital discipline. BP's remaining five sites will need to earn their keep against that same test. The next checkpoint is BP's third-quarter results, due in late October, when analysts will be watching whether the freed-up capital shows up in higher shareholder returns or gets redirected toward the upstream and transition spending O'Neill has flagged as the company's priority.
