FDA approves first mRNA flu vaccine and new narcolepsy drug

FDA headquarters entrance, White Oak campus, Silver Spring, Maryland. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The FDA cleared three first-of-kind therapies in 48 hours — Moderna's mRNA flu shot, Takeda's narcolepsy drug and Replimune's oncolytic virus for melanoma — each a platform win with muted market reaction.

Three unrelated approvals landed at the Food and Drug Administration within a 24-hour window this week — a vaccine, a sleep drug and a cancer therapy, each validating a different corner of biotech that has spent years promising more than it delivered. None of the three companies involved coordinated the timing. That they arrived together says more about the FDA's approval calendar than about any shared scientific thread.

The vaccine that took two tries

Moderna's mFLUSIVA, cleared on August 5th, is the first mRNA-based influenza vaccine approved anywhere in the world — a notable extension of the platform that produced the company's COVID-19 shot into a market it has chased for years. The approval covers adults 50 and older outright; for those 65 and up, clearance came via the accelerated pathway, contingent on a postmarketing study, after the FDA initially refused to even review Moderna's application in February. The dispute was narrow but consequential: the agency's then-vaccine chief objected to Moderna benchmarking mFLUSIVA against a standard-dose comparator in older adults rather than the high-dose shot the FDA recommends for that group. Moderna resubmitted with the population split into two age-based tracks, and the review restarted.[^1] In trials, the vaccine was reported to be roughly 27% more effective than a standard flu shot — though whether that translates to real-world uptake is unproven, and analysts are not treating the approval as an immediate windfall. Moderna shares actually fell about 1.5% on the news; Jefferies kept a Hold rating and a $45 price target, arguing meaningful flu revenue is unlikely before 2027.[^2]

A sleep disorder's root cause, and a virus that hunts tumors

A day earlier, the FDA approved Takeda's Orzeyful (oveporexton) for narcolepsy type 1 in adults — the first drug designed to address the disease's underlying mechanism, a deficiency of the brain signalling molecule orexin, rather than just its symptoms. Across two 12-week placebo-controlled trials totalling 273 patients, the twice-daily tablet improved wakefulness and cut cataplexy episodes, sleep paralysis and hallucinations.[^3] Takeda estimates roughly 120,000 people in the US live with narcolepsy type 1, a small population that explains the company's own guidance that the approval won't materially move its fiscal 2026-27 earnings — shares rose only about 1.4%. One caveat worth flagging for anyone pricing in a near-term launch: Orzeyful's scheduling under the Controlled Substances Act is still pending a decision from the Drug Enforcement Administration, which could delay availability.[^4]

The third approval, granted August 6th to Replimune's Tudriqev (vusolimogene oderparepvec-wtpg) in combination with Bristol Myers Squibb's nivolumab, treats advanced melanoma that has progressed despite prior anti-PD-1 therapy. It works by a genetically modified herpes simplex virus engineered to replicate inside and destroy tumor cells — an approach known as oncolytic viral therapy that has circled clinical relevance for over a decade without a major US approval. This was Replimune's third attempt at the FDA after two prior rejections, in July 2025 and April 2026. The supporting trial enrolled 140 patients with 91 evaluable; 24% achieved an objective response, lasting a median of nearly 14 months.[^5] Replimune shares rose more than 8% intraday to $12.86 before slipping in after-hours and premarket trading — a reminder that accelerated approval, granted on a surrogate endpoint, still leaves the company on the hook for a confirmatory trial to prove real clinical benefit. Two sell-side desks, Leerink and Wedbush, upgraded the stock regardless, with Leerink lifting its target to $17 from $11.[^6]

What ties the three together, if anything, is that each represents a first-of-kind clearance for a platform technology — mRNA vaccines beyond pandemic use, orexin-targeted neurology, and engineered oncolytic viruses — rather than an incremental label expansion. The next test for all three is commercial, not regulatory: Moderna's flu vaccine faces its first real readout during the fall season now underway, the DEA's scheduling decision will determine when Orzeyful actually reaches patients, and Replimune's confirmatory melanoma trial will decide whether Tudriqev's accelerated approval converts to full approval or gets withdrawn.
