US pledges $1bn security aid to Colombia as new right-wing president takes office

The Palacio Nacional in Cali, near where Abelardo de la Espriella was sworn in as Colombia's president — the first inauguration held outside Bogotá in recent memory. — C arango / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Hours after Abelardo de la Espriella's inauguration, the US pledged $1bn in security aid, reversing a year of Trump-Petro tension over Colombia's drug war.

Fourteen hours. That is roughly how long it took Washington to convert a change of government in Bogotá into a nine-figure security commitment. On Friday, Abelardo de la Espriella took the oath as Colombia's president in Cali; by the same evening, the US State Department had announced it intends to provide $1 billion in security assistance to his incoming government, pending congressional approval.

The speed matters as much as the sum. It signals that Washington sees the change of government not as a routine transition but as an opening — a chance to rebuild a security partnership that spent the past year in near-total breakdown under outgoing president Gustavo Petro.

A tiger takes the oath

De la Espriella, a 48-year-old lawyer who had never held elected office before this campaign, won June's run-off by 0.96 percentage points — 250,830 votes out of more than 26 million cast, the narrowest margin in Colombian electoral history. He calls himself "El Tigre," favours a stiff military salute as a personal gesture, and ran on a platform built almost entirely around rejecting Petro's approach to the country's armed groups.

He was sworn in not in Bogotá but in Cali — the first Colombian inauguration held outside the capital in recent memory — before Congress, Spain's King Felipe VI, and more than 60 ambassadors. Hours later he flew by helicopter to the Pichincha military garrison and delivered a very different kind of speech to assembled troops, pledging an "all-out war" on what he calls narco-terrorism. "The option of dialogue is completely exhausted," he told the soldiers, adding that his government would allow "no room for manoeuvres that undermine the stability of the nation." His platform includes a 90-day offensive against armed groups, mega-prisons modelled on El Salvador's, aerial fumigation of coca crops, and an open invitation for US troops to operate on Colombian soil.

From decertification to alliance

That pivot only makes sense against what came before. Last September, the Trump administration formally decertified Colombia as a partner in the drug war — the first such move against Bogotá since the mid-1990s — after a UN report found coca cultivation had risen 53% in a single year, leaving Colombia responsible for roughly two-thirds of global cocaine production. Petro called the decertification an attempt to install "a puppet president" and argued that fumigation was no substitute for reducing demand in the US and Europe. By October, he and Trump were trading public threats; Colombia's government pursued record cocaine seizures — nearly 837 metric tons between January and October 2025 — even as output kept climbing.

The $1 billion package reverses that trajectory in a single announcement. The State Department, in a statement crediting Secretary of State Marco Rubio's outreach to the incoming administration, framed the aid as "a cornerstone of this renewed partnership." Rubio had already met Colombia's vice-president-elect, Cielo Rusinque Restrepo, in July, pledging that Washington wanted to be Colombia's "partner of choice" on both security and economic ties. The money still needs congressional sign-off, and no line-item breakdown has been published, but the direction is unambiguous: military and interdiction cooperation, not diplomacy, is again the currency of the relationship.

A test within a day

Colombia did not wait to test the new president's resolve. On Saturday, a car bomb exploded at a toll plaza on the Pan-American Highway in the country's southwest, injuring two security guards. The army attributed the attack to dissident factions of the demobilised FARC — groups that never accepted the 2016 peace accord and have expanded their footprint in regions like Catatumbo, where fighting between FARC dissidents and the ELN has displaced tens of thousands of people since January. Petro's "Total Peace" strategy, built on negotiating with these groups rather than fighting them, is the policy de la Espriella has explicitly promised to bury; the bombing landed as a blunt reminder of what replaces it.

For a region watching how a Trump-aligned government translates into concrete commitments, Colombia is now the clearest test case: a $1 billion bet that militarised enforcement can succeed where five years of negotiated peace-building could not. The immediate variables to watch are procedural and human at once — whether Congress appropriates the funds, whether de la Espriella's 90-day offensive produces action beyond rhetoric, and whether armed groups respond to "all-out war" talk with escalation of their own, as Saturday's bombing suggests they might.
