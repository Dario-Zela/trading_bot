Meta ordered to pay $567m in largest-ever child-safety ruling, taking total to $942m

Meta's headquarters in Menlo Park, California, where the company is weighing an appeal of the New Mexico ruling. — Nokia621 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A New Mexico judge ruled Meta a 'public nuisance' and ordered a 90-hour monthly cap and messaging bans for minors, on top of $567m in new penalties — $942m total.

Meta will have to redesign parts of Instagram and Facebook for teenagers in New Mexico — not just pay for the damage its apps have allegedly done. That is the sting in a ruling handed down this week by a state court judge who found the company liable for $567m on top of penalties already levied earlier this year, and, more unusually, ordered the company to change how its products work for anyone under 18 in the state.

The order, filed August 6 by Judge Bryan Biedscheid in Santa Fe, closes out the second phase of a case brought by New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez in 2023. It follows a jury verdict in March that found Meta had committed 75,000 violations of the state's Unfair Practices Act and imposed a $375m civil penalty — the statutory maximum. Combined, Meta's liability in this one case now stands at $942m.

A nuisance, legally speaking

What sets this ruling apart from Meta's long list of prior settlements is the label the court attached to it. Biedscheid concluded that Meta's products constitute a "public nuisance" in New Mexico — a legal category historically reserved for hazards that harm the public at large regardless of individual fault, such as air pollution or contaminated water supplies. It is, by most accounts, the first time a social media company has been found liable under that doctrine for the design of its product rather than for a discrete harmful act.

That distinction matters because it opens the door to the kind of remedy public-nuisance law typically produces: not just damages, but abatement — an order to stop causing the harm. The $567m the court ordered is earmarked for exactly that, structured as a five-year fund with $420m going to treatment services for young people and $147m to prevention, screening and related programs, according to the New Mexico Department of Justice.

What Meta has to change

The more consequential part of the ruling is the list of product mandates, which read less like a fine and more like a design brief written by a judge. Under the order, Meta must:

  • Cap use of Facebook and Instagram by under-18 users in New Mexico at 90 hours a month — roughly three hours a day
  • Pause push notifications to minors overnight and during school hours
  • Bar adults who are not a minor's confirmed friends or followers from messaging that minor
  • Hide public "like" counts by default, restoring them only with parental consent
  • Ban sexualized chatbot interactions with users under 18
  • Build an "under-13-years-of-age prediction model" within two years and delete personal data already collected from users found to be under 13
  • Partner with schools to flag underage accounts, and file compliance reports with the court twice a year

These requirements run for five years and apply specifically to Meta's operations in New Mexico, so they will not automatically change what a teenager in Texas or Toronto sees on their phone. But building state-specific product logic is expensive and operationally awkward for a company that has generally preferred uniform, global features — which is precisely the leverage regulators are betting on.

Fines Meta can absorb, redesigns it can't shrug off

Meta reported roughly $60bn in profit last year, so a $942m hit is a rounding error against its balance sheet — its stock barely moved after the ruling, off less than half a percent. That asymmetry is the reason fines alone have rarely changed Big Tech's behavior: penalties get priced in as a cost of doing business. Forced product changes are a different proposition. Engagement-driving features like notifications, visible like counts and open messaging are not incidental to Meta's model — they are close to the point of it, especially for the younger users advertisers pay a premium to reach.

"Meta is paying for that choice," Torrez said of the ruling, adding that it "forces real changes to how Meta operates in New Mexico." He had earlier called the March verdict "a victory for every parent who has worried about what social media is doing to their child." Meta spokesperson Andy Stone said the company "remain[s] confident in our record of protecting teens online and will continue to defend ourselves," and the company has said it intends to appeal.

Why this ruling travels

Meta is already fighting a separate multistate case in California, brought by more than 40 attorneys general, alleging its products were deliberately designed to be addictive to minors. New Mexico's judgment gives that litigation — and any state considering similar action — a working template: a public-nuisance theory paired with court-ordered product mandates rather than a settlement check. Advocates for stricter platform regulation have said openly they hope other courts adopt the same approach, according to reporting following the ruling.

Meta's appeal will test whether the mandates survive in their current form, and how a state court's authority to order changes to a global product's design holds up on review. If the ruling stands, the next question is whether attorneys general in other states — who have watched years of federal legislation on kids' online safety stall in Congress — start filing public-nuisance claims of their own. That would turn a New Mexico courtroom into the unlikely origin point for how Meta redesigns its apps everywhere else.
