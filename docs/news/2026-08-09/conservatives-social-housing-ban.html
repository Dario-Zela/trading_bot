Conservatives pledge to ban foreign nationals from social housing

Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch, who framed the social housing pledge as ending Britain's role as a "cash machine for the world." — ©House of Commons / Roger Harris, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Conservatives pledge to evict foreign nationals from social housing within six months of taking power, freeing an estimated 228,000 homes.

The Conservative Party has unveiled a pledge to strip foreign nationals of access to social housing in England, promising that if elected the party would free up nearly a quarter of a million homes for British citizens by evicting non-EU tenants within six months of taking power.

The policy, announced this week by Shadow Work and Pensions Secretary Helen Whately, would bar anyone without British, Irish or EU citizenship from signing a new social tenancy, and would revoke the tenancies of those who already hold one. Couples in which one partner holds British, Irish or EU citizenship — including EU nationals with settled status — would remain eligible. A single tenant who holds none of those, the party says, would lose their home regardless of whether they have children.

"Making Britain your home should be earned by working, paying tax and integrating," Whately said. "It should not come with a free ride on the benefits system."

Party leader Kemi Badenoch framed the announcement in blunter terms, vowing to end Britain's role as a "cash machine for the world." The Conservatives point to figures showing that 89% of lead social housing tenants in England were UK or Irish nationals in 2024–25 — a share the party wants at 100%, and one it says would liberate 228,144 homes for people further up council waiting lists.

A crowded field on the right

The timing is telling. Reform UK, which has led national polling for months, proposed a similar ban earlier this year, and its response to the Tory announcement was pointed rather than welcoming. Shadow Home Secretary Zia Yusuf dismissed Badenoch's party as "shameless arsonist firefighters," arguing the plan doesn't go far enough because it still lets EU nationals with settled status keep their homes. "Under Reform," he said, "all social housing — old and new — will be for Brits." Nigel Farage was characteristically terser on social media: "The Tories copy every policy of ours."

Labour dismissed the proposal from the opposite direction, calling it "an unserious, unworkable proposal, copied from Reform, that does nothing to tackle the housing crisis." Housing charities were sharper still. Shelter chief executive Sarah Elliott said the plans "would brutally kick people out of their homes, including thousands of families and pensioners," while Kate Henderson of the National Housing Federation called evicting settled tenants "against our sector's values." The Chartered Institute of Housing flagged unresolved legal and operational questions, and the Royal College of Nursing warned that housing insecurity among migrant nursing staff could ripple into patient care.

The labour-supply wrinkle

What the announcement does not address is where the workers go. Migrant labour is deeply embedded in exactly the sectors most reliant on lower-cost housing near their place of work: care, construction and hospitality. Health and Care Worker visa issuance has already fallen sharply — down from over 34,000 in one quarter of 2023 to around 2,000 a year later, as the last government tightened the route — and the construction industry says it is already short by more than 200,000 workers against a target of 225,000 new entrants by 2027. Hospitality has drawn more than one in ten of its workforce from arrivals since 2010.

None of that means social-housing tenants are the same people plugging those shortages — eligibility rules already exclude most migrants on temporary visas from social housing under the "no recourse to public funds" condition, a point Crisis chief executive Matt Downie made in response to the announcement. But the policy lands as a signal of direction rather than a costed plan: no timeline for implementation, no mechanism detailed for enforcement, and no assessment published of what tightening the wider immigration system further — a stated ambition alongside this pledge — would do to sectors already advertising for workers they cannot find.

What's next

The Conservatives remain a distant third in national polling behind Labour and Reform, so this is a positioning exercise rather than imminent legislation. The next test is whether it holds up under scrutiny of the party's 2029 manifesto process, and whether Reform escalates with a still-tougher offer that pulls the debate further rightward before then.
