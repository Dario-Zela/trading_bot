Todd Blanche confirmed as US attorney general in narrow 50-49 Senate vote

Todd Blanche, confirmed as US attorney general in a 50-49 Senate vote. — Ryan M. Biller / U.S. Department of Justice
In one sentence
Todd Blanche was confirmed attorney general 50-49, with Republicans Collins and Murkowski defecting over DOJ independence concerns.

Todd Blanche was confirmed as attorney general of the United States early Saturday by the narrowest possible margin — a 50-49 Senate vote that saw two Republicans break with their party and underscored how fragile institutional confidence in the Justice Department's independence has become under the second Trump administration.

Senators Susan Collins of Maine and Lisa Murkowski of Alaska voted no alongside every Senate Democrat. With Mitch McConnell absent, Blanche could afford to lose only two Republicans and still be confirmed — and he lost exactly that many. The deciding vote came from Louisiana's Bill Cassidy, who announced his support a day earlier after Blanche put in writing that a controversial $1.8 billion "anti-weaponization" fund, created to compensate Trump allies who said they were unfairly investigated, had been rescinded.

A lawyer turned law-enforcement chief

Blanche's path to the country's top law enforcement job runs directly through Trump's own legal troubles. A career prosecutor who spent more than fifteen years at the Justice Department — including as co-chief of the violent crimes unit at the Southern District of New York — he left for private practice in 2014 before returning to public life as Trump's personal defense lawyer. He led the defense in the New York hush-money case that ended in Trump's conviction on 34 felony counts, and in two federal cases brought by special counsel Jack Smith over the 2020 election and classified documents at Mar-a-Lago.

Trump made him deputy attorney general last year, then elevated him to acting attorney general in April after abruptly sidelining Pam Bondi. Even Blanche has said he does not know why. "Nobody has any idea why the attorney general is no longer the attorney general, and I'm the acting attorney general, except for President Trump," he told reporters at the time.

Why Collins and Murkowski said no

Both Republican defectors framed their opposition around independence rather than qualifications. "While I believe Mr. Blanche is a capable lawyer, the Department of Justice has become increasingly political," Collins said. Murkowski was blunter: "The country needs an Attorney General who will check the worst impulses of this administration. I hope Mr. Blanche is able to achieve that, if confirmed, but I simply do not have confidence that will be the case."

Their concerns centered on the department's handling of the Jeffrey Epstein files and on the now-rescinded compensation fund, which critics saw as a vehicle for repaying Trump loyalists at taxpayer expense. Cassidy voiced similar unease — he specifically criticized Blanche's role in settling Trump's lawsuit against the IRS with a tax-immunity deal for the president's family businesses — but said he ultimately trusted Blanche to give the president candid legal advice.

The regulatory stakes

For markets, the attorney general's chair matters less for its politics than for what it means for enforcement discretion. Blanche has already signaled a lighter touch in some areas: in April, as deputy attorney general, he issued a memo directing prosecutors to stop pursuing digital-asset cases that amount to "superimposing regulatory frameworks" on crypto firms, focusing instead on outright fraud, hacking and theft. The department under his watch has also rolled out a department-wide corporate enforcement policy that offers firms declinations to prosecute in exchange for self-disclosure and cooperation — a framework companies will now be negotiating with a Trump loyalist rather than a career appointee.

At his confirmation hearing, Blanche committed to prioritizing antitrust enforcement, citing recent DOJ work easing anticompetitive pressure in cattle markets. But a razor-thin, cross-party-defection confirmation is itself a signal: institutional guardrails that once made the Justice Department's posture toward business relatively predictable regardless of the White House occupant are visibly under strain. Traders pricing regulatory risk into sectors from crypto to banking to Big Tech antitrust exposure now have to weight not just DOJ's stated policy but the possibility that a department led by the president's former personal lawyer moves faster and more unevenly than its predecessors did.

What's next

Blanche is expected to be sworn in within days, after which his early personnel and policy choices — particularly any successor to the rescinded compensation fund, and the pace of digital-asset enforcement guidance — will be the first concrete test of whether Collins and Murkowski's warnings about DOJ independence prove out.
