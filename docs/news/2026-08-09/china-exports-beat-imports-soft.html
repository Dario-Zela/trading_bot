China's exports beat forecasts even as import demand softens

The Yangshan deep-water container terminal near Shanghai, part of the port complex handling China's export boom. — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
China's July exports beat forecasts but import growth cooled sharply from a five-year high, underscoring soft domestic demand Beijing has yet to fix.

China's customs bureau reported on Friday that exports rose 23 percent in July from a year earlier, edging past the 22.2 percent gain economists polled by Reuters had pencilled in. Imports told a quieter story: up 27.5 percent year-on-year, just short of the 27.9 percent forecast, and a sharp step down from June's 36 percent jump — the fastest import growth in five years. The trade surplus came in at $112.5 billion, ahead of expectations but narrower than June's $125.6 billion.

On the surface, both numbers still look robust — imports grew faster than exports, even after the miss. But the deceleration matters more than the headline beat. China's factories are riding a global technology cycle: high-tech exports rose more than 40 percent, semiconductor shipment values nearly doubled, and orders from ASEAN climbed 38 percent as supply chains keep rerouting around US tariffs. Traditional categories look thinner — ceramics exports fell 28 percent — a reminder that the strength is concentrated, not broad-based.

The demand gap underneath

The trade figures land a month after data showing China's economy grew just 4.3 percent in the second quarter, the weakest pace since late 2022 and below both the 5.0 percent logged in the first quarter and consensus forecasts of 4.5 percent. Fixed-asset investment fell 5.7 percent year-on-year in the first half, dragged down by an 18 percent collapse in real-estate investment, while retail sales managed only 1.3 percent growth over the same period. Set against that backdrop, July's cooler import growth reads less like a one-month blip and more like confirmation: households and builders are still not spending, and the trade-in subsidy schemes that briefly propped up consumer purchases earlier in the year have been losing effect, according to Caixin reporting on the programme's fading sales boost.

That is the mechanism behind the divergence in Friday's release. Export income is arriving in China from abroad; it is not, by and large, being recycled into domestic orders for imported goods, components or commodities at the pace it was even eight weeks ago.

Beijing's calibrated response

The Politburo's July 31 meeting pointed to the same conclusion policymakers keep landing on: incremental, targeted support rather than a broad stimulus package. "While a large-scale stimulus package appears unlikely, selective and targeted measures to bolster consumption and investment could help stabilise China's economic momentum," said Woei Chen Ho, an economist at UOB covering Greater China. Macquarie analysts have made the political logic explicit — Beijing has little incentive to open the fiscal taps for households and property as long as exports and manufacturing keep the annual growth target within reach.

That calculus cuts both ways for foreign exporters, including British ones. UK goods and services exports to China ran at £32.3 billion in the year to the first quarter, up 2.5 percent, aided by tariff cuts on categories like whisky and a fresh push into services. A China that needs external demand more than it needs to import has less appetite to concede ground at the negotiating table — Pinpoint Asset Management's Zhiwei Zhang expects "intense negotiations between China and the major trading partners in the coming months" — even as its own consumers stay on the sidelines.

The next test comes with August trade data in early September, alongside any follow-through from the Politburo's pledge of "incremental" support. Until household spending and property investment turn, China's trade numbers will keep telling two stories in one release: a factory floor still winning abroad, and a home market still waiting to be convinced.
