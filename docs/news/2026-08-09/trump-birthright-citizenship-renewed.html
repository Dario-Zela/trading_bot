Trump renews push to restrict birthright citizenship

President Donald Trump, whose administration has signed new executive orders renewing its push to restrict birthright citizenship. — Shealah Craighead / The White House
In one sentence
Weeks after the Supreme Court struck down his first attempt, Trump signed narrower orders targeting birth tourism and specific exemption categories for birthright citizenship.

President Trump has gone back to the birthright citizenship fight he lost at the Supreme Court five weeks ago — this time with a narrower target and a different legal theory.

On Thursday the president signed two executive orders reopening a battle that seemed settled on June 30, when the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Trump v. Barbara that his original attempt to strip citizenship from children born on US soil to undocumented or temporary immigrants was unconstitutional. Chief Justice John Roberts, writing for the majority, held that children born in the US to parents "unlawfully or temporarily present" are still "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States and are citizens at birth under the 14th Amendment — the same reading the Court reached in 1898's United States v. Wong Kim Ark, the case that has anchored birthright citizenship for well over a century.

That first order, signed on Trump's first day back in office in January 2025, tried to deny automatic citizenship broadly to babies born to parents who were in the country illegally or on temporary visas. It never took effect; lower courts blocked it almost immediately, and the Supreme Court finished the job in June.

A narrower wedge

The new pair of orders tries a different angle. One identifies specific categories of children the administration says fall outside constitutional protection — among them children of foreign government employees and of people classified as "alien enemies," including members of federally designated terrorist organizations. The White House frames this as consistent with, not opposed to, the Barbara ruling, pointing to narrow historical exceptions — diplomats' children, for instance — that the Court itself has long recognized. The second order directs the State Department and Department of Homeland Security to crack down on so-called birth tourism, which a White House fact sheet describes as people working with "malign syndicates" to enter the country and "exploit their temporary admission to obtain American citizenship." Neither order spells out a legal definition of birth tourism, and enforcement details are left to the two departments.

The administration is betting that a tighter, more surgical order will survive where a sweeping one failed — and that framing the categories as extensions of existing exceptions, rather than a rewrite of the 14th Amendment, gives it better odds in court.

Immigrant-rights groups are unconvinced. "The Supreme Court already decided this issue: birthright citizenship is guaranteed by the Constitution," said Cody Wofsy, deputy director of the ACLU's Immigrants' Rights Project, who argued Barbara. "No additional executive order can change the meaning of the Constitution. Any executive order that tries to rewrite birthright citizenship will meet the same fate as the last one."

The orders arrive alongside other citizenship-adjacent moves the White House touted this week, including 88 denaturalization cases filed against people it deems to have obtained citizenship improperly and tighter visa screening for 75 countries it classifies as high-risk.

Litigation is expected within days — the same coalition of ACLU affiliates, the Legal Defense Fund and immigrant-advocacy groups that won Barbara is the likely plaintiff again. Whether a federal judge grants an early injunction, as happened within weeks of the 2025 order, will be the first signal of how much runway this narrower version gets before it lands back in front of the same Supreme Court that just ruled against its predecessor.
