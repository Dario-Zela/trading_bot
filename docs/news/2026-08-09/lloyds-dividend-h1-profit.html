Lloyds lifts dividend 30% on strong H1, but 217% rally raises valuation questions

Lloyds Banking Group's headquarters at 25 Gresham Street, London. — Carcharoth / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Lloyds raised its interim dividend 30% on a 17.1% return on tangible equity, but a 217% five-year rally has split analysts on whether the stock is now overpriced.

Lloyds Banking Group is paying its shareholders more money than it has in years. It is also, by its own investor materials, running one of the more contested valuation debates on the London market. Both things are true at once, and the tension between them is the story.

The UK's largest domestic lender said on 30 July that first-half statutory profit after tax rose 23% to £3.1 billion, with pre-tax profit reaching £4.3 billion. Return on tangible equity — the number banks and their investors watch most closely as a gauge of how efficiently capital is being put to work — came in at 17.1%, comfortably ahead of the group's own guidance of above 16% for the year. Net income rose 9% to £9.7 billion, with growth split fairly evenly between lending income and fees.

On the back of that, Lloyds lifted its interim dividend 30%, to 1.58p per share from 1.22p a year earlier, payable on 15 September. It also launched a fresh £1 billion share buyback, on top of the £1.75 billion programme announced alongside February's full-year results — taking total capital returned to shareholders this half to roughly £1.9 billion.

A recovery that shows up in the numbers

The scale of the payout increase reflects a bank that, on paper, is in the best shape it has been in for a decade and a half. Lloyds also used the results to unveil "Accelerate 2030", a strategy built around roughly £2 billion of AI-driven cost savings, positioned by chief executive Charlie Nunn as the next phase of a turnaround that began with the bank's post-financial-crisis clean-up and, more recently, its exit from most legacy PPI liabilities.

The market had already priced in a good chunk of that improvement before results day. Lloyds shares have returned about 217% over five years, a run that outstrips most of its UK banking peers and has pushed the stock's trailing price-to-earnings ratio to around 14 times — against a sector average closer to 11.7 to 12.5 times, according to Simply Wall St's tailored valuation screen. On that basis, the shares pass just two of six standard valuation checks the research site applies. A separate excess-returns model, which weighs Lloyds' profitability against its cost of equity, points the other way, implying the stock trades at roughly a 45% discount to fair value. The two models disagreeing this sharply is itself a signal: the bull and bear cases both have genuine support, which is a different situation from a stock that is simply cheap or simply expensive.

It is worth being precise about what the 217% figure actually measures, since it can read as a comment on recent momentum when it is not. It is a five-year cumulative return, spanning Lloyds' recovery from pandemic-era lows and a low base after years of underperformance versus the broader FTSE — not a reaction to Wednesday's results specifically. Shares were trading around 116p in early August, having drifted slightly as the buyback's purchasing power shrinks in real terms the higher the stock climbs — a dynamic flagged by analysts watching the pace of the £1.75 billion programme announced in February.

The overhang that won't quite clear

The one significant asterisk on the recovery narrative is motor finance. Lloyds, through its Black Horse lending arm, is the UK bank most exposed to the industry-wide car loan commission mis-selling scandal, and the size of the eventual bill remains unresolved. The Financial Conduct Authority's proposed redress scheme could cover an estimated 14 million agreements, with average payouts put at around £700 each — a range that, extrapolated across the industry, runs into billions of pounds. Lloyds has already set aside sums against the liability in prior periods and says it expects clarity on the FCA's final scheme in the first quarter of next year.

Nunn has been unusually blunt about the risk, warning that the redress scheme "as it's currently proposed" would hand borrowers "a windfall... that isn't linked to harm and wouldn't be fully proportionate," and cautioning that the episode risked becoming a broader "investability issue" for UK financial services if regulators are seen retroactively rewriting the terms of legal lending. That is a chief executive making a public case to a regulator mid-consultation — a sign of how much is riding on the final number, both for Lloyds' capital position and for the credibility of the dividend growth it is now promising investors.

What the payout actually signals

Dividend increases of this size are usually a management team's clearest public statement about confidence in forward earnings, since cutting a dividend back is far more damaging to credibility than raising one. A 30% lift, paired with an enlarged buyback, suggests Lloyds' board views the motor finance liability as bounded and largely provided for, and views 17.1% return on tangible equity as sustainable rather than a peak. Whether that confidence is well-placed is precisely the question the market is now pricing two different ways at once.

The next real test arrives in stages: third-quarter results will show whether the 9% income growth holds without the tailwind of prior rate rises, the FCA's final motor finance decision is due in the first quarter of 2027, and Accelerate 2030's cost savings will need to start showing up in the numbers rather than the slide deck for the higher multiple to hold.
