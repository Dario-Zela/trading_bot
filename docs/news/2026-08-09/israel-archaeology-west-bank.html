Israel accused of 'weaponising archaeology' at West Bank heritage sites

The Roman colonnaded street at Sebastia, the West Bank archaeological site at the centre of the dispute. — Wikimedia Commons / Panoramio
In one sentence
A BBC investigation finds Israeli archaeological digs at Sebastia and Herodium preceding hundreds of acres of West Bank land expropriation, over Palestinian and UNESCO objections.

The dig sits inside a Palestinian town, but the signage, the funding and the security perimeter around it are Israeli. At Sebastia, a hilltop of Roman columns and Ottoman stonework north of Nablus, archaeologists working for Israel's Civil Administration have spent the past 15 months excavating what officials call the Shomron National Park — and Palestinian residents call the slow annexation of their land.

A BBC investigation, reported by Sebastian Usher and Callum Tulley, examined how digs at Sebastia and at Herodium, King Herod's fortress-palace near Bethlehem, have become instruments in Israel's territorial claim over the occupied West Bank. The reporters found that archaeological designation is now routinely followed by expropriation orders: roughly 450 acres around Sebastia and about 80 acres at Herodium have been earmarked for seizure, according to Civil Administration filings reviewed by the BBC and by the Israeli monitoring group Peace Now.

A biblical capital, a modern land dispute

Sebastia's ruins span three millennia — Iron Age fortifications from when the hill was Samaria, capital of the northern Israelite kingdom in the ninth century BCE, layered under a Roman forum, a Crusader hall and a mosque built into the remains of a Byzantine church. Israel's Heritage Ministry has allocated 113 million shekels (£27.8m) to develop West Bank archaeological sites this year, with 32 million shekels earmarked for Sebastia alone, restoring a colonnaded street, building a new access road and constructing paid-entry visitor facilities.

For Abu Mudif, a local landowner, the excavation has meant losing olive groves his family has worked for generations. "It is like having your soul ripped away," he told the BBC. "I cannot imagine myself without my land." Mohammed Azem, the village mayor, and Nahed Sakha, a restaurant owner facing a demolition order for his premises near the ruins, describe a town increasingly cut off from the acropolis it grew up around — the archaeological zone falls in Area C, under full Israeli control, while the modern town sits in Area B.

Scholarship as leverage

Alon Arad, director of the Israeli civil-society group Emek Shaveh, which tracks archaeology's political uses in the occupied territories, put the mechanism plainly: "You use archaeology to excavate and rewrite narrative, but also to reallocate lands from Palestinian to Israeli." Palestinian officials make the same charge from the other direction, accusing Israel of foregrounding Jewish-era finds at Sebastia while downplaying the site's Roman, Crusader and Islamic layers.

Israel rejects the framing entirely. UNESCO's decision in July to add Sebastia to the World Heritage List — and simultaneously to its List of World Heritage in Danger, a designation reserved for sites under acute threat — passed over Israeli objections. Foreign Minister Gideon Saar dismissed the vote as a Palestinian attempt to erase Jewish history: "No vote in an international organisation can change history."

The dispute now sits with two authorities that rarely move fast. UNESCO's in-danger listing carries no enforcement power beyond diplomatic pressure, and Israel's Civil Administration has continued issuing expropriation orders at Sebastia through this year, most recently in February. Watch for whether that pace holds through the annual UNESCO monitoring report, and for whether the Shomron National Park's paid-entry infrastructure — due for further construction this year — becomes the fact on the ground that outlasts the argument over who gets to write the history beneath it.
