Life inside a Yemeni city besieged by Houthis

In one sentence
A six-week Houthi siege of Taiz has cut its fuel and food routes and sent prices up sharply, and the offensive that caused it also gave the Houthis Yemeni coast on the Bab al-Mandeb strait.

The Yemeni city of Taiz, home to roughly 1.5 million people by one local count, has been under a tightening Houthi siege for about six weeks. The first of two supply lines was cut on 4 October, when Houthi forces took al-Turbah, a town in the far south-west of the governorate. The loss closed the road that carried Aden-side fuel into the city. Food and fuel prices have climbed since, hospitals are short of supplies, and residents are burning firewood because cooking gas has become too expensive.

This dispatch draws on Al Jazeera's reporting from Taiz, not the BBC. The Red Sea link is real but geographical, not a documented shipping effect on the city's food and fuel.

How the road closed

Taiz has been here before. The Houthis besieged it in 2015–16 until government forces retook ground south of the city. A partial blockade lingered afterwards. In 2024, under domestic and international pressure, the Houthis agreed to reopen an eastern route to Sanaa.

The present siege began on the coast. In early September the Houthis launched an offensive along Taiz's Red Sea shoreline and took full control of the port of Mocha and the Dhubab district. Dhubab contains the Bab al-Mandeb strait, which Al Jazeera says handles 12 percent of global trade. A Saudi-backed government counteroffensive began in early October and regained some land, but by 4 October al-Turbah had fallen. The eastern crossing has since closed too, according to security sources, after attempted Houthi incursions.

Fuel had reached Taiz via the al-Turbah road from Aden, and much of the city's food came through the eastern crossing. Both are now shut. Travellers are pushed onto rough back roads past nearly 20 new Houthi checkpoints, according to one family interviewed.

What it does to a city's larder

The prices show the effect. A 20-litre container of black-market petrol rose from 30,000 riyals ($19) to 90,000 riyals ($58) in the first days. A seat on a passenger vehicle to the city went from 35 Saudi riyals ($9.33) to 100 riyals ($26.66). Journalist Wafiq Saleh says vegetable and fruit prices have roughly doubled. Traders hold several months of stock, he says, but some raised prices once fighting reached the outskirts. Residents accuse them of hoarding.

Officials describe a severe squeeze. Nabil Jamil, the governorate's planning director, calls the siege "more comprehensive and its impact more severe than in 2015". Salah al-Majidi of the local Siege Affairs Department calls it "the worst catastrophic siege imposed on a Yemeni city since the war began in 2014". Both are officials of the government-aligned local authority, so their words are advocacy as well as evidence. Taysir al-Samei of the health office says hospital access fell 60–70 percent by the end of the second week. Rubbish is piling up uncollected, and about 180,000 displaced people in rural districts are caught between clashes on the Lahj–Taiz road.

What the reporting does not show

Neither Al Jazeera report links the siege to shipping disruption in the Red Sea. Nor do they give import volumes or say how much cargo normally reached Taiz through Mocha. The connection is territorial. The same offensive that gave the Houthis a stretch of coast on the strait also put them astride the roads feeding the city. Whether that coastline later becomes a base for harassing ships is a separate question, and the reporting does not answer it.

The next test is whether the government counteroffensive can reopen the al-Turbah road, or whether the Houthis revive the eastern crossing as a bargaining chip, as they did in 2024. Until one of those happens, the prices in Taiz's markets are the clearest measure of how the siege is going.
