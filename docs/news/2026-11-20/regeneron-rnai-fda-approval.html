Regeneron wins FDA approval for first RNAi drug in autoimmune disease

In one sentence
Regeneron is reported to have won FDA approval for an RNAi drug, probably cemdisiran for myasthenia gravis, but the approval and trial details could not be independently confirmed.

Regeneron is reported to have won US approval for an RNA interference drug in an autoimmune disease. If confirmed, that would take a technology used mainly in rare genetic and metabolic conditions into immunology for the first time. The approval itself could not be checked against the FDA or a second outlet before this piece was written. The only named source is BioPharma Dive, whose report was not available for review. The details below separate what is on the public record from what is not.

What is known, and what is not

The drug most likely at issue is cemdisiran, Regeneron's small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy for generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG). That is an inference: the triage notes give no drug name, indication or approval date. It rests on the public regulatory timeline. The FDA accepted Regeneron's new drug application for adults with anti-acetylcholine receptor (AChR) antibody-positive gMG. It granted priority review, with a target action date in November 2026, after Regeneron used a priority review voucher (Managed Healthcare Executive). A decision on 20 November would fall inside that window. A search for a published approval notice or label turned up none, so the approval remains a reported event, not a verified one.

Myasthenia gravis is an autoimmune disease in which antibodies disrupt signalling between nerves and muscle. It causes fluctuating weakness that can affect the eyes, limbs, swallowing and breathing. The complement system, part of the innate immune defences, contributes to the damage in AChR-positive patients. Complement inhibitors are already an established treatment class.

How the drug works

Cemdisiran targets C5, a complement protein. Instead of blocking C5 with an antibody after it is made, an siRNA silences the gene message so that less of the protein is produced. The compound is conjugated to GalNAc, a sugar that directs it to the liver, where C5 is made. Company materials say this supports subcutaneous dosing about every 12 weeks (Allsci). Regeneron licensed global rights from Alnylam, which is entitled to regulatory milestones and royalties on an approved product.

The claim of a first is plausible on the evidence available. Regeneron has described cemdisiran as it would be the first siRNA therapy for gMG. Approved RNAi drugs to date have largely addressed conditions such as hereditary transthyretin amyloidosis and rare metabolic disorders. A caution applies: whether the approval is the first RNAi drug in any autoimmune disease, as the headline puts it, depends on how that category is defined, and the FDA's own announcement should settle it.

What the trial data show

In the Phase 3 study, cemdisiran as monotherapy produced an average 74% inhibition of complement activity, according to reports of the data. One analyst quoted in coverage said the efficacy results fall short of those of drugs that block FcRn, another established class in myasthenia gravis. That framing matters commercially. The drug's case is likely to rest on dosing convenience and mechanism rather than on being the most effective option. This piece could not verify the primary endpoint, the placebo-adjusted effect size or the safety profile. Those are the figures that determine whether the approval is a major development or an incremental addition.

What to watch

The first test is the FDA's own announcement and label, which should confirm the drug name, the approved population and any safety warnings. Complement inhibition carries a known infection risk, particularly meningococcal disease, so the label's vaccination language will be telling. Next come the price and launch positioning against existing complement and FcRn therapies. A decision from the European Commission is anticipated in the second half of 2027, and Regeneron plans a Japanese filing in early 2027. Rival developers working on RNAi in immune-mediated disease will read the label and the commercial uptake closely.
