Dutch watchdog tells consumers not to buy AI smart glasses

In one sentence
The Netherlands' privacy regulator has reportedly advised consumers against buying AI smart glasses, citing data protection concerns, extending its earlier push to keep camera glasses out of sensitive places.

The Netherlands' data protection authority has told consumers not to buy AI-enabled smart glasses, according to a BBC Technology report. A regulator advising people against a product, rather than fining a company after the fact, is an unusual move. The BBC's stated reason is data protection concerns. The report as summarised does not name specific products or set out the regulator's full reasoning, and the regulator's own wording has not been checked here.

What the regulator has already said

The Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP) has been working on camera glasses for months, and its earlier positions show where the concern lies. Dutch trade and legal coverage reports that the AP wants camera glasses kept out of sensitive places such as hospitals, courts and places of worship. It has asked the owners and managers of those places to write a ban into their house rules. One Dutch law-firm summary says the AP also advised against wearing them as everyday spectacles. These accounts are secondary and carry inconsistent dates, from September to October 2026.

The core worry is about bystanders, not the buyer. A conventional camera is obvious. A pair of glasses with a lens and an AI assistant built in is not, and the small indicator light that is meant to signal recording is easy to miss. The AP has also said that filming other people with such glasses is, in most circumstances, not allowed under Dutch privacy law, and that recording in public can still break the rules when it is not necessary.

Complaints and enforcement

The AP had logged three complaints from people who were filmed without consent and whose images were then published, NL Times reported in August, citing Dutch broadcaster NOS. The same report said publishing recognisable people online falls under strict privacy rules, but that enforcement is difficult. Once footage is posted, the regulator has to find the wearer first, and the wearer is usually a private individual, not a company.

The August report named Meta's AI glasses, made with Ray-Ban and Oakley, as the product under discussion. The broader alarm in that coverage came from the digital-rights group Bits of Freedom and a privacy lawyer, not the AP. They pointed to near-invisible cameras and to reports that footage had been reviewed by human moderators at a facility in Kenya. Those are their claims and were not confirmed by the regulator in the coverage reviewed.

The Dutch consumer association Consumentenbond has also run a piece headlined "Pas op voor Meta's camerabril" (beware of Meta's camera glasses), and the European Data Protection Board has published a statement on the data protection challenges of smart glasses. Dutch politicians have raised the idea of banning AI camera glasses, though the local government association VNG has reportedly seen no need yet for new rules.

Why a purchase warning matters

A purchase warning is a different instrument from the house-rules guidance. Telling venues to ban a device targets a place. Telling consumers not to buy it treats the product itself as a risk. Most of that risk falls on people who never agreed to be recorded. The warning also leaves the law unchanged: it carries no penalty and is not a ban. Its force is in signalling how the AP is likely to read complaints.

It also leaves open a question for other European regulators and for manufacturers. If the Dutch view is that the combination of a hidden camera and AI processing is hard to square with the GDPR, other authorities will be asked whether they agree. Makers will be asked what their glasses do with footage, where it is processed and who can see it.

What to watch: the AP's own publication, which should settle the exact wording and whether any product is named. After that comes whether other EU authorities, or the EDPB, adopt a similar line. Any move from guidance to enforcement would be the clearer test.
