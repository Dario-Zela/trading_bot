Oral pancreatic cancer drug reported to make tumours vanish in trial

In one sentence
A BBC-reported oral pancreatic cancer drug is said to make tumours vanish, but trial size and phase are unstated and published data on a similar pill show shrinkage, not disappearance.

A pill for pancreatic cancer is reported to have made tumours disappear in trial patients. The claim comes from a single BBC UK report, and the summary of it gives no patient numbers, trial phase or length of response. Without those, it is hard to say how far the finding reaches. Pancreatic cancer is among the deadliest cancers, so even a modest signal would count for more here than in most tumour types.

What could be checked

The BBC report was not available to this desk in full, and its underlying trial data was not checked. A search found no source describing tumours vanishing. It did find recent coverage of an experimental daily pill, daraxonrasib, made by Revolution Medicines. The BBC report is not confirmed to concern that drug, so what follows is context rather than verification.

The published evidence on daraxonrasib describes shrinkage and longer survival, not disappearance:

  • Phase 3 (RASolute 302): In about 500 previously treated patients, median survival was reported as 13.2 months on the pill against 6.7 months on chemotherapy. Results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2026, according to search summaries of the paper.
  • Tumour shrinkage: Tumours shrank in 31.6% of patients on the pill, against about 11.2% on chemotherapy, per the American Cancer Society's summary.
  • Earlier phase 1/2 work: At the 300 mg daily dose, roughly 30% of patients who had one prior line of treatment had an objective response, according to Dana-Farber.

An "objective response" is a measured shrinkage threshold. It is not the same as a tumour disappearing. A complete response, in which no detectable disease remains, is a stricter and much rarer category. Headline language can blur the two.

Why the distinction matters

If some patients did have complete responses, that would be notable. It would still need a denominator: whether it was 2 patients in 20 or 20 in 500. It would also need a duration, because responses in advanced pancreatic cancer are often short-lived. A single reported case, or a handful in an early-phase cohort, supports a very different conclusion from a pattern across a randomised trial.

The trial's sponsor is also a relevant fact. The daraxonrasib studies were funded by Revolution Medicines. That does not undermine the data, but independent replication and regulatory review carry weight that company-sponsored results alone do not.

What is not yet known

Several questions remain open, whichever drug the BBC report covers:

  1. How many patients were treated, and in which phase of trial.
  2. How many had complete rather than partial responses, and for how long.
  3. Whether the results have been peer-reviewed or are preliminary.
  4. Whether the benefit applies to patients treated earlier in their disease. The phase 3 population had already received treatment.

Coverage of the phase 3 result also notes that more data are needed before the drug could be considered for full approval by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Nothing found suggests it has reached that stage.

What to watch

The next useful data points are the full trial publication, with response categories broken out, and any regulatory filing. An FDA or UK Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) decision would show how reviewers weigh the evidence. Until the numbers are published, "tumours disappeared" is best read as an unverified description of a promising but early result.
