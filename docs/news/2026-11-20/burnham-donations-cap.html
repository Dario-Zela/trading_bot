Burnham urged to cap all political donations, not only overseas money

In one sentence
Campaigners and opposition parties are pressing Andy Burnham to back a cap on all political donations, not just overseas money, after reports he retreated from an earlier pledge.

Andy Burnham is being pressed to back a limit on all political donations in Britain, not just those from abroad. The demand comes from campaigners and opposition parties. It matters because the current rules leave the largest domestic cheques unrestricted, and Burnham has said things on the subject that are hard to square with each other.

This piece rests on secondary reporting. The Guardian Q&A that prompted it could not be read directly, and the Express & Star report on the £72m donations was blocked when fetched. Where details come from search summaries or advocacy groups, that is stated.

What the rules allow

Individual donations to UK parties are not capped. As the New Statesman put it in September, a Labour MP told the magazine that the only cap in practice falls on overseas donors. Search coverage describes a government plan for a £100,000 limit on overseas donations only. Ministers are reported to have said they do not intend to cap UK donations.

The argument over domestic money sharpened this autumn. The New Statesman reported on 16 September that the crypto billionaire Ben Delo had reportedly given Reform UK £36m, followed by a second £36m. It called the sums record-breaking. Headlines elsewhere put the total at £72m in two days. A Labour MP in a party WhatsApp group said the money "fundamentally alters our chances of stopping Reform". The discussion then turned to a cap.

Burnham's shifting position

In June, Byline Times reported that Burnham backed a cap on political donations to stop undue influence by big donors. Search summaries describe it as "hard and low", possibly starting higher and falling over time. By late August and early September, several outlets, including Byline Times and the Guardian as cited in search results, were reporting an apparent retreat from that pledge. Campaigners called it a U-turn.

The accounts differ on how far he retreated. Some say he dropped the idea. Others say he stopped short of an immediate cap. Those details are unconfirmed here. The New Statesman headline said Burnham had been avoiding the subject, and it reported no new statement from him.

The pressure is cross-party and organised. The Liberal Democrats said Burnham must "walk the walk" after Labour blocked a donation cap amendment. They have also pushed for a commission on a cap. A Lib Dem MP, Luke Taylor, accused him of failing to "make the tough decisions" needed to take power from billionaires. A 38 Degrees petition and an open letter from civil society figures, reportedly including Carol Vorderman, ask him to hold to his earlier support.

The case against him is not only from the opposition. One commentary piece argues he must face down the unions over a cap. A cap on all donations would also cover trade-union money, which Labour relies on.

Why it matters now

A Westminster return would normally be a story about positioning. A commitment on funding would make it a test of whether he will legislate against the party's own donors as well as its rivals. Backing a low cap on all donations would hit Reform's biggest gifts and the unions' contributions to Labour alike. Holding to an overseas-only limit would let critics say the reform stops where it would cost his own side.

Reform's prospects, which the Guardian Q&A also covers, are a secondary thread. They are left out here because no figures could be verified.

What to watch

The next marker is whether Burnham gives a specific figure and timetable, or whether the Lib Dem push for a commission and further amendments reaches a Commons vote. Without one of those, the gap between his June position and the government's overseas-only plan will stay open.
