Covid inquiry: findings on testing, tracing and the 'pingdemic'

In one sentence
The inquiry has already said no test-and-trace system existed in 2020; a dedicated report on Test and Trace and the pingdemic could not be confirmed as published.

The UK Covid-19 Inquiry has been working through the pandemic in separate modules, and its verdict on NHS Test and Trace has been a long time coming. The 2021 'pingdemic' is part of that story. This brief sets out what the inquiry has already said, what is still outstanding, and which parts could not be confirmed.

What is established

The inquiry, chaired by Baroness Hallett, reports module by module. Two published reports already touch on testing and tracing.

The first, on pandemic preparedness (July 2024), found that a test, trace and isolate system "did not exist in the UK when the pandemic struck". It said the country had no containment capacity that could be scaled up quickly. [1]

The second, on core decision-making (Module 2, November 2025), found that the government and civil service had failed the public. The BMA's summary of the report quotes it as saying the response was "too little too late". The same summary says the report found that ministers had been warned that test and trace capacity was inadequate for a pandemic. [2][3]

NHS Test and Trace itself was launched in England in May 2020 and led by Dido Harding. The inquiry has a dedicated module on it, covering testing, tracing and isolation. [4]

What could not be confirmed

The dedicated Test, Trace and Isolate report was the likeliest source of fresh findings on the app and the 'pingdemic'. In summer 2021, NHS Covid-19 app alerts told large numbers of people to self-isolate, and staff shortages followed across several sectors. Searches for this piece did not locate a published report on that module. They also found no inquiry finding specific to the app alerts. One search summary said the inquiry had indicated this report would follow in the autumn, but that could not be checked against a primary source.

The inquiry has published an "Every Story Matters" summary on the test, trace and isolate system. It records people's experiences of isolating after a positive test or a contact alert. That is testimony, not a finding. It draws on accounts gathered online and at drop-in events, so it should not be read as a measure of how the system performed overall. [5]

Two things follow. If the report has appeared, its conclusions on the pingdemic could not be verified here, so none are attributed to it. If it has not, the lessons usually drawn from the crisis stand as before. Those lessons are that capacity has to exist before an emergency, and that a system built in a hurry will be judged partly on the disruption it causes.

What to watch

The inquiry has said it intends to publish all its reports by summer 2027. [6] The test, trace and isolate report is the one to read for any assessment of the system's cost, its use of private contractors, and whether app-driven isolation alerts did more good than harm. Until it is confirmed as published, claims about what the inquiry found on the pingdemic should be treated as unverified.
