Boeing hits a 52-week low despite a $12bn 787 order, as US lawmakers push to curb China aerospace ties

In one sentence
Boeing sat at a 52-week low even as a reported $12bn 787 order landed, while a reported congressional push on Chinese aerospace adds a long-run risk to its China market.

Two stories met in Boeing's share price this week, and only one of them is about aircraft. The first is a reported congressional push to restrict US capital flowing into China's aerospace industry and to keep Chinese-built aircraft out of US certification. The second is a $12bn order for 787 widebodies that, on the day it was announced, did nothing for a stock already sitting at a 52-week low. Neither the order's size nor the legislative details could be confirmed against primary documents. What can be said is that the order was too small to offset a policy risk that points at the most contested part of Boeing's market.

What lawmakers are reportedly asking for

The trigger is an exclusive Reuters report, relayed through a triage brief, that US lawmakers are urging limits on US investment in China's aerospace sector and a refusal to certify Chinese-built aircraft. The brief names no bill, sponsors or vote dates, and a search of public sources did not turn up the original. This is therefore a reported push, not enacted policy.

It does fit a pattern visible in the public record. In September 2025, Representative John Moolenaar's China select committee wrote to the administration urging restrictions on outbound investment in Chinese aviation and a review of export controls on aircraft, parts and maintenance services. That letter was tied to rare earths access and to curbs on Chinese airline landing rights. Separately, a senior lawmaker warned Ryanair against buying Chinese-made jets, in a letter seen by Reuters. Chinese certification of COMAC's C919 came in 2022, domestically. It carries no weight with US or European regulators.

What the new push adds, if it is as described, is a second lever. Capital restrictions would hit US funds with exposure to Chinese aerospace issuers. A certification refusal would close the largest Western market to Chinese aircraft. I could not find any public record of lawmakers explicitly calling for the certification step, so that part rests on the Reuters report alone.

Why Boeing and Airbus are exposed

The duopoly benefits from a Chinese industry that has not yet built a credible competitor. Any measure that hardens the line between US and Chinese aviation is, on its face, protective of Boeing. The risk runs the other way. Beijing has shown it can use aircraft purchases as a bargaining chip, and a dispute framed around national security gives it more reason to do so. Both manufacturers draw a meaningful share of their long-run demand forecasts from China, where fleets are expected to expand faster than almost anywhere else.

The duopoly's exposure is also unequal. Boeing's China deliveries have been politically hostage for years, whereas Airbus assembles aircraft in Tianjin. Airbus may be the less vulnerable of the two, but a retaliation aimed at American suppliers would still reach engines and components that cross the Atlantic. That reasoning is inference, not reporting, and it depends on whether the proposal moves beyond letters.

A stock that ignored the order

Boeing shares reached a new 52-week low in mid-November, according to the aggregator TIKR, with an intraday low of $164.71 on 16 November and a roughly 24% decline for 2026 so far. TIKR ties the weakness to a slower 737 ramp flagged on the third-quarter call, not to China. The same piece points to a record backlog of $745bn at the end of September and a 140-jet 737 MAX order from Avolon on 4 November. Orders are not in short supply. The market is pricing delivery rates and cash conversion.

The $12bn 787 order fits that pattern. At list prices, $12bn would buy a few dozen aircraft, a small increment against a backlog of that size, and the order's customer, count and delivery slots were not available. A fresh policy risk in a market that matters over the long run gives investors another reason to hold back.

What to watch

The test is procedural. A letter commits no one. A bill with sponsors, a committee markup, or an executive order on outbound investment would. Boeing's next catalyst is its monthly delivery figures, which will show whether the 737 ramp is recovering. The China question matters most if Beijing answers the Washington proposals with action on orders or deliveries.
