Government sets timetable for dismantling leasehold in England and Wales

In one sentence
The government has introduced a bill with dated milestones: a £250 ground rent cap around 2028, commonhold around 2029, and a ban on new leasehold flats around 2031.

The government has put dates on the end of leasehold. On Thursday it introduced the Commonhold and Leasehold Reform Bill to Parliament, and published a schedule: a £250 cap on ground rents around 2028, a rebuilt commonhold model around 2029, and a ban on selling most new leasehold flats around 2031. The dates are targets, not statute. Both Houses must still pass the bill, which is expected to take several months.

About five million homes in England and Wales are leasehold, according to coverage of the announcement. Scotland and Northern Ireland have separate property law and are outside the scheme.

What the timetable says

The schedule has three steps.

  • Ground rents, around 2028. Existing ground rents would be capped at £250 a year. Reporting says the cap is expected to take effect six months after the bill becomes law, with rents falling to a peppercorn, a nominal sum, after a 40-year transition.
  • Commonhold, around 2029. A modernised commonhold model, in which owners hold the freehold of their flat and jointly run the building, is expected to be operational. Existing leaseholders would need the support of 50% of owners to convert a building, down from unanimity.
  • New flats, around 2031. The sale of most new leasehold flats would be banned, and new flats would mostly be sold as commonhold.

The second and third dates sit on a longer fuse than the first, and the ban is the least certain. In May, legal analysts at Gowling WLG, summarising a parliamentary committee's report on the draft bill, wrote that the ban was "highly unlikely" to take effect before the next general election. A 2031 target therefore depends on this Parliament's calendar and on who governs after it.

Existing leases are largely untouched by the ban. Sales, extensions and variations of existing leasehold flats fall outside it. The cap and the commonhold conversion route are the main tools for the millions already in leasehold. Ground rents on new residential leases have been effectively zero since the Leasehold Reform (Ground Rent) Act 2022 took effect on 30 June 2022.

Who is exposed

The brief for this piece names housebuilders and property managers as the affected sectors. The published material available at the time of writing does not quantify their exposure, and no listed company is named in the coverage reviewed. Any estimate of lost revenue would therefore be speculation.

The mechanism is clearer than the size of the effect:

  • Freeholders and ground rent investors. The cap is the nearest-dated change and hits an income stream directly. Rents above £250 would be cut, and all would fall to a peppercorn after 40 years. The Residential Freehold Association, which represents large freeholders, called the bill "an unprecedented abuse of property rights". The Gowling summary notes that freeholders and institutional investors have signalled possible human rights challenges, so litigation is a live risk to the 2028 date.
  • Housebuilders. Most flats are currently sold leasehold. From around 2031 they would be commonhold, so the income that some developers have historically retained or sold on, such as ground rents, would not exist for new schemes. Ground rents on new homes have been near zero since 2022, which limits the change. The larger variable is whether commonhold demand and mortgage availability match leasehold.
  • Property managers. Under commonhold, owners run the building collectively through a commonhold association, which could shift work and fees away from third-party managing agents or change their contracts. The Gowling summary says regulation of managing agents is absent from the draft bill, so the effect on the sector is unclear.

The case for caution is that the dates are indicative and the timetable depends on legislation not yet passed. Equity markets typically price such shifts only once passage looks likely.

Reaction, and what remains open

Abena Oppong-Asare, the Labour MP who chairs the Commons Housing Committee, said leaseholders "have been waiting far too long for successive governments to tackle the unfair leasehold system". She added that she hoped the government would "consider accelerating the timetable for introducing the £250 cap on ground rent". The committee had suggested a cap in late 2027, earlier than the government's 2028.

Leaseholders interviewed described the cost of the system. One in Oldham said her ground rent would rise from £150 to £300 in January, and that her service charge exceeds £4,000 a year for a one-bedroom flat, which her managing agent attributes to costs outside its control, including insurance linked to fire safety. The National Leasehold Campaign said the real test would be what the reforms mean for existing leaseholders, and End Our Cladding Scandal's Giles Grover asked whether the complex measures "will actually benefit everyone".

The gaps are significant. The committee noted that Law Commission recommendations on enfranchisement and the right to manage were missing from the draft, which could keep buying a freehold or converting to commonhold expensive. It also raised concerns about broad delegated powers, and said the 2024 leasehold act remains largely unimplemented.

The next data points are procedural. The bill's second reading will show how much opposition there is, in particular to the 40-year transition, which the committee questioned because the previous government proposed 20. The timing of Royal Assent, which the committee expected by mid-2027, will then fix when the 2028 cap can take effect. Any legal challenge from freeholders would be the main threat to the first date, and listed housebuilders' disclosures on commonhold would show the first quantified exposure.
