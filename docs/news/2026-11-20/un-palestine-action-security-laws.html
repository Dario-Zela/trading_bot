UN experts warn against stretching terrorism law as UK weighs Palestine Action ban

In one sentence
UN experts again caution the UK against stretching terrorism law as the Palestine Action ban, upheld by the Court of Appeal in June, remains a test of how far such powers reach protest.

The United Nations' independent human rights experts have again warned London against stretching terrorism law to cover protest, as the status of Palestine Action under the Terrorism Act 2000 stays contested. The group is the direct-action network that sprayed paint on RAF aircraft and attacked arms-industry sites. Whatever happens next, the case has become a test of how far counter-terror powers can reach into dissent.

The details of the latest intervention are not independently confirmed. A Guardian summary says UN experts have cautioned the government against misusing security laws and that ministers are considering the group's status. The date of the statement, the experts' names and their exact wording could not be checked. Nor could the timetable for any ministerial decision. The warning does follow a consistent line of UN argument.

How the ban came about

The government proscribed Palestine Action in July 2025, after members broke into RAF Brize Norton and damaged two aircraft. Proscription made membership and public support for the group criminal offences under the Terrorism Act, which carries penalties of up to 14 years in prison.

UN special rapporteurs objected before and after the order. Their central argument was that international standards, including the Security Council's Resolution 1566, confine terrorism to acts intended to cause death, serious injury or hostage-taking. On that reading, property damage alone falls outside the definition. Volker Türk, the UN human rights chief, later called the ban "disproportionate and unnecessary" and at odds with the UK's human rights obligations. Rapporteurs also wrote to the government about arrests of people who expressed support for the group.

The UK's statutory definition is wider. It includes serious damage to property when it is designed to influence the government or intimidate the public, and that gap is what the argument turns on. A review led by the former judge Sir Declan Morgan found the definition risks "uncertainty and overreach".

The courts have so far sided with the government

The legal route has not gone the critics' way. The High Court's Divisional Court found the proscription unlawful and quashed it. In June 2026 the Court of Appeal reversed that decision in R (Ammori) v Secretary of State for the Home Department. A five-judge court led by the Lady Chief Justice held that the Home Secretary had not breached her own proscription policy. It also held that the question was not whether the judges would have proscribed the group, but whether the decision was reasonably open to her on the evidence. Human Rights Watch reported that the court acknowledged a "chilling effect" but found the ban justified and proportionate because the group had used violence against property.

Huda Ammori, the group's co-founder, said she would seek to take the case to the Supreme Court. Whether an application has been filed or heard was not established in sources available for this article, and the ban's current position should be checked against the latest court record.

What is at stake

The court ruling settled that this particular decision was lawful. It did not answer the UN's wider objection, which is about the breadth of the definition itself. If ministers keep the ban, or extend similar powers, direct-action groups of any cause could point to the precedent, and so could the authorities. That is why human rights bodies are watching. The same powers that reach a group damaging aircraft could in principle reach other campaigns that use criminal damage as a tactic.

The next markers are any Supreme Court decision on permission to appeal, and any formal response from the Home Office to the UN experts. A government reply, if one is published, would show whether ministers accept the UN's reading of the definition or defend the UK's wider one.
