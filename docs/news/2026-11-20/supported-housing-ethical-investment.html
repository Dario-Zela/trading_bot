Mouldy, infested supported housing sold to overseas investors as 'ethical'

In one sentence
A BBC investigation found supported housing flats, some mouldy and rat-infested, sold to overseas investors as 'government-backed' wealth, with no clear regulator over the marketing.

Flats for people on benefits, many with mental health problems, have been sold to individual investors in Europe, Asia and Africa as "Secure, Government-Backed Wealth". A BBC investigation published on 20 November found tenants in some of those blocks living with black mould, rats and cockroaches. It also found marketing that claimed a level of care the sector's regulator says the landlord is not registered to provide.

The BBC identified more than 40 companies marketing supported housing flats to individual investors in the UK and abroad, including in Hong Kong, Macau, Nigeria, Turkey and the Netherlands. About a dozen of them work with the developer Lee Jones. The BBC says none of the individuals or companies it names has broken the law. The complaint, from critics, is that the sector's weak regulation lets the model operate without anyone being clearly responsible for the result.

A flat in Runcorn

The clearest example is Castleview House in Runcorn, Cheshire. Mr Jones's company, The Investment Room, converted the former office block into more than 200 flats in 2019 and sold the freehold in 2021. Nurture Housing Association holds a 25-year lease on some of the flats and houses people on benefits there.

One tenant, Phil, who has severe depression and anxiety, told the BBC he has had no care or support from Nurture and described mould and rats. A Czech investor bought his flat in May 2025 for £163,150. The BBC reports that similar flats in the block cost about £75,000, so the price was more than double.

The Czech agent Britské Reality marketed the flat on the basis that Nurture provided Section 117 aftercare, a statutory entitlement that Phil says does not apply to him. The Regulator of Social Housing told the BBC that Nurture is not registered as a specialised supported housing provider, and called its claim to offer that care "shocking".

Nurture said Castleview is run under a general housing model rather than its supported housing portfolio. It described the Czech advert as "historic marketing material" from third parties and said it would remind agents to keep information accurate. Mr Jones said the properties are "good quality accommodation" and denied a conflict of interest with Nurture's chief executive. He did not answer questions about his marketing role. The investor and the agent did not respond.

Another promotion, by a firm called Bhenito, described "specialist supported living investment" offering "stable, hands-off income for 25 years".

Where the money goes

The London example shows how the income is split. In Cricklewood, Gemelli London 2 Limited, run by French nationals based in the Czech Republic, bought a converted house for £1.83m in January 2025. The provider there, Windrush Alliance, receives nearly £2,000 a month per flat through Barnet Council. Gemelli says it receives about £1,365 per flat per month from Windrush and none of the extra housing benefit.

One tenant, Mo, has mental health issues. He receives £340 a month in universal credit for living costs and pays Windrush £120 a month in service charges. A leak in his flat went unrepaired for more than a year and brought down his ceiling. Gemelli says it was fixed after the BBC made contact, and that it pressed Windrush to act after being told of problems in August 2025.

Windrush has itself been criticised by the Regulator of Social Housing. It admitted it "fell short", wrote off £1.8m sent to companies linked to former directors, and now has a new board. It said its "priority is residents, not investors".

In Bradford, residents of two other Jones conversions, City Exchange and Colonnade House, reported cockroaches, faulty fire alarms, broken lifts, leaks and black mould. Nurture says it manages only individual homes, not communal areas. The freeholder of Castleview and City Exchange blamed "defects" by Mr Jones and said it is taking legal action. The latest management company at Colonnade House said it is fixing the problems.

The accountability gap

The pattern is a chain of owners, leaseholders, providers and councils, with the investor at the far end and the tenant, who has little choice of housing, at the other. The BBC piece does not report yields or investor returns, so the actual return on these flats is not known. The pricing gap at Castleview suggests that at least some buyers paid well above local value.

Abena Oppong-Asare, the Labour chair of the Housing Select Committee, called the marketing of these properties as investments "outrageous", given the public money involved, and said no one is directly accountable. Jasmine Basran of the charity Crisis said the delay in fixing the sector "has taken too long".

The Supported Housing Act, passed three years ago, would create a licensing regime and give councils stronger enforcement powers. It has still not been implemented. Reports elsewhere say a consultation on draft licensing regulations opened in October.

What the licensing rules cover will determine whether they reach the marketing as well as the buildings. The Act regulates landlords and providers in England. The sales pitches in Prague, Hong Kong or Lagos sit outside that remit, and the BBC piece does not name an authority that polices them. The consultation outcome and any commencement date for the Act are the next markers.
