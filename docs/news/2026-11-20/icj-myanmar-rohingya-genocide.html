ROHINGYA GENOCIDE RULING · By C. Tanaka · 2026-11-20 · 5 min read

World court rules Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya

The Peace Palace in The Hague, seat of the International Court of Justice — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ICJ ruled 9-6 that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya in 2017, a binding judgment with no appeal but no obvious enforcement route.

The International Court of Justice has found that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya. The ruling, delivered in The Hague on Thursday, ends a case that began more than seven years ago and is the first time the court has held a state responsible for committing genocide itself. The judgment is final and binding, and there is no appeal.

President Yuji Iwasawa took about three hours to read the decision. The court held, by nine votes to six, that Myanmar's security forces and other state organs committed acts prohibited by the Genocide Convention during the army's 2017 "clearance operations" in northern Rakhine State. It also found that Myanmar had failed to prevent and punish genocide.

A case brought by a third party

The Gambia, a small West African state with no direct stake in Rakhine, filed the case on 11 November 2019, acting on behalf of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. Its standing rested on the idea that the Genocide Convention imposes obligations owed to all parties, so any of them may bring a claim. Myanmar contested this. In 2022 the court rejected its preliminary objections and let the case go forward.

The court had already acted once. In January 2020 it unanimously ordered Myanmar to take provisional measures to prevent genocide against the Rohingya and to preserve evidence. The final judgment found that Myanmar also breached those orders on preserving evidence.

The underlying events are not in dispute in outline. Following attacks by insurgents on police posts in August 2017, the army swept through northern Rakhine. About 700,000 Rohingya fled to Bangladesh, where most of them remain in camps around Cox's Bazar.

What the court found

The judgment rests on three of the five acts listed in Article II of the Convention: killing members of the group, causing them serious bodily or mental harm, and imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group.

The court put the number of Rohingya killed at at least 10,000. It counted at least 1,834 rapes and found that nearly 400 villages, roughly 40 per cent of almost 1,000 in three northern Rakhine townships, were partly or wholly destroyed. It cited executions of men and boys, the rape of girls and women, and villages and bridges burned and bulldozed. On births, it pointed to restrictions on marriage and childbearing in Maungdaw Township and the denial of legal registration to Rohingya children. It also cited the killing of infants, which Iwasawa said "supports the inference that Myanmar's security forces acted with the intent to prevent births among the group."

Genocide is distinguished from other atrocity crimes by the requirement of dolus specialis: an intent to destroy a protected group, in whole or in part. Proving it is the hard part. It is why Croatia's claim against Serbia failed in 2015, and why the court in 2007 found that Serbia had not itself committed genocide at Srebrenica, only that it had failed to prevent it.

Here the court applied its standard of "fully convincing evidence" and concluded that intent was the only reasonable inference. It looked at the context, the selective targeting of Rohingya, the attacks on children and the use of sexual violence, and the scale and systematic character of the conduct. It rejected alternative explanations, namely counter-insurgency, displacement without destruction, and collective punishment. It also held that a state's failure to destroy a group at every opportunity does not rule out genocidal intent. The 815,000 Rohingya living in northern Rakhine were treated as a substantial part of the group on numbers alone.

The finding is limited in time and place: the 2017 operations in northern Rakhine. The court was not fully convinced on every count the Gambia advanced, and its reasoning on responsibility for the conduct of private actors was narrower than the claimant had hoped.

Orders, and the limits of enforcement

The remedies are broad. Myanmar must stop ongoing violations by enacting legislation that gives full effect to the Convention. It must investigate and, where warranted, prosecute and punish those reasonably suspected of the acts. It must give guarantees of non-repetition. And it must provide restitution, which the court defined to include safe, voluntary and dignified return and family reunification for displaced Rohingya, whether inside Myanmar or abroad, along with compensation to the group and its members. The amount and method of compensation will be decided in a later phase. Myanmar must report to the court within two years.

The weakness is enforcement. The ICJ has no police. If Myanmar ignores the judgment, the Gambia can take the matter to the UN Security Council under the UN Charter, where China and Russia, which have shielded Myanmar's military, hold vetoes. The council has never used its powers to enforce an ICJ ruling. Myanmar's army-run government, which seized power in the 2021 coup, has little incentive to pass laws prosecuting its own officers.

The nine-to-six split also matters. All fifteen judges wrote separately, and the dissenters' reasoning will shape how far the judgment is cited. That reasoning has not yet been analysed in detail.

Reaction and reference value

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the ruling "powerful recognition of the Rohingya victims of genocide" and said it reaffirmed that impunity for such acts must not stand.

Its practical weight lies in what it gives others. It is the court's clearest finding of state responsibility for mass atrocities since Bosnia v Serbia in 2007, and it goes further, because that case found only a failure to prevent. Prosecutors at the International Criminal Court, who have a separate investigation into deportations of Rohingya, and courts exercising universal jurisdiction, notably in Argentina, can now treat the existence of genocide in 2017 as established by the principal judicial organ of the UN. Aid donors and host governments weighing the future of the camps in Bangladesh get a legal definition of what return must involve. The court's insistence on safe, dignified return, including from abroad, sets a high bar for any repatriation scheme Myanmar's authorities might propose while fighting continues in Rakhine.

What comes next

Two dates matter. The first is the compensation phase, in which the court will set the scale and form of reparations, a process that in other ICJ cases has taken years. The second is the two-year deadline for Myanmar's compliance report. Beyond the court itself, the questions are whether Bangladesh and donors tie support for repatriation to the judgment's terms, and whether the Security Council is ever asked to act on it.
