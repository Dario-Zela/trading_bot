BP weighs West Africa LNG stake sale; Equinor and Eni in focus

In one sentence
Bloomberg reports BP is considering selling its stake in a West African LNG project as it prunes assets, but the project, stake size and timing are not given and nothing is confirmed.

BP is weighing a sale of its stake in a West African liquefied natural gas project, according to a report from Bloomberg relayed by Investing.com. The move would fit the company's continuing effort to prune its portfolio. The report as available does not name the project, the size of the holding, any prospective buyers or a timetable, and BP has not confirmed it.

What is reported

The substance is thin, and it is worth being plain about that. Bloomberg's account, as summarised by Investing.com, says BP is considering divesting its interest in an LNG venture in West Africa. The stated motive is streamlining: shedding assets that no longer sit in the group's chosen core. No valuation has been attached, and no adviser or counterparty has been named in the material seen here.

"Considering" is a modest verb in deal reporting. Companies weigh sales they never launch, and sales they launch often fail to clear at the hoped-for price. Until BP or a counterparty says more, the report describes an intention, not a transaction.

The portfolio logic

The story gains weight from context rather than detail. BP has spent the past two years telling investors it intends to raise cash from disposals and concentrate capital on fewer, higher-returning businesses. Its publicly stated target, set out in early 2025, is roughly $20bn of divestments by the end of 2027. A stake in an LNG project in West Africa is the kind of holding that could plausibly feature in such a programme: capital-intensive, long-dated, and exposed to a single host-country regime and a single export route.

That is an inference from the stated strategy, not something the report establishes. The absence of a project name matters here: West Africa's gas developments differ widely in maturity, operatorship and partner structure, and the price and pool of buyers would differ with them. A producing asset with a long offtake contract attracts infrastructure funds and national oil companies. An undeveloped one attracts fewer.

Why buyers and producers watch

For LNG buyers, a change of owner rarely alters physical flows overnight, but it can change who holds marketing rights and who funds the next phase of a project. For West African host governments and local producers, a major's retreat can open room for state-linked or mid-sized operators, or leave a gap where capital is scarce. Neither effect can be sized without knowing which project is involved.

The other names in the frame

The cluster of European energy headlines around the report includes Equinor, whose third-quarter results are drawing investor attention, and Eni, which is reported to be expanding a partnership with the engineering firm Lummus. Neither item carries figures or terms in the material available, and neither has been checked against primary sources. They are context for a sector in which European groups are trimming and reshaping portfolios, not evidence that either company is a buyer of BP's stake. No link between them and the BP report has been reported.

What to watch

The first test is whether BP or the project's partners confirm the interest, or whether a fuller Bloomberg or Reuters account names the asset. After that comes any sign of a formal sale process: an appointed adviser, a data room, or an indicated price range. BP's next scheduled results and strategy update are the likeliest venue for management to say how far its disposal target has progressed.
