Airbnb's new house rules come into force

In one sentence
Airbnb's new house rules, in force 20 November, bar hosts from demanding checkout chores and limit them to a standard rule list, though the details rest on secondary reports.

Airbnb's revised house rules for hosts and guests take effect today, 20 November. They limit what a host can demand of a guest on departure and fix the list of rules a host can set. The changes are not a regulatory measure. They are platform policy, set by the company itself. Even so, they alter the terms on which millions of stays are booked.

The account below rests on secondary press coverage, including trade outlet Skift and UK regional news sites, and on a search summary. It has not been checked against Airbnb's own policy text. Details may differ in the final wording, and some may vary by market.

What changes

The central change is a ban on checkout chores. Hosts may no longer ask guests to wash and put away dishes, strip beds or start a washing machine. Hosts may still ask guests to put dirty dishes in the sink, gather used towels in one place and sort rubbish and recycling into the indoor bins. Reports say Airbnb allows exceptions for local conditions, though how far those extend is unclear.

Instead of free-text instructions, hosts will choose from a list of seven standard house rules. Two are new: a maximum number of overnight guests, and locking up at checkout.

The rules must be disclosed before booking. Hosts cannot add new ones after a reservation is made, whether by message, by a sign in the home or by instruction in person. Guests must follow house rules that comply with Airbnb's policies, and repeated or serious breaches can bring consequences for them.

Why it matters commercially

The change targets a long-running source of friction: fees charged to guests for failing to meet checkout instructions, sometimes long lists of them. Airbnb's stated rationale is that house rules can be "confusing, onerous and hard to manage." For guests, the practical effect is more predictable terms and fewer surprise charges. For hosts, it removes a tool for shifting cleaning labour, and cost, onto the guest. Hosts will have to price that work into nightly rates or cleaning fees. Whether they do is the thing to measure.

The rules have drawn a heated reaction online, with guests broadly welcoming them and many hosts objecting. That is anecdote, not data.

What the rules do not do

The policy does not touch local short-let law. Licensing schemes, night caps and registration requirements in cities and countries remain with governments, and the headline coverage cites no regulator and no figures. Nothing found so far shows the change reducing listings or affecting Airbnb's financial results.

The next evidence will be host behaviour: whether cleaning fees rise, whether listings leave the platform, and whether Airbnb reports any effect in its next quarterly results. Airbnb's policy text and help pages, once confirmed, will settle the open questions on exceptions and enforcement.
