Government to curb SLAPP claims against whistleblowers; Meta whistleblower urges NDA ban

In one sentence
The UK will curb SLAPP claims against whistleblowers, while Meta's Sarah Wynn-Williams urges PM Burnham to extend NDA limits retroactively and to cover AI insiders.

The government is preparing to limit the use of SLAPPs, the strategic lawsuits that wealthy people and companies use to frighten critics into silence, against whistleblowers. Meanwhile, a former Meta executive has used a London lecture to press Prime Minister Andy Burnham to go further and restrict the non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) that keep many workers quiet.

The two measures target different tools. A SLAPP is a legal claim, often for defamation or breach of confidence, whose aim is to impose ruinous costs instead of winning in court. An NDA works earlier and more quietly: a worker signs it, often as part of a severance deal, and the threat of legal action does the rest.

What is known about the SLAPP move

The detail is thin. BBC Politics and the Guardian report that the government will curb SLAPP-style claims aimed at whistleblowers. At the time of writing, no bill name, timetable or definition of the claims covered had been confirmed in the sources reviewed, so how wide the protection will run is not yet clear.

The backdrop is slow progress. Campaigners have pressed for general anti-SLAPP legislation for years, and two private members' bills, one in each House of Parliament, have been introduced. Reports in recent months have said the government has promised its own legislation "as soon as time allows". Any such protection would apply to England and Wales only, because the matter is devolved.

The case for going further

Sarah Wynn-Williams, Meta's former director of global public policy, made the argument on 27 November at London's annual Orwell lecture. She welcomed Mr Burnham's earlier commitment to restrict NDAs, which is aimed at victims of crime and workplace harassment. She said it does not go far enough.

She made two demands. The first is that any restrictions apply retroactively, to agreements already signed. Without that, she argued, many people would stay muzzled. The second is that the limits reach beyond harassment to cover insiders who know about the risks of artificial intelligence and are contractually barred from saying so. She likened NDAs to the "memory hole" in Orwell's 1984. She also cited cases involving David Walliams and Russell Brand, where reports suggest NDAs were used to silence women on matters of public concern.

Her own position gives the argument force. She remains restricted by an interim arbitration ruling that Meta obtained over her memoir, Careless People. Meta disputes the book's accuracy and has called her legal challenge a commercial scheme.

Where the government stands

The government's current NDA work is narrower than she wants. A consultation, launched on 15 April and closed on 8 July, asked what conditions an NDA must meet to stay valid, whom workers must be free to speak to, and whether protections should reach agency workers and the self-employed. It covers harassment and discrimination, and changes are expected to take effect in 2027. Employment Rights Minister Kate Dearden said at the launch: "We are committed to ending a culture of silence and impunity." A separate review of the whistleblowing framework was also promised.

That leaves the real question. Limiting SLAPPs would stop employers suing whistleblowers after they speak. A wider NDA ban would stop the silence being bought before anyone speaks, and would reach technology firms where the issue is a safety concern with no harassment or crime involved. Which way the government goes should become clearer when it publishes its response to the NDA consultation and sets out the scope of its SLAPP measure.
