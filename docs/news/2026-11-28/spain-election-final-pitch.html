Spanish PM and rivals make final pitch ahead of election

In one sentence
Spain votes on 29 November after Sánchez called a snap election; polls favour the PP but it likely needs far-right Vox for a majority, a coalition question that matters for euro-area fiscal credibility.

Spain votes on Sunday in a snap general election that the Socialist prime minister, Pedro Sánchez, called after parliament rejected two of his government's housing decrees. The final campaign rallies are over. The last published polls, from 23 and 24 November, put the conservative Popular Party (PP) of Alberto Núñez Feijóo ahead. Whether it can govern depends on a party that has never sat in a Spanish government since the end of the Franco dictatorship.

The result matters beyond Spain's borders. Spain is the euro area's fourth-largest economy, and a durable government in Madrid underpins confidence in its budget and reform plans. That confidence is what holds down borrowing costs across the euro-area periphery. Bond investors are the obvious audience, though neither of the sources used here reported any bond-market data. The piece is therefore framed around the stakes rather than a market reaction.

How Spain got here

The last general election was in July 2023, and the legal deadline for the next was 2027. Sánchez's minority coalition of the Socialists (PSOE) and the left-wing Sumar alliance never held a stable majority. It relied on EH Bildu, the BNG, the PNV and ERC. Junts, the Catalan party whose votes helped secure his 2023 investiture, has since repeatedly voted against the government.

Housing finished the arrangement. Mass protests over rents and prices preceded the defeat of the government's relief measures in parliament, and the early vote followed. In an October YouGov survey, 53% of respondents named housing as one of the two most important issues. Home prices nearly doubled in the decade to the second quarter of 2026, according to Eurostat. Analysts cited by Al Jazeera put the shortfall at 750,000 homes.

The two main parties offer opposite remedies. The PP would repeal the 2023 housing law and scrap rent caps in high-pressure areas. The Socialists would extend renter protections and require at least a quarter of new homes to be public or permanently price-restricted.

The arithmetic

The Congress of Deputies has 350 seats, so a majority needs 176. The final El País average, published on 24 November, had the PP on 31.4%, the Socialists on 29.1% and the far-right Vox on 17.9%. Four polls published a week before the vote, by Ipsos, Data10, GAD3 and Hamalgama Métrica, all put the PP first. They differ on the size of its lead and on Vox's result. Several seat projections place a PP–Vox bloc above 176. At least one aggregator, Election Trend, still shows a result without a clear majority. Spanish law bans publishing new polls in the final five days, so these are the last numbers.

A PP win alone would therefore not settle the question. The PP is expected to win the most seats but to need Vox to reach 176. That would put the far right in a Spanish cabinet for the first time since 1975. Immigration is the likely point of friction in such a coalition, particularly after the Ceuta crossings in July. Vox also campaigns on lower taxes and against Catalan and Basque separatism. Sánchez's route back is narrower. He would need to rebuild the same patchwork of regional and left-wing parties, with Junts a less reliable partner than before and a new Broad Front led by Ada Colau replacing Sumar. Podemos is running separately.

What the campaign argued about

Feijóo ran on cost of living and corruption, telling a rally in Murcia on 22 November that voters should not forget that "inflation is eating into your pay check". Inflation reached 4.9% in October, against 4% for the euro zone, and a November flash estimate showed it easing to 4.1%. The government also faces criticism over two deadly train collisions and several corruption cases.

Sánchez's pitch leaned on the economy and on foreign policy. Spain grew 2.7% year on year in the third quarter, against 1.3% for the euro zone. He also used the campaign to distance himself from Washington, asking what good President Trump had brought to the United States and the world. Neither main party has committed to NATO's target of spending 5% of GDP on defence, a figure that would strain any budget.

What to watch

Polls close at 8pm local time on Sunday, and first results are expected that night. The first test is whether the PP and Vox together reach 176 seats. If they do not, Spain may face weeks of negotiation or a repeat of the stalemates it endured in 2019. The second test is the budget. A government that cannot pass spending plans would leave Madrid's fiscal path less predictable. A PP–Vox coalition would instead raise questions about how far tax cuts and defence commitments can be reconciled with deficit targets. Neither outcome is a crisis on current evidence, but the coalition that emerges will decide how that fiscal path reads to investors. Elsewhere in the euro area, France's slide into recession leaves less room for a second large economy to wobble.
