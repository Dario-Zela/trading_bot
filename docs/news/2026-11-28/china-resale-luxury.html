Inside China's $4bn secondhand luxury boom

In one sentence
A reported $4bn Chinese secondhand luxury market raises the question of whether shoppers are trading down from European brands, but the figure is unverified and the evidence is thin.

China's secondhand luxury market is being described as a $4bn business, and the label is attracting attention in Europe. The question for investors is whether resale is a sideline for Chinese shoppers or a sign that they are moving away from the primary channels run by LVMH, Kering, Hermès and their peers. The data published so far don't settle it.

The $4bn figure comes from a Wall Street Journal report. It was not independently verified for this article, and the base year and growth rate were not available. Other estimates differ widely. SCMP carried a headline putting the resale boom at US$30bn, without a definition that could be checked. A 2020 Jing Daily report put the market at RMB 17.3bn (about $2.7bn), roughly double the previous year. The gaps probably come down to definitions: whether a figure counts only branded handbags and watches or also jewellery and apparel, and whether it measures resale value or platform commissions. A single number without a stated year or scope therefore says little.

What is known about the market

The consistent finding in earlier research is that resale in China is small relative to the overall market. One estimate put it at about 5% of China's luxury sales, against roughly 28% in Japan and 31% in the US. That gap is the basis of the bullish case: if China moved toward Japanese penetration, resale would grow several times over from its present level.

The buyers skew young. One survey found 52% of secondhand luxury consumers in China were aged 29 to 36. Growth has run through domestic platforms, including Alibaba's Xianyu and specialist resellers such as Feiyu and Plum. Authenticity is the main obstacle, since counterfeits are common. Much of the trade rests on third-party verification, which is why resale platforms tend to advertise inspection services.

The read-across to Europe

The argument that resale hurts the luxury houses has two parts, and each needs evidence. The first is substitution: a shopper who buys a used bag in place of a new one is a lost primary sale. The second is that resale can support new-goods demand. Strong secondary prices for Hermès, Chanel and Rolex make new purchases look like stores of value, and brands that hold their price on the resale market can defend list-price rises.

The WSJ report as summarised gives no company names, platform data or quoted executives, and the claim that resale is taking share from new goods is unproven. A rise in secondhand turnover could coincide with softer new-goods demand without causing it. Both might reflect weaker confidence among Chinese consumers and a squeeze on discretionary income. Mainland spending has been the weak point for European luxury groups in recent reporting periods. Resale growth in that setting is consistent with trading down, but it doesn't demonstrate it.

What to watch

The test will come in the groups' next reporting cycle. Investors will look for China revenue trends at LVMH, Kering and Richemont, any commentary on resale or pre-owned programmes, and whether platforms publish a transaction base for the $4bn figure. Until the WSJ's definition, year and growth rate are checked against a second source, the figure is best read as a sign of direction, not a measure of how much business is shifting.
