Gamers use AI and leaked files to clone Grand Theft Auto 6

In one sentence
Fans are using AI tools and a leaked Xbox build to port GTA 6 to PC, and Take-Two has responded by getting thousands of GitHub copies taken down.

Take-Two Interactive is trying to shut down a wave of fan projects that use leaked files and AI coding tools to run Grand Theft Auto 6 on PCs. The game exists officially only on consoles. The episode shows how little it now takes for hobbyists to try to rebuild a studio's work, and how hard that is to police.

What happened

Earlier this month a hacker or group calling itself Cyberleek released what it said was a near-complete Xbox development build of GTA 6. It later claimed to have leaked the missing executable file needed to launch it. Rockstar and Take-Two have not confirmed that the build is genuine.

The game has since launched on consoles. One report puts its first full day of retail sales at $3.8bn (£2.9bn), which would make it the biggest entertainment launch on record. Rockstar has not announced a PC version. For comparison, GTA 5 reached PC 19 months after its console release. That gap leaves an obvious opening for fans.

One leak-based effort was called Leonida. Its Discord server had almost 180,000 members when it went offline. Developers there tried to get the leaked Xbox build running on Windows. The project's defenders said users would "bring their own files". Take-Two called that framing "intentionally misleading". Its argument was that the only files anyone could realistically bring were the leaked ones. Its notice called the project a derivative work of GTA VI. GitHub processed the takedown against 3,007 repositories, covering the main project and its forks. One notice said: "There are no remedies available to rectify this clear attempt to facilitate copyright infringement."

Where AI comes in

There are two different things going on, and they carry different legal weight.

The first is replication. A user asks a chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT to build a new game that resembles an existing one, working from screenshots or descriptions. That produces a lookalike. It does not copy the original's code or assets.

The second is reverse engineering. Tools decompile a compiled game back into approximate source code, or recompile it to run on different hardware. That work depends on having the leaked build. Teams are trying to use AI agents to do it, and the recent surge is linked to Anthropic's Opus 5.5 model, released in September, and to agentic tools such as Claude Code. Anthropic has reportedly told Claude Code to avoid recreating specific existing games, saying that would breach its usage policy.

The second route is the one Take-Two is pursuing. AI shortens the labour, but the legal problem starts with the leaked files.

Can Take-Two police it?

Take-Two has done this before. In 2021 it had the fan rebuilds re3 and reVC, of GTA III and Vice City, removed from GitHub, and it later sued their developers. Chief executive Strauss Zelnick has called piracy a "fact of life" in entertainment, but said it had been "less of an issue" for games because of their size and complexity. He reportedly told investors the company is "utterly focused on enhancing our technical defences and... legal activities appropriately". Take-Two did not respond to the BBC's request for comment.

The approach has limits. A takedown can remove a public repository, but copies and forks move to other sites. Observers also disagree about how far cloning can go. Mark Brown of Game Maker's Toolkit compared a replica to "looking at a painting and making your own replica". The Game Business's Christopher Dring said PC versions are complex because of the range of hardware involved. He added that a delayed official PC release could give Rockstar a second marketing moment.

Two things will show where this goes. One is whether Take-Two follows its notices with lawsuits, as it did in 2021. The other is whether Rockstar announces a PC release date, which would take away much of the demand these projects feed on.
