Zelensky calls Trump-Putin friendship 'strange' in BBC interview

In one sentence
Ukraine's president described the Trump-Putin friendship as "strange" in a BBC interview, voicing Kyiv's unease while peace diplomacy remains unresolved.

Volodymyr Zelensky has publicly questioned the closeness of Donald Trump's relationship with Vladimir Putin. In an interview published by the BBC, Ukraine's president described the friendship between the American and Russian leaders as "strange". The word is mild, but Kyiv's president is the one using it, and he is talking about the man whose support Ukraine most needs.

The interview was published on 28 November. This account rests on the BBC's headline and summary of it. The fuller transcript was not available, so it does not reproduce Zelensky's remarks beyond that one word.

A relationship Kyiv cannot ignore

The unease is not new. Zelensky has long had to manage a US president who praises Putin as readily as he presses Kyiv. In an exchange with Trump at a joint appearance earlier in the war, Trump himself conceded that his warm words about the Russian leader "sound a little strange". Zelensky smiled and agreed. In a BBC interview with Jeremy Bowen in February 2026, he said he believed Putin had already started a wider war and had to be stopped. He also has said he does not trust Putin.

The latest remark fits that pattern. It is a public expression of doubt about whether Washington's channel to Moscow works for Ukraine's interests. It stops short of open criticism. Zelensky depends on American weapons, intelligence and diplomatic weight, and he has generally framed disagreements with Trump as differences over method rather than ruptures.

Diplomacy without a result

The timing matters because peace diplomacy has produced no settlement. Talks involving Washington, Kyiv and Moscow have repeatedly stalled, and the disputes over territory, security guarantees and sequencing that held them up have not been resolved. Without a deal, the personal rapport between the two presidents is one of the few variables Kyiv can comment on, even if it cannot control it.

There are two plausible readings of the remark. The first is that it is a signal of concern: Kyiv fears that a president who feels warmly towards Putin may press Ukraine to accept terms it considers unsafe. The second is that it is positioning. It puts on the record a doubt about Moscow's intentions in front of an international audience, including European governments that are being asked to carry more of the financial burden. The interview text itself would be needed to say which emphasis Zelensky intended.

What to watch

The next test is whether the White House responds, and whether any new round of talks is scheduled. A firm date for direct negotiations would show whether the "strange" friendship is producing movement or only warm words. Until then, Kyiv will keep describing Washington's conduct in terms that are careful to stay short of an open break.
