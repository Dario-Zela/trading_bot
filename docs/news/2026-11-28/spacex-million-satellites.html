Regulators approve SpaceX plan for a million data satellites

In one sentence
The FCC has approved SpaceX's plan for up to a million orbital data satellites, but the licence's terms are unconfirmed and astronomers and environmental groups are objecting.

US regulators have approved SpaceX's plan to put up to a million data-processing satellites into orbit. It is the largest satellite licence ever contemplated, and astronomers and environmental groups are already objecting.

The Federal Communications Commission cleared the constellation, which SpaceX calls Starmind, on Wednesday, according to reporting by the BBC and advocacy group accounts. SpaceX filed the application on 30 January. It describes a network of solar-powered satellites that would carry out computing in orbit rather than on the ground, to meet demand from artificial intelligence. Deployment is targeted by 2040.

What was approved, and what is not yet clear

The headline number needs care. The reports describe approval of a plan for "up to" a million satellites. They do not make clear whether that is a full licence or a staged authorisation, or what conditions attach. The FCC's order was not available to this writer, and the reporting does not set out the terms. Earlier precedent argues for caution: in 2022 the FCC approved 7,500 additional Starlink satellites, well short of the nearly 30,000 SpaceX first requested.

The filing proposed narrow orbital shells, each up to 50km deep, at altitudes of roughly 500km to 2,000km. Nothing public says who would buy the computing, or how SpaceX would deal with heat. Dumping heat is hard in a vacuum, and launch costs and debris are further engineering doubts. FCC chairman Brendan Carr called the plan "a bold new frontier" and said it would take the world into "the era of super intelligence."

The objections

The sharpest objections come from two groups. The American Astronomical Society said it was "disheartened and dismayed" and that the constellation "would significantly undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities". It warned that radio astronomy in particular was at risk. Optical astronomers have complained for years that existing satellite constellations leave streaks across images. A fleet of this size is a different order of magnitude: there are only some 15,000 working satellites in orbit today.

Environmental groups make a legal argument as well. Earthjustice's Jan Hasselman said that "the law requires the FCC to consider all the risks and impacts of these proposals." Critics say the approval came without the environmental review required under the National Environmental Policy Act. Tim Whitehouse of Public Employees for Environmental Responsibility called it "reckless". A challenge in the courts is plausible, though none has been reported yet.

What to watch

Three things will show how big this really is. The first is the text of the FCC order: the number of satellites authorised at each stage, any milestones, and any debris or astronomy conditions. The second is whether a legal challenge is filed over the missing environmental review. The third is whether SpaceX starts launching Starmind hardware, and whether any customer for orbital computing emerges. A licence is permission, not a working system, and the distance between a million-satellite filing and a million satellites is very large.
