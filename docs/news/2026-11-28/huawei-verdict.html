Huawei found guilty of stealing US trade secrets but acquitted of racketeering

In one sentence
A Brooklyn jury convicted Huawei on 10 of 12 counts, including trade-secret conspiracy, but acquitted it of racketeering; Huawei will appeal and sentencing is set for September 2027.

A federal jury in Brooklyn has convicted Huawei of conspiring to steal trade secrets from American companies. It has also cleared the company of the racketeering charge that prosecutors had used to tie the whole case together. The verdict, returned on 24 November, leaves the US government with a conviction on most of what it alleged and without the single count that framed it as a criminal enterprise.

What the jury decided

According to reports of the verdict, the jury convicted Huawei Technologies and three subsidiaries on 10 of 12 counts. The subsidiaries are Huawei Device, Futurewei Technologies and Huawei Device USA. The convictions cover conspiracy to steal trade secrets, bank fraud, wire fraud, money laundering and violations of US sanctions on Iran.

Jurors acquitted on two counts: racketeering conspiracy and conspiracy to obstruct justice.

The headline needs a qualifier. The trade-secret conviction is one of ten, and the bank-fraud and sanctions convictions sit alongside it. The racketeering acquittal is the largest single loss for prosecutors, but it is not the whole of the verdict.

Why the racketeering count mattered

Racketeering law, usually called RICO, lets prosecutors treat a series of separate offences as the output of one organisation. That makes it a useful way to present a sprawling case to a jury. Huawei's lawyer, Brian Heberlig, said the racketeering count was "the only count that brought all of the allegations together", and that the jury rejected it.

This reading comes from the defence, so it should be weighed accordingly. Even so, the outcome is consistent with it. Jurors seem to have been willing to convict on specific conduct, such as the sanctions violations and the theft of secrets. They were not willing to find that the conduct amounted to a company-wide criminal enterprise.

That distinction affects how the case can be used. A racketeering conviction would have supported the broader government argument that Huawei's misconduct was systemic. Without it, the convictions stand as findings on particular schemes.

Huawei's response and what comes next

Huawei called the remaining convictions "equally without legal or factual merit". It said the five trade-secret disputes cited by prosecutors had previously been resolved as civil matters. Heberlig said the company would appeal. Reports put the trial at roughly three months, with accounts differing between about 11 and 12 weeks.

Sentencing is scheduled for September 2027. Until then, the penalties are unknown. A corporation cannot be jailed, so the practical consequences will come through fines, any forfeiture and the knock-on effects for Huawei's dealings with banks and Western partners. None of that has been set. An appeal could take years and may target the trade-secret findings, where the company's argument rests on the earlier civil settlements.

The case is a different matter from a 2019 Texas civil trial, in which a jury cleared CNEX Labs of stealing Huawei's trade secrets. That dispute is unrelated to this prosecution.

The next markers are the post-trial motions and the September 2027 sentencing. Those will show how heavy the penalties are and whether the convictions survive Huawei's challenge.
