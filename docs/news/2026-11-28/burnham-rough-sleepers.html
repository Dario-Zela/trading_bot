Burnham's Christmas vow: a third of rough sleepers offered a bed or support

In one sentence
Ministers say just over a third of rough sleepers have been offered a bed or support under Burnham's Christmas pledge, but the figures do not show who accepted or who is off the street.

Ministers say a little over a third of people sleeping rough in England have been offered a bed or support since the prime minister's Christmas pledge on rough sleeping. The government calls this "early progress". The published figures do not show how many people accepted an offer or are still on the street.

Andy Burnham has promised a route off the street for everyone sleeping rough in England by Christmas. The drive is backed by £442m of winter funding, and government material puts the longer-term commitment at £874m over three years for extra bed spaces and expanded outreach. It borrows from Greater Manchester's A Bed Every Night scheme, which Burnham championed as mayor and which now offers about 600 beds a night. Ministers have said the pledge does not mean everyone will have permanent housing by Christmas, only an offer.

What the figures say

Official figures released on Friday 27 November show that councils made offers to 3,276 people in October, after the funding was distributed on 30 September. Set against an estimate of 9,369 people sleeping rough in September, that is about 35%, which is where the "third" comes from.

The comparison is looser than it looks. The two numbers cover different months and different methods: one counts people offered help, the other is an estimate of people sleeping rough. They do not show what share of those counted in September received help. Some councils were also unable to report how many people they had helped. Reports differ on the total as well. One cites 4,251 offers made so far, while 3,276 is the number of people who received at least one offer.

Ministers also said 497 people were occupying nearly 2,000 new bed spaces. That figure is closer to an outcome measure than the offers count, though it is a small share of the people involved.

What is not yet known

Campaigners say the government has not disclosed how many offers were declined, and the published data do not show how many people left the streets. That matters because rough sleeping is not a fixed group. People who have been outside for long periods may not accept an offer straight away. Others who are offered a bed return to the street within days.

The homelessness charity Porchlight has warned that a short stay without a lasting home can leave people trapped in the cycle, and Shelter has argued that ending rough sleeping has to include building council homes. Burnham himself has acknowledged that government money alone may not be enough.

Councils are only partly confident. About 63% told the government they expect to meet the target of offering everyone a route off the street by 1 December. 14% said they were not confident, and just over a fifth did not give a view.

What to watch

The pledge is worded as an offer, so the first test is whether the offers count approaches the true number of people sleeping rough by the 1 December deadline. The harder test is what follows: how many accept, how many are still in accommodation in the new year, and whether the next official rough sleeping snapshot shows a fall. Until the government publishes acceptance and retention figures, "early progress" describes activity rather than results.
