Labour MPs press Burnham to block Rosebank as Badenoch makes net-zero abandonment a test for Tory candidates

In one sentence
About 100 Labour MPs are pressing Burnham to reject Rosebank, possibly via an amendment to the energy independence bill, while Badenoch makes scrapping net zero a Tory candidate test.

Andy Burnham's first test of how far a prime minister can hold a line against his own benches is arriving over an oilfield 80 miles north-west of Shetland. Roughly 100 Labour MPs, among them ministerial aides, have told him not to approve Rosebank. Some are reportedly ready to put that into law. The decision is meant to be taken as a quasi-judicial one, which in theory shields it from exactly this kind of pressure.

The letter and the process

The Guardian reports that 92 backbenchers and at least four frontbenchers signed a letter urging the prime minister not to back the project. Other outlets put the figure at around 100, counting ministerial aides. The signatories argue that approval would "permanently damage our climate credentials as a party and as a country" and could be a "point of no return" for Labour's standing on the issue. They also dispute the economic case. They call Rosebank "not a serious revenue raising measure", estimating that it would raise less than 0.1 per cent of annual UK tax revenue. Another Labour MP, writing in the Guardian, argued that it would not cut household energy bills, because the oil would be sold to the highest bidder.

The Department for Energy Security and Net Zero says any decision will go through the quasi-judicial process. That process weighs environmental assessments and the public representations received during regulation. It is not a straight ministerial call. In practice this limits what Burnham can say and do. The energy secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh, is reportedly leaning towards rejection but cannot state a view without prejudging the case. The Western Morning News and other reports suggest that eight cabinet ministers oppose approval, and that Chancellor John Healey is keen on it. These accounts are attributed to unnamed sources and have not been confirmed on the record.

No date has been given for the decision. One company with a stake in the project expects an answer by the end of the year. Fahnbulleh approved the Jackdaw field last month, so the department has already shown it will sign off North Sea projects.

Legislation as the lever

The sharper threat is procedural. The Times reports that opponents could amend the energy independence bill, which has its second reading next week, to write Labour's ban on new oil and gas licences into statute and extend it to Rosebank. One source told the paper that a one-line amendment could be tabled "very easily". The Telegraph reports that the roughly 80 MPs who back new oil and gas projects would be willing to use the same bill to strip the ban out.

That would turn a planning-style decision into a floor fight. It would also put the government in an awkward position. Ministers have to defend a process that is supposed to be insulated from politics, while their own backbenchers show the insulation is thin. A ruling reached under open parliamentary pressure would be easier to challenge in court. Approving the field against it would be costly inside the party.

Public opinion is less settled than the letter suggests. An Independent-reported poll found 52 per cent in favour of approval and 19 per cent against. Support is far higher, at 73 per cent, among the 35 per cent who think approval would lower their bills. Among everyone else it falls to 41 per cent. Backers stress jobs, particularly in north-east Scotland. Critics point to the recent collapse of the Cop31 climate talks in Turkey as a reason for Britain not to loosen its own commitments.

Badenoch raises the stakes

The Conservatives are making it harder for Labour to find a middle path. The BBC reports that Kemi Badenoch, who has led the party since November 2024, will require candidates to sign a pledge to scrap net-zero targets. The exact wording, scope and timing of the pledge could not be verified for this article. It builds on a position she has held for some time. As leader she has promised to repeal the Climate Change Act, which made the 2050 target legally binding, and to scrap carbon levies on industry. A candidate pledge turns that into a condition of standing for the party.

The two fights feed each other. A Tory party that treats net zero as a loyalty test gives Labour's pro-drilling wing a ready-made attack on its colleagues and gives Labour's climate wing a reason to say the party's identity is at stake. Polling cited in the coverage of Badenoch's policy found that 37 per cent of 2024 Conservative voters would not consider a party that did not back net zero. That suggests the pledge carries electoral risk for the Tories too.

What to watch

The energy independence bill's second reading next week will show whether an amendment on Rosebank is tabled and how many Labour MPs sign it. The next marker is Fahnbulleh's ruling, which could come before the end of the year. Whichever way it goes, it will set the precedent for how a government with a restive party handles the next North Sea decision.
