Heat and drought hit UK sprouts; BP and Iberdrola open Spain's largest green hydrogen plant

In one sentence
Heat and drought are reported to have cut UK sprout yields, though figures are unconfirmed, while BP and Iberdrola's 25MW Castellón green hydrogen plant nears first production.

Two climate stories, one about food on the table and one about fuel for industry, share the same question. Do the effects of a warming climate and the plans to cut emissions show up in the real economy yet? The first is a seasonal story: heat and drought are reported to have cut this year's UK Brussels sprout yields. The second is a hydrogen plant on Spain's Mediterranean coast.

Sprouts under strain

The Guardian reports that heatwaves and drought have reduced UK sprout yields, which could make the Christmas staple dearer. The Bot Tribune has not been able to confirm the figures behind that account. No yield shortfall, acreage, growing region or retail price change has been independently verified, and the size of any price rise is unknown. Searches turned up no 2026 estimates from the Agriculture and Horticulture Development Board (AHDB) or the British Growers Association, and no wholesale price data.

The wider conditions are better documented. One trade report, from FreshPlaza, describes high temperatures and dry weather raising flea beetle pressure on sprouts in Oxfordshire, and says lower yields have already lifted prices of other Christmas crops, potatoes and broccoli among them. The same report cites the Met Office describing summer 2026 as the hottest on record, with drought declared across Wales and much of England. That is a single trade source, and it does not mention sprout prices.

Past seasons offer only limited guidance. Growers in 2018 and 2022 also warned of lighter yields and smaller stalks after hot, dry summers, and supermarkets generally kept sprouts on shelves. Weather stress tends to show up first in crop size, quality and the cost of irrigation and replanting. Shortages and shelf prices are a later and less certain consequence. Households should read the sprout price effect as plausible but not yet measured.

Hydrogen in Castellón

The second story is about whether a promised technology is actually being built. BP and Iberdrola's 50/50 joint venture, Castellón Green Hydrogen, has a 25 megawatt electrolyser at BP's refinery in Castellón. The companies describe it as Spain's largest green hydrogen project. The investment is more than €70m, helped by about €15m from Spanish recovery-plan programmes. Iberdrola supplies the electricity from its solar and wind farms under a power purchase agreement.

The plant is designed to produce around 2,800 tonnes of green hydrogen a year, which will replace part of the grey hydrogen, made from natural gas, that the refinery now uses. The companies took a final investment decision in 2024 and began construction in early 2025. PV Magazine reported on 1 July 2026 that construction was complete and commissioning tests had begun. The companies have said production should start before the end of the year.

The "largest" label needs context. At 25 MW the plant is large for Spain but small beside the multi-gigawatt projects that have been proposed worldwide. Its significance is that it exists, with refinery demand and a power contract behind it. That is a more demanding test than an announcement. Whether it reaches full, steady output is not yet confirmed.

What to watch

Two data points will settle most of this. The AHDB and the British Growers Association should publish crop and price figures as Christmas approaches, and those will show whether the sprout shortfall is real and how large it is. For Castellón, the question is whether the companies confirm first hydrogen production before the year ends, and at what output.
