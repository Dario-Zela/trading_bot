DWP takes almost all online pensions and benefits systems offline after 'serious cyber incident'

In one sentence
The DWP pulled universal credit, pension credit and other portals offline after a 'serious cyber incident'; payments continue, but claims that no data was compromised rest on an unfinished inquiry.

The Department for Work and Pensions has taken almost all of its public-facing online services offline after what Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden called a "serious cyber incident". The affected services include the universal credit and pension credit portals and the Tell Us Once service. Unusual activity was detected on Thursday 26 November, and the department shut systems down as a precaution. Regular benefit and pension payments are continuing. The question is how much weight the government's early assurances can bear while the investigation is still open.

What has been said

The public account is thin. McFadden told reporters: "It's a serious cyber incident. I can't say more at this stage about what caused it." He said that taking the systems down protected the public and their personal information, and that keeping payments flowing was the priority. GCHQ's National Cyber Security Centre and other government experts are investigating a suspected hack.

A Downing Street spokeswoman said the inquiry was continuing and that there was no other evidence of a malicious actor targeting other government departments. She did not rule out that the DWP outage was a hack, and said services were being restored as quickly as possible. The Information Commissioner's Office, which enforces data protection law, said it was engaging with the DWP to establish what had happened.

The assurance that no personal records were compromised has been reported alongside these statements. The on-record wording that could be checked is narrower. It says there is no evidence of wider targeting of government, and that the shutdown was intended to protect personal information. Neither is the same as a finding that claimant data was untouched.

How far the reassurances stretch

There are two separate assurances, and they carry different weight.

The first is that payments continue. This is the easier claim to test, because a missed payment would show up quickly. Payment runs are typically scheduled in advance and handled by systems that sit behind the claimant-facing portals. A stoppage at the front door does not necessarily mean the cash machinery has stopped. Even so, a payment run covers only part of what claimants rely on the portals for. Universal credit claimants use their online journals to report changes in circumstances, upload evidence and message work coaches. Pension credit applicants and people notifying a death through Tell Us Once also depend on the digital routes. A claimant can be paid on schedule and still be unable to report a new job or a change of rent.

The second assurance, that no personal records were compromised, is harder to rely on. It is a statement about the absence of evidence, and early in an incident that absence is weak. Forensic work to establish what an intruder reached, and whether anything was copied out, usually takes far longer than the first few days. The wording matters too. "No evidence" is a statement about what has been found so far, not a statement that nothing happened. McFadden's own refusal to say what caused the incident suggests the department does not yet have a settled account.

So does the decision to pull nearly everything offline. Organisations tend to cut services that widely when they cannot quickly establish how far an intruder got, and the department has not said why it needed to. That is a defensible precaution. It also sits uneasily with an assurance that nothing sensitive was reached.

A pattern of disruption

The incident arrives after a run of corporate attacks. Marks & Spencer's online sales were halted for about six weeks last year, and a cyber attack on Jaguar Land Rover stopped production at its UK factories for five weeks, at an estimated cost of almost £2bn. This month UK meat suppliers reported their refrigeration overheating overnight after hacks targeting software used by European supermarkets. A third-party payment system used for Gatwick Airport drop-off charges may have exposed some customers' information. Next and Asos have each reported unauthorised access to some data.

Dame Chi Onwurah, who chairs the Commons Science and Technology Committee, said: "Today's disruption at DWP should be a wake-up call." Her committee, she said, had long warned about weaknesses in government digital infrastructure. Suzy Pallett, president of the Black Hat cybersecurity conference, said the question for organisations was no longer whether they would face a cyber incident but when.

A department running benefits for millions of people differs from a retailer in one respect. When a shop's website goes down, customers go elsewhere. Claimants have no alternative provider, and the state has to restore the service itself or fall back on telephone and paper routes that were scaled down as the digital ones grew.

What to watch

Three things will show how reliable the early assurances were. The first is the restoration timetable. A quick return of the portals would suggest the department had contained the problem, and a prolonged outage would suggest otherwise. The second is whether the DWP or the NCSC names the cause. McFadden has so far declined to, and No 10 has not ruled out a hack. The third is any statement from the Information Commissioner's Office, or a formal notification of a data breach. If personal data was taken, the department would be expected to tell affected claimants. Until then, the statement that no personal records were compromised remains a claim about what has not yet been found.
