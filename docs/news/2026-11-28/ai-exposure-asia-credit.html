AI exposure under the microscope: Asia's Nvidia bet and HSBC's discounted Stargate loan sale

In one sentence
Asian markets and suppliers lean heavily on Nvidia's order flow, and a reported 15% discount on HSBC's Stargate loan sale hints that lenders are starting to price AI-infrastructure risk.

Much of Asia's technology industry now depends on one American chip designer. Nvidia does not make its own processors. It relies on contract manufacturers, memory makers and server assemblers, many of them in Taiwan, South Korea and Japan. When Nvidia's order book swells, their revenues and share prices rise with it. A sustained fall in Nvidia's fortunes would travel back along the same route.

The exposure is real, but the figures are harder to pin down than the narrative suggests. The BBC Business analysis that prompted this piece describes Asian markets and suppliers as heavily exposed to Nvidia. The detailed numbers behind that claim, such as index weights and supplier revenue shares, could not be independently checked here, so none are quoted below. What follows sets out how the exposure works and where a shock would show first.

The channels of exposure

There are three, and they are worth separating because they behave differently under stress.

The first is equity-index weight. Regional benchmarks in Taiwan and South Korea are concentrated in a few hardware names. If those names are priced on AI demand, an index fund in either market is an indirect bet on Nvidia's order flow, whatever its mandate says.

The second is revenue concentration at the supplier level. Foundries, memory makers and board and server assemblers sell into a market where one customer sets the volume. A company with a large share of sales tied to Nvidia's products has little room to redirect capacity quickly if that demand softens. Advanced packaging and high-bandwidth memory are specialised, and they cannot be pointed at other customers overnight.

The third is trade. Chips, modules and servers flow from Asian factories into American and global data centres. Export figures for the region therefore carry an AI-capex component, which makes them sensitive to decisions made in a handful of corporate boardrooms in the US.

A sell-off in Nvidia would reach these channels in a predictable order. Regional equities would react first, often on the same trading day as the US move. Supplier guidance would follow at the next earnings round. Export and capital-spending data would show the damage last, with a lag of a quarter or more.

The credit signal

A second thread in the same story concerns who is paying for the hardware. Per TIKR, HSBC sold loans linked to Stargate at about a 15% discount to face value. Stargate is the AI data-centre venture announced in January 2025 by OpenAI, Oracle and SoftBank, with Oracle as a central cloud and construction partner. That role should be read as of the announcement, and it has not been re-confirmed for this piece.

The TIKR account is the only source for the sale. The size, date, buyer and counterparties are not given, and no HSBC statement or filing has been located. The discount should be treated as reported, not established.

If the report is accurate, the logic is simple. A loan sold at 85 cents on the dollar is a loan the seller preferred to hold as cash. That could reflect balance-sheet housekeeping and concentration limits as easily as doubt about the borrower. Banks routinely offload exposure to keep within internal ceilings, and a discount can also reflect a thin secondary market for large project loans. A single trade is not a verdict on the sector. Several such trades, at similar prices, would be.

The link to Asia is indirect but material. Data-centre construction financed by debt becomes orders for chips, memory and servers. If lenders charge more, or lend less, for that construction, the orders that supply Nvidia, and through it the Asian suppliers, become less certain. Equity markets have priced AI spending as a given. The loan market is a place where it is priced as a risk.

What would test the thesis

The two threads meet at the cost of capital. Asian suppliers are exposed to Nvidia's demand, and Nvidia's demand depends on customers continuing to build, which in turn depends on financing staying available at tolerable prices. A 15% haircut on one bank's loan sale does not break that chain. A pattern of them could strain it.

Several data points would show which way this is moving. Oracle's borrowing costs on Stargate-linked projects are the cleanest read-through, since a widening spread there would confirm that lenders want more compensation. Nvidia's next quarterly results will show whether order growth is holding, and its guidance matters more than its trailing numbers. In Asia, the next monthly export prints from Taiwan and South Korea will indicate whether shipments of AI-related goods are still climbing. Supplier earnings calls will show how much of the order book is tied to Nvidia and how much slack the suppliers have.

On the evidence available, the exposure is concentrated and the funding signal is ambiguous. The BBC's central claim, that Asia's markets and suppliers are heavily exposed, is plausible on the structure of the supply chain. Putting numbers on it will need the underlying data, and the HSBC sale needs confirmation from a primary source before it is read as a trend.
