Bailey says banks are likely to pass the £1.5bn tax rise to customers, as the BoE also eases capital rules

In one sentence
Bank of England governor Andrew Bailey says banks will likely pass the £1.5bn bank tax rise to customers, as the Bank also loosens capital rules for lenders.

The governor of the Bank of England has said that banks are likely to recover the cost of the government's higher bank tax from their customers. His remarks, reported by the Guardian, put the central bank's view of pricing and pass-through into a fiscal argument that has so far been conducted between the Treasury and the lenders. They land in the same weeks as a separate loosening of the Bank's own capital rules.

The Guardian report is the only source for Andrew Bailey's remarks, and the full text was not available at the time of writing. The quotation and its context therefore need checking against the original. The tax measure itself is documented elsewhere.

The tax

The Budget, delivered on 28 October, raises the banking surcharge from 3% to 6% for 15 months, starting in January. The surcharge applies to a bank's chargeable profits above £100m. The Office for Budget Responsibility expects the higher rate to raise about £1.5bn. The government has presented the rise as temporary. City commentary has been sceptical that a measure billed as time-limited will stay that way.

The industry's objections have been consistent. Lloyds' finance chief said the bank hoped the rise would be limited. The chief executive of UK Finance called it "a major disincentive to investment". One estimate puts the combined hit to Lloyds, NatWest, Barclays and HSBC at almost £900m in 2027, a little under 60% of the total take. That estimate comes from a single source and has not been independently checked.

A bank profits tax is not charged on customers, so any pass-through has to be indirect. Economists quoted in coverage of the Budget say it would most likely appear as lower savings rates, higher loan pricing, or changes to account fees and lending offers. Bailey's comment is notable because it comes from the official who sets the policy rate and regulates the same banks. The governor is not describing a risk to banks. He is describing a likely outcome for their customers.

Why the pricing matters

Pass-through is not a simple switch. Banks set loan and deposit prices in competitive markets, and a lender that widens its margin to absorb a tax risks losing business to a rival that does not. The surcharge also falls on profits above a threshold, so its incidence varies by lender. Large banks with big retail books have more room to adjust pricing than smaller ones.

For the FTSE banks, the question is whether a £1.5bn levy comes out of earnings or out of the customer. If it is passed on in full, shareholders are roughly unaffected, and borrowers and savers carry the cost. If competition limits repricing, the hit goes to net interest margins and to the profit available for distribution. The governor's remark reads as a bet on the first outcome. The banks' own warnings about investment lean toward the second.

There is a second reading, for monetary policy. A tax-driven rise in lending rates, or a squeeze on deposit rates, is a cost-push effect of a kind the Bank has to weigh when it assesses inflation. Bailey's comment suggests the Bank has already begun to model it.

The capital offset

The Bank's capital policy is moving the other way. In December 2025 it cut its benchmark for Tier 1 capital from 14% to 13%, the first significant loosening since the 2008 crisis. It also began reviews of how usable the capital buffers are and of the leverage ratio. In July 2026 it published further proposed reforms intended to simplify the framework. It has said a further update on capital linked to domestic exposures will come in its fourth-quarter Financial Stability Report. Triage material citing Zacks describes a further easing now under way. No Bank of England document confirming it was located, and its details are not verified here.

The latest stress test supports the case for easing. All seven institutions tested passed. After management actions, Barclays' common equity tier 1 ratio fell to 9.3% against a 7.2% minimum. HSBC's fell to 11.9% against 5.8%, Lloyds' to 10.9% against 5.9% and NatWest' s to 11.1% against 6.0%.

The benefits are uneven. Coverage points to domestic lenders such as Lloyds and NatWest gaining most. A Risk.net analysis of the leverage overhaul found that HSBC and Standard Chartered would be the only banks facing higher requirements.

The two policies work against each other in an awkward way. Lower capital requirements free up money that could fund lending or buybacks and dividends. The higher surcharge takes some of it back. For the banks the net position depends on how much the surcharge is passed on and on how much capital they release and where they put it. None of these numbers has been published.

What to watch

The first test is the January start of the higher surcharge, when banks' savings and mortgage pricing will show whether the governor's prediction holds. The next Bank of England Financial Stability Report, due in the fourth quarter, should show whether the capital easing goes further. Full-year results from the big four in early 2027 will show how far the cost was absorbed in earnings and how far it was passed on to customers.
