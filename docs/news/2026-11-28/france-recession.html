France slips into recession as exports sag

In one sentence
French GDP fell 0.1% in Q3 on a 1.7% export drop, but with Q2 flat it is not yet a technical recession, leaving the ECB weighing weak growth against 3.4% inflation.

France's economy shrank in the third quarter, and exports were the main cause. The data fall short of a recession by the usual textbook definition, and that gap matters for how much weight they put on the European Central Bank.

The national statistics office, INSEE, put GDP down 0.1% on the quarter in its 30 October flash estimate. Forecasters had expected growth of about 0.1%. The triage brief for this piece, citing the Wall Street Journal, described a second straight quarterly contraction. The published figures do not support that description. Output was flat in the second quarter, at 0.0% after INSEE's August revision from an initial +0.2%. The only other negative quarter this year was the first, at -0.2%. The WSJ article could not be retrieved, so its wording is unconfirmed. The official series shows a contraction, a stall and an earlier contraction. It does not show two consecutive declines.

What the numbers show

Trade did the damage. Exports fell 1.7% in the quarter, reversing a gain of roughly 3% in the second quarter that was led by aeronautics. Net trade took 0.6 percentage points off growth. Business investment fell 0.6%, after a 0.5% fall in the second quarter.

Some components held up. Household consumption rose 0.4%, up from 0.2%. Inventories added 0.4 points, and domestic demand added 0.1. Without that restocking, the headline would have been weaker. The pattern is of an economy kept afloat by households and stockbuilding while foreign sales and capital spending retreat. Export swings of this size are common in French data, because a few large aircraft deliveries can move a quarter. So one weak reading does not establish a trend.

The label "technical recession" is still some way off. It needs two consecutive quarterly declines. Because the second quarter was flat, a fourth-quarter contraction would meet the test only if INSEE does not revise the second quarter. The Bank of France expects a return to moderate growth in the fourth quarter, which would avoid a technical recession. BCA Research's Jérémie Peloso has said a technical recession is highly likely over the coming quarters. Forecasters disagree, and the next quarterly print will settle it.

Why the ECB cares

France is the euro area's second-largest economy, so its weakness feeds directly into the area-wide growth numbers the ECB's forecasters work from. Price pressure complicates the picture. French inflation rose to 3.4% in October from 3.1% in September, which keeps the ECB's attention on prices. Analysts quoted by Euronews have flagged speculation about more aggressive tightening.

That is the tension. A contracting French economy argues for caution on further rate rises, but inflation near 3.4% argues for the opposite. Commentary on the flash release, including from ING, said the data strengthen the case for caution, though price stability remains the bigger concern. No ECB statement or official quote tying policy to the French data has been found, so the link remains analysts' inference.

The fiscal backdrop

France has little room to cushion a slowdown with government spending. Finance Minister Roland Lescure has called the economy "at a standstill" and warned the country would "hit the wall" if the government's budget is not adopted. On 4 November the National Assembly rejected the amended revenue section of the budget by 458 votes to none. Bank of France Governor Emmanuel Moulin said on 3 November that the bank's 0.4% growth forecast for the year would probably need to be lowered.

What to watch

The detailed Q3 breakdown was scheduled for 27 November, and it could revise the export and stockbuilding figures. The ECB's next policy meeting and its updated staff projections will show whether it treats France as an isolated stumble or a drag on the whole area. The first estimate of fourth-quarter French GDP, due around the end of January, will show whether the economy has contracted again.
