NHS boss warns of 'horrible spike' in flu as hospital admissions surge

In one sentence
England's flu admission rate rose nearly 80% in a week, NHS England's chief warns the peak is still ahead, and vaccine uptake among at-risk groups trails the usual level.

Hospitals in England are heading into the first weeks of winter with more flu patients than they had a year ago. The head of NHS England says the worst has yet to arrive. Sir Jim Mackey told reporters that more beds are occupied by people with flu than at this point last year, and that the service is approaching "that horrible spike in flu". The warning comes with fresh surveillance data that backs it up, and with vaccination figures that explain some of the worry.

A note on sourcing: the figures below come from reports of the UKHSA's latest data, relayed through news outlets and aggregators. They have not been checked against the primary GOV.UK tables, and small differences between outlets (8.98 per 100,000 in one, "about 9.0" in others) are rounding. No source consulted gave a national count of occupied beds.

What the numbers show

The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) reported that, in the week ending 22 November, the flu hospital admission rate in England rose from 5.03 to 8.98 per 100,000 people. That is a rise of nearly 80% in seven days. Reports describe it as the highest level for this time of year since before the Covid-19 pandemic. One account puts the rate at 4.9 per 100,000 at the same point in 2025.

Flu positivity in tested samples rose from 11.5% to 17.8% over the same week. Positivity is a cruder gauge than admissions, because it depends on who is tested. Even so, both measures point the same way.

RSV, another winter respiratory virus, is circulating more quietly, with positivity of 6.9% and an admission rate of 2.34 per 100,000, concentrated in children under five. Dr Alex Allen, a UKHSA consultant epidemiologist, said Covid activity has also increased. He expects it to build over the next five to 10 days and said conditions would feel like "proper winter" within a fortnight.

There is a limit to what the data can show. A weekly rate per 100,000 is a measure of how many people are arriving at hospital, not of how full hospitals are. Mackey's comparison on occupied beds is the nearest thing to a capacity figure in the public reporting. Its size has not been published in the sources reviewed here. Whether the pressure is serious depends on how it combines with baseline occupancy, delayed discharges and staffing, none of which the headline figures capture. The Guardian reported that some NHS trusts have declared critical incidents because of demand. That suggests strain is already acute in places, though it does not show how widespread it is.

An unprotected population

The vaccination figures are the least comfortable part of the picture. As of 22 November, reports say:

  • about 34% of the nearly 9.5 million under-65s in clinical risk groups had been vaccinated, leaving almost 7 million unprotected;
  • just under 68% of adults aged 65 and over had been vaccinated, below the usual level for this time of year;
  • 37% of toddlers had been vaccinated, as had just over a third of school-aged children.

Children matter here because they are efficient spreaders of flu. Dr Suzanna McDonald, the UKHSA's flu lead, urged parents not to dismiss vaccination for children. Dr Ellen Fragaszy, a UKHSA epidemiologist, said the vaccine may not always prevent infection but can reduce its severity. That distinction is relevant to hospitals: the aim for older and at-risk patients is fewer admissions and shorter stays, rather than zero cases.

The formal response so far is mostly persuasion. UKHSA and the Department of Health and Social Care are promoting free vaccines for over-65s and for 18-to-64-year-olds with qualifying conditions. A catch-up vaccine for school-aged children is available in pharmacies until 1 December. Mackey praised local clinical teams for acting quickly to limit the spread of respiratory illness, and he criticised "corridor care". He said he was asked to wear a mask on a hospital visit for the first time in years. The sources do not describe any new funding, extra beds or changes to elective activity.

What to watch

Timing is the main uncertainty. Mackey expects admissions to keep rising over the coming days, but flu seasons are hard to call, and the sources give no confident estimate of when this one will peak. Last winter offers a warning in both directions. Flu pressure was severe in December 2025 and eased later than some had feared, which shows how much the shape of a season can change after the early weeks.

Three data points will say more than any single warning. The next weekly UKHSA surveillance report will show whether the 80% weekly jump continues or levels off. The 1 December end of the school-age catch-up window will show whether uptake among children improves. NHS England's weekly winter situation reports will show whether Mackey's comparison on occupied beds turns into sustained pressure, and whether trusts start cancelling planned operations to make room.

The pattern is familiar. Flu arrives, vaccine uptake lags, and hospitals that start winter nearly full are asked to absorb the surge. What is different this year is the timing. The spike is arriving before December, with vaccination in the groups most likely to be admitted still short of where it normally stands.
