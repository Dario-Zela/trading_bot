US court lifts Trump administration's freeze on offshore wind projects

Offshore wind turbines at the Kentish Flats wind farm in the Thames Estuary — the kind of infrastructure now caught between court rulings and federal buyback offers in the US. — Phil Hollman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
A federal appeals court finalized the death of Trump's offshore wind permitting freeze, but the administration is now buying out projects instead — nearly $4bn and counting.

A federal appeals court has formally closed the book on one of the Trump administration's earliest attempts to choke off offshore wind development, dismissing its appeal against a ruling that struck down a nationwide freeze on wind permitting. The result, now final, is that federal agencies must keep processing the approvals offshore wind developers need to build — the opposite of what the White House tried to order eighteen months ago.

The freeze dates to January 20, 2025, when President Trump signed a memorandum halting federal approvals for wind projects, onshore and offshore alike, pending a review of leasing and permitting practices. A coalition of eighteen state attorneys general, led by Massachusetts' Andrea Joy Campbell, sued that May, arguing the indefinite pause exceeded the administration's authority and put billions of dollars in committed investment at risk. In December 2025, a judge on the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts agreed, vacating the freeze as arbitrary, capricious and contrary to law. The administration appealed to the First Circuit in February — then quietly walked away from that appeal, which the court dismissed in mid-June.

A losing streak in court

The permitting-freeze case was only the broadest of the administration's defeats. Separately, the Interior Department issued stop-work orders in December 2025 on five offshore projects under construction — Revolution Wind, Vineyard Wind 1, Empire Wind, Coastal Virginia Offshore Wind and Ørsted's Sunrise Wind — citing undisclosed national-security concerns. Judges in Washington were not persuaded. Royce Lamberth called the Revolution Wind order "the height of arbitrary and capricious action" in a January 12 ruling, and by early February all five projects had preliminary injunctions allowing work to resume. The government did not appeal any of them before the deadline lapsed.

The pivot: buying projects out instead

Having lost the legal fight to block permits outright, the administration switched tactics: paying developers to abandon their federal leases voluntarily. On Wednesday, RWE agreed to relinquish leases off New York, California and Louisiana — roughly 7 gigawatts of potential capacity, enough to power more than five million homes — for $1.22 billion. RWE said it saw "no path forward to permit the projects for the foreseeable future" and is redirecting the money toward a Louisiana gas-export project and gas turbines elsewhere. That deal pushes total federal spending on offshore wind lease buybacks to nearly $4 billion, following earlier settlements with TotalEnergies, Invenergy, and the developers behind Golden State Wind and Bluepoint Wind.

Interior Secretary Doug Burgum framed the buybacks as a return to "common sense" energy policy. Senator Sheldon Whitehouse called it something closer to bribery — taxpayer money used to kill projects the administration couldn't stop by decree. Both readings point to the same underlying fact: courts have consistently found the administration's regulatory shortcuts unlawful, forcing it to achieve the same end through negotiated, and costlier, means. A parallel case shows the pattern holding — on Thursday, a federal judge in Oregon ordered the Pentagon to resume stalled reviews of onshore wind farms, finding it had blown past statutory deadlines under a separate freeze imposed in the name of radar interference.

What's next

With the appeals track exhausted, the fight over offshore wind's future now runs through Interior's checkbook rather than the courts — and through state capitals, where officials who won this case are already signaling more litigation if new lease buyouts or permitting delays surface. The next marker to watch is whether other developers follow RWE's lead and cash out rather than wait on permits the government has shown little appetite to issue.
