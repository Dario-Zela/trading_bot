Trump imposes 15% tariff on polysilicon to counter China's chip and solar dominance

Polycrystalline silicon, the high-purity raw material used in both semiconductors and solar cells. — Hi-Res Images of Chemical Elements / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Trump's 15% tariff and price floors on polysilicon target China's 96% grip on the chip and solar feedstock market, but risk raising costs for U.S. solar and chip makers.

President Trump signed an executive order on Wednesday imposing a 15% tariff on imported polysilicon, along with a set of minimum import prices covering the material all the way through to finished solar panels. The target is a chokepoint few outside the industry think about: a grey, unglamorous chemical that happens to sit at the start of the supply chain for both semiconductors and solar cells, and one that China has come to produce almost all of.

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, who stood beside Trump for the signing, put the logic plainly: "We're setting prices so that the Chinese can't dump any more, and we're setting tariffs to say build it here." Trump, for his part, predicted the U.S. would "have a big percentage of the chip business by time I leave office."

The mechanics

The order, following a Section 232 national-security investigation the Commerce Department opened in July 2025, layers a flat 15% duty on top of minimum import prices set at $21 per kilogram for raw polysilicon, $100 per kilogram for ingots and wafers, 22 cents per watt for solar cells and 38 cents per watt for finished modules. Importers who invest in U.S. wafer or cell capacity can offset part of the cost — a carrot alongside the stick.

The timing is deliberately staggered: the measures don't bite until December 4, 2026, 120 days out, which pushes the pain past both the November midterms and a planned September summit between Trump and Xi Jinping. Customs and Border Protection has been given authority to restrict imports in the interim if importers try to stockpile ahead of the deadline.

A market China already won once

The scale of the concentration is the whole rationale. China accounts for roughly 96% of global polysilicon production, according to the administration's figures, with Bernreuter Research putting 2024 output closer to 93.5% and noting nine of the world's ten largest producers are Chinese. Four companies alone — Tongwei, GCL Technology, Daqo New Energy and Xinte Energy — control 65% of global supply.

Washington has tried to answer this before, with mixed results. The domestic "big three" of polysilicon — Hemlock Semiconductor in Michigan, Wacker Chemie in Tennessee and REC Silicon in Washington state — saw their combined merchant market shrink from roughly $1 billion in 2011 to $107 million in 2018 after Beijing hit U.S. polysilicon with retaliatory duties. REC Silicon has since exited the merchant business entirely. Only Hemlock, partly owned by Corning, and the German-owned Wacker plant remain as meaningful U.S. producers — which is precisely why Corning shares jumped 9.4% on the news, with First Solar, T1 Energy and Canadian Solar rallying alongside it.

Protection with a price tag

The bind is the familiar one in trade policy: shielding a domestic industry from cheap imports also raises input costs for the industries downstream of it. Jon Toomey, president of the Coalition for a Prosperous America, called the order "the most significant global trade protection action for the American polysilicon and solar industry in the modern era." But Martin Pochtaruk, chief executive of solar panel maker Heliene, warned that if the price floors land too high, some solar projects will simply be cancelled, and the industry risks becoming "collateral damage" in an investigation framed around chip security.

That framing is itself a little strained — semiconductors consume only about 2.4% of global polysilicon output, according to the Semiconductor Industry Association, with the solar sector absorbing nearly all the rest. U.S. panel assemblers currently have capacity for close to 70 gigawatts a year against roughly 50 gigawatts of domestic demand, but American-assembled panels run about 31 cents a watt versus 14 cents for panels assembled in India — the gap the new price floors are designed to close. Whether that produces a durable U.S. polysilicon industry, or just more expensive solar power and pricier chip inputs, depends on how many of the offsetting wafer-and-cell investments actually materialize before December.

The next marker is the effective date itself: December 4, 2026, by which point the September Trump-Xi summit will have come and gone and the market will have a clearer read on how much of this holds. Watch Hemlock and Wacker for capacity announcements between now and then — the size of their expansion plans will say more about whether this tariff builds an industry or just a price floor.
