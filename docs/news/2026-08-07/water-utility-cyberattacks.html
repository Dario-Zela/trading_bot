Cyberattacks hit water utilities in seven US states, Iran suspected

A U.S. water treatment facility of the kind targeted in the campaign — operators lost control of equipment, but treated water remained safe to drink. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Hackers breached water-system controllers in 30+ towns across seven states since late July — Iran is suspected, supplies stayed safe, funding fights loom.

Since late July, someone has been breaking into the computers that run American water systems — not to steal anything, but to see what they could switch off.

Across at least seven states, hackers gained remote access to programmable logic controllers, the small industrial computers that open valves, run pumps and regulate chemical dosing at drinking-water and wastewater plants. More than 30 municipal systems in Minnesota were hit, including the utility serving Plymouth, a suburb of roughly 80,000 people outside Minneapolis. Michigan reported intrusions at nine more. By the time the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) issued a nationwide advisory on July 30, some reports of new incidents were arriving from as far afield as South Dakota and Georgia. No utility has reported water that was unsafe to drink — operators lost visibility and control of equipment, not the ability to treat water safely — but several were reduced to running plants by hand while engineers rebuilt locked-out systems from scratch.

How you break into a water plant

The method was not sophisticated, which is part of what alarmed federal investigators. CISA said it was tracking "a significant increase in cyber threat actors targeting programmable logic controllers" made by Rockwell Automation, Siemens and Schneider Electric — three of the biggest names in industrial automation — plus the cellular modems many utilities use to check on remote pump stations, a blind spot that rarely shows up in routine security scans. Former FBI agent Michael McAndrews described the technique bluntly: "The attackers will scan the internet, and they'll look for the signature of known devices. When they find one, they simply try the default password." Once inside, the intruders changed passwords and reassigned IP addresses to lock operators out, in some cases triggering pressure loss and localized flooding before staff regained control.

There is a precedent, and it points toward Tehran. In November 2023, an Iranian Revolutionary Guard-linked group calling itself CyberAv3ngers hijacked an Israeli-made Unitronics controller at a water authority in Aliquippa, Pennsylvania, defacing its display with an anti-Israel message. The Treasury Department later sanctioned six Iranian officials over that intrusion. This summer's campaign is quieter — no defacements, no ransom demands, which several analysts read as a sign the goal was reconnaissance or pre-positioning rather than extortion — but U.S. intelligence agencies have told NBC News and the Washington Post they suspect the same state actor is behind it.

Iran, or "gross incompetence"?

The FBI has not formally attributed the campaign, and the White House has muddied the politics further. At a Cabinet meeting, President Trump dismissed the Iran theory and instead blamed Minnesota's "gross incompetence," a swipe widely read as aimed at Gov. Tim Walz. Walz fired back on social media:

"Trump knows exactly who is responsible for this attack, and knows that other states were hit too. This is what modern warfare looks like, and it further illustrates there's no plan to win a war with Iran."

The exchange lands amid an unresolved standoff over Iran's nuclear program and shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, and it underscores an awkward reality: attributing a cyber intrusion with certainty takes weeks, but the political reaction takes hours.

The bill nobody has agreed to pay

What makes this episode more than a security footnote is timing. A Government Accountability Office report published in May — two months before the attacks began — warned that the EPA lacks the legal authority to require cybersecurity risk assessments across most of the country's roughly 170,000 drinking-water and wastewater systems, leaving compliance largely voluntary. Congress has since been sitting on two levers utilities say they need: the State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program, which funds smaller utilities' defenses and is authorized only through September 30, and CISA's authority under the 2015 Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act, which lets the agency and private companies swap threat data and which expires the same month. A coalition of operational-technology security groups, backed by CISA and the FBI, is now pushing Congress to reauthorize both before the deadline rather than let a funding lapse coincide with an active foreign intrusion campaign.

Separately, new rules under the Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act will soon require utilities to report major breaches to CISA within 72 hours and ransom payments within 24 — a mandate this episode will likely be cited to justify, given how long some utilities took to realize their controllers had been compromised. For the industrial-automation vendors named in the CISA advisory — Rockwell, Siemens, Schneider Electric — the episode cuts two ways: their equipment is the attack surface, but retrofitting tens of thousands of small utilities with segmented networks and modern authentication is exactly the kind of spending cycle that follows a scare like this one. The global market for critical-infrastructure cybersecurity was already estimated near $56 billion in 2025 and growing steadily; a coordinated, attributed nation-state campaign against a sector that mostly still runs on default passwords tends to pull that growth curve forward rather than change its direction.

The FBI's attribution finding, whenever it lands, will settle the Trump-Walz argument but not the underlying problem. The nearer deadline is September 30, when the grant program and the information-sharing law both lapse unless Congress acts — a bureaucratic date that, this year, doubles as a test of whether Washington treats a foreign hand on the tap as an emergency or just another line item.
