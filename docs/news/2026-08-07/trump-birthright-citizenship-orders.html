Trump signs narrower executive orders targeting birthright citizenship and 'birth tourism'

The U.S. Supreme Court building, whose June 2026 ruling in Trump v. Barbara struck down the administration's original birthright citizenship order. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump signed narrower orders denying birthright citizenship to diplomats' children and targeting birth-tourism schemes, sidestepping the Supreme Court ruling that killed his broader 2025 order — but litigation is already forming.

President Trump signed two executive orders on Thursday narrowing who qualifies for automatic American citizenship at birth — a far more modest attempt than the sweeping order the Supreme Court struck down five weeks earlier, and one built specifically to avoid the same fate.

The first order, titled "Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship," denies birthright citizenship to children born in the United States to parents who are designated foreign terrorists or "alien enemies," to foreign government employees covered by diplomatic immunity, and to parents involved in commercial or fraudulent birth-tourism schemes. The second, "Ending Birth Tourism," directs the State Department and Homeland Security to deny visas, block entry and remove foreign nationals found to be travelling to the US for the specific purpose of giving birth. Federal agencies have 30 days to issue implementing guidance, and are barred in the meantime from accepting state-issued documents to certify citizenship for children who fall into the excluded categories.

"They're buying their way in, and we're not going to let it happen," Trump said at the signing, calling the current system "a disgrace." Deputy chief of staff Stephen Miller, standing beside him, described the targets as "alien enemies of the United States, members of foreign terrorist organizations and large categories of people who lobby and act on behalf of foreign governments," and declared birth tourism "hereby banned."

A retreat dressed as an advance

The contrast with Trump's first attempt is deliberate. The executive order he signed on his first day back in office, in January 2025, would have denied citizenship to any child born in the US to a parent who was undocumented or present only temporarily — sweeping in the American-born children of visa holders, students and guest workers alongside those of unauthorised immigrants. In a 6–3 ruling on June 30, the Supreme Court struck it down, with Chief Justice John Roberts writing that children born to parents "unlawfully or temporarily present" are nonetheless "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States and are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. Roberts's opinion did carve out the categories courts have long recognised as exceptions: children of accredited diplomats, of hostile occupying forces, and those born aboard foreign sovereign vessels or in a handful of outlying territories.

Thursday's orders are built almost entirely inside those carve-outs. Unlike the 2025 order, they do not touch the children of H-1B holders, international students or undocumented immigrants generally — the categories that drove most of the anxiety, and most of the litigation, the first time around. Instead the administration is wagering that a much narrower order, hewing closely to exceptions the Court itself just endorsed, can survive where the broad version could not.

The legal fight resets, not ends

Civil rights groups are not conceding the point. Cody Wofsy, deputy director of the ACLU's Immigrants' Rights Project, said any renewed attempt to "rewrite" birthright citizenship "will meet the same fate as the last one," and lawyers involved in Trump v. Barbara — the case that produced June's ruling — are preparing new filings. The dispute this time is narrower but not trivial: whether "large categories of people who lobby and act on behalf of foreign governments," as Miller put it, can be read into an exception the Court intended for accredited diplomats with formal immunity, and whether denying entry to pregnant travellers on suspicion of birth-tourism intent — rather than after any fraud is established — survives due-process scrutiny. Immigration attorneys note the order's own list of exclusions is labelled "non-exhaustive," leaving room for the administration to expand it through agency guidance rather than further executive orders, which could itself become a point of legal attack.

An industry braces for ambiguity, again

Birth tourism itself is a small and specific business — clusters of consultancies in Los Angeles and Miami and Chinese-, Russian- and Taiwanese-run "maternity hotels" charging $20,000 to $85,000 for packages that include a US birth certificate, according to immigration researchers who have tracked the practice for a decade. It is not the scale of that industry that concerns immigration lawyers and employers now, but the compliance fog the order creates around it. Consular officers will face pressure to question any visa applicant of childbearing age about pregnancy status and travel intent, a practice immigration firms warn is difficult to apply consistently and easy to challenge as discriminatory. Employers sponsoring foreign workers, universities enrolling international students, and hospitals accustomed to treating visiting patients are all bracing for slower, more unpredictable visa processing — even though none of those groups is a direct target of the new order.

That is the pattern immigration-dependent sectors have now lived through twice in seven months: an order, an injunction fight, a Supreme Court reckoning, and a redrafted order that starts the clock again. Each round adds friction — to consular wait times, to relocation planning, to the confidence with which a hospital, a university or a tech employer can tell a foreign hire what their child's legal status will be. None of it depends on the order ultimately being upheld; the uncertainty itself is the cost, and it resets every time Washington tries again.

What's next

Civil rights groups are expected to file suit within days, most likely in the same federal courts that heard the original Barbara litigation, seeking a preliminary injunction before the 30-day agency guidance deadline lapses in early September. Whether a judge treats this order as meaningfully distinct from its predecessor — narrow enough to fit inside the Fourteenth Amendment exceptions the Supreme Court just reaffirmed — will be the first test of how much room, if any, that June ruling actually left the administration to manoeuvre.
