Starmer made misleading claim on defence spending, watchdog finds

Former prime minister Keir Starmer, whose £270 billion defence spending claim was ruled misleading by the UK Statistics Authority. — Chris McAndrew / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The UK statistics watchdog ruled Keir Starmer's £270bn defence spending claim was "incorrect and misleading," a verdict landing weeks after he left office.

The claim sounded generous even by the standards of election-year defence pledges: Britain, the prime minister told MPs in March, would spend "£270 billion more on defence than we would otherwise have done" over the course of this Parliament. It was repeated by government accounts, including the Wales Office, which posted on social media that ministers had "delivered a £270 billion increase in defence spending." Neither statement was true, the UK's statistics watchdog has now ruled — and the ruling lands on a prime minister who has already left office.

In a letter dated 5 August, Penny Young, interim chair of the UK Statistics Authority, told shadow defence secretary James Cartlidge that the £270 billion figure was "incorrect and misleading." The number, an Office for Statistics Regulation investigation found, was not an increase at all — it was the total Ministry of Defence budget projected across the whole 2025/26–2028/29 period, worth £272.2 billion. Presenting the entire departmental spending envelope as new money, the watchdog concluded, overstated what had actually changed. Full Fact's own analysis of the underlying budget lines found the real year-on-year increases were far smaller: £1.9 billion between 2024/25 and 2025/26, and a projected £3.3 billion the year after — a fraction of the headline sum. The government's actual commitment has been to raise defence spending to 2.5% of GDP by April 2027, rising to 2.6% under NATO's broader accounting.

Cartlidge, who filed the original complaint, said the finding exposed "Labour's lies" on defence funding. The Wales Office has since corrected its post to describe the £270 billion as investment "over this Parliament" rather than an increase, and the Ministry of Defence said its statisticians were now working with communications staff to ensure "precise and consistent language" in future.

A verdict that outlives the prime minister who earned it

What makes the timing awkward is that the ruling arrives for a government that has already changed hands. Keir Starmer resigned as prime minister on 20 July, succeeded by Andy Burnham, and the £270 billion claim now reads as a parting mark against a premiership that is over. But the institutional thread runs straight through to the present cabinet: John Healey, defence secretary when the disputed figure was first defended in Parliament, is now Burnham's chancellor, tasked with closing a defence budget gap estimated near £5 billion ahead of the autumn Budget.

That continuity matters more to bond investors than the rebuke itself. Thirty-year gilt yields have already climbed to their highest since 1998 as markets weigh Burnham's appetite for fiscal flexibility, and a watchdog finding that a predecessor inflated defence numbers by nearly £267 billion is unlikely to reassure anyone doing sums on the public finances. The story is not that Starmer misspoke — politicians routinely round generously in the chamber. It is that the same spending categories, the same funding gap and largely the same personnel are now back in front of the OBR, whose autumn verdict on the Budget will be the next real test of whether Burnham's government can describe its own numbers more carefully than its predecessor did.
