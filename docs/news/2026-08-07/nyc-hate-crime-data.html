NYC hate crime data show anti-Jewish incidents made up 57% of 2026 total

An NYPD vehicle in Manhattan. — André Gustavo Stumpf / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
NYPD data show anti-Jewish incidents made up 57% of NYC's confirmed hate crimes this year, though Jews are about 10% of the population.

New figures released by the New York Police Department this week show that anti-Jewish incidents accounted for 56.9% of all confirmed hate crimes in the city during the first seven months of 2026 — a share so lopsided that the group with the next-highest count, victims targeted over sexual orientation, trailed by nearly six to one.

The numbers: 205 confirmed anti-Jewish hate crimes from January through July, against 34 tied to sexual orientation and a comparatively small tail of other bias categories. Jewish New Yorkers make up roughly 10% of the city's population of 8.3 million. Confirmed hate crimes citywide rose 9.4%, from 329 incidents in the same period last year to 360 this year — meaning almost the entire year-over-year increase in hate crime is attributable to incidents targeting Jews.

The July spike

The month of July alone did much of the damage to the annual figures. Of 33 confirmed hate crimes citywide that month, 23 were classified as antisemitic — up 53% from the 15 recorded in July 2025. NYPD data show that works out to roughly one confirmed antisemitic hate crime every 25 hours across the seven-month window.

July also produced the incident that crystallized the numbers into a political flashpoint. On July 23, a 51-year-old man, Raul Morales, allegedly stabbed a 57-year-old man near Central Park West before walking several blocks to an Orthodox synagogue on West 86th Street and stabbing a 50-year-old Jewish man with a screwdriver as he stepped outside. Witnesses said the suspect shouted "Allahu Akbar" and "Justice for Islam" during the attacks. He was charged with attempted murder and assault as hate crimes and is being held without bail.

A mayor under scrutiny

The data landed a little over seven months into Mayor Zohran Mamdani's administration, and Jewish community leaders were quick to draw a line between the two. At a press conference on the Upper West Side the day after the stabbing, several elected officials and rabbis pressed the mayor to speak more forcefully against antisemitism; some accused him of tolerating rhetoric, tied to his past criticism of Israeli policy, that they say has emboldened attackers. Mamdani called the stabbings "hateful and despicable" and said afterward that antisemitism "will never be tolerated and will never be accepted" in the city, while rejecting the broader allegation that his own language contributed to the trend. City Hall has also noted, accurately, that overall violent crime — murders and shootings in particular — is running at or near historic lows for the same period.

What the numbers don't settle

NYPD hate crime statistics count only incidents the department has classified and confirmed, a process that lags initial complaints and can shift as investigations close — a caveat researchers at groups such as the Nexus Project have raised when comparing year-to-year totals. The figures also don't distinguish motive with much granularity: an attack tied to reaction against Israeli military action in Gaza and one rooted in older forms of antisemitism land in the same bucket. What the data do establish cleanly is scale and trend — a rising count, concentrated overwhelmingly on one community, in a city where confirmed hate crime overall is also climbing.

The next data point arrives when the NYPD publishes its August hate crime tally, typically in the first half of the following month, which will show whether the July spike was an aftershock of the stabbing case or the start of a steeper climb.
