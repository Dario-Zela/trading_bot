UK 30-year gilt yield hits highest since 1998 as fiscal jitters mount before Budget

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose gilt-holding runoff adds to the supply pressure facing the long end of the market. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 30-year gilt yield hit 5.7%, its highest since 1998, as investors price in the scale of tax rises needed at November's Budget.

The yield on Britain's 30-year government bond climbed to around 5.70% this week, its highest level since May 1998 — a milestone that says less about any single headline than about a slow, grinding loss of patience in the market that lends Britain money.

Gilts are UK government bonds, and the 30-year is the one insurers, pension funds and long-horizon investors use to price decades-out liabilities. When its yield rises, it costs the Treasury more to borrow long-term, and it signals that investors are demanding extra compensation to hold UK debt for the long haul. That extra compensation — economists call it the term premium — has been creeping higher for months, and this week it pushed the 30-year yield past levels last seen when Tony Blair was a year into his first term and the Bank of England had only just been handed independent control of interest rates.

The relentless rise in yields intensifies pressure on the Chancellor to show the Office for Budget Responsibility that the government can hold its fiscal rules together.

A budget still three and a half months away

Chancellor Rachel Reeves is not due to stand up with her Autumn Budget until late November, but the bond market has already started voting. Thirty-year yields have been grinding higher through the summer as traders price in the scale of the fiscal repair job ahead: a shortfall in the public finances that most City economists now put in the tens of billions of pounds, against a backdrop of anaemic growth and stubborn inflation that has kept the Bank of England cautious about cutting rates further.

That combination — a growth rate too slow to grow the tax base, and inflation too sticky to let the Bank ride to the rescue with cheap money — is precisely the bind that unsettles long-duration bond buyers. A pension fund holding a 30-year gilt is making a three-decade bet on the UK's capacity to service its debt without resorting to inflation or default. Every data point that makes that bet look shakier gets priced in immediately, because unlike a company's earnings, a government's fiscal trajectory is reassessed daily by a market that never closes.

Reeves has ruled out a repeat of last autumn's record £40 billion tax-raising Budget in scale, but has stopped short of ruling out further increases altogether, and that ambiguity is itself a cost. Investors who spent 2025 watching the UK's fiscal rules — chiefly the requirement that debt-to-GDP be falling by the end of a five-year forecast window — treated as a hard constraint are now pricing the possibility that political pressure loosens it. The lesson of the 2022 mini-budget crisis under Liz Truss, when 30-year yields spiked over a single fortnight, still sits close to the surface of every gilt trading desk: credibility, once spent, is expensive to rebuild.

Why 1998 is the wrong comparison and the right one

The last time 30-year yields traded this high, the fiscal backdrop was almost the opposite of today's. In 1998 the UK was running a shrinking budget deficit, inflation had only recently been tamed under the newly independent Bank of England, and yields were high in large part because the era of ultra-low interest rates that followed the 2008 financial crisis hadn't happened yet — 5-6% long yields were closer to the historical norm than the exception.

What makes today's level notable is the reverse: after nearly two decades in which government borrowing costs anchored below 3%, the market is now demanding compensation more typical of the pre-crisis era, but against a fiscal position that is far more stretched. Debt as a share of GDP sits close to 100%, more than double where it stood in 1998, and a growing share of that debt is index-linked, meaning debt-interest costs move directly with inflation. The Institute for Fiscal Studies and others have flagged that rising yields become both symptom and cause of fiscal strain: higher borrowing costs inflate the annual interest bill, which itself eats into the headroom Reeves needs to meet her own rules, in a loop that gets harder to break the longer yields stay elevated.

The Bank of England's own hand in the mix

The Bank has not been a bystander. Its programme of quantitative tightening — actively selling down the roughly £900 billion in gilts it accumulated during years of crisis-era stimulus — adds supply to a market already absorbing heavy fresh issuance from the Treasury's borrowing needs. The Bank slowed the pace of that runoff earlier this year, from around £100 billion a year toward £70 billion, partly in response to concerns about long-end market functioning. Most analysts still judge the fiscal picture, not the mechanics of QT, as the dominant force behind the rise in the term premium, but the two are not unrelated: a central bank shrinking its balance sheet at the same moment the Treasury needs to issue more debt leaves fewer natural buyers to absorb it, at any price.

Sterling has held up better than gilts through this repricing, trading in a narrow range rather than selling off in tandem — a pattern that traders read as the market distinguishing between a fiscal-credibility problem, which weighs on bonds, and a broader loss of confidence in UK assets generally, which would hit the currency too. That distinction matters for the Bank of England, which held rates in a split 6-3 vote this month and will be watching gilt-market stress for any sign it is starting to bleed into the wider economy through mortgage and corporate borrowing costs, both of which are priced off longer-dated gilt yields.

What the market is waiting to hear

For now, the 30-year yield is a running scoreboard of investor confidence in the November Budget before a single line of it has been written. Every speech, leak or briefing between now and then that hints at the scale of tax rises or spending restraint will move it, in either direction — a credible consolidation plan could see yields retrace some of this year's rise, much as they did after the Autumn Budget last November, while any sign that fiscal discipline is loosening risks pushing the 30-year toward levels not seen even in the late 1990s.

The next scheduled catalyst is the Bank of England's September rate decision, followed by the OBR's pre-Budget forecasts, both of which will sharpen the picture of how much fiscal headroom Reeves actually has to work with. Until then, the gilt market — not the opinion polls, not Westminster commentary — is functioning as the clearest real-time verdict on whether investors believe the sums will add up.
