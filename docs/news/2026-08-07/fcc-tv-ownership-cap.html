FCC scraps national cap on local TV station ownership

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr led the 2-1 vote to eliminate the national TV station ownership cap. — Federal Communications Commission (public domain)
In one sentence
The FCC voted 2-1 to scrap its 39% cap on national TV station ownership, clearing the way for broadcasters like Nexstar and Gray Media to consolidate further.

The Federal Communications Commission voted on Wednesday to scrap the rule capping how much of the country a single television broadcaster can own, ending a two-decade-old ceiling that had increasingly looked like a formality anyway.

The 2-1 vote, split along party lines, eliminates the so-called national ownership cap, which barred any company from owning local stations that collectively reach more than 39% of U.S. television households. In its place, the commission says it will judge station acquisitions case by case — a shift that hands Chairman Brendan Carr and his successors far more discretion over who gets to own how much of American local television.

"The cap prevents local broadcasters from competing on a level playing field."

That was Carr's rationale, echoing the industry's own argument for years: that a rule written for an era of three broadcast networks makes little sense when local stations compete for eyeballs and ad dollars against Netflix, YouTube and TikTok, none of which face any ownership ceiling at all.

From 35% to zero

The cap's lineage runs back to 1990s-era limits Congress set at 35% of national household reach, lifted to 39% in 2004 after News Corp and Viacom breached the original ceiling and lobbied Capitol Hill for relief. It has stood untouched since — until now. Commissioner Anna Gomez, the panel's lone Democrat, dissented, calling the repeal "unlawful on its face" and arguing that only Congress, which wrote the 39% figure into law in 2012, has the authority to remove it. She warned the change strips away a "structural safeguard to preserve localism, viewpoint diversity, and competition," and predicted it will accelerate newsroom consolidation as station groups chase the cost savings that come with centralizing production across markets.

The timing is not incidental. Nexstar Media Group's pending $6.2 billion acquisition of Tegna — a deal that would push the combined company's reach to roughly 80% of U.S. households, nearly double the old cap — has been sitting in front of the FCC awaiting a waiver. Wednesday's vote removes the need for one.

The market's verdict

Broadcast stocks had already priced in the outcome. Nexstar shares jumped 8.8% in the run-up to the vote after Carr signaled it was coming; E.W. Scripps rose 12% and Gray Media climbed 9%, both seen as candidates to bulk up now that the ceiling is gone. Sinclair, which made an unsolicited bid for Scripps last year and has been reviewing its own broadcast portfolio, traded little changed — a reminder that not every station group is positioned the same way for a consolidation wave. Paramount, CBS's parent, and Fox are among the groups already near or past the old 39% threshold and stand to gain the most room to add stations outright rather than through side deals and shared-services arrangements that broadcasters have long used to work around the cap.

What it means for local news

The National Association of Broadcasters, which lobbied hard for the repeal, argues that scale is what keeps local newsrooms funded at all, pointing to the newspaper industry's collapse as the cautionary tale of what happens to outlets that stay small and undercapitalized. Critics counter that prior waves of station consolidation brought exactly the opposite — shared scripts, centralized "must-run" segments, and thinner local reporting staffs — and that a 39% national reach cap was one of the few remaining checks on how much of the local news diet a handful of companies could control.

Legal challenges are expected, likely centered on Gomez's argument that the commission overstepped a boundary Congress itself drew. Watch the Nexstar-Tegna deal for the first real-world test of the new regime, and watch state attorneys general, who have already sued to block that merger on separate antitrust grounds, for whether they broaden their case now that the FCC's own ceiling is gone.
