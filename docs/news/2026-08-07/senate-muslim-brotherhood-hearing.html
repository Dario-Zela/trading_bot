Senate Judiciary hearing on Muslim Brotherhood network boycotted by Democrats

Sen. Ted Cruz (R-TX), who chaired the subcommittee hearing as the only senator in attendance. — U.S. Senate Photographic Studio / Rebecca Hammel, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ted Cruz held a Senate hearing on the Muslim Brotherhood network alone after all Democratic subcommittee members boycotted it as a 'political creepshow.'

Ted Cruz walked into Room 226 of the Dirksen Senate Office Building on Wednesday, looked down the dais, and found himself alone. Every Democratic member of the Senate Judiciary Committee's subcommittee on federal courts had stayed away from his hearing, titled — without much subtlety — "Hidden in Plain Sight: Confronting the Muslim Brotherhood Network in America." No other Republicans showed either. For roughly two hours, it was Cruz, three witnesses, and a lot of empty chairs.

"The American people should ask themselves why not a single member of the minority decided that a hearing on terrorism was worth their time," Cruz said, calling the vacant seats "an embarrassment."

The boycott wasn't a last-minute snub. Five Democratic senators — Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island, Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut, Mazie Hirono of Hawaii, Alex Padilla of California and Peter Welch of Vermont — announced days beforehand that they'd skip it, calling the whole exercise a "political creepshow."

"Scapegoating Muslims may be a Texas Republican strategist's idea of 'solid gold' to whip up their voters, but Democrats have no intention of indulging this political creepshow, particularly after the recent violence targeting places of faith," the senators said in a joint statement. "Our focus is the costs families face thanks to rampant, non-stop Trump corruption."

A hearing with a name attached

What gave the standoff its edge was timing. The hearing landed a day after Abdul El-Sayed, a physician and former Detroit health director, won Michigan's Democratic Senate primary. Cruz used his opening statement to draw a line from El-Sayed to the network he says his subcommittee was investigating, noting that El-Sayed's father-in-law, Jukaku Tayeb, has held leadership roles at CAIR-Michigan and other groups Cruz's witnesses linked to Brotherhood-affiliated organizing. One of those witnesses, Kyle Shideler of the Center for Security Policy, testified at length about Tayeb's history. The other two — Lara Burns, a former FBI special agent now at George Washington University's Program on Extremism, and Arielle Klepach of the National Jewish Advocacy Center — largely stuck to the network's broader domestic footprint.

El-Sayed has dismissed the line of attack as a substitute for engaging his policy platform. Democrats, for their part, framed the whole hearing as an attempt to import that same argument into a congressional record with the imprimatur of a Senate committee behind it.

The backdrop: designations already in motion

The hearing didn't emerge from nowhere. In November 2025, President Trump signed an executive order directing the State and Treasury departments to assess Muslim Brotherhood chapters for potential terrorist designation. By January, Washington had followed through, designating the Egyptian, Lebanese and Jordanian branches as specially designated global terrorists and imposing sanctions. Cruz's hearing was pitched as building the public case for scrutinizing the network's reach inside the United States — a domestic complement to those overseas designations. Critics counter that no such domestic organization has been designated, and that the hearing conflated foreign branches with American Muslim civic groups.

What the empty seats signal

Congressional oversight hearings work, in theory, because both parties show up and contest the record — even adversarially. A hearing conducted by one senator, with witnesses chosen by that senator alone, produces a transcript but not much scrutiny. That's the Democrats' bet: better to deny the hearing legitimacy than to lend it a foil. It's also a gamble, since an empty dais lets Cruz's framing stand unchallenged in the official record, which is precisely why he made a point of narrating the absences on camera.

Whatever the merits of either side's read, the episode says more about the state of congressional oversight than about the Muslim Brotherhood itself. A subcommittee built for shared fact-finding on terrorism policy is instead functioning as a one-party stage — mirrored, not coincidentally, by a Senate floor increasingly organized around competing messaging operations rather than joint inquiry. The next test of whether that dynamic holds arrives with El-Sayed's general election campaign, where the Brotherhood allegation is likely to resurface as a live campaign issue rather than a hearing-room talking point.
