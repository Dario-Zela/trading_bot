FA withdraws support for Fifa's Infantino as Uefa boycott threat persists

Gianni Infantino, Fifa president, whose scrapped plan to sell a stake in the World Cup has triggered a governance revolt among European federations. — ©UNIS Vienna/Nikoleta Haffar, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
England's FA has pulled its backing for Infantino's re-election, but Uefa says its boycott threat over the scrapped World Cup stake sale still stands.

England's Football Association has told Gianni Infantino it no longer backs his bid for a fifth term as Fifa president — the clearest sign yet that the row over his abandoned plan to sell off pieces of the World Cup has curdled into something more personal, and more dangerous to his hold on the job.

The FA's letter, sent this week, retracts an earlier endorsement and calls for "a full and robust review of Fifa's leadership and governance to ensure that the global game is run transparently, for the benefit of all 211 member nations and with the long-term stewardship of football at its heart." It joins Wales, Sweden and Serbia, whose federations withdrew support on Monday. None of them have to make good on that threat for months yet — the next Fifa presidential election isn't until March 2027, in Rabat — but the direction of travel matters more than the date.

The plan that started it

The trigger was a proposal, unveiled in late July, to raise $4.2bn by selling roughly 20% of a new subsidiary called Fifa Forward Enterprise — the commercial engine that would have run World Cup broadcasting, sponsorship, ticketing and hospitality — at a valuation of about $20bn. The anchor investor was reported to be Thrive Eternal, a vehicle launched by Joshua Kushner, brother of Jared Kushner. For a body that markets itself as a not-for-profit steward of the world's game, selling a fifth of its crown jewel to a fund with White House family ties was always going to invite scrutiny.

It invited more than that. Uefa's 55 member associations voted to boycott Fifa competitions if the sale went ahead, calling it a case where "some things are simply too important to sell." Concacaf and the Asian confederation raised objections of their own, and inside Fifa two senior figures pushed back publicly — one resigned as a presidential adviser, another said staff had been misled about the scheme. Infantino scrapped it on 1 August, announcing the climbdown from Fifa's Zurich headquarters.

Scrapping the plan wasn't enough

What happened next is the more interesting story. Uefa didn't stand down. It said it had "lost confidence in Gianni Infantino's presidency" and would keep its boycott in place — covering the Under-20 Women's World Cup in Poland this September, the Under-17 Women's World Cup in Morocco, the men's Under-17 tournament in Qatar, the Women's Champions Cup in Miami, and, most consequentially, the Women's World Cup in Brazil in mid-2027 — unless Fifa gives binding assurances that nothing like the stake sale is attempted again.

Infantino tried to draw a line under it at a crisis meeting in Rabat on 5 August, where Fifa's management board and secretary-general Mattias Grafström reaffirmed their backing and the organisation apologised in a letter to member associations, acknowledging "mistakes" and conceding the "process should have been handled differently." It didn't land. Uefa confirmed the following day that its position "holds." Former Portugal captain Luis Figo went further in a newspaper column, calling Infantino's conduct "the lowest, most deceitful and cravenly self-interested behaviour I have ever witnessed" and writing simply: "Infantino must go."

Why the cohesion matters more than the proposal

The stake sale itself is dead. What's still live is whether Fifa's six confederations can be made to pull in the same direction, and that's the part with real money attached. The World Cup is Fifa's only genuinely global, quadrennial cash event — broadcast deals, sponsorship packages and betting markets are all priced on the assumption that the tournament happens on schedule with full participation. A Uefa boycott of lesser tournaments is an inconvenience; a Uefa boycott of the 2027 Women's World Cup, involving the confederation that supplies the bulk of the sport's commercial value, would be a different order of problem, and one that starts to look like fragmentation rather than a governance dispute.

Infantino, for now, has the votes that count inside Fifa's own structures. What he's losing is the assumption that his re-election is a formality. An emergency congress can be called if 20% of the 211 member associations request one, opening a path to a no-confidence vote — a low bar in percentage terms, a high one in practice, given how many federations still depend on Fifa's development funding. The FA's letter doesn't force that vote. It removes one more reason not to hold it.

The next marker is whether other major federations — Germany, France, Italy, Spain among them — follow England and Wales before Uefa's full membership meets again, and whether Fifa offers the written guarantees Uefa says it wants ahead of the September Under-20 Women's tournament in Poland, the first event actually at risk from the boycott.
