Bank of Japan holds rates, watching yen and Iran-war fallout

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda, who says the board will not take up the case for a rate hike until its September meeting. — Cabinet Secretariat Public Relations Office, Government of Japan (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
The BoJ held rates at 1% in an 8-1 vote while Tokyo and Washington's joint intervention pushed the yen to 155, leaving traders with currency support but no rate guidance.

The Bank of Japan left its policy rate at 1% on July 31, an outcome nobody in the market disputed, and yet the yen has moved more in the week since than it did across most of the spring. The gap between those two facts is the story: a central bank standing still while the finance ministry, with an assist from Washington, does the moving.

The vote itself was 8-1. The dissenter, board member Hajime Takata, pushed for an immediate quarter-point hike to 1.25%, arguing that external demand shocks and shifting global financial conditions had pushed Japan into "a new phase" requiring a nimbler central bank. He was outvoted, but Governor Kazuo Ueda's press conference did the real signalling: core inflation, he said, is likely to run "clearly above" the BOJ's 2% target through the second half of the fiscal year, and the board will "begin closely discussing whether to raise rates" only from the September meeting onward — leaving a six-week window in which the policy rate is frozen but the case for moving it is not.

Intervention fills the vacuum

Into that window stepped the finance ministry. Tokyo and Washington confirmed a rare joint intervention on Friday, August 1 — the first time the US has helped defend the yen in 28 years, and its first participation in any yen intervention in 15. Bank of Japan data show roughly ¥5.33 trillion spent on Friday's operation alone, on top of a reported ¥8.45 trillion the day before, itself a record for a single day. The yen firmed to 155.23 per dollar, its strongest since May, a roughly 5% swing over three sessions.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called the move a response to "disorderly yen movements" and said Washington "will not hesitate to participate in further joint intervention." President Trump framed it as a favour to an ally: "They wanted a little bit of help, and we're always there for Japan." Behind the diplomacy sits a less flattering number — the yen has been the worst-performing G10 currency over the past year, weighed down by low bond yields, doubts about Tokyo's fiscal and monetary credibility, and a terms-of-trade shock from the US-Iran conflict that has kept oil import costs elevated.

Two watch items, one message

Ueda named the same two pressures that show up in every recent BOJ communication: AI-linked capital spending and the Middle East. On the first, the bank has started treating weak returns on AI infrastructure investment as a financial-stability risk alongside a more conventional inflation one. On the second, oil has swung on Iran headlines — sliding on talk of a Monday sit-down before an Iranian cruise-missile incident near a US tanker in the Strait of Hormuz undercut the de-escalation story. Both add uncertainty the BOJ has chosen to sit out rather than pre-empt.

For traders, the practical effect is a policy corridor with an official floor set by intervention and no official signal on the rate side to match it — a combination that raises the cost of hedging without narrowing the range of outcomes. The next test lands at the September meeting, the point at which Ueda has promised the hike debate actually starts.
