Carney says aluminium tariff concessions open path to US-Canada trade deal

A Rio Tinto aluminium smelter in British Columbia — Canada supplies 44% of US aluminium imports, giving Ottawa leverage in tariff talks. — Iain Cameron / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Carney says easing US aluminium tariffs — which he blames for a 52% domestic price jump — could unlock a broader Canada-US trade deal before an August 19 deadline.

Canada's aluminium sector has become the test case for whether Ottawa and Washington can strike a broader trade deal — and Prime Minister Mark Carney is treating it as exactly that.

Speaking Thursday outside a Rio Tinto smelter in Saguenay, Quebec, Carney said progress on aluminium tariffs could "open the way" to a comprehensive trade agreement with the Trump administration, ahead of an August 19 deadline that carries the threat of fresh 50% tariffs on a wide range of Canadian goods, from dairy to hockey equipment.

The pitch is built on a number Carney has been repeating in public: the US aluminium producer price index rose 52% between June 2025 and June 2026, since Trump imposed a 50% tariff on the metal. "Most of that is pass-through of the tariff," Carney said, arguing the levy has raised costs for American manufacturers without reshoring meaningful capacity. "That's not a good situation for American companies." Canada supplied 44% of US aluminium imports last year, giving Ottawa leverage that officials are now trying to convert into a negotiating opening.

A comprehensive deal, not a carve-out

Washington has already shown some flexibility: last month Trump proposed cutting the aluminium tariff to 25% for firms that build, expand or refurbish smelting capacity inside the US. Carney's response has been to press for more, not less, framing aluminium as a template for a wider settlement rather than settling for a standalone fix. "We're not interested in a very targeted deal without connections to other sectors," he said, insisting any agreement also cover steel, autos and forest products — all currently under separate US tariffs.

The tone around the talks remains combative even as both sides describe the substance as constructive. Canadian negotiators spent 90 minutes with US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer this week, a session Ottawa's Dominic LeBlanc called "constructive and detailed." Trump, meanwhile, told a Las Vegas audience that "Canada's nasty," prompting Carney to concede the negotiation itself has been "nasty" — his word for a process he says is fundamentally about protecting Canadian jobs.

What it means for markets

For traders, the aluminium file is now a proxy for the broader US-Canada relationship. A partial rollback in tariff rates would ease input costs for North American automakers, packaging firms and can manufacturers that have absorbed higher metal prices for over a year, while a breakdown before August 19 would trigger the next round of tariffs and likely reignite volatility in aluminium futures and industrials shares on both sides of the border. Genuine progress on aluminium — even short of a full deal — would be the clearest signal yet that Washington is willing to negotiate tariffs sector by sector rather than hold a blanket line, a template that could shape how other US trading partners approach their own talks over the next year.

The next concrete marker is the August 19 deadline itself: either a framework emerges from the Greer talks, or the threatened 50% tariffs on additional Canadian goods take effect.
