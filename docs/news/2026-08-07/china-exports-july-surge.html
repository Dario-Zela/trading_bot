China's exports jump 23.9% in July as AI-driven electronics offset US trade drag

Container ships and cranes at Yantian port near Shenzhen, one of the terminals handling China's record export volumes. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's exports rose 23.9% in July on AI chips, EVs and batteries even as US-bound shipments fell, underscoring a supply chain shifting away from America.

China's customs bureau reported Friday that exports rose 23.9% in July from a year earlier, comfortably beating the 22-23% forecasts economists had penciled in, even as growth cooled from June's 27% pace — the fastest in nearly five years. Imports climbed 27.5%, and the country posted a $112.5bn trade surplus, down from $125.6bn in June but still enormous by any historical measure.

The headline number tells a simple story: Chinese factories are still finding buyers. The composition tells a more interesting one. Growth is no longer coming from the toys, furniture and low-margin assembly that built China's export machine over three decades. It's coming from semiconductors, electric vehicles, batteries and the physical infrastructure of the global AI buildout — even as the United States, still China's single largest national export market, becomes a smaller part of the picture.

Chips, cars and batteries do the heavy lifting

Strip out the aggregate and the divergence is stark. High-tech exports rose roughly 40% in the January-July period from a year earlier, according to customs data, with vehicles up 55% and electronics and machinery up 26%. Integrated-circuit exports — the catch-all customs category covering everything from memory chips to power-management silicon — climbed more than 90% by value over the first half of the year, and semiconductor shipments have continued to roughly double year-on-year through July.

Much of that is a price story rather than a volume story. DRAM and NAND flash prices have been rising sharply worldwide as data-center operators scramble to secure memory for AI servers, and Chinese exporters — who package and re-export a large share of mature-node and memory chips rather than cutting-edge logic — are capturing that price inflation directly. Analysts at ING and elsewhere have noted the average value per exported chip implied by the data is closer to a dollar than to the price of an advanced processor, a reminder that the number reflects global memory economics as much as any leap in Chinese fabrication capability.

Electric vehicles and batteries show a cleaner volume story. EV export value rose 68.7% in the first half of the year, with June shipments topping one million units for the first time, according to customs figures reported by Chinese state media. Lithium battery exports rose 42.7% over the same period. BYD, the country's largest EV maker, has built a fleet of purpose-built car-carrier ships — the newest, BYD Shenzhen, holds more than 9,000 vehicles — specifically to move that volume to Europe, Latin America and Southeast Asia without depending on chartered capacity. Combined, EVs, batteries and solar cells brought in $118.4bn in the first half, up 51.6% from a year earlier.

The US slice keeps shrinking

Set against that strength is a steady erosion in the American leg of the trade relationship. A China Beige Book survey of more than 1,400 businesses, conducted July 20-28, found US-bound shipments fell outright for the first time in several months, reversing a roughly 14% jump in June that had been driven largely by exporters front-running an anticipated tariff increase. That increase duly arrived: Washington applied a 12.5% levy on Chinese goods in late July, replacing a temporary 10% rate that had lapsed, layering onto existing measures including a 15% tariff on polysilicon imports aimed at China's solar and chip supply chains.

The pattern is now familiar enough to have a rhythm — front-load ahead of a tariff deadline, pull back once it lands. China's exports to the US fell more than 26% year-on-year in March, then recovered as shippers raced cargo out before summer tariff changes, then softened again once the new rate took effect. Exporters have had two years of practice absorbing tariff shocks by routing more of their growth through the EU, ASEAN and South Korea instead. EU-bound exports rose in the mid-to-high teens over the summer months and China's surplus with the bloc hit a record $32.9bn in June, while exports to South Korea — itself a major electronics and chip buyer — jumped 46.6%. Gary Ng, an economist at Natixis, has argued that diversified markets mean the latest US measures will have only a limited overall effect on China's export machine, even as individual sectors like consumer goods and toys — down nearly 10% — feel the pinch more directly.

What the divergence signals

For traders, the story is less about the surprise beat on the headline number than about what is driving it. A trade surplus built increasingly on AI-linked electronics, EVs and batteries — rather than the low-margin, US-dependent manufacturing of the past decade — points to a supply chain that is reorganizing around new demand centers and away from Washington's tariff reach. That has direct implications: it supports the case for continued strength in Chinese industrial and battery-supply-chain equities, adds to global memory-chip pricing pressure that is already squeezing margins at firms like SanDisk and Western Digital, and reduces China's near-term sensitivity to US tariff escalation relative to a few years ago, even as it keeps the yuan's trade-weighted strength intact.

It is also a fragile kind of resilience. China's factory activity contracted unexpectedly in July, hit by weak domestic orders and disruptive typhoons along the coast, according to the official manufacturing PMI — a reminder that export strength is partly compensating for soft demand at home rather than confirming broad-based economic health. Lynn Song, an economist at ING, has pointed to a widening divergence between China's advanced, export-facing industries and its more traditional, domestically oriented ones, with external demand doing more of the work to keep growth on target.

The next test comes with August trade data, due in early September, which will show whether the 12.5% US tariff rate keeps suppressing American-bound shipments through a full month rather than the tail end of one, and whether chip and EV volumes hold up as memory prices — which have inflated much of the semiconductor figure — begin to normalize.
