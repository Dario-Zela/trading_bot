Australian aviation crew carries out daring midwinter Antarctica rescue

A Skytraders Airbus A319, the aircraft type used in the rescue, on the ice runway at McMurdo Station, Antarctica. — Eli Duke / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
An Australian crew flew an A319 into McMurdo Station in −43°C midwinter darkness to evacuate a US researcher, in a mission pilots called at the edge of any aircraft's capability.

The plane that flew the mission does not normally do this. The Australian Antarctic Division's leased Airbus A319 spends its working life shuttling scientists and cargo between Hobart and Wilkins Aerodrome during the Antarctic summer, when there is at least some daylight to land by. In the early hours of Friday, 31 July, it took off in winter darkness instead, bound for McMurdo Station on the other side of the continent, to bring home an American researcher who needed medical care no Antarctic base can provide.

Five hours later it touched down at McMurdo in complete darkness, with the temperature at −43°C and falling. A ground crew — facing the same cold, plus a stiff wind — had spent hours grading and compacting the station's snow runway to keep it usable as conditions worsened. Onboard were specialists from the AAD and the Centre for Antarctic, Remote and Maritime Medicine, sent at the request of the US Antarctic Program. The crew transferred the patient, refuelled, and flew on to Christchurch, where the patient is now receiving treatment.

"Every Antarctic mission demands absolute precision, but winter operations raise the complexity significantly," said Captain Al Wallach, who piloted the flight. "The conditions we encountered were right on the edge of even the most specialised aircraft capability." Asked about the patient, he kept it simple: "Our thoughts remain with the patient, and we wish them a full recovery."

Nothing about this can be scheduled

What made the flight remarkable wasn't the distance — Hobart to McMurdo and back is well within the A319's 5,000-nautical-mile range — but the fact that almost everything about it had to be decided in real time. There is no instrument approach that accounts for a snow runway going soft in a rising wind, no dispatch system that reroutes around six hours of grading work that either finishes in time or doesn't. Weather forecasters, runway crews, flight planners and medical staff were reading conditions and adjusting the plan continuously, with no fallback airfield within reach once the aircraft passed a certain point south.

Emma Campbell, the AAD's head of division, called it "a complex operation carried out in the depths of the Antarctic winter" that "highlights both the capability of our people and the strong partnerships that underpin Antarctic programs around the world." It is not the first time the same aircraft has done something like this: it flew a similar winter evacuation from McMurdo in 2020, in milder −30°C conditions, on that occasion prompting the AAD's then general manager to credit "the great spirit of cooperation between Antarctic nations."

Midwinter medevacs from Antarctica remain rare by design — bases are provisioned and staffed each year on the assumption that nobody is getting out until spring. When the exception is unavoidable, the response still comes down to trained people making judgment calls in the dark, not a system executing a plan. The patient's recovery in Christchurch is the next thing to watch; the flight itself will be filed away as one more entry in a short, closely studied list of missions attempted only when there is no other option.
