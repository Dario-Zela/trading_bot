Siemens posts record AI-fuelled industrial profit but shares fall 5% on valuation worries

Siemens' Munich headquarters — the company posted record industrial profit driven by AI data-centre demand, yet shares fell 5% on valuation concerns. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Siemens posted record €3.52bn industrial profit on AI data-centre demand, but shares fell 5% as investors judged the growth already priced in.

Siemens delivered the best quarter in its history on Thursday — record orders, record profit, record backlog — and its shares fell anyway. By the close, the stock was down roughly 5%, having dropped as much as 6% intraday, wiping out well over €10bn in market value in a single session. The mismatch is the story: the fundamentals were unambiguously good, and the market sold them.

The headline number was industrial profit of €3.52bn, up 25% year-on-year and a record for the German conglomerate, with margins widening to 17.3% from 14.9% a year earlier. Orders rose 14% to €27.9bn, also a record, leaving Siemens with a backlog of €132bn worth of contracted work — more than a year and a half of revenue already booked. Free cash flow jumped 42% to €4.1bn. Management raised full-year earnings guidance for the second time this year, to €11.20–€11.50 a share from €10.70–€11.10. Every one of those figures beat analyst consensus.

Data centres did the heavy lifting

The engine behind the beat was unmistakably artificial intelligence — or rather, the physical infrastructure AI runs on. Siemens' Smart Infrastructure division, which sells the electrification and building-automation gear that data centres need, posted orders up 42% to a record €8bn, with triple-digit growth in the data-centre vertical specifically. Data-centre orders for the division have run at roughly €6bn over nine months, and management said the backlog now stretches into 2027 with no capacity constraints in sight.

Chief executive Roland Busch framed it as validation of a strategy built around what the company calls "industrial AI" — software and automation tools, including a newly launched Eigen Engineering Agent, sold alongside the traditional hardware business. "We delivered another very successful quarter with record orders and profit," he said, pointing to technological leadership across the portfolio. CFO Veronika Bienert noted the company had offset cost inflation with pricing, keeping the economics net positive even as input costs rose.

This is the same demand story that has lifted Schneider Electric, ABB and, in a separate listing, Siemens Energy — all beneficiaries of the buildout of AI compute capacity. It is real, and by Siemens' own numbers, still accelerating.

So why did the stock fall

Two things pulled investors' attention away from the headline beat. First, Digital Industries — Siemens' factory-automation arm, and the part of the business least tied to AI infrastructure — turned in results that were merely in line rather than another upside surprise. Orders rose 9% to €4.9bn with a book-to-bill ratio just below 1, hardware orders were up but software bookings lagged on delayed design-automation deals, and German revenue within the division slipped 1%. Morgan Stanley analysts wrote that "DI orders were only in line, which may offset the positives," and kept an equal-weight rating with a €276 price target — below where shares had been trading before the fall.

Second, and more tellingly, valuation had already run ahead of the story. Siemens shares had rallied hard into the print, and by GuruFocus's fair-value model the stock was trading somewhere between 30% and 39% above estimated intrinsic value even before Thursday's results. The trailing price-to-earnings ratio, near 29x, sat close to a three-year high and well above the roughly 18x average for European industrials. Put simply: the AI data-centre tailwind that showed up in Thursday's numbers had already been priced into the shares, arguably several quarters in advance. A record quarter that merely confirmed the narrative, rather than upgrading it, gave momentum investors a reason to take profits rather than add to positions.

A read for the rest of the sector

The reaction matters beyond Siemens itself. It lands in the same week SpaceX shares sank 13% on the scale of its own AI infrastructure capital spending, and it echoes the caution creeping into how markets price AI-adjacent industrials more broadly. The lesson traders are drawing is not that the data-centre buildout is slowing — Siemens' backlog says otherwise — but that the bar for justifying premium multiples on that story keeps rising. Good news that simply confirms an existing thesis no longer moves a stock that has already re-rated on the promise of it.

Whether Thursday's drop is a buying opportunity or the start of a broader rotation out of industrials trading on AI optimism will depend on the next data points: Digital Industries' order trend into the fourth quarter, whether Smart Infrastructure's 2027 backlog converts to revenue without margin slippage, and how peers ABB and Schneider Electric price in their own upcoming results. Siemens reports full-year fiscal 2026 results in November — the next moment the market can check the narrative against the numbers again.
