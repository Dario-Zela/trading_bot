Green Party membership surge threatens to overtake Labour's

Zack Polanski, elected Green Party leader in September 2025 on an "eco-populist" platform, pictured in 2022. — Gary Lawson Photography / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Green Party membership has roughly tripled to about 225,000 since September 2025, as Labour's has fallen to its lowest in a decade.

There is a specific, slightly absurd number that Green Party organisers have taken to repeating: a new member roughly every ten minutes for the past eleven months. Multiply that out and it explains how a party that stood at around 66,000 members last September now claims 225,000 — while Labour, the party that has governed Britain since July 2024, has been losing members at almost exactly the same clip in reverse.

The Greens' growth tracks neatly to the arrival of Zack Polanski as leader. Elected in a landslide in September 2025 on an explicitly "eco-populist" platform — his words, borrowed as much from movement politics as from Westminster — Polanski said the party needed to "connect with that anger and turn it to hope, turn it to possible solutions" rather than keep talking, as he put it, in "stats and spreadsheets." Membership passed 100,000 within a month of his election. It crossed 200,000 in early March 2026, days after Hannah Spencer won the Gorton and Denton by-election — the Greens' first-ever Westminster by-election victory and their fifth MP — with the party adding more than 2,000 members a day in the fortnight that followed. A YouGov poll for Sky News taken around the same time put the Greens on 21%, ahead of a Labour and Conservative party tied on 16% apiece.

Labour's numbers, by contrast, have been sliding since Sir Keir Starmer took the leadership in April 2020, when the party still counted 532,046 members. The Electoral Commission's most recent annual return, published this week, put Labour's membership at the end of 2025 at 264,754 — its lowest in a decade, and a drop of 68,481, or roughly a fifth, in a single year. Donation income fell even harder, from £39.4 million in 2024 to £6.5 million in 2025.

Two different kinds of number

Worth noting: the Greens' 225,000 figure is the party's own live count, not an audited one. The Electoral Commission's official year-end 2025 tally for the Greens was 186,441 — still a huge jump on prior years, but short of what the party is claiming for mid-2026. Real-time counters tend to run ahead of regulatory filings, and parties have every incentive to publicise the bigger number. The comparison with Labour's 264,754 is therefore a little apples-to-oranges, though the trend lines — one rising steeply, one falling — are not in dispute.

What membership numbers do not straightforwardly translate into is seats. The Greens hold four English constituencies plus Gorton and Denton, against Labour's hundreds. Members pay dues, knock on doors and vote in internal contests; they do not, by themselves, win general elections. Still, a party swelling toward a quarter of a million members while the party of government sheds a fifth of its own in a year says something about where activist energy is currently pooling. The Greens' Autumn Conference, in Brighton in late September, will be the next moment to watch — the first big stage for Polanski to make the case that the surge is durable rather than a post-by-election spike.
