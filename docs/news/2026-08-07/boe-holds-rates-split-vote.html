Bank of England holds rates in 6-3 split as Bailey plays down further tightening

Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey, who said inflation has fallen faster than expected even as three MPC members pushed for a rate rise. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BoE held rates at 3.75% in a 6-3 split — the widest MPC dissent this year — as Bailey said disinflation remains on track despite Middle East-driven energy risks.

The Bank of England left its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 3.75% on Thursday, but the vote behind that decision — 6 to 3 — was the most divided the Monetary Policy Committee has delivered all year, and it tells its own story about where UK monetary policy goes next.

Three of the nine MPC members — Chief Economist Huw Pill, along with Catherine Mann and Megan Greene — voted for an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%. The other six, including Governor Andrew Bailey, opted to hold, judging that underlying price pressures were softening faster than the hawks believed. It was the fifth hold of 2026 and keeps Bank Rate at its lowest since June 2023, a level reached after back-to-back quarter-point cuts last August and November.

Bailey told reporters that UK inflation had cooled faster than expected, to 2.6% in the most recent reading, and used the press conference to play down the prospect of the Committee reversing course toward tightening. The Bank's line, as set out in the minutes, was that it "stands ready to act as needed" to keep inflation returning to the 2% target over the medium term — language that reads as a floor under expectations rather than a promise of further cuts.

Three votes, and rising

The dissent is the detail worth sitting with. In June, two members — Greene and Pill — voted to raise rates; Mann's defection to their camp in July pushed the hawkish bloc to three, the largest since the Committee's rate-cutting cycle began. The stated reason is familiar: renewed fighting in the Middle East has kept energy prices elevated and volatile, and the hawks worry that will feed back into headline inflation before the disinflation trend is secure. Bailey has previously acknowledged as much directly, saying in June that the Bank would likely be at its inflation target already were it not for the Iran conflict's effect on energy costs.

That tension — a majority confident enough in the data to hold, a growing minority not willing to bet on it — is why economists have taken to calling this a "hawkish hold." Felix Feather at Aberdeen described the outcome as more hawkish than markets had priced in, noting that inflation-risk concern is spreading rather than fading within the Committee.

Market reaction was muted

Sterling ticked up just 0.08% against the dollar to $1.3376 in the minutes after the decision — a small move, mostly explained by the fact that the Federal Reserve had held its own rate at 3.5%-3.75% the day before, leaving little relative-policy gap for currency traders to react to. Gilt markets showed a similarly contained response, though yields at the long end have been under separate pressure from fiscal concerns ahead of the autumn Budget, a dynamic distinct from Thursday's vote.

The practical read for traders: the hiking cycle looks over, but a pause is not the same as a clear path to cuts. With three votes now on the hawkish side of the ledger, any data surprise — an energy spike from the Middle East, or inflation proving stickier than the 2.6% print suggests — could tip the balance the other way. The Committee's next chance to show its hand comes on September 17, when it reconvenes for its sixth rate decision of the year.
