SpaceX shares sink 13% as debut earnings reveal huge AI infrastructure spending

In one sentence
SpaceX beat revenue estimates in its debut earnings report but shares fell 13% after quarterly capex jumped sixfold to $18.4bn, almost all aimed at AI.

SpaceX posted its first earnings report as a public company this week, and by most conventional measures it was a strong one: revenue nearly doubled from a year earlier, losses narrowed sharply, and the core satellite-internet business kept adding customers at a pace most tech companies would envy. Investors sold the stock anyway, knocking 13% off its value and wiping $81 billion off Elon Musk's net worth in a single session.

The reason sits in one number. SpaceX spent $18.4 billion on capital expenditure in the second quarter — six times what it spent a year earlier, and well above the roughly $13.2 billion Wall Street had modeled. Of that, $15.8 billion went toward AI infrastructure: data centers on the ground and, more strikingly, the beginnings of a plan to put AI compute into orbit.

A strong quarter, on paper

Revenue came in at $7.81 billion for the quarter, up 92% year-on-year and comfortably ahead of the $6.9 billion analysts expected. The net loss narrowed to $541 million from roughly $1 billion a year earlier, and adjusted EBITDA nearly tripled to $3.5 billion.

Starlink remains the engine. The satellite-internet unit now serves more than 12 million subscribers, double the figure a year ago, after adding 1.7 million net new customers in the quarter alone. It now accounts for more than half of company sales and is solidly cash-flow positive, even as average revenue per user fell 22% as SpaceX expanded into cheaper international markets. Launch Services — the rockets business that made SpaceX famous — grew revenue 29% to $962 million but continues to lose money as the company pours resources into Starship, whose next test flight is expected in late August pending regulatory sign-off.

Where the money is actually going

What rattled investors wasn't the quarter that happened — it was the one implied by the spending. SpaceX is building terrestrial data centers and, on a longer timeline, orbital AI compute satellites it wants operating by 2028, designed to run AI inference workloads in space with Starlink providing the connectivity back to Earth. It is a genuinely unusual bet: rather than renting cloud capacity or building conventional data centers alongside everyone else chasing AI demand, SpaceX is trying to turn its rocket and satellite manufacturing base into an advantage in a market it didn't previously compete in.

Management spent much of the earnings call trying to reframe the number rather than defend it. Chief financial officer Bret Johnsen told analysts that "all capex is not the same," arguing the AI compute spending was generating "less than a one-year payback" — a payback period that, if accurate, would be unusually fast for infrastructure investment of this scale. Musk went further, telling investors he now expects annual revenue to top $1 trillion by 2029 or 2030, sooner than the 2031 timeline he had floated previously.

A market not yet convinced

Analysts split roughly along the lines you'd expect. JPMorgan raised its price target to $240 from $225, pointing to SpaceX's "extreme vertical integration" as a structural edge as "the pace of change in AI is incredibly fast." Wells Fargo cut its target to $215 from $230, calling the plan "an ambitious start" while flagging the capex trajectory as a genuine risk. XTB research director Kathleen Brooks put the market's unease plainly: "The concern for investors is how fast expenditure growth is outpacing revenue growth." IG's Chris Beauchamp was blunter still, joking that a SpaceX rocket crashing into the moon that morning was "probably a good metaphor for the share price performance so far."

The timing compounded the reaction. SpaceX priced its June IPO at $135 a share, raising $85.7 billion in the largest listing on record, and the stock touched $225 within days before drifting back down through the summer. The first tranche of the post-IPO lockup — roughly 20% of restricted shares, about 911.5 million of them — unlocked this week, just two trading days after the earnings release, adding a fresh supply of sellable stock at precisely the moment sentiment turned. Musk's own shares remain locked for the full restriction period.

What happens next depends on whether SpaceX can show the AI spending translating into revenue rather than just backlog — the company's order book stood at $47.5 billion at quarter-end. The next lockup tranche releases after third-quarter results, giving another test of whether investors read the capex ramp as a well-timed bet on a business Musk understands better than most, or as a private company's habits meeting public-market discipline for the first time.
