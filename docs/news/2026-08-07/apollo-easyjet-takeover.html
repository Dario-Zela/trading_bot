Apollo clinches £5.7bn takeover of easyJet after Castlelake walks away

An easyJet Airbus A320 — the carrier will delist from the London Stock Exchange once Apollo's takeover completes. — Anna Zvereva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Apollo will pay £5.7bn (715p/share, an 81% premium) to take easyJet private after rival bidder Castlelake withdrew; the deal closes in early 2027.

Apollo Global Management has won control of easyJet, ending a two-month bidding contest for Europe's largest budget carrier after rival suitor Castlelake walked away rather than match the US private equity firm's price. The board, chaired by Sir Stephen Hester, has unanimously recommended Apollo's offer of 715p a share in cash — valuing the airline at £5.7bn — and founder Sir Stelios Haji-Ioannou has confirmed he will back it too, ending easyJet's 26-year run as a public company.

The price is steep by the standard of recent airline dealmaking: an 81% premium to the £3.94 easyJet shares fetched before takeover speculation began, and a 54% premium to the £4.64 close on 27 February — the last trading day before the outbreak of the Israel-Iran conflict knocked travel stocks and pushed oil higher. Under the terms, easyJet shareholders can take cash or roll their stakes into unlisted shares in Apollo's holding vehicle, capped at just under half its issued capital.

A bidding war that ran hot

The contest began as a considerably smaller affair. Castlelake, a US private credit firm, had already struck an agreed £5.5bn deal with easyJet's board before Apollo gatecrashed the process in July with a higher bid. Castlelake responded by lifting its own offer to £6.90 a share, only for Apollo to top it again at £7.15. Facing an 3 August deadline to make a binding counter-offer under UK takeover rules, Castlelake instead confirmed it would not proceed, handing Apollo a clear run.

Hester framed the outcome as vindication rather than surrender, saying Apollo's offer "appropriately recognises the quality of the business we have built and delivers immediate, certain and attractive value for shareholders." Apollo's European private equity lead, Alex van Hoek, called it a chance to be "trusted to play a lead role supporting the easyJet Group in this next phase."

Stelios cashes in, but he was never in the cockpit

Much has been made of Stelios's blessing, and it matters — his family holds roughly 15% of the stock, the largest single position, built up since he founded the airline in 1995. But it is worth being precise about what is actually changing for him. He quit easyJet's board in 2010 to campaign more freely against the company's expansion plans and has held no executive or director role since; his influence has come from public letters, proxy fights and AGM interventions rather than a seat at the table. Apollo's takeover doesn't remove him from management he wasn't in — what it does is retire the stage on which he staged those fights. A private easyJet, majority-owned by Apollo's funds, will not hold the same kind of open shareholder meetings that gave Stelios his leverage.

What doesn't change is the money. Stelios says he and his family intend to "remain invested as long-term major shareholders," rolling part of their stake into the private structure, while continuing to collect licence fees from easyGroup, his separate holding company, for the use of the "easy" brand — an arrangement he has said has produced "a steady stream of income" for 26 years and funds his charitable foundation regardless of who owns the operating airline.

Why a buyout firm wants a budget airline

The deal is a bet that Europe's low-cost carriers are cheaper than they should be. easyJet has been consistently profitable through the post-pandemic recovery, but its public market valuation has lagged the recovery in passenger volumes and fares — squeezed this year by the same Middle East volatility that hit the wider travel sector. Apollo has signalled it wants to accelerate easyJet's existing plan rather than tear it up: continued fleet modernisation with larger Airbus jets, growth in ancillary revenue and loyalty schemes, and expansion of easyJet Holidays, the package-tour arm that has become a disproportionately profitable slice of the business. Analysts expect the £5.7bn price tag to function as a fresh valuation benchmark for Ryanair and Wizz Air, both of which trade on multiples the easyJet deal now makes look conservative.

The transaction still needs sign-off from easyJet shareholders at a vote, and completion is not expected before the end of the first quarter of 2027 — plenty of time for Ryanair and Wizz Air's own results, and for the geopolitical backdrop, to test whether Apollo's confidence in aviation's recovery holds.
