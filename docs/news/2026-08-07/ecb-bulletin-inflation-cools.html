ECB bulletin: euro-area inflation cools to 2.8% but Middle East risk keeps outlook uncertain

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where the Governing Council held rates at 2.25% on July 23. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Euro-area inflation cooled to 2.8% in June, but a fresh Hormuz-conflict flare-up has the ECB leaving September rate options open rather than declaring victory.

Euro-area inflation cooled to 2.8% in June, down from 3.2% in May, the European Central Bank said in its latest economic bulletin — a reading that puts the currency bloc within sight of the ECB's 2% target for the first time since the Middle East conflict scrambled energy markets. It should be good news. It mostly isn't being treated that way.

The bulletin's own numbers explain the caution. Energy inflation fell to 8.5% from 10.8%, core inflation eased to 2.4% from 2.6%, and services inflation slipped to 3.2% from 3.5% — a broad-based cooling, not a one-month blip. But the ECB is not coming off a cutting cycle here. It raised its deposit rate by 25 basis points in June, to 2.25%, specifically to guard against an oil-driven inflation spike after fighting around the Strait of Hormuz reignited following a short-lived US-Iran ceasefire. The Governing Council held rates at that level on July 23, unanimously, but council members reportedly debated whether a second increase was warranted.

A cut case that isn't quite a cut case

That is the paradox in the headline number. Falling inflation weakens the argument for another hike in September. It does not settle the argument, because the disinflation is running alongside a live geopolitical risk that could reverse it within weeks. Brent crude was trading roughly 1% below its June 11 level at the time of the bulletin, but gas prices remain 97% above pre-conflict levels, a gap that reflects depleted European storage rather than any near-term fix. Christine Lagarde told reporters after the July meeting that "the full inflationary impact of the energy shock has yet to play out," and that the ECB would put "the burden of proof" on incoming data before committing to a path either way.

Growth tells the same hedged story. Euro-area GDP expanded just 0.3% quarter-on-quarter in the first quarter, and the ECB's own staff projections — cut in March to 0.9% growth for 2026 — remain below the roughly 1.0-1.2% pencilled in before the conflict began. Wage growth, at least, offers no complication: the bulletin found no evidence companies are passing energy costs through into pay demands, which is the scenario the ECB most wants to avoid.

Oil still holds the pen

The bulletin's own language is unusually blunt about where the risk sits. It cites a return to military operations in the Middle East after the collapse of the ceasefire, and flags reports of Houthi attacks on Saudi oil tankers in the Red Sea as a fresh threat to shipping routes through the Gulf. None of that shows up in the June inflation print, which was compiled before the latest flare-up. It will show up in the next one, which is precisely why the ECB is unwilling to treat 2.8% as the end of the story.

The next test comes on September 10, when the Governing Council meets in Frankfurt am Main alongside new staff projections — the first quarterly forecast update since the ceasefire's collapse. Markets will be watching Brent and Hormuz shipping data as closely as the inflation print itself; on the ECB's own telling, oil, not the disinflation trend, is now the variable doing the deciding.
