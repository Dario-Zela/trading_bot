UK gilt yields hit 2007 highs as Bailey resists case for another hike

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where policymakers are resisting market bets on further rate hikes. — Photo by David Iliff, CC BY-SA 3.0 / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 10-year gilt yield hit 5.3%, a 2007 high, as oil-driven inflation bets defied Bailey's pushback against a further BoE rate hike.

The last time UK government borrowing cost this much to insure, Northern Rock had not yet failed, Lehman Brothers still existed, and the financial crisis was a rumour rather than a memory. On Thursday the yield on the 10-year gilt touched 5.3%, its highest level since 2007, as oil-driven inflation fears swept through bond markets and traders bet that the Bank of England is nowhere near finished raising rates — whatever Governor Andrew Bailey told Parliament just two days earlier.

The move was not confined to the 10-year benchmark. Thirty-year gilt yields pushed close to 6%, a level last seen in 1998, and even short-dated paper felt the pressure, with two-year yields at their highest since October 2023. The scale of the repricing — across the entire curve, in a single trading session — is the kind of move that normally accompanies a specific shock rather than a slow grind of expectations.

This time the shock has a name: oil. Brent crude has pushed back above $100 a barrel amid the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz, reviving exactly the kind of imported inflation that central banks find hardest to look through. Susannah Streeter, an analyst at Wealth Club, put it plainly: "Steamy energy prices appear to be the trigger for these latest moves higher in yields, but there's been an underlying structural shift in the global flow of money." That second half of her observation matters as much as the first — investors are not just marking up near-term inflation risk, they are rotating away from government debt more broadly, a dynamic playing out simultaneously in Japanese and US bond markets.

A hawkish market, a cautious Bank

What makes the UK move striking is that it runs directly against the message coming from Threadneedle Street. Bank Rate has sat at 3.75% since a hold in July, and on September 8th, Bailey appeared before the Treasury Select Committee alongside Deputy Governor Sir Dave Ramsden and external MPC members Megan Greene and Alan Taylor — and pushed back hard against the idea that another hike is already decided.

"What I want to dispel is the idea that we've really got a secret plan, we know where we're going to go to and it's unconditional," Bailey told MPs.

His point was that policy remains conditional on incoming data, not locked onto a preset path toward higher rates. Bailey went further, arguing that the market's own pricing reflects a "risk premium" tied to fears of further energy-price spikes rather than a considered central-bank forecast — in effect, telling traders they may be getting ahead of themselves.

The committee hearing also exposed the split inside the MPC that produced July's hold in the first place. Megan Greene had voted against the majority, preferring an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%; Alan Taylor sided with the hold. Markets are not pricing a coin toss so much as a drift toward Greene's position — futures curves imply one further quarter-point hike by year-end and two more moves in 2027, a materially more hawkish path than the Bank's own guidance suggests. Bailey's testimony was, in effect, an attempt to talk that curve back down. On the evidence of Thursday's trading, it has not worked yet.

The market's verdict: an auction that priced it all in and sold anyway

If investors were nervous about UK debt, the Debt Management Office got an unusually clean answer on Thursday. The DMO sold £5bn of the 2030 gilt at a yield of 4.786%, up sharply from the 4.613% fetched at the previous comparable sale — a jump that captures, in one number, how much medium-term borrowing costs have moved even before Thursday's long-end blowout. Demand was still comfortably ahead of supply, with the bid-to-cover ratio running above 3 times, in line with recent sales despite the higher yield on offer.

That combination — sharply higher yields, still-solid demand — is the least dramatic but arguably most important part of the story. It suggests the market is repricing the cost of UK debt rather than losing confidence in it outright; investors are still showing up to lend to the Treasury, just at a steeper price. Whether that distinction holds if oil keeps climbing, or if the Bank does eventually validate the market's hawkish pricing with a hike, is the open question hanging over the next few gilt sales.

What it means for borrowing costs

Gilt yields are the reference rate against which nearly all sterling borrowing is priced, from fixed-rate mortgages to corporate bond issuance to the government's own debt-service bill. A 10-year yield near 5.3% — roughly double the levels seen through much of the 2010s — flows relatively quickly into higher fixed-rate mortgage offers and pricier corporate financing, even before the Bank of England moves its own policy rate an inch. For a Treasury already managing a large financing programme, it also means every fresh gilt sale funds the country's deficit at a steeper price than the one before it.

The next test comes fast: the Bank's Monetary Policy Committee delivers its rate decision on September 17th, barely a week after Bailey's testimony and Thursday's yield spike. A hold would validate his message that hikes are not preordained; a surprise move, or even hawkish language accompanying a hold, would suggest the market's scepticism about the Bank's patience was justified all along. Either way, the price of UK government borrowing has already reset to a level unseen in nearly two decades — and oil markets, not Threadneedle Street, are currently doing most of the talking.
