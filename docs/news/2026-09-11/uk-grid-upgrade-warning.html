UK watchdog warns bills will rise without faster grid upgrades

Transmission pylons carrying high-voltage power lines across UK wetlands, part of the network due for a multibillion-pound upgrade. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The National Audit Office warns UK grid-upgrade delays could push consumer bill costs to £7.8bn a year by 2030, even as Ofgem sanctions £70bn in network spending.

Britain's spending watchdog has put a number on a warning the energy industry has been making quietly for years: fix the grid faster, or households will keep footing an escalating bill for the delay.

The National Audit Office said on Thursday that so-called constraint costs — the payments made to wind farms to switch off and gas plants to switch on when the transmission network cannot carry the power that has already been generated — hit £1.9bn in 2025-26. Left unaddressed, the NAO warned, that figure could climb to £7.8bn a year by 2030.

"The planned upgrades of the electricity grid will test systems not designed for activity at this pace or scale," said Gareth Davies, the NAO's head. "Failure to implement these necessary grid upgrades will hamper economic growth as well as increase consumer bills."

The bottleneck

The problem is a familiar one in infrastructure economics: supply of clean power is arriving faster than the wires needed to move it. NESO, the system operator, identified 80 grid projects in its 2024 Clean Power advice as necessary to hit the government's decarbonisation targets. The NAO found 64 are under way, but most are still at an early stage and are not on track to connect by the dates originally judged optimal for keeping constraint costs in check through 2030.

Ofgem has already sanctioned roughly £70bn of transmission investment between 2025 and 2031 to clear the backlog — money that flows straight through to network charges on consumer bills. The regulator's own maths shows a typical household paid £44 this year towards building and maintaining the grid, a figure it expects to rise to £104 by March 2031.

The trade-off Ofgem is selling

Ofgem's pitch is that the upfront cost is worth it. It reckons that even after the higher network charges are counted, accelerating the projects still leaves consumers roughly £30 a year better off by 2030 than they would be paying escalating constraint costs under a slower build-out. It is a bet that near-term bill pain is smaller than the alternative — but it depends on delivery actually speeding up, which is precisely what the NAO says has not been happening.

The watchdog was equally pointed about the information gap around the programme: neither Ofgem, NESO nor the government publishes enough data on individual projects' cost and progress for outsiders — including Parliament — to judge whether the £70bn is being well spent or simply absorbed by delay.

What it means

For a UK economy already grappling with a stalling recovery — GDP grew just 0.4% last quarter — a multi-billion-pound annual bill overhang from grid constraints adds another line item to the inflation debate the Bank of England is trying to manage. It sits alongside gilt yields sitting at multi-decade highs and a governor reluctant to sanction further rate rises. Energy costs feeding through to consumer prices is exactly the kind of second-round effect the Bank watches closely when weighing policy.

The next marker is Ofgem's transmission price control review, where network operators' spending plans and penalty regimes for missed connection targets will be set out in more detail. Until delivery timelines on those 64 live projects start moving, the NAO's £7.8bn number stands as the cost of standing still.
