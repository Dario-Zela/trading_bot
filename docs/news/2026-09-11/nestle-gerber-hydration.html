Nestlé pushes Gerber baby-food brand into hydration drinks

In one sentence
Nestlé is stretching its Gerber baby-food brand into electrolyte drinks, a bet that parental trust travels beyond the jar as it tries to fix a 'stubborn underperformer.'

Nestlé is putting its century-old baby-food brand into bottles of electrolyte water. Gerber, the company's US infant-nutrition label since 1928, has launched Gerberlyte — a coconut-water-based pediatric rehydration drink — marking the brand's first serious move beyond purées, cereals and snack pouches into the drinks aisle.

The product is pitched as the first USDA-organic-certified oral rehydration solution for children, aimed at treating mild-to-moderate dehydration rather than simply refreshing. It comes in three flavours — strawberry kiwi, berry blend and tropical punch — sold as 32-fluid-ounce multi-serve bottles ($5.49) and four-packs of 4-fluid-ounce bottles ($4.49), rolling out across US retailers now. Nestlé says each 12-fluid-ounce serving delivers a quarter of a child's daily zinc requirement, with no artificial dyes or sweeteners.

"The team is working relentlessly to get innovation back on shelves and to revive the brand." — Nestlé CEO Philipp Navratil

A brand under pressure

The launch is less a confident expansion than a rescue attempt. Nestlé took a write-down on Gerber earlier this year, and chief financial officer Anna Manz has described it as "one of our more stubborn underperformers" within the group's North American portfolio. Navratil said in February he was "unhappy" with the unit, calling it "a drag in terms of market share," and warned — without setting a hard deadline — that his patience "is not infinite." Rather than sell Gerber, as Nestlé has done with other laggards, he opted to fund a turnaround: reformulating more than half the food range with simpler recipes and less added sugar, and now pushing the brand into an adjacent category altogether.

The timing leans on a real trend. The pediatric electrolyte market — long dominated by Pedialyte — is forecast to reach $451 million by 2031, and Gerber is betting that decades of parental trust in its jars translate into trust in its bottles. An early regional rollout at Texas retailer H.E.B. this summer reportedly captured a 5% share of the infant-nutrition category within a month, an encouraging, if narrow, data point.

What it tests

For Nestlé, the wager is about elasticity of brand equity rather than the size of the hydration market itself, which is a rounding error against the group's roughly CHF 90 billion in annual sales. If Gerberlyte gains real shelf space against Pedialyte and store brands, it argues for stretching trusted names into higher-margin adjacent categories elsewhere in the portfolio — a template management will want as it works through its list of underperforming units. If it stalls, it will reinforce the read that Gerber's problem is deeper than its product mix. The next checkpoint is Nestlé's full-year results, when investors will look for whether Gerber's North American sales trend has turned, and whether Gerberlyte gets national distribution beyond its early state-by-state rollout.
