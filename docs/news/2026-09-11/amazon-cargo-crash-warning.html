Pilot warned of excessive speed before Amazon cargo plane crash

An Amazon Prime Air Boeing 767 freighter, the same aircraft type involved in the Miami crash. — Josh Lintz (formulanone) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
Cockpit recordings show a pilot warned for nearly two minutes that an Amazon-contracted cargo jet was too fast before it overran a Miami runway, killing five.

Cockpit voice recordings from the Amazon-contracted cargo jet that overran a runway in Miami last weekend show one pilot repeatedly warning the other that the aircraft was coming in too fast — for nearly two minutes, with no consistent response from the other side of the flight deck, according to a National Transportation Safety Board summary released this week.

21 Air Flight 7598, a 32-year-old Boeing 767-300 freighter flying under the Prime Air livery from San Juan, touched down on Runway 30 nearly a mile short of where it should have and roughly 50 miles per hour faster than a stabilized approach calls for. It briefly attempted to take off again, then barreled about 1,300 feet past the runway's end, struck navigational equipment, tore through a cleaning contractor's van carrying seven workers and hit a compact SUV before coming to rest in flames. Five people died; five more were injured. NTSB Chair Jennifer Homendy called the scene "utter devastation."

The automated warnings kept coming too — sink-rate alerts, then repeated "too low terrain" calls — as the autopilot disconnected during the descent. The full cockpit transcript has not been released, and the NTSB has not assigned a probable cause; that process typically takes twelve to eighteen months.

Amazon's exposure looks narrow

The crash lands squarely on Amazon's logistics operation without, so far, touching Amazon's balance sheet much. The company does not own the aircraft, hold the operating certificate, or employ the crew — Captain Joseph Carroll, 55, and First Officer Jaime Felipe Silva Molina, 37, both worked for 21 Air, a Greensboro, North Carolina-based carrier that flies eight of the roughly 100-plus aircraft in the Amazon Air network. Amazon Air farmed out well over 200 flights that day alone across eight contractors, a structure that insurance analysts say is designed, deliberately, to keep primary liability with the operating carrier rather than the retailer whose boxes are in the hold.

Amazon shares barely moved on the news, closing down 0.2% the session after the crash — a smaller drop than the Nasdaq's own decline that day. A wrongful-death suit filed by one victim's widow names Amazon, 21 Air and both pilots as defendants, and more claims are expected from the other victims' families. But absent findings that Amazon exercised operational control over the flight, lawyers tracking the case expect most claims to settle between plaintiffs and 21 Air's insurers, well outside Amazon's own cost structure.

What regulators do with it

The open question is whether the NTSB's eventual findings — on pilot training, crew resource management, or the age of a 1994-built airframe — prompt the FAA to tighten oversight of the patchwork of regional cargo carriers that keep Amazon's and other retailers' overnight networks running. That's the scenario logistics investors are watching more closely than the liability question: added compliance costs or grounded aircraft at any of Amazon's eight Air contractors would ripple into delivery reliability well before any lawsuit resolves. The NTSB's preliminary report, expected within days, is the next marker.
