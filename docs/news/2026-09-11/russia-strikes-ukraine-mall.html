Russian strike on Ukrainian shopping centre kills five

A shopping centre burns after a Russian strike in Kyiv, May 2026 — an earlier instance of the pattern of attacks on civilian retail sites that continued in Pavlohrad this week. — Павло Петров / State Emergency Service of Ukraine (Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
A Russian strike on a Pavlohrad shopping centre killed five and injured 67, part of a same-day barrage on Izium, Kryvyi Rih, Kyiv, Dnipro and Sumy.

A Russian strike tore through a shopping centre in the eastern Ukrainian city of Pavlohrad on Thursday, killing five people and injuring 67, in an attack that Ukrainian officials say deliberately targeted a crowd of shoppers at the height of the afternoon.

Interior Minister Ivan Vyhivskyi said the strike hit "a shopping centre in central Pavlohrad, in the middle of the day, when there were many people on the streets." The attack on the Gulliver shopping and entertainment centre destroyed stores, shopping pavilions and parked cars, and tore a section of the city's main street apart. Among the 67 injured were eight children — including four boys aged 13 to 17, two four-year-old girls and an 11-year-old — along with two emergency workers who arrived to help.

Pavlohrad sits roughly 75 kilometres from the front line in Dnipropetrovsk Oblast, a distance that had made it something of a haven for Ukrainians displaced from fighting further east. That has changed in recent months: this was at least the fourth reported Russian strike on the city since July, with an earlier one in August also proving fatal.

A wider wave

The Pavlohrad strike was one front in a broader barrage the same day. Russian forces hit Izium with an aerial bomb, injuring two people and damaging several buildings. In Kryvyi Rih, a nighttime attack injured five, two seriously enough to require hospital treatment, and damaged the building housing Ukraine's public broadcaster. Strikes also reached Kyiv, where a jet-powered drone hit a Ukrnafta petrol station — reportedly the first such attack on a fuel facility in the capital — and Dnipro, where two people died in a strike on a sunflower-oil processing plant. Sumy was hit twice in under 24 hours, the second strike coming after an earlier one had already killed two and wounded 23.

None of the targets — a mall, a fuel station, a food-processing plant, a broadcaster's offices — carried obvious military value. Foreign Minister Andrii Sybiha said Russia was "deliberately targeting civilian gatherings to inflict maximum casualties and spread fear," and called for tighter sanctions on Russian weapons production. President Volodymyr Zelensky said talks with international partners on additional air-defence support were under way to "protect Ukrainians from such Russian terror."

What it signals

More than four and a half years into the full-scale invasion, strikes of this kind no longer register as inflection points — for markets or for the diplomatic track, such as it is. There is no territorial gain attached to hitting a mall in Pavlohrad, no negotiating lever pulled. What the pattern does confirm is that Ukraine's air-defence gaps, thinned by the sheer volume of drone and missile traffic Russia is now sending nightly, leave provincial cities like Pavlohrad exposed even at a considerable remove from the front. Kyiv's appeal for more interceptors will be renewed at the next round of talks with Western backers; whether it is answered will matter far more to the next Pavlohrad than anything said in a communiqué.
