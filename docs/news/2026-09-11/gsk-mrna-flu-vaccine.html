GSK's mRNA flu vaccine shows early promise, late-stage path unclear

In one sentence
GSK's mRNA flu vaccine beat standard shots in a Phase 2 trial, but with no efficacy data yet and Moderna already approved, the execution path is still vague.

GSK said its experimental mRNA flu vaccine produced stronger immune responses than the shots already on the market, clearing the way for a late-stage trial due to start this month. The data are real. What is missing — from GSK's own disclosures and from the analysts weighing in since — is any specific account of how the company gets from here to an approved product, or how much it will cost to find out.

The results come from Flu-028, a randomised, observer-blind Phase 2 trial in 971 adults aged 18 and older[^1]. GSK's candidate generated higher immune responses against all tested flu strains than the standard-dose inactivated vaccine in younger adults, and than the high-dose version in older adults — the group flu vaccines are least reliable for and most needed by. Safety and reactogenicity were, in GSK's words, acceptable across both age groups.

A two-target bet

What differentiates GSK's shot isn't just the mRNA platform — Moderna got there first — but the target. Flu vaccines have historically trained the immune system against haemagglutinin (HA), the protein that lets the virus latch onto cells. GSK's candidate also targets neuraminidase (NA), the enzyme the virus uses to break free of infected cells and spread. The Phase 3 trial, set to begin this month, will be the first late-stage study of an mRNA flu vaccine built around both antigens. GSK's head of vaccines R&D, Sanjay Gurunathan, called the data support for the idea that dual-antigen targeting could "better protect people from flu"[^1] — a claim the efficacy trial, not the immunogenicity one, will actually have to prove.

The FDA granted the candidate Fast Track designation in July, a procedural nod to unmet need rather than a verdict on the science[^2].

The gap analysts are pointing at

GSK is not first to market. Moderna's mFlusiva won FDA approval on 5 August for adults 50 and older — standard approval for the 50-64 group, an accelerated approval contingent on a postmarketing study for those 65 and up — and posted efficacy roughly 27% better than a standard-dose comparator in a late-stage trial[^3]. That is the bar GSK will eventually be measured against, and Flu-028 did not generate an equivalent efficacy number, because Phase 2 trials aren't designed to.

That gap is the crux of the skepticism flagged around the data: stronger antibody titres are a promising signal, not proof of fewer flu cases prevented, and GSK has not disclosed enrollment targets, a completion date, or a regulatory filing timeline for the Phase 3 study[^4]. Layered onto that is a capacity question — GSK has said it plans to start more than 20 late-stage trials across its pipeline by the end of 2026, spreading R&D resources and execution risk across a crowded slate rather than concentrating them behind any one flu candidate[^4].

None of that means the programme is in trouble. It means the market has an immunogenicity readout and a start date, and not much else to price against Moderna's head start.

What's next

The Phase 3 trial's enrollment pace and its published protocol — expected in the coming weeks — will be the first real test of how GSK plans to close the efficacy-data gap with Moderna before the 2027-28 flu season. Until GSK discloses trial size, endpoints and a submission timeline, the stock case rests on a Phase 2 immunogenicity signal that has not yet been validated against actual flu prevention.
