Fitch models AI-bust scenario that could crash US stocks 35%

Fitch's scenario models a 35% US equity drawdown over six months if AI investment returns are sharply reassessed. — Eduard Hueber / Asymptote Architecture, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Fitch modeled a scenario where an AI-return reassessment drives US stocks down 35%, triggers recession and cuts 2027 GDP.

Fitch Ratings has done something markets have mostly avoided doing out loud: put a number on the AI bubble popping.

In a scenario published this week, Fitch's economists modeled what happens if investors suddenly decide artificial intelligence spending isn't going to pay off the way they'd assumed. The answer: US equities fall 35% over six months, the economy tips into recession, and global growth slows to levels the agency associates with stagnation. Fitch is careful to call this a severe downside scenario, not a forecast. But the fact that a top-tier ratings agency built a full macro model around it — complete with GDP paths, Fed reaction functions, and country-by-country spillovers — says something about how mainstream the tail risk has become.

What Fitch actually modeled

The trigger, in Fitch's telling, is a "major re-evaluation of the ability of AI investments to generate returns." That's analyst-speak for the market waking up one morning and deciding the hundreds of billions of dollars pouring into data centers, chips and model training aren't going to show up in corporate earnings on the timeline — or at the scale — currently priced in.

"A severe AI-related equity price shock — that could possibly result from a major re-evaluation of the ability of AI investments to generate returns — would likely entail a US recession," wrote a team of analysts led by Alex Muscatelli, a director at Fitch.

The mechanics of the scenario: US stocks fall 35% over six months, overseas equities in China, Japan and the UK drop around 15%, and business confidence around AI craters alongside the selloff. From there, the model runs the shock through the real economy. US private capital spending falls more than 6%; US business investment overall contracts 4.8%. GDP growth turns negative in the second quarter of the following year, falling to roughly -1.5% year-over-year, before settling into a full-year 2027 contraction of about 0.6%. Fitch assumes the Federal Reserve responds by cutting rates 325 basis points — a scale of easing that itself signals how deep the agency thinks the hole would be.

The global math is uglier in places than the US figure suggests. Canada and Mexico, tightly bound to US demand, see GDP hits exceeding 2%. China and the eurozone each lose roughly 0.8 percentage point of growth. Worldwide, GDP growth falls below 1% — a threshold Fitch treats as synonymous with global stagnation.

Why the numbers are plausible enough to model

What makes the scenario worth taking seriously isn't the drama of a 35% drawdown — plenty of analysts have floated similar figures informally. It's the plumbing Fitch points to as the reason the shock would actually hit the economy, not just portfolios.

US stock market capitalization now sits at roughly 200% of GDP, a post-war record, according to Fitch's analysis. That means equity markets have grown large enough relative to the real economy that a sharp correction doesn't stay contained to Wall Street — it shows up in household wealth, consumer spending and, eventually, hiring. Fitch's July risk outlook had already flagged that AI-related equity gains were propping up consumer spending even as other parts of the economy slowed, and that IT capital investment rose 18% year-on-year in the first quarter, contributing 1.4 percentage points to US GDP growth on its own. IT sector fixed investment now represents about 5% of GDP, a full percentage point above its pre-pandemic share.

In other words: AI capex has become load-bearing for headline US growth. Strip it out, or reverse it sharply, and the deceleration shows up fast — which is exactly what the recession scenario captures.

A risk flagged for months, now given a number

This isn't a new concern for Fitch. Its Global Risk Outlook for the third quarter, published in late July, had already named an AI-driven market correction — alongside Middle East geopolitical risk — as one of the two dominant near-term threats to global credit conditions. The concern then was structural: AI valuations have become deeply embedded not just in equity indices but in corporate bond issuance, and banks carry exposure through tech-sector lending, market-making in tech equities, and concentrated holdings tied to data-center and cloud-infrastructure financing. A sentiment shift in one corner of that ecosystem, Fitch warned, could propagate quickly across asset classes.

What's changed since July is specificity. Fitch has now run the numbers rather than gesturing at the risk, and the resulting figures — a 35% equity drawdown, a US recession, sub-1% global growth — give traders, risk managers and central bankers something concrete to stress-test against rather than a vague warning to file away.

That matters because pricing for AI-related tail risk in equity and options markets has, by most measures, stayed muted relative to the scale of capital committed to the buildout. A modeled scenario from a ratings agency doesn't move markets the way an actual correction would. But it does put an official number in circulation that portfolio managers, credit committees and policymakers can point to — and numbers, once they exist, tend to get used.

The next test of how seriously markets take this is less about Fitch's model and more about the earnings and capex disclosures still to come from the hyperscalers driving the buildout — Q3 reports will show whether AI-linked capital spending is still accelerating or beginning to plateau, the kind of shift that would lend the scenario's trigger some real-world footing.
