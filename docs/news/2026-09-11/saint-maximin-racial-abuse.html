Saint-Maximin speaks out after his children racially abused

Allan Saint-Maximin in action for Newcastle United, May 2023. — SonoGrazy / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Saint-Maximin says racist abuse of his children in Mexico and online forced two club exits — with no sanctions, bans or investigations following either.

Allan Saint-Maximin has spent the best part of a year explaining, in increasingly blunt terms, that he will not treat racist abuse of his children as the cost of playing football abroad. In an interview with BBC Sport this week, the French winger — now at Charlotte FC in Major League Soccer — described the moment he decided he could no longer stay quiet.

"Whatever your colour is, you don't have to accept this," he said. "I will never accept stuff like this." Asked how he explained it to his own kids, he was matter-of-fact: "I explained to the kids what happened was not normal and they knew already."

Two countries, one target

The abuse came in two waves, on two continents, through two different channels. The first happened in Mexico City last winter, where Saint-Maximin's daughters were racially targeted at school during his spell at Club América — a club he otherwise remembers fondly ("one of the best places I've been with my family"). He left by mutual consent in February, and teammates held up a "No to racism" banner before the next match, against Necaxa. América called it a "real shame" to lose him and offered its "absolute solidarity."

The second wave arrived almost immediately, this time online. When Ligue 1 side Lens announced his signing on transfer deadline day, the comments under the video filled with what the club called a "flood of hateful and racist insults" directed at his daughters. Lens's response was to switch the comments off.

Gestures, not enforcement

What's notable, six months on, is what didn't happen. No one was named, banned or prosecuted over either incident. A Mexican school's internal matter sits outside any football authority's reach; anonymous social-media comments sat outside the reach of a French club that could only mute, not identify, the people posting them. Banners and statements of solidarity are the reflexive response football has built for these moments — cheap to produce, easy to photograph, and largely disconnected from any mechanism that holds an abuser to account.

Saint-Maximin's own answer was to keep moving: after helping Lens finish second in Ligue 1 behind Paris Saint-Germain and win the Coupe de France, he signed a three-year deal with Charlotte FC in July, taking the designated-player slot vacated by Wilfried Zaha. His January Instagram post on the subject — "the problem is not skin colour, it's the colour of people's thoughts" — drew more than 159,000 likes, which says something about the audience's appetite for the message, and nothing about whether platforms or leagues acted on it.

For now there is no investigation to watch, in Mexico, France or the United States — which is itself the point his BBC interview was making. The next test will be whether any of the bodies involved, from Liga MX to Instagram's moderators, produce a response with more teeth than a closed comment section.
