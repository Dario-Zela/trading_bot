Alstom to build battery-electric trains for northern England

In one sentence
Alstom will build 29 battery-electric trains for TransPennine Express in a €1.2bn deal, betting battery traction can beat diesel on cost, not just emissions.

Alstom has signed contracts worth €1.2 billion (roughly £1 billion) to build 29 battery-electric trains for TransPennine Express, the operator that runs services across Liverpool, Manchester, York, Hull, Scarborough and Saltburn. It is the first order for mainline battery-electric trains anywhere on Britain's rail network, and it puts a concrete price tag — and a delivery date — on a technology that has mostly lived in pilot schemes and press releases until now.

The deal splits into roughly €930 million for the rolling stock itself and €230 million for eight years of maintenance, with an option to extend that servicing contract for a further eight. The trains, badged Adessia Stream, will be five-car, 121-metre units seating around 300 passengers, capable of 110mph, with level boarding at every door. Alstom will build them at its Litchurch Lane works in Derby, where the company says the order supports more than 350 jobs directly and roughly 5,500 more across the UK supply chain, plus 36 apprenticeships and T-Level placements.

"This agreement is a landmark moment for Britain's railway," said Rob Whyte, Alstom's UK managing director, of trains "designed and built in Britain."

The economics, not just the emissions

What makes this order notable for markets rather than just environmental policy is the framing: TransPennine Express and Alstom are pitching battery power as an operating-cost improvement, not merely a carbon one. The trains will run on batteries across non-electrified stretches of line, recharging at Hull, Scarborough and Saltburn, and drawing power conventionally where overhead wires exist — avoiding both diesel fuel and the multi-billion-pound cost of electrifying those branches outright. Combined with infrastructure upgrades under the Transpennine Route Upgrade, the operator expects journey times between Manchester and Leeds to fall by ten minutes, and Manchester to York by fourteen.

That is the bet worth watching: battery traction has long been sold on environmental grounds, but full electrification of Britain's remaining diesel branch lines has repeatedly stalled on cost. If a battery fleet can deliver faster journeys and lower running costs than the diesel units it replaces — TPE's existing fleet includes ageing Class 185 units — without wiring an entire route, it becomes a procurement decision rather than a subsidy-dependent one. Alstom has already sold the same Adessia Stream battery platform to Wellington, New Zealand, in a €538 million deal last September, suggesting the company sees a genuine export line here, not a one-off.

Neither Alstom nor TransPennine Express has published unit costs or a like-for-like comparison against diesel operating expenses, so the claimed savings remain a company assertion rather than an audited figure. The gap between manufacturing start (2028) and trains entering service (2034) is also long enough that today's cost assumptions could shift well before the first Adessia Stream unit carries a passenger.

The next marker will be the start of construction at Litchurch Lane in 2028, and whatever cost detail emerges as the Transpennine Route Upgrade — the wider infrastructure project this fleet depends on — reports its own progress in the meantime.
