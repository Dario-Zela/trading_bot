Eutelsat places €1bn order for 229 more OneWeb satellites

A OneWeb satellite manufacturing facility in Merritt Island, Florida — Airbus will build the new batch of 229 satellites at its Toulouse plant. — N2e / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Eutelsat ordered 229 more OneWeb satellites from Airbus for €1bn, taking its total order to 669 — its biggest capex bet since buying OneWeb, despite the unit still not turning a profit.

Eutelsat has placed a €1 billion order with Airbus Defence and Space for 229 additional OneWeb satellites, taking its total commitment to the European manufacturer to 669 spacecraft — the largest single capex swing the company has made since it absorbed OneWeb in 2023.

The order, signed as an Authorisation to Proceed, sits on top of 440 satellites already contracted from Airbus in 2024 and 2025. Eutelsat says the new batch will carry advanced digital channelisers for better onboard processing and will be capable of hosting third-party payloads — features aimed at squeezing more commercial capacity out of each launch slot. Deliveries are planned to run through 2034, replenishing and replacing first-generation OneWeb satellites, which have a design life of five to seven years, while adding capacity alongside them.

"This is an exciting next step for OneWeb and for Eutelsat's LEO strategy," said chief executive Jean-François Fallacher, framing the order as part of a "powerful roadmap to serve our customers, grow our LEO business and reinforce our role at the heart of Europe's sovereign connectivity future." The satellites will be built at Airbus's Toulouse plant.

The scale problem

The number that frames this order is Starlink's. SpaceX's constellation had passed 10,300 active satellites by earlier this year, against roughly 650-660 for OneWeb — a gap of better than fifteen to one. Eutelsat had been adding an average of around 15 OneWeb satellites annually since the 2023 merger, a pace that looked increasingly token next to a rival adding terabits of fresh capacity every week. The 669-satellite Airbus programme is Eutelsat's answer: not parity, which is not realistically on the table, but enough scale to hold a credible second position as Amazon's Kuiper constellation also enters commercial service.

That competitive math is also why the order is being read as a statement of intent rather than a straightforward financial calculation. LEO revenue at Eutelsat rose almost 70% to €297 million in the year to June, now a quarter of group turnover, and management expects another 30%-plus rise this year. But adjusted EBITDA across the group — €632 million in the last fiscal year — is guided to stay broadly flat even as that growth continues, and OneWeb as a segment is still far from turning a profit that justifies its own capital base.

Financing room, not free money

Eutelsat says the €1 billion sits within its existing capital envelope: roughly €4 billion of investment planned across FY26–FY29, of which about €2 billion was already earmarked for OneWeb replenishment, and it does not change guidance through FY29. That framing matters because Eutelsat spent much of the past year repairing its balance sheet rather than expanding it — a €5 billion refinancing package, including a €1.5 billion capital increase, cut net debt to €1.46 billion and brought leverage down to 2.3 times EBITDA from 3.9 times a year earlier. Capex is still set to nearly double, from around €594 million last year to roughly €1.2 billion this year, largely on the back of satellite orders like this one.

The order also threads into Europe's IRIS² sovereign-connectivity programme, the EU-backed multi-orbit network due to reach commercial service around 2032, in which Eutelsat is a lead contractor. Positioning OneWeb's expansion as complementary to IRIS² — rather than a competing use of capital — gives Eutelsat political cover for spending that a purely commercial reading would still call speculative.

What happens next is less about the satellites than the subscribers. OneWeb's next full-year results, due alongside Eutelsat's half-year report in February, will show whether the near-70% revenue growth rate holds as capacity comes online faster than demand has historically arrived — the gap investors will be watching to see whether this bet closes.
