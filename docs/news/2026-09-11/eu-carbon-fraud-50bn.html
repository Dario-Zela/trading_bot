€50bn EU carbon-fraud scheme outstrips HSBC's annual profit

HSBC's London headquarters at Canada Square — the bank's 2025 net income was smaller than Europe's estimated annual carousel-fraud losses. — Christine Matthews / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A €50bn-a-year EU carousel VAT fraud scheme — bigger than HSBC's annual profit — is drawing fresh scrutiny as carbon markets, its historic favourite target, prepare to roughly double in size by 2027.

The bank comparison is the kind of detail that makes an abstract fraud figure concrete. Carousel VAT fraud — a decades-old scam that exploits the gap between cross-border and domestic tax rules — is estimated to drain roughly €50bn a year from European treasuries. That is larger than the $22.3bn (about €21bn) HSBC, one of the world's biggest banks, reported as net income for 2025. A criminal racket built on paperwork and shell companies is, on paper, more profitable than a lender with $3.4trn in assets and operations in more than 50 countries.

The figure comes from Oliver Bullough, the journalist and author, speaking on Bloomberg's Odd Lots podcast, and it has circulated widely enough this week to prompt renewed attention from EU officials on enforcement gaps — particularly in carbon markets, where the same fraud technique has a long and costly history and where the assets at stake are set to grow sharply over the next decade.

How the carousel spins

The mechanic is unglamorous. A trader imports goods, or increasingly emissions allowances, from another EU country VAT-free, since cross-border trade within the bloc isn't taxed. The trader then sells domestically, charging the buyer VAT — typically 20% — and vanishes before remitting a cent of it to the tax authority. The buyer, meanwhile, may claim the VAT back as a legitimate business expense. Regulators call the vanishing party the "missing trader"; the scheme itself is known as missing trader intra-community fraud, or MTIC.

"There is no limit to the amount of money that a criminal gang can make out of this," Bullough said on the podcast. "So there is no reason for criminal gangs to compete." That is the detail that separates MTIC from more familiar organised crime: rival gangs in drugs or extortion fight over finite turf, but a tax loophole with no ceiling rewards cooperation instead, letting schemes scale into the billions with multiple crews working the same product.

Carbon's cautionary tale

Emissions allowances have been an especially efficient vehicle for this fraud because they are intangible, instantly transferable across borders, and — for years — carried no requirement to prove physical delivery. Europol estimated that carousel trading in EU carbon permits cost European governments roughly €5bn in the 18 months to December 2009 alone, with fraudulent trades accounting for as much as 90% of volume in some national registries at the fraud's peak. France alone lost an estimated €1.6bn before Paris, along with the Netherlands, the UK and Spain, rewrote VAT rules to zero-rate carbon trades and killed the loophole.

That 2009 crackdown worked, largely because it happened quickly and in concert. But the fix was narrow — a reverse-charge mechanism that shifts VAT liability from seller to buyer, applied specifically to emissions allowances and later extended to gas, electricity and energy certificates. It has needed periodic renewal ever since; EU finance ministers most recently extended it to 31 December 2026, after energy industry groups warned that letting it lapse would reopen the door.

A market about to get much bigger

What makes the enforcement question pressing now is scale. The EU's existing Emissions Trading System is being joined in 2027 by ETS2, extending carbon pricing to buildings and road transport and roughly doubling the share of the EU economy under a carbon price to about 80%, pulling in some 20,000 additional entities. BloombergNEF projects ETS2 alone could generate €342bn-€570bn in revenue between 2027 and 2032, and separately forecasts the value of carbon credits globally could approach $1trn as soon as 2037. A market that size, built substantially on intangible, cross-border-tradeable certificates, reproduces the exact conditions that made carbon permits a fraud magnet the first time.

Enforcement has not stood still. The European Public Prosecutor's Office was managing 3,602 active cases with an estimated €67.27bn in combined damage to EU and national budgets at the end of 2025, and its case list this year already includes emissions-specific matters — a Sofia probe into a verification body accused of falsifying Bulgarian power-plant emissions data since 2017, among others. Europol and Eurojust continue to run joint investigation teams spanning multiple member states, the model credited with unwinding the original carbon carousel in 2009.

What's next

For the carbon-trading desks and algorithmic strategies now treating EU allowances as a mainstream tradeable asset, the practical takeaway is less about headline fraud losses than about counterparty and settlement risk in a market whose plumbing was hastily patched fifteen years ago rather than rebuilt. The reverse-charge mechanism's 31 December 2026 expiry is the immediate date to watch — a lapse, even a brief one, would strip the main defence just as ETS2 prepares to roughly double the market's footprint in January 2027. Whether Brussels renews the fix well ahead of that deadline, rather than in the scramble energy groups are already warning about, will say a good deal about how seriously the €50bn lesson has actually landed.
