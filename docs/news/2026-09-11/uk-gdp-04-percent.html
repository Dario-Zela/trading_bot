UK GDP grows 0.4% as expansion slows on cost pressures

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the Monetary Policy Committee meets on 17 September to weigh slowing growth against persistent inflation risks. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK GDP grew 0.4% in the three months to July, half the spring's pace, leaving the Bank of England torn between a slowing economy and sticky, energy-driven inflation ahead of its 17 September rate decision.

The UK economy grew 0.4% in the three months to July, the Office for National Statistics said, extending a run of expansion that stretches back to the start of the year but at a pace roughly half of what it was managing in spring.

That compares with 0.8% growth in the three months to Q1 2026 — a deceleration that officials at the ONS attributed to a mix of easing services momentum and mounting cost pressures across energy, wages and imported goods. Services still did the heavy lifting, professional and scientific activity and information and communication both posting solid gains, but production and construction each went the other way, dragged down by softer new orders and pricier input costs.

A slowdown with an inflation problem attached

In a normal cycle, a growth figure that halves in four months would be an unambiguous green light for the Bank of England to keep cutting rates. This one is not normal. Bank Rate has sat at 3.75% since the last move, and policymakers have spent the summer warning that energy prices — inflated by the standoff around the Strait of Hormuz and the wider disruption to Middle East supply — are pushing headline inflation back up just as growth loses steam.

Governor Andrew Bailey has tried to walk a line between the two pressures. Speaking at Jackson Hole in late August, he said the Bank was "seeing quite subdued second-round effects" from the earlier bout of inflation, pointing to a softening labour market as evidence that wage-driven price rises were not taking hold. But he has also told MPs that the balance of risks to inflation remains "tilted to the upside," driven chiefly by energy costs rather than anything domestic. That is a central bank trying to avoid committing to a path it cannot control, given how much now depends on events in the Gulf.

Why the July data complicates the calculus

The practical problem for the Monetary Policy Committee, which meets on 17 September, is that this release gives it evidence for both sides of the argument. Growth this weak — an eighth consecutive quarter of expansion, but a shrinking one — supports the case for cutting rates to support activity. Cost pressures this persistent argue for holding, or at least for caution about how fast any cuts should come. Business surveys cited alongside the GDP release show labour costs as the top concern for larger employers, at 35%, with around seven in ten firms with ten or more staff also flagging worries about energy and fuel prices.

Gilt markets have already been pricing in a more hawkish-for-longer Bank than traders expected earlier in the year, with yields pushing toward levels last seen before the financial crisis. A weak growth print that fails to soften the inflation outlook does little to change that trade — if anything, it reinforces the market's read that the Bank is stuck rather than easing.

What comes next

The MPC's 17 September decision is the immediate test of how policymakers weigh a slowing economy against an inflation backdrop still at the mercy of oil markets. NIESR has already pencilled in growth of just 0.1% for the third quarter as a whole, which would mark the weakest quarter since the expansion began. If that forecast holds, the argument for cuts strengthens — but only if energy prices stop climbing. For now, the July data confirms the drift rather than resolving it: an economy still growing, but running out of the room it once had to absorb higher costs.
