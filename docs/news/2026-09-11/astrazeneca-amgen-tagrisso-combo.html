AstraZeneca-Amgen lung cancer combination shows survival benefit, eyes $80bn Tagrisso push

AstraZeneca's research headquarters at the Cambridge Biomedical Campus, the hub of its oncology pipeline. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca reported two distinct lung-cancer combination wins in eight days — Tagrisso+Orpathys and Amgen's Imdelltra+Imfinzi — but withheld the hazard ratios investors need to size them.

AstraZeneca put out two separate pieces of lung-cancer trial news within eight days of each other this month, and taken together they explain why the company keeps repeating the same number to investors: $80 billion. That is the total annual revenue AstraZeneca says it wants to reach by 2030, roughly double where it sits today, and oncology — already 44% of sales — is supposed to carry most of the load.[^1] Lung cancer, where AstraZeneca now sells three separate medicines that can be combined in different ways, is the proving ground for that ambition.

It is worth being precise about what actually happened, because the two readouts are not the same drug, the same partner, or even the same disease.

Two trials, not one

The first, announced August 31st, comes from the SANOVO trial, run with Hutchmed in China. It tested Tagrisso (osimertinib), AstraZeneca's dominant EGFR-inhibitor pill for non-small-cell lung cancer, alongside Orpathys (savolitinib), a MET inhibitor the company licenses from Hutchmed. In 326 treatment-naïve patients whose tumours carried both an EGFR mutation and elevated MET protein, the combination produced a "statistically significant and highly clinically meaningful" improvement in progression-free survival over Tagrisso alone — in both the high-MET group and the broader intention-to-treat population. Overall survival, a secondary endpoint, showed an "encouraging" trend, though AstraZeneca has not yet called it statistically mature.[^2] No hazard ratio has been published; that level of detail is expected at a forthcoming medical congress.

The second readout, on September 8th, involves Amgen — and a different AstraZeneca drug entirely. The Phase III DeLLphi-305 trial paired Amgen's Imdelltra (tarlatamab), a bispecific T-cell engager, with AstraZeneca's Imfinzi (durvalumab) as maintenance therapy in extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer, a faster-growing and historically grimmer diagnosis than the EGFR-mutant NSCLC that Tagrisso treats. Five-year survival in extensive-stage disease has typically run below 5%.[^3] In 563 patients who had not progressed after induction chemotherapy plus durvalumab, adding tarlatamab met its primary endpoint — a statistically significant overall-survival benefit over durvalumab alone at a pre-specified interim analysis — with progression-free survival and response rate also improved.[^4] Susan Galbraith, AstraZeneca's oncology R&D chief, called the results "among the most compelling survival results I have seen" in her career treating the disease, and said they suggest the trial "represents an unprecedented milestone."[^5] Amgen's head of R&D, Jay Bradner, framed it as tarlatamab moving earlier into the treatment sequence. Neither company has released the actual survival numbers or hazard ratio; both say full data will follow at a medical meeting and go to regulators from there.

Why AstraZeneca is stacking combinations

The common thread is not the drugs but the strategy. Tagrisso alone already generates more than $5 billion a year, but its growth has slowed as it approaches the ceiling of its original approved population.[^6] Rather than chase a new blockbuster from scratch, AstraZeneca is trying to extend the useful life and addressable population of drugs it already sells, by combining them with newer assets — its own, licensed, or a rival's — in patient subgroups where monotherapy underperforms. MET-overexpression in EGFR-mutant NSCLC is one such subgroup; the maintenance setting in small-cell lung cancer, historically treated with chemotherapy and immunotherapy alone, is another.

This is also, unavoidably, a competitive response. Merck's Keytruda remains the reference immunotherapy across multiple lung-cancer settings and faces patent expiry toward the end of the decade, which is prompting Merck and others to defend share through their own combination trials.[^7] AstraZeneca's bet is that layering combination data onto an already-entrenched drug — physicians already prescribe Tagrisso and Imfinzi widely — is a faster route to incremental share than launching something new against Merck's installed base.

What the data don't yet show

A caution belongs here, in keeping with how these results have actually been disclosed. Both announcements are corporate press releases describing top-line outcomes — "statistically significant," "clinically meaningful" — without the underlying hazard ratios, confidence intervals, or median survival figures that oncologists and analysts need to judge magnitude. That is standard practice ahead of a congress presentation, but it means the market is, for now, pricing a direction rather than a number. The SANOVO trial's overall-survival signal is explicitly described as immature. The DeLLphi-305 result stopped at a planned interim analysis, which is good news for patients but leaves open how the benefit evolves with longer follow-up, and whether the toxicity of adding a T-cell engager — which requires hospital monitoring after early infusions for cytokine-release risk — will limit uptake outside specialist centres.

Neither trial, on the numbers disclosed so far, obviously reorders the competitive landscape in lung cancer. What they do is reinforce AstraZeneca's argument that its overlapping portfolio — Tagrisso, Imfinzi, Orpathys, and now access to Amgen's tarlatamab through a marketed combination — gives it more paths to incremental revenue than a single-drug rival would have. Whether that adds up to real progress toward $80 billion depends on pricing, regulatory approval of the combinations rather than just the trial results, and how quickly oncologists adopt regimens that, for now, are still light on the numbers that would let anyone outside the companies check the claims.

What comes next

Full datasets for both trials, including the hazard ratios and survival curves so far withheld, are expected at an oncology congress in the coming months — the most likely venues are the European Society for Medical Oncology meeting or next year's ASCO. Regulatory filings with the FDA and EMA for label expansions would follow those presentations. Until the granular numbers are public, the $80 billion target remains a management ambition underwritten by directional trial results, not yet a figure with new numbers behind it.
