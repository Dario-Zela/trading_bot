Google to invest €13bn in Finland AI data centres, backed by 22-year nuclear deal

Fortum's Loviisa nuclear plant, whose life extension to 2050 is now underwritten by a 22-year Google power contract. — Finnish Radiation and Nuclear Safety Authority (STUK) / Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
Google will spend €13bn on four Finnish data centres, backed by a 22-year deal for up to half the output of Fortum's Loviisa nuclear plant.

Google is putting €13 billion into Finland over the next two years — its largest single investment anywhere in Europe — and the deal that makes it possible has little to do with servers or fibre. It is a 22-year contract to buy nuclear power.

The company announced on Tuesday that it will build three new data centres in Finland, at Kajaani, Muhos and Vaala, and expand its existing site at Hamina, a converted paper mill on the Gulf of Finland that has anchored Google's Nordic operations since 2009. Alongside the construction spend, Google signed a power purchase agreement with the Finnish utility Fortum covering up to half the output of the Loviisa nuclear plant, starting in 2028 and running through 2049. Ruth Porat, Alphabet and Google's president and chief investment officer, called it the company's first nuclear energy deal outside the United States and "a really important cornerstone to everything that we are doing."

Why a power contract is now the headline, not the footnote

For most of the past decade, data centre announcements were framed around construction cost, chip supply or planning permission. This one is framed around a power plant that predates Google's founding by three decades. Loviisa's two reactors, commissioned in 1977 and 1980, currently supply about a tenth of Finland's electricity. Fortum has said the plant cannot keep running past 2030 without a life-extension programme worth roughly €1 billion — money the utility did not have firm revenue to justify spending, until Google agreed to buy the power.

That is the structural shift worth naming plainly: a hyperscaler is no longer just shopping for cheap land and a fast grid connection. It is underwriting the survival of specific generating assets, because AI workloads need power that is both large in volume and available every hour of the year — something wind and solar, on their own, cannot promise. Fortum's chief executive, Markus Rauramo, put it in utility-speak: long-term partnerships give the company "the visibility needed to invest in low-carbon generation and strengthen energy security." In plainer terms, Google's signature turned a plant with an uncertain shutdown date into one licensed to run to 2050.

The mechanics of the deal

The PPA starts small in 2028 and scales up to 50% of Loviisa's capacity across 2030 to 2049 — a 22-year commitment that is unusually long even by the standards of corporate clean-power contracts, which typically run 10 to 15 years. Google is pairing it with 629 megawatts of new wind capacity and a 94-megawatt battery storage system going in next to the Kajaani site, intended to smooth output and support the wider grid rather than just the data centre behind the fence.

The scale of the wider commitment is what distinguishes this from a routine expansion. Google has invested roughly €3.5 billion in Finland since 2009; the new €13 billion figure — around $15 billion — is more than triple that cumulative total, committed in a single two-year window. Finnish officials have pointed to a projected €3.6 billion contribution to GDP during construction and around 7,000 jobs once the four sites are fully running, though those figures come from Google and Fortum's own estimates rather than an independent assessment.

What it signals for AI infrastructure spending

Google's move follows a pattern that has been building across the industry: Microsoft's agreement to help restart a reactor at Three Mile Island, Amazon's nuclear-adjacent data centre deal in Pennsylvania, and a string of smaller-reactor investments from OpenAI-linked ventures. What sets the Fortum agreement apart is duration and geography — it is the first such deal struck outside the US, and 22 years is longer than the operating horizon most executives plan around at all.

The read-through for investors is that power availability, not chip supply or capital, is becoming the binding constraint on AI infrastructure build-out — and that constraint is pushing hyperscalers toward the kind of multi-decade offtake agreements previously associated with airlines buying jet fuel or utilities buying gas. That reframes how to value both sides of the trade: data centre operators increasingly need a credible power story to justify a build, and generators with stranded or ageing baseload capacity — nuclear plants in particular — have a new class of buyer willing to sign contracts long enough to fund a life extension outright.

What comes next

Construction at Kajaani, Muhos and Vaala is expected to begin as the investment period runs through 2027–2028, with the Loviisa PPA taking effect in 2028 and scaling toward its full 50% share of plant capacity by 2030. Watch Fortum's capital allocation decisions on the remaining roughly €700 million of Loviisa upgrade spending, still pending final sign-off, as the next concrete marker of whether the deal is translating into steel and concrete rather than just an announcement.
