ECB raises rates to 2.65% as war keeps eurozone inflation at three-year high

ECB President Christine Lagarde, who called the September rate hike a "no-brainer" — European Union 2023 / European Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ECB raised its main rate 25bp to 2.65% — its second hike since the US-Iran war began — as eurozone inflation hit a three-year high of 3.3%.

The European Central Bank raised its main refinancing rate by a quarter point to 2.65% on Thursday, its second increase since war between the United States and Iran began rattling energy markets in June. The deposit rate, the benchmark that matters most for banks, rises to 2.50%; the marginal lending facility to 2.90%. All three take effect September 16.

The move was unanimous, and the Governing Council did not pretend otherwise. "It was a no-brainer," ECB President Christine Lagarde told reporters in Frankfurt, adding that the decision proved "robust" against all three economic scenarios the bank had modelled for the region. That is uncommonly blunt language from an institution that spends most of its public communication hedging.

Inflation that won't cooperate

The case for the hike was made largely by the data itself. Eurozone inflation hit 3.3% in August, the highest reading since September 2023 and well above the ECB's 2% target. Energy prices are doing most of the damage: energy inflation jumped to 14.3%, its highest since January 2023, as Brent crude has traded above $100 a barrel for weeks on fears that the conflict could disrupt shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. Underlying pressures are milder — core inflation, which strips out food and energy, actually eased to 2.4%, and services inflation slowed to a four-month low of 3.0%. The split matters: this is a hike aimed squarely at an energy shock, not a broad-based wage-price spiral.

The divergence across the currency bloc is also widening. Germany's inflation rate sits at 2.9%, France at 2.7%, and Italy at 3.2% — all uncomfortable but manageable. Spain, more exposed to energy imports, is running at 4.5%. A single rate now has to do very different jobs in Madrid than in Paris.

The ECB's own forecasters expect the pressure to persist rather than fade quickly. The bank held its 2026 inflation projection at 3.0% but nudged 2027 and 2028 higher, to 2.5% and 2.1% respectively — both still above target and both revised up from earlier projections. Lagarde said the Council now expects price growth to stay "well above target for an extended period," which is central-bank shorthand for: don't expect this to be the last hike.

Growth holds up — for now

What makes the decision less straightforward than the inflation numbers alone suggest is that the eurozone economy has kept expanding despite the war, the energy shock, and now tighter money. The ECB raised its 2026 growth forecast to 0.9% from a more pessimistic earlier estimate, and lifted 2027 to 1.4%. Lagarde said the Council had been "surprised by the resilience of our economy," citing broad-based strength through the second quarter alongside a boost from AI-related investment and demand.

That resilience is precisely what gives the ECB room to keep tightening without, so far, tipping the currency bloc into recession — a very different position from central banks elsewhere navigating their own war-driven trade-offs. It is also why markets did not treat Thursday's move as a one-off. Two-year euro-area swap rates jumped roughly 15 basis points on the announcement, and the curve flattened as short-end yields led the move — a sign traders are pricing in more hikes, not a pause. By some estimates, markets now see close to 90 basis points of further tightening priced in over the coming year, with the cycle's peak pushed out toward September 2027. Analysts at several desks flagged October and December as the likeliest dates for the next moves, given the ECB's demonstrated focus on energy costs in its reaction function.

The bet the ECB is making

The tension in Thursday's decision is the one central banks always face when a supply shock hits: raise rates and risk choking off growth that is otherwise holding up reasonably well, or hold steady and risk letting energy-driven inflation bleed into wages and expectations. Lagarde's answer, for now, is to keep hiking while the growth data still allows it — betting that 2.65% is restrictive enough to anchor expectations without derailing an economy that has proven more durable than the bank itself expected.

That bet depends heavily on variables the ECB does not control. If the Strait of Hormuz standoff eases and oil prices retreat, the current inflation spike could fade on its own, vindicating a Governing Council that moved early. If it escalates further, the ECB may find itself hiking into a slowdown rather than ahead of one. The next flash inflation estimate, due at the start of October, will be the first real test of whether August's spike was a peak or the start of a longer climb — and whether Thursday's "no-brainer" gets an equally easy sequel at the ECB's October meeting.
