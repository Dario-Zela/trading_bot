2026 European heatwave toll revised to over 36,000 deaths, €180bn damage

A wildfire burns near Trévillach in France's Pyrénées-Orientales region, 4 July 2026, part of the wave of fires that accompanied the summer's record heat. — Frenchbluesman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Europe's 2026 heat season killed over 36,000 and cost €180bn, but only a sliver of that damage — mostly wildfire claims — is actually insured.

The final tally from Europe's 2026 heatwave season has come in, and it is worse than the running estimates suggested through the summer. Across the continent, excess deaths linked to the heat now stand at roughly 36,210, with economic damage estimated at €180 billion — a figure equivalent to about 1% of the European Union's GDP, or very nearly the entire growth the bloc was expected to post this year.

Germany recorded the highest toll, with roughly 14,000 excess deaths, followed by France at about 7,300, Spain at 4,458, the United Kingdom at 2,877 and Italy at 2,700. Smaller but still significant counts came from Belgium, the Netherlands and Poland. The season ran in waves from late May through August, with the most severe stretch — mid-to-late June — producing the sharpest spike in mortality: France alone recorded 5,764 more deaths than expected between 17 June and 2 July, a 36% jump over the seasonal norm and the worst rate since the 2003 heatwave that reshaped European public health policy.

World Weather Attribution, the scientific consortium that runs rapid-response studies on extreme weather, concluded that the heat was, in its words, "the most severe ever recorded" over the region it studied, and that comparable temperatures would have been "virtually impossible" without human-caused warming. The peak reading of the summer, 46.5°C, was recorded at Noto in Sicily on 22 July. National records fell in France (44.3°C), Germany (41.8°C) and the Czech Republic (41.9°C).

Where the €180 billion actually went

The headline damage figure, first put forward by researchers at Triodos Bank, is the part of this story that has drawn the least scrutiny — and it deserves more, because it is not a single line item. It is an aggregate of losses spread unevenly across the economy, most of which never touch an insurance claims department.

Labour productivity is the largest and least tractable component. Allianz Research estimates that output per hour worked falls by roughly 3% for every degree of sustained heat above 30°C, driven by physical strain, cognitive slowdown and disrupted sleep rather than any single closure or shutdown. Transport disruption — buckled rail lines, closed autobahns, a near-collapse in Danube barge traffic as water levels hit the lowest since records began in 1976 — accounts for another 0.15 percentage points of GDP. Energy markets absorbed a similar hit: French nuclear plants throttled output as river cooling water ran too warm, Belgian day-ahead power prices briefly exceeded €1 per kilowatt-hour, and German wholesale prices jumped 29% in a single day in May. Agriculture lost an estimated 15-20% of expected grain harvest in hard-hit regions such as Austria.

France bears the sharpest edge of this: Triodos put the country's growth hit at 1.4 percentage points, enough on its own to tip an economy expected to expand modestly into a mild contraction.

The insurance gap nobody is pricing

The financial detail conspicuously missing from most coverage of this revision is how little of the €180 billion is actually insured. Munich Re and Swiss Re both put global insured catastrophe losses for the first half of 2026 at around $42-44 billion — a figure that covers every flood, storm and wildfire on the planet, not just Europe's heat season, and comes in below the long-term average. Heat itself is barely an insurable peril in the conventional sense: there is no policy that pays out for a factory running 3% slower because its workers are too hot to concentrate, or for a farmer's dairy yield falling because cows stopped grazing at midday.

Where insurers are exposed is the secondary damage — wildfire. Swiss Re Institute estimated that fires in France, Spain and Greece alone caused as much as €19.1 billion in damage and economic loss this summer, and the firm's catastrophe-perils lead, Balz Grollimund, warned that "hotter and drier conditions are making large wildfires more likely, and — with more homes, businesses and infrastructure built in risk-exposed areas — also more costly." Insured wildfire losses in Europe have compounded at 8-11% a year in real terms since 1970, a trend line that predates this year's extremes and suggests reinsurers were already repricing the peril before the Gironde fires forced 220,000 evacuations around Bordeaux in July.

What this leaves is a widening gap between total economic damage and what capital markets actually cover — a gap that falls, by default, on households, employers and governments rather than reinsurers' balance sheets. That is not a solvency crisis for Munich Re or Swiss Re, whose H1 figures remain within normal range. It is a slower-moving problem: the uninsured share of climate damage keeps growing, and the fiscal and corporate exposure that replaces missing insurance capacity has no equivalent stress test.

What comes next

Munich Re and Swiss Re publish full-year natural catastrophe figures each January, which will show whether wildfire claims from August's later flare-ups push 2026 insured losses above the H1 pace. Separately, the European Commission's post-summer resilience review — expected before year-end — is likely to test appetite for an EU-level heat-adaptation fund, an idea that has circulated since the 2003 heatwave but never secured budget. Until either lands, the €180 billion figure stands mostly as a statement of loss already absorbed, not a signal of who absorbs the next one.
