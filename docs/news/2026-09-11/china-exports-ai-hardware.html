China's exports lean on AI-hardware demand as domestic demand stalls

Yantian port in Shenzhen, one of China's key gateways for high-tech and electronics exports. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's exports rose 25% in August on AI-hardware demand, masking retail sales up just 0.6% and investment down 6.7% — a one-sector economy.

China's economy is running on one engine. In August, exports jumped 25% from a year earlier — the fastest pace in three months — while retail sales and fixed-asset investment barely moved. The gap between those two numbers is now the single most important fact about the world's second-largest economy, and it comes down almost entirely to one thing: the rest of the world's appetite for the chips, servers and components that power artificial intelligence.

Outbound shipments topped $400 billion in August, beating the 23.3% growth economists had penciled in, according to customs data. Imports rose 28.2% to $282 billion, leaving a trade surplus of $119.1 billion for the month. Over the first eight months of the year the cumulative surplus reached $805.5 billion, putting China on course to clear $1 trillion in trade surplus for a second consecutive year.

The AI line item carrying the trade tape

Strip out the technology component and the export story looks far less impressive — much of the August headline number reflects rising prices for AI-linked hardware rather than a broad-based rebound in shipping volumes. Integrated-circuit exports alone came to $40.7 billion in August; over January-August they totaled $256.75 billion, up 103.9% year-on-year. Goldman Sachs and other desks have been building trade lists around Chinese suppliers positioned to keep benefiting from the wave of AI-hardware exports, and Nvidia's own China disclosures illustrate the shifting map: the company's share of China's AI-accelerator market has fallen from roughly 95% to about 8% as export controls bit, while Huawei's Ascend line has moved from near-zero to an estimated 50-60% domestic share. Huawei is targeting roughly 600,000 of its 910C chips in 2026, double last year's output, though analysts note actual shipments are capped well below that by a domestic shortage of high-bandwidth memory.

What matters for the trade numbers is less who wins that contest than the fact that global data-center operators are still buying, in volume, from Chinese supply chains for components, assembly and packaging that sit around the chips themselves. AI-related products made up 22.7% of China's total exports in the first half of the year and added 8.6 percentage points to overall export growth — an outsized contribution from a category that barely registered five years ago.

Everything else is stalling

Set against that, the domestic side of the ledger is flat to falling. Retail sales rose just 0.6% year-on-year in July, missing a Reuters-polled forecast of 1.5% and slowing from June's already-weak 1% pace. Urban fixed-asset investment — the catch-all for real estate, infrastructure and industrial capital spending — contracted 6.7% in the first seven months of the year, a steeper drop than the 6% economists expected and worse than the 5.7% decline recorded through the first half. Property development investment, the biggest single drag, was down 19.2% year-to-date, with private investment off 9.4%. The one bright spot inside that data set is itself telling: high-tech investment accelerated to 5% year-to-date growth, the only investment category moving in the right direction, and it is moving because of the same AI buildout that is propping up exports.

The GDP arithmetic tracks the split. Growth came in at 5.0% in the first quarter, then slowed to 4.3% in the second — below the 4.5% consensus and the softest quarterly reading in some time. "China continues to rely on exporters to support the economy," said Zhiwei Zhang, president and chief economist at Pinpoint Asset Management, of the pattern now well established through 2026: net exports doing the heavy lifting, consumption and investment doing almost nothing.

A single point of failure

The concentration is the risk. China's growth model has leaned on exports before, but rarely on a single sub-sector as narrow as AI hardware, and rarely with domestic demand this becalmed. A property sector still shedding value, local governments constrained in how much they can borrow and spend, and consumers who have simply stopped accelerating their spending — none of that goes away because chip exports are strong. It is being masked by them.

That is a bet on the durability of the global AI capital cycle staying intact. Should the buildout in US and European data centers slow — whether from a demand air-pocket, a financing squeeze, or the kind of valuation reset some analysts have already modeled for AI-exposed equities — China loses the one lever currently offsetting its weakness at home, with no obvious replacement queued up. It also means the health of Chinese exporters, and the semiconductor and component makers feeding them, is now a reasonably direct proxy for how long the AI infrastructure spending cycle has left to run. For traders positioned in either market, the two are no longer separable.

The next test comes with China's September trade and activity data, due in mid-October, and with whatever the Politburo signals about further stimulus at its year-end economic work conference. Until domestic demand shows an independent pulse, the export line is doing more than supporting the numbers — it is the numbers.
