Bayeux Tapestry goes on public display in UK for first time in nearly 1,000 years

The opening scenes of the Bayeux Tapestry, depicting events preceding the Norman invasion of 1066. — Université de Caen Normandie / CNRS / ENSICAEN, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
The Bayeux Tapestry opened at the British Museum for the first time in nearly 1,000 years, with roughly a million visitors expected before it closes in July 2027.

It last crossed the Channel roughly a millennium ago, embroidered by hand to commemorate a conquest. On Thursday the Bayeux Tapestry went on public display in Britain for the first time since it was made, opening at the British Museum under a loan deal that took years of diplomacy — and a fair amount of French heartburn — to arrange.

The 70-metre strip of linen, stitched with wool thread in the 11th century, narrates the events leading to the Battle of Hastings in 1066 and the Norman conquest of England: Harold's oath, Halley's Comet, the crossing of the Channel, the death of a king with an arrow near his eye. It normally hangs in its own museum in Bayeux, Normandy, which has closed for renovation until October 2027 — the gap that made the loan possible. The British Museum has it laid flat in a purpose-built case in the Sainsbury Gallery, long enough to show the whole thing in one unbroken run for the first time outside France.

Demand has been extraordinary by museum standards. Tickets went on sale on 1 July and produced the biggest single day of sales in the institution's 273-year history — more than £2.5 million taken in 24 hours, with a digital queue that peaked above 80,000 people. Everything through the end of December sold out almost immediately; bookings for January to March 2027 open on 21 October. Standard adult admission runs £33, on top of the museum's usual free entry. The British Museum has said it expects around a million visitors before the exhibition closes on 11 July 2027, part of what it is calling the biggest year in its history.

A loan with strings, and a treasury bill

The arrangement is reciprocal: British treasures including the Sutton Hoo helmet and the Lewis chessmen are due to travel to museums in Normandy in exchange. The UK Treasury is covering the tapestry itself with an £800 million government indemnity — the largest ever placed on a single object in a British museum, a bill taxpayers rather than a commercial insurer will absorb if anything goes wrong in transit or display. Museum director Nicholas Cullinan has framed the loan as diplomatic as much as curatorial, calling the tapestry "one of the most important and unique cultural artefacts in the world, which illustrates the deep ties between Britain and France," and likening its hold on the national imagination to Mount Rushmore's.

Not everyone in France agreed it should leave. Art historian Didier Rykner's petition against the move gathered close to 50,000 signatures, branding the trip "a true heritage crime" given the tapestry's condition — conservators have catalogued some 24,000 stains, thousands of creases and around 30 tears in the linen. The painter David Hockney, who lives in Normandy, called the whole scheme "madness." French officials pushed back, insisting the cloth was sturdy enough for the move; it evidently survived it.

For an audience more used to tracking rate decisions than relics, the exhibition's real interest is as a stress test of discretionary spending: a sold-out, premium-priced cultural draw in central London, arriving just as UK growth data and gilt yields tell a more cautious story about consumer appetite. The next marker is 21 October, when the spring 2027 ticket window opens — a cleaner read than the Christmas rush on whether demand for the tapestry, and the museum's £33 add-on price, holds up once the novelty wears off.
