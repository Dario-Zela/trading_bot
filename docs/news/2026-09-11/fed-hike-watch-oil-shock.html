Fed weighs first hike since 2023 as oil shock complicates FOMC path

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Fed funds futures price roughly 59% odds of a September 16 rate hike — the first since 2023 — as a Hormuz oil shock overrides the Fed's dovish stance.

The Federal Reserve heads into next week's meeting facing a problem it thought it had put behind it: an oil shock forcing its hand on rates, rather than allowing it to set policy on its own terms.

Fed funds futures are pricing roughly a 59% chance of a 25-basis-point hike at the September 15–16 FOMC meeting, according to the CME's FedWatch tool — up sharply from under 30% a month ago. A hike would be the first increase in the federal funds rate since 2023, reversing a cutting cycle that had, until recently, been the market's base case for the rest of the year.

The reversal traces to the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's naval confrontation with US forces has cut tanker traffic through the strait by more than three-quarters since late 2025, and Brent crude has climbed above $101 a barrel — its highest level since May — as the standoff drags into its sixth month with no resolution in sight. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil supply moves through Hormuz; when it narrows, the price of nearly everything that depends on transport, plastics or fertiliser eventually follows.

"While this summer's inflation readings were better than expected, they do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved," Fed Chair Kevin Warsh said at the Jackson Hole symposium on August 28.

From dovish to defensive

Warsh's remarks were the pivot point. Confirmed in May after Jerome Powell's term ended, Warsh had given the Fed little forward guidance through the summer, and a July hold had suggested policymakers were content to wait out the energy spike. Jackson Hole ended that impression. Warsh argued that with growth solid, employment stable and financial conditions still loose, disinflation had stalled well short of target — leaving, in his words, "work to do." He stopped short of committing to a September move, but futures markets did the committing for him: odds of a hike jumped from around 56% to over 60% within a day of the speech, per FedWatch data, before settling near the current 59% reading.

That market reaction matters because it reflects a judgment about the Fed's reaction function, not just the oil headline. A central bank willing to raise rates in response to a supply-side shock — one that, unlike demand-driven inflation, a rate hike does nothing to fix at the source — is signalling that it no longer trusts the disinflation trend enough to look through it. Higher rates will do little to unclog the Strait of Hormuz; what they can do is choke off the risk that expensive energy filters into wages and pricing expectations broadly, the mechanism that turned the 1970s oil shocks into a decade of inflation.

What a hike would reprice

A move to raise rates against a cutting-cycle backdrop forces a broader repricing of the 2026 policy path that investors had already built into equity valuations, credit spreads and rate-sensitive trades. Futures curves had priced further easing into next year; a September hike, plus any accompanying language from Warsh about the balance of risks, would push that timeline out and lift the entire front end of the curve. Leveraged and rate-sensitive strategies — anything positioned for cuts — are the most exposed.

Not every forecaster agrees a hike is coming. Goldman Sachs has maintained a hold call, arguing the energy shock is a level effect on prices rather than a durable inflation driver and should unwind if the Hormuz standoff eases. J.P. Morgan Wealth Management is in the hike camp. The split explains why the market's own odds sit close to a coin flip rather than clustering near certainty in either direction — traders are hedged against both outcomes because Warsh, deliberately, has not told them which one is coming.

The next scheduled data point is the FOMC decision itself on September 16, alongside updated Summary of Economic Projections and a Warsh press conference that will be parsed as closely as the vote count. Barring a diplomatic breakthrough that reopens the strait before then, oil will keep setting the terms of the debate the Fed didn't choose to have.
