Japan's 10-year yield hits 30-year high as yen defence drains reserves

The Bank of Japan's Nihonbashi headquarters in Tokyo, where policymakers meet September 17-18 amid bets on a rate hike. — Fg2 / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Japan's benchmark bond yield hit a 30-year high of 3% even as Tokyo burned a record $80bn defending the yen, exposing limits to its policy toolkit.

Japan's benchmark government bond yield broke through 3% on September 1 for the first time since 1996, a level the finance ministry had not budgeted for and one that has been building for months under pressure from rising inflation expectations and a currency the government has been fighting to prop up. The 10-year Japanese Government Bond (JGB) touched 2.995% that morning before a debt auction found enough buyers to keep it anchored near the round number; it has since eased slightly to around 2.92-2.93%, but the breach itself was the headline. It is the clearest sign yet that Tokyo's decades-long era of near-zero borrowing costs is over, and that ending it is proving expensive on more than one front.

A budget built on a number it just blew through

The 3% threshold is not arbitrary. Japan's Ministry of Finance assumed a 3% long-term interest rate when it calculated debt-servicing costs for the fiscal 2026 budget — a planning assumption, not a hard ceiling, but one that matters given the scale of the liability behind it. Japan's public debt runs above 200% of GDP, the highest ratio among major economies, and every basis point on the 10-year note compounds across an enormous stock of outstanding bonds. A yield that sits durably above the government's own working assumption pushes debt-servicing costs higher just as Prime Minister Takaichi Sanae's administration, in office since October 2025, has leaned toward fiscal expansion rather than restraint.

Defending the yen has drained reserves at a record pace

While bond traders were pushing yields higher, the government was spending heavily on the other end of the policy problem: a yen that fell to roughly ¥164 per dollar in late July, its weakest in 40 years. On July 31, Japan and the United States carried out a rare joint intervention — Tokyo alone spent an estimated ¥8.45 trillion (roughly $53-59 billion) buying yen and selling dollars, part of a broader campaign between July 30 and August 26 that totalled close to $99 billion. The bill showed up in the reserves data: Japan's foreign exchange reserves fell by a record $79.6 billion, or 6.18%, in August to $1.208 trillion, driven almost entirely by sales of foreign securities and US Treasuries rather than deposits.

Finance Minister Satsuki Katayama, who has fielded most of the public commentary on the campaign, said this month that Japan's stance on currencies "hasn't shifted" since the joint intervention and that she would keep in close contact with US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, signalling Tokyo remains ready to act again. The effort has, on its own terms, worked: the yen has since strengthened to around ¥153 per dollar, its strongest level since February, reversing most of the July slide. Japan still holds over $1.1 trillion in reserves and is far from exhausting its capacity to intervene, but a record monthly drawdown is a costly way to manage a currency, and it does nothing to address the reason traders keep testing the yen in the first place: a wide interest-rate gap with the Federal Reserve.

The BOJ's dilemma: hike into a fragile bond market, or don't

That gap is the mechanism linking the two halves of this story. The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate on July 30, its most recent move in a gradual tightening cycle, but held steady at 1% at its most recent meeting in an 8-1 vote, with board member Hajime Takata alone pushing for a rise to 1.25%. Governor Kazuo Ueda has since signalled a hike is likely at the BOJ's September 17-18 meeting, telling reporters the bank needs to pay "greater attention than before to upside risks" as underlying inflation approaches its 2% target. Ueda pointed to three specific pressures: the conflict in the Middle East, investment demand tied to artificial intelligence, and the inflationary pass-through of a weaker yen — the same currency the finance ministry has just spent a record sum defending.

Herein lies Japan's bind. Raising rates further would help close the gap with the Fed and take pressure off the yen — but it would also push JGB yields higher still, worsening the debt-servicing arithmetic that just breached the government's own 3% assumption. Leaving rates alone protects the bond market in the short term but leaves the currency exposed to the carry trade that has driven repeated bouts of yen weakness over the past two years. Market pricing has moved to treat a September hike as close to certain, which suggests investors expect Ueda to prioritise inflation control over bond-market comfort, at least for now.

What to watch

The BOJ's September 17-18 meeting is the next concrete test: a hike would validate the market's near-certainty and likely push JGB yields higher again, testing whether demand at auction — as seen on September 1 — continues to absorb the supply. Beyond that, the September reserves data, due out in early October, will show whether Tokyo's intervention campaign is winding down now that the yen has stabilised near ¥153, or whether renewed weakness forces another costly round of defence. For traders running yen carry trades, the combination of a firming currency and a rate hike on the horizon narrows the trade's margin considerably; for holders of Japanese debt, the question is whether 3% becomes a ceiling or a floor.
