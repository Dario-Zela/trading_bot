Brent tops $101 as Hormuz standoff widens to Saudi Arabia, US-Iran talks collapse

The Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne crude normally passes, remains the epicentre of the widening conflict. — Perry-Castañeda Library Map Collection, University of Texas at Austin (public domain)
In one sentence
Brent crude closed above $101 as US strikes on Iranian tankers and a Houthi assault on Saudi Arabia and Yemen's Mokha port derailed Hormuz talks.

Brent crude settled at $101.21 a barrel on Wednesday, up 3.4% on the day and its highest close since May 22, after the U.S. military destroyed Iranian oil tankers in the Persian Gulf and Yemen's Houthi movement opened a new front against Saudi Arabia's energy infrastructure. The move pushed the international benchmark decisively back above the psychological $100 line it first breached in March, when Brent spiked as high as $126 during the opening weeks of the war between the United States and Iran.

This is not a fresh shock so much as the latest flare-up in a conflict that has been running, with pauses, since February. What changed this week is the geography: a dispute that had been largely confined to the Strait of Hormuz has now spread to Saudi soil and to the Red Sea, and preliminary U.S.-Iran talks aimed at containing it have gone nowhere. Equity and bond markets took notice — the Dow fell 316 points, the S&P 500 slipped 0.6% to 7,591.70, and the 10-year Treasury yield pushed above 4.95%, its highest since October 2023 — as traders priced in an energy shock that looks less temporary by the week.

A tanker war that won't stay contained

The immediate trigger was military, not diplomatic. U.S. Central Command said it struck three Iranian oil tankers after Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps fired ballistic missiles at two Navy warships patrolling the Gulf — the first direct U.S. strikes on Iranian tankers since a month-long lull in the fighting. Iran responded in kind, claiming attacks on three further tankers and three U.S.-linked vessels in the strait. Two tankers were separately reported hit by naval mines. UKMTO and shipping-insurance data have tracked dozens of such incidents since the crisis began, and war-risk premiums for Gulf transits — which sat around 0.125% before the war — have at points run four to six times higher week-on-week.

The strait itself remains the crux of the problem. Roughly 20 million barrels of crude move through Hormuz on a normal day — about a fifth of global oil demand and a quarter of seaborne crude — making it the single largest structural chokepoint in the energy system. HSBC estimates actual flows have been running around 6 million barrels a day through the crisis, a fraction of pre-war volume, with a gradual recovery to perhaps 8 million by year-end if the current fragile equilibrium holds. It hasn't held well: an April ceasefire collapsed within weeks, a June memorandum of understanding between Presidents Trump and Pezeshkian broke down by early July when Iran resumed striking commercial vessels, and a 60-day deadline to reach a permanent deal lapsed in mid-August without one. Washington's negotiating channel now runs largely through Oman, with Secretary of State Marco Rubio describing the talks as focused on securing "safe passage of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz" rather than a broader settlement — a narrower ambition than the war-ending deal both sides discussed in June.

Saudi Arabia becomes a target, not just a bystander

What moved oil this week was the opening of a second front. On September 8, Houthi forces launched a coordinated wave of drone and missile strikes against Saudi energy sites — hitting the Aramco fuel terminal in Abha, King Khalid Air Base at Khamis Mushait, the Jizan refinery and gas plant, and a fuel terminal in Najran. Saudi state media confirmed fires at the Jazan complex, a $21 billion, 400,000-barrel-a-day refinery whose Integrated Gasification Combined Cycle unit and tank farm sustained damage severe enough that Aramco halted parts of operations there. More than 70 people were reported injured. Riyadh answered with roughly 40 retaliatory strikes on Houthi positions.

The timing matters. With Hormuz throughput still running well below capacity, Saudi Arabia has leaned increasingly on pipelines running west to its Red Sea terminal at Yanbu to keep crude moving — a workaround that only works if the Red Sea stays open. It is now visibly not staying open. On September 10, Houthi ground forces seized the strategic port city of Mokha, roughly 50 miles from the Bab el-Mandeb strait, after several days of fighting against forces loyal to Yemen's internationally recognized government. Yemen's Presidential Leadership Council said government units suffered losses in the fighting and ordered an alternative command centre stood up to regroup. Combined with the Jazan strikes, the Houthi advance gives the group meaningful leverage over both ends of Saudi Arabia's remaining energy export lifeline — a scenario traders and analysts have long flagged as the market's deepest fear: Hormuz and the Red Sea constrained simultaneously.

HSBC stops calling it temporary

The repricing showing up furthest from the front line is in bank forecasts. HSBC raised its 2026 Brent forecast to $90 a barrel from $80, with analyst Kim Fustier telling clients the oil market "is unlikely to rebalance until the middle of 2027." The bank's own numbers explain why: even its base case has Hormuz flows recovering only to around 9.5 million barrels a day by mid-2027, still barely half of pre-war levels near 19–20 million bpd. That base case assumes a fragile U.S.-Iran détente that could still break down entirely — in HSBC's downside "stalemate" scenario, with diplomacy failed and flows locked at current levels, Brent reaches $120.

The distinction HSBC is drawing matters for how the move gets read. A geopolitical spike is something markets typically fade, on the assumption supply eventually normalizes. A structural constraint is something that gets built into curves, hedges and central-bank forecasts, because it doesn't unwind on its own. Six months into a war that has already survived two ceasefires and a missed 60-day deadline, HSBC's framing looks like the more honest one.

What the shock feeds into

The broader read-through is inflationary, and it's arriving at an inconvenient moment. Fed funds futures were already pricing roughly 60% odds of a rate hike at the September 15–16 FOMC meeting — the first hike since 2023 — and a sustained run of $100-plus crude complicates that calculus by pushing headline inflation the wrong direction just as the central bank debates tightening. Friday's producer-price data will be the first test of how much of this move has already leaked into costs beyond the pump.

For now, the three threads converge on one point: a war that many traders had started treating as a slow-burning, largely contained risk has, in the space of a week, shown it can still widen. Whether Brent holds above $100 depends less on any single battlefield outcome than on whether Riyadh's Red Sea lifeline stays open — and whether Washington and Tehran can get back to a table neither side has shown much interest in returning to since August.
