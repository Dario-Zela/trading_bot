UK medicines watchdog says new laws needed for AI in healthcare

AI tools already give a 'second read' of chest X-rays at more than 40 NHS trusts in England. — O'Dea / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The MHRA says NHS rules built for hip implants can't govern AI that keeps learning after approval, and wants new legislation to fix that.

AI tools are already reading chest X-rays, flagging strokes and spotting suspicious moles inside the NHS. The trouble, according to the body that is supposed to police medical technology, is that the rulebook it is working from was written for hip replacements and stethoscopes — not software that keeps changing after it has been approved.

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) published a report on 10 September setting out 44 recommendations for a new regulatory framework covering AI in healthcare, and said new legislation is needed to make it work. The report was compiled by an independent National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, which the MHRA set up a year ago and which consulted more than 12,000 patients, clinicians and members of the public — including deliberative panels run in Cardiff, Milton Keynes and York earlier this year.

"Unlike most of the medical products we're used to regulating, these products continue to change after the point of authorisation. As new data gets fed in, they learn, they adapt, they drift."

That line, from MHRA chief executive Lawrence Tallon, gets at the core problem. A hip implant behaves the same way on the day it's approved as it does ten years later. A machine-learning model retrained on fresh patient data does not — which makes the traditional approve-once-and-move-on model of medical device regulation poorly suited to the job.

Already in the ward

This isn't a hypothetical. AI chest X-ray tools funded through the NHS's AI Diagnostic Fund are now live in more than 40 trusts across England, and the government says over four million patients have received a faster lung cancer diagnosis or all-clear as a result. Stroke-detection software and skin cancer identification tools are also in routine use in parts of the NHS, alongside a growing number of voice-enabled tools that support clinicians during consultations. Ministers want AI chest X-ray analysis rolled out to every NHS trust in England by 2029.

Tallon told the BBC that AI use would soon become a normal part of NHS care. The commission's report doesn't dispute that this is broadly good news — faster diagnoses, more of a clinician's time freed up for the human parts of the job — but it argues the pace of adoption has outrun the pace of oversight.

What the commission wants

The report, chaired by NHS consultant Professor Alastair Denniston with Professor Henrietta Hughes — England's Patient Safety Commissioner — as deputy, sets out four central proposals. AI models would get staged authorisation, similar to L-plates for learner drivers, rather than a single one-off approval. Devices already in use would face continuous, real-world monitoring rather than a point-in-time sign-off. A public, searchable register would let patients look up the safety record of a specific AI tool, including any adverse incidents. And the MHRA itself would get stronger enforcement powers to act when a system falls short of the standard patients expect.

The report is candid about the risks that make this necessary: biased training data producing worse outcomes for some patient groups, and chatbot-style tools giving incorrect advice with a confident tone that masks the error.

What happens next

The recommendations are not yet law — they now go to the Department of Health and Social Care, which will decide whether and how to legislate. For companies selling AI diagnostic tools into the NHS, the direction is nonetheless clear: continuous post-market monitoring and a public incident register would raise the compliance bar well above what a one-time CE-style approval currently requires, with cost implications for smaller vendors in particular. The next marker to watch is the government's formal response, which will show how much of the commission's blueprint survives contact with Whitehall drafting.
