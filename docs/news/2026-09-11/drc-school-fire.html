More than 20 children killed in DR Congo school fire

Bukavu, capital of South Kivu province in eastern DR Congo, where the school fire broke out in the densely populated Kadutu commune. — Chrispin Murhabazi C. / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A fire and stampede at two Bukavu schools in rebel-held eastern DR Congo killed at least 24 children and destroyed hundreds of homes.

A fire that tore through two adjoining primary schools in the eastern Congolese city of Bukavu on Thursday morning killed at least 24 children, most of them girls, and injured dozens more in a stampede as pupils tried to flee the flames.

The blaze broke out around 8 a.m. local time in the Chimpunda district of Bukavu, capital of South Kivu province, engulfing the adjacent Ujamaa and Furaha primary schools. According to Dunia Masumbuko Bwenge, vice governor in the rebel administration that controls the city, 19 girls and five boys were killed and 29 people wounded, 21 of them in intensive care. The death toll, officials warned, could still rise.

"The fire was followed by a violent stampede that forced students to trample over one another or look for a dangerous way out," a local official, Patrice Buduge, said.

The fire did not stay confined to the schools. It spread through the densely built Kadutu commune, destroying — by different local estimates — between 300 and 500 wooden houses and a nearby clinic. Lawrence Kanyuka, spokesperson for the Rwanda-backed AFC/M23 rebel coalition that has administered Bukavu since seizing the city in February 2025, said investigations were under way to establish the cause. No official finding had been announced.

A city already stretched thin

Bukavu, a lakeside city of more than a million people on the southern shore of Lake Kivu, has been under de facto rebel rule for a year and a half. M23's advance through North and South Kivu in early 2025 displaced more than seven million people across the region and left parallel administrations struggling to run basic services — policing, sanitation, and, as Thursday's fire underscored, fire response — in cities never built for the population crush that war has driven into them.

It was not an isolated incident. Local media reported another major fire in Bukavu the previous Sunday that destroyed more than 45 homes, a reminder that the timber-and-tin construction common in the city's poorer districts, combined with crowded, narrow streets, leaves fires able to spread from a single house — which is how Thursday's blaze is believed to have started — into a catastrophe with almost no warning.

What it signals beyond the tragedy

For a resource-rich country that depends on external investment to develop its mining and infrastructure sectors, episodes like this are a quiet but persistent drag on how the state is perceived by outside capital. DR Congo holds some of the world's largest reserves of cobalt and coltan, critical inputs for batteries and electronics, yet swathes of its east have been outside government control for years, run instead by armed factions with limited capacity — or incentive — to fund fire services, building codes, or emergency medicine. Qatar has been brokering talks between Kinshasa and M23, and diplomats have described some momentum toward a ceasefire framework, but the UN has repeatedly noted that the situation on the ground remains volatile regardless of what is agreed at the negotiating table.

None of that changes the calculus for a mining investor evaluating a concession hundreds of kilometres away, but it is part of the broader pattern that keeps risk premiums on Congolese assets elevated: a state whose writ does not reliably extend to the safety of a schoolyard is not one where infrastructure, however lucrative the ore beneath it, gets built or financed cheaply.

The immediate question is medical — whether the 21 children in intensive care survive, and whether the toll climbs further as more remains are identified. The investigation into the fire's cause, promised by the AFC/M23 administration, is the next fact to watch, though few observers expect it to be swift or independently verifiable given the absence of a functioning state apparatus in the city.
