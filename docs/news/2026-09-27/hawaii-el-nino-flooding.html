Hawaii braces for flooding as El Niño supercharges hurricane season

Hurricane Nolo stalls south of Hawaii's Big Island in NOAA-20 satellite imagery captured September 26, 2026. — NOAA-20/VIIRS via NASA EOSDIS Worldview, Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A third hurricane in three months is stalling near Hawaii as El Niño compresses warning times and strains insurers and tourism.

Hurricane Nolo has spent two days doing something Hawaii forecasters dread most: sitting still. The storm stalled roughly 160 miles south of the Big Island through Friday and Saturday, dumping rain on saturated slopes rather than pushing through, and by Saturday its winds had reached 90 mph with the National Hurricane Center forecasting strengthening into a major hurricane by Monday. Direct landfall isn't expected — Nolo is now tracking west, away from the main islands — but forecasters say that distinction barely matters. "Don't venture out if you don't have to into the dangerous flooding environment," NHC director Michael Brennan warned as rainfall totals on the Big Island approached 20 inches.

Nolo is the third hurricane to threaten Hawaii's main islands this season, an unusual clustering that meteorologists tie directly to an El Niño pattern that has proven stronger and more disruptive than initial forecasts suggested. In August, Hurricane Lala became the first hurricane to make direct landfall on the Big Island since an unnamed storm in 1871, killing one person and sweeping at least two homes off their foundations in the Ka'ū district. In early September, Hurricane Lowell triggered Hawaii's first-ever tornado watch as it swept past Kaua'i, cutting power to roughly 90% of Kaua'i County and more than 55,000 customers statewide.

NOAA's Central Pacific outlook, issued before the season began, put the odds of above-normal activity at 70%, with five to 13 named systems against a historical average of four to five. The Climate Prediction Center attributed the shift to moderate-to-strong El Niño conditions, which suppress the wind shear that normally tears young storms apart and warm the sea-surface temperatures that feed them. That forecast has held. What it undersold, meteorologists say, is a second-order effect: El Niño years also see a higher share of storms form inside Hawaii's own basin rather than tracking in from Mexico's coast — as Nolo did, regenerating from a dissipated system on September 22 after first forming as a tropical depression more than a week earlier.

A shrinking warning window

Storms that spin up close to the islands leave less runway. Hawaii's emergency planners have long built their public messaging around 30 to 60 days of seasonal lead time, but a storm that forms in-basin can go from tropical depression to landfall threat in days, not weeks. Hawaii Emergency Management Agency administrator James Barros has urged residents to be "Two Weeks Ready" — holding 14 days of food, water and medication on hand — precisely because the state's warning systems, built around an older storm-track pattern, aren't guaranteed to give the same notice this year.

The compounding costs are landing on insurers and travelers alike. Hawaii home premiums rose more than 50% last year following the 2023 Maui wildfires, and only 4.2% of properties statewide carry flood coverage, according to state insurance regulators. Lawmakers reactivated the Hawaii Hurricane Relief Fund in 2025 to backstop condominium associations, which have seen hurricane premiums jump as much as 300–800%; it has since written roughly $2.7 million in premiums covering $2.6 billion of insured value. Meanwhile, at least 21 flights were cancelled at Hilo airport this weekend, Norwegian Cruise Line pushed back a Honolulu departure, and the University of Hawai'i Economic Research Organization now projects visitor arrivals will slip slightly and visitor spending nearly 2% for the year.

Nolo is forecast to peak Monday before steady weakening begins. The next test comes in October and November, historically the tail end of the Central Pacific season — and the point at which insurers and emergency planners will find out whether three near-misses in one year were an anomaly or the new baseline for how fast Hawaii's storms arrive.
