Supreme Court blocks Trump order limiting mail-in voting ahead of midterms

The United States Supreme Court building, which on September 14th declined to let a Postal Service mail-ballot regulation take effect before the midterms. — Mathieu Landretti / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The Supreme Court refused, 7-2, to let a Postal Service mail-ballot rule take effect before November, preserving the voting status quo without settling who wins the House.

The mail ballot due to land in an Arizona retiree's mailbox next month, or a Pennsylvania nurse's, or a first-time voter's in North Carolina, will get there the way it always has. That was not guaranteed as recently as three weeks ago. On September 14th the Supreme Court refused, 7–2, to let the Trump administration enforce a Postal Service regulation that would have reshaped how mail-in ballots move through the system just as the 2026 midterm campaign enters its final stretch.

The order was unsigned and brief, the kind of procedural ruling that rarely gets a headline of its own. But it closed out — for now — one of the more consequential legal fights of the election cycle, and it did so by doing nothing at all: leaving a lower court's injunction in place, and leaving the rules of mail voting exactly where they were before the administration tried to change them.

A rule born from a rejected bill

The dispute traces back to March, when President Trump signed an executive order on mail-in voting after Congress declined to pass his SAVE America Act, which would have imposed nationwide citizenship-verification and voter-ID requirements. Unable to get that through the legislature, the administration turned to the Postal Service instead. The resulting rule required states to submit their voter lists to USPS for verification before ballots went out, and imposed new specifications on ballot envelopes — ostensibly to ensure ballots reached only eligible voters.

A coalition of 23 states and the District of Columbia, led by California, sued in federal court in Massachusetts, arguing the Postal Service had no authority to dictate election administration — a power the Constitution assigns to the states, subject to congressional oversight of federal races, not to a mail carrier. U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani agreed, blocking the rule in late June and warning that rushing it into effect risked "significant disenfranchisement."

What followed was a summer of legal ping-pong. On August 24th the Supreme Court sided with the administration and lifted Talwani's block, over public dissents from the Court's three liberal justices — a moment that looked, briefly, like clearance for the rule to take effect before November. Talwani reissued a restraining order days later, then converted it into a fuller preliminary injunction in September. That set up the administration's second trip to the high court, asking the justices to clear the way once more. This time they declined.

What the justices actually said

The majority's reasoning was narrow and practical rather than sweeping: the government was "unlikely to succeed on the merits," and the balance of harms didn't favor letting the rule take effect with the election so close. Justice Brett Kavanaugh, concurring separately, went further than the majority in acknowledging the administration's underlying argument — he saw a "fair prospect" that the Postal Service does have some rulemaking authority here — but agreed the timing made intervention untenable, since state and local election officials lacked "sufficient time to reasonably implement the rule before" the vote. Justice Samuel Alito, joined by Justice Clarence Thomas, dissented, arguing USPS has "broad authority to regulate the mail" and that the government would suffer lasting harm if barred from applying the rule before November.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta, who helped lead the states' case, called it "a victory for our democracy." It is worth being precise about what that victory amounts to, though: the Court has not ruled the regulation illegal. Talwani's injunction remains in effect while the underlying case proceeds, and the administration could yet prevail on the merits — just not in time to matter for this election. Kavanaugh's opinion in particular reads less as an endorsement of the states' constitutional argument than as an admission that the clock has run out on this attempt.

The bigger contest this decides nothing about

The ruling removes one source of chaos from an already turbulent midterm season, but it settles nothing about who wins in November. Republicans hold the House by a narrow margin; Democrats need a net gain of only a handful of seats to retake it, and recent generic-ballot polling has shown them running several points ahead nationally amid Trump approval ratings hovering in the high 30s. Whether that translates into an actual majority depends on turnout in a batch of competitive districts reshaped by a fresh round of mid-decade redistricting — the same battleground where mail ballots, and the rules governing them, matter most.

For markets watching the election as a proxy for the next two years of fiscal and regulatory policy, the practical upshot is that one variable has been removed from the uncertainty column, not resolved in either party's favor. The underlying case over USPS authority continues in the Massachusetts district court, but the next real catalyst is simpler: November 3rd, and whichever party ends up holding the gavel in the House.
