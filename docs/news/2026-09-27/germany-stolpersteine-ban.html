German town's ban on Holocaust 'stumbling stone' memorials draws backlash

A Stolperstein, or 'stumbling stone,' set into a German sidewalk to mark the last home of a Nazi victim. — Vattigustav / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Heidenau's council, with AfD and CDU votes, banned new Holocaust 'stumbling stone' memorials, prompting accusations from Berlin to Munich that it is erasing Nazi victims from public memory.

In Heidenau, a town of roughly 16,000 people on the Elbe southeast of Dresden, the city council has voted to stop something Germany has spent three decades doing almost everywhere else: laying small brass memorial plaques in the pavement outside the last known homes of Nazi victims.

The vote came on a Thursday evening session by secret ballot. Thirteen council members backed the ban, six opposed it, and one abstained. The motion came jointly from the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), the center-right Christian Democratic Union (CDU), and a local group called the Bürgerinitiative Oberelbe für mehr Demokratie. Heidenau currently has five Stolpersteine — the German word means "stumbling stones" — set into its sidewalks. The immediate trigger was a request from AKuBiZ, a local alternative culture and education association, to install seven more, with the cost covered entirely by donations. The council said no, and went further: no new Stolpersteine may be placed in Heidenau's public streets at all, going forward.

In one sentence: A Saxon town council, with AfD and CDU votes, has banned new Holocaust memorial "stumbling stones," breaking with a German remembrance practice now used in more than 30 countries.

A ritual object of German memory

Stolpersteine were invented by the artist Gunter Demnig in the early 1990s: a 10-centimeter brass-topped cobblestone, engraved with a victim's name, birth year, and fate, cemented into the pavement outside the building where they last lived freely before deportation or murder. There are now more than 100,000 of them across Europe, forming what is often described as the world's largest decentralized memorial. Passersby encounter them by accident, mid-errand, which is the point — remembrance as an unavoidable, everyday interruption rather than something visited on a special occasion.

That is also precisely what the Heidenau council objected to. In the written justification for the ban, proponents argued that stones set into the ground force pedestrians to walk on, or even step directly onto, victims' names — a formulation that recasts the memorial's central design feature as a form of disrespect. The council's alternative proposal is a single, centralized memorial site, preferably at the town's North Cemetery, honoring victims of war, terror, dictatorship, expulsion, and National Socialism together, built so that no memorial element can be walked on.

Backlash from Munich to Berlin

The reaction was swift and unusually blunt for a municipal planning dispute. Christoph Heubner, executive vice president of the International Auschwitz Committee, called the ban "a transparent and shabby attempt to throw Heidenau's Nazi victims out of the city again." Federal education minister Karin Prien, herself a CDU politician, described it as "a scandal," warning that pushing memorials for Jewish victims out to a cemetery would make Jews "invisible once again" in the town's everyday life. Israel's ambassador to Germany, Ron Prosor, wrote on social media simply that he was "ashamed."

Charlotte Knobloch, the Holocaust survivor who leads the Jewish community of Munich and Upper Bavaria, added a pointed nuance: she has long argued for a different model — memorial plaques mounted at eye level on buildings rather than underfoot — and considers it more dignified. But she called Heidenau's decision to erase memorial sites from the everyday cityscape entirely "the completely wrong path," and Katja Demnig of the Stiftung Spuren foundation, which documents the Stolperstein project, called it "highly concerning" and said she could not recall another instance of mainstream democratic parties voting for such a measure.

The CDU's participation is the detail drawing the most attention nationally, since it is the party of Angela Merkel and, currently, of the federal government whose minister just condemned the vote. Heidenau itself carries a specific weight in this argument: in August 2015, the town became a byword for the darker side of Germany's refugee crisis, when a mob of over a thousand people attacked police and tried to block an asylum shelter from opening, drawing visits from Merkel and then-Vice Chancellor Sigmar Gabriel in a show of solidarity with the town's targeted newcomers. A decade on, the same town is now at the center of a dispute over how — and whether — to remember an earlier generation of victims.

What the ban actually changes, and what it doesn't

Heidenau cannot undo the practice nationally; Stolpersteine remain a matter of municipal discretion in Germany, and thousands continue to be laid elsewhere each year. But the vote matters as a precedent because Stolpersteine have functioned, since the 1990s, as something close to civic consensus — a bipartisan, grassroots way for ordinary German towns to acknowledge Nazi crimes without waiting for state-led commemoration. A council vote that combines AfD and CDU support to roll that back, even in one town of 16,000, tests whether that consensus is eroding at the local level even as it holds at the federal one.

What happens next will likely be watched closely: AKuBiZ has not said whether it will challenge the ban legally or seek to install its seven planned stones on private property instead, which would sidestep the council's authority over public streets. Saxony's state government, where the AfD has been polling strongly ahead of elections, will face pressure to say whether it considers the vote a template or an aberration.
