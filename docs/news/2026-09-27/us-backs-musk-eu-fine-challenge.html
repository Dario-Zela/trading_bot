US backs Musk's legal fight against €120m EU fine on X

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which imposed the €120 million fine on X. — EmDee / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The US Justice Department has asked to join X's court fight against a €120m EU fine, and Brussels says it will defend the penalty regardless.

Washington has decided that a European fine on Elon Musk's X is, in effect, an attack on American business — and it is now saying so in a European courtroom.

On September 24, the US Justice Department filed to intervene in X's legal challenge against a €120 million ($137 million) fine the European Commission imposed on the platform last December. It is the first penalty issued under the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA), the bloc's flagship law for policing large online platforms, and Brussels used it to make an example: X, the Commission found, let anyone buy a blue "verified" checkmark without meaningful identity checks, kept an opaque advertising archive, and made it needlessly hard for outside researchers to study risks like disinformation on the platform.

X and Musk are fighting the decision at the General Court in Luxembourg, in two linked cases — X Internet and X Holdings v. Commission and Musk v. Commission. The Justice Department's filing asks to join that fight on their side, arguing the Commission overstepped. Assistant Attorney General Brett Shumate put it bluntly: "The European Commission inappropriately attempted to expand its regulatory authority to reach American companies not present or operating within its jurisdiction." The department's brief goes further, objecting that the case reaches past the company to Musk personally — a move it likens to piercing the corporate veil, a concept with no real equivalent in how the EU frames the DSA.

Brussels holds its line

The European Commission was not moved. Spokesperson Thomas Regnier told reporters in Brussels the following day that Washington's filing changes nothing.

"We are enforcing our legislation objectively, transparently and with a solid case. We are ready to defend our position in court. We have a lot of evidence at our disposal, and it will be for the court to decide as always."

Regnier framed the DSA as an exercise of the EU's "sovereign right" to legislate for its own citizens — a rebuttal aimed squarely at the extraterritoriality argument now sitting in the DOJ's filing.

This is not the first time the fine has drawn Washington into a fight that used to be between a company and a regulator. When the penalty was announced in December, Secretary of State Marco Rubio called it "an attack on all American tech platforms and the American people by foreign governments," and Musk himself responded by declaring the EU "should be abolished, and sovereignty returned to individual countries." The Justice Department says it coordinated the intervention with the State Department, underlining that this has become a diplomatic dispute as much as a legal one.

For other US platforms operating in Europe, the case is now a bellwether. If a US government literally standing behind a company in a European court doesn't move the needle, the DSA's enforcement machinery looks durable regardless of who occupies the White House — meaning compliance costs and legal exposure for platforms doing business in both jurisdictions are likely to keep climbing, not fall away. The General Court has not set a hearing date; that scheduling, expected in the coming months, is the next concrete marker in a case that has already outgrown the courtroom it's being argued in.
