Fed holds at 3.75%-4.00% but futures price a steeper tightening path

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Futures now price the Fed funds rate at 4.2% by December and 4.8% by next September — well past the Fed's own dot plot — as Hormuz-driven oil costs keep inflation elevated.

The Federal Reserve's benchmark rate sits at 3.75%–4.00%, a quarter-point higher than it was in August and, for now, the resting point until policymakers meet again on October 27–28. That pause is doing little to settle markets. Futures traders are pricing the federal funds rate to reach roughly 4.2% by December and 4.8% by next September — a path steeper than the one the Fed's own committee laid out ten days ago, and one that presumes inflation has further to run before it breaks.

The gap between what the Fed signaled and what the market now expects is the story. It is rare for traders to out-hawk the central bank. It is happening because the inflation driving this cycle is not the kind rate policy handles cleanly.

A hawkish hold, a hawkish market

On September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee voted 12–0 to raise its target range by 25 basis points, the first increase since 2023. Sixteen of eighteen officials indicated they see at least one more hike this year in their updated projections; four penciled in two. The median dot lands near 4.00%–4.25% for both year-end 2026 and 2027.

Chair Kevin Warsh, who succeeded Jerome Powell in May, did not submit a dot of his own — the second straight meeting he has abstained, having also skipped in June. He has been explicit about disliking forward guidance, telling reporters that anchoring policy to a distance from some theoretical neutral rate is "useful academically" but carries no "operational effect" on the decisions the Committee actually makes. The result, intended or not, is a committee that is already leaning hawkish and a chair who won't commit to a number — leaving more of the guesswork to the futures curve, which has now moved past the dot plot itself. CME-tracked pricing implies a fed funds rate near 4.635% by late 2027, arguing for three or four more quarter-point moves from here, not the one or two the median dot implies.

Oil, not demand, is doing the work

The inflation the Fed is fighting has an unusual source. The Strait of Hormuz has been effectively blockaded for close to seven months, the most severe disruption to global oil flows on record, with cumulative lost exports through the strait approaching 2.8 billion barrels by mid-September. Brent crude traded around $106 a barrel on September 25, and diesel has hit fresh per-gallon records domestically. Talks to reopen the strait stalled months ago; President Trump has said Washington is under no pressure to negotiate, prolonging the standoff.

Warsh has been careful to frame this as a relative-price problem rather than a demand problem: the Fed, he told reporters, "can't affect individual prices like oil," but it can try to stop a one-time price shock from broadening into the kind of second- and third-order effects that become embedded, self-sustaining inflation. That distinction matters for how far rates ultimately go. A committee convinced this is a temporary supply shock hikes less. A committee — or a market — that suspects the shock is durable, and that businesses are already passing energy costs into wages and prices elsewhere, hikes more. The futures curve has made its view plain, and it is echoed across the Atlantic: ECB President Christine Lagarde has separately pushed back her own inflation target date to 2027, citing the same Middle East energy shock.

The bond market's verdict

The clearest read on how markets are processing this sits in the Treasury market. The 10-year yield touched 5.23% on September 26, its highest level since 2007, up sharply from levels that would have seemed implausible before the strait closure. For trading desks, that repricing does two things at once: it raises the discount rate applied to future equity earnings, compressing valuations for anything priced on long-duration cash flows, and it forces volatility models built on a lower-rate regime to recalibrate. Warsh's aversion to forward guidance compounds the effect — without a clear signal from the Fed on where policy stops, the market has filled the vacuum with its own, more aggressive, path.

The next test arrives at the October 27–28 meeting, where futures pricing already implies better-than-even odds of another quarter-point move. Between now and then, the data that matters most is not the usual core-PCE print but whatever comes out of the stalled Hormuz talks — because until oil stops climbing, the argument that this inflation is temporary gets harder to make, and the market's steeper path looks less like speculation and more like a forecast.
