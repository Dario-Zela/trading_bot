Man City found guilty on 114 of 115 FFP charges, plans appeal

The Etihad Stadium, Manchester City's home since 2003, at the centre of a decade-long financial reckoning. — mjtmail (tiggy) / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Man City was found guilty on 114 of 115 Premier League financial charges spanning 2009-2018; sanctions are undecided, City will appeal, and rivals are readying compensation claims.

Manchester City has been found guilty on 114 of the 115 charges brought against it by the Premier League for breaching financial rules between 2009 and 2018, according to a verdict from the independent commission that has now leaked ahead of official confirmation. It is the most sweeping finding against a top-flight English club in the Premier League's history, and it leaves the competition facing a question it has spent nearly three years trying to avoid: what do you do when the most successful club of the era is found to have broken the rules almost everywhere you looked?

The charges, filed in February 2023 after a four-year investigation, alleged that City disguised owner funding as sponsorship income and failed to give accurate financial details across nine seasons — the same stretch in which the club, bankrolled by Abu Dhabi's Sheikh Mansour since 2008, won three league titles and built the squad that would eventually deliver a Treble in 2023. Fifty-four counts concerned inaccurate financial reporting; fourteen more involved undisclosed payments to players and managers; the rest covered failures to cooperate with the league's investigators. City contested all of it. The commission, after a 12-week hearing that concluded in December 2024 at London's International Dispute Resolution Centre, has now sided with the league on all but one count.

A verdict without a punishment yet

What the ruling does not do is say what happens next. Sanctions are a separate matter for the panel, and the range on the table runs from the modest to the existential: fines, transfer restrictions, points deductions, or — at the sharpest end of a very long menu — expulsion from the league. Precedent offers some guide but little comfort for anyone hoping for a tidy number. Everton lost eight points for breaching profit-and-sustainability rules in 2023; Nottingham Forest lost four. Both cases involved single-season breaches measured in tens of millions of pounds. City's case spans nine seasons and, on the league's own charge sheet, a pattern of concealment rather than an accounting overshoot — which is why lawyers on both sides expect the sanctions phase to look for a penalty with no real comparator.

There is also the question of what happens to the trophies won during the period in question — three league titles among them. No mechanism in the Premier League's rulebook currently allows for stripping a title after the fact, and nobody involved has suggested that will happen imminently. But the phrase now attached to City's decade of dominance, repeated by rival executives and pundits alike, is that every honour from that period now carries an asterisk.

City heads to appeal, again

City's position, laid out in a statement pointing back to its original February 2023 denial, is that "the Premier League process remains ongoing, with significant elements to be completed, and subject to strict confidentiality." Translated: this is not over, and the club intends to appeal. It has reason for confidence in that forum. City's legal team is understood to again be led by Lord Pannick KC, the barrister who successfully overturned the club's two-year Champions League ban imposed by UEFA in 2020 — a result that still shapes how seriously rivals take City's ability to win on procedure even after losing on substance.

Pep Guardiola, who managed City throughout the period under scrutiny, has maintained for years that the club would be vindicated, framing the process at one point as a case of the other nineteen clubs "accusing us without us having the ability to defend" ourselves. Former City midfielder Rodri, reacting to the verdict, said simply that he trusted in the club he knew well and hoped "justice can be done" — a reminder that for the players who built the trophy haul, the case is now inseparable from their own legacy.

Rivals start counting what they might claim

The more consequential fallout may be the one furthest from the pitch. In 2024, with the verdict already anticipated, Arsenal, Liverpool, Manchester United and Tottenham served legal notices on City reserving the right to seek compensation should the club be found guilty of serious breaches. That reservation has now matured into something closer to a live claim: rival clubs are taking legal advice on what a decade of alleged financial advantage cost them in prize money, sponsorship, and Champions League qualification, and several are expected to file individual claims rather than pursue a joint action.

That is the mechanism that turns a regulatory ruling into a genuinely open-ended financial liability for City, regardless of what sanction the commission eventually settles on. A points deduction is a known, bounded cost. A wave of individual compensation suits from four of the biggest clubs in the country, each pricing its own lost revenue over nine seasons, is not — and it could run in the courts for years after the Premier League's own case is closed.

What's next

The immediate catalyst is the sanctions hearing, where the same commission will decide what City's punishment actually is — the number that turns Friday's finding from a legal abstraction into a concrete cost. City's appeal, once lodged, will run in parallel and could stretch well into 2027 given the scale of the case. Watch, too, for the first individual compensation claim to be filed by a rival club: that will set the template, and likely the price tag, for the others to follow.
