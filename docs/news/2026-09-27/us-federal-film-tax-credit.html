Trump pushes federal film and TV production tax credit

The Hollywood sign overlooking Los Angeles, the industry hub competing with states like Georgia and countries like Canada for production dollars. — Thomas Wolf, www.foto-tw.de / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
Trump backs a federal film tax credit industry groups say could add 140,000 jobs a year, but no bill, cost estimate, or structure exists yet.

Donald Trump has never been shy about Hollywood's decline. In late August he took to Truth Social to declare the industry a "Complete and Total Disaster," lamenting that production had "moved to Canada, and other Countries, with very little work being done anymore in the United States." What followed, though, was not another tariff threat — it was an invitation. "I am going to suggest that Republicans and Democrats get together, and immediately craft Legislation to save the Movie, Television, and Entertainment Business in America," he wrote, calling on Congress to "approve, immediately, a Federal Production Incentive to create Entertainment Jobs in America."

That single post has done more to unite Hollywood's fractious lobby than anything in years. IATSE, the crew union with roughly 180,000 members, welcomed the endorsement. So did SAG-AFTRA. A newly formed U.S. Film and Television Production Coalition — an alliance of the Motion Picture Association, the Writers Guild, the DGA, IATSE, the Teamsters and actor Jon Voight, who has served as one of Trump's informal Hollywood ambassadors — has been pushing exactly this idea for more than a year.

The pitch: 140,000 jobs, a quarter-trillion dollars

The industry's case leans on a study commissioned by the MPA and conducted by the consultancy Olsberg SPI, released this month. It argues a 20% federal production credit would generate roughly 143,500 full-time-equivalent jobs a year, add $125.3 billion in direct U.S. production spending between 2027 and 2035, and contribute $249.1 billion in total added value to the economy — effectively doubling domestic production volume by 2035. Proposed rates have ranged from Voight's original 20-25% pitch to a $7.5 billion-a-year federal program floated separately by California Governor Gavin Newsom, and a 15-20% range favored by Senator Adam Schiff. None of these are competing bills so much as competing opening offers; no legislation has been introduced, no committee has scheduled a markup, and the White House has not attached a cost estimate or a mechanism — refundable credit, direct grant, tax deduction — to Trump's post.

Why the states already tried this

The pitch also arrives with an inconvenient case study already running: state film credits. Thirty-nine states now offer some version of one, and Georgia — the most aggressive — issued more than $1 billion in film tax credits last year, a figure state auditors expect to climb toward $1.28 billion by 2028. Georgia's own audit found the program returns about 19 cents in economic benefit for every dollar of credit issued. New York's analysis puts its return at roughly 15 cents per dollar. A widely cited 2010 study from the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities found the median state subsidy was worth 25 cents for every dollar of subsidized production spending, and concluded flatly that the credits don't pay for themselves — the tax revenue generated by the activity they induce falls well short of their direct cost. Economists across the political spectrum have reached similar conclusions for over a decade: incentives shift where a shoot happens far more reliably than they create durable new economic activity, and states competing against each other for a fixed pool of production mostly transfer profit to studios rather than building lasting industries.

What's actually missing

Strip away the advocacy numbers and what's left is a presidential social media post, an industry-funded study, and a handful of rival rate proposals with no shared bill number. There is no text of legislation, no Congressional Budget Office score, no indication of how a federal credit would interact with the state credits already in place, and no word on whether streamers and studios — Netflix, Paramount, Disney, Warner Bros. Discovery — would see it as pure upside or as one more offset against other tax provisions under negotiation in Washington. For a policy pitched as a jobs program, the arithmetic is currently one-sided: the benefits are quantified by the industry that stands to receive the credit, while the costs are not quantified by anyone.

The next marker to watch is whether any lawmaker actually files a bill translating Trump's post into a text with a rate, a cap and a revenue estimate attached — until then, this is a lobbying campaign with a presidential co-sign, not a policy.
