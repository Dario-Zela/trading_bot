Rayner stands by attack on care-worker visa reforms as Labour MPs revolt

Deputy Prime Minister Angela Rayner has repeatedly called the Home Office's care-worker visa plan "un-British." — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Rayner won't back down from calling Labour's care-worker visa plan "un-British" as 100+ MPs revolt and Burnham wavers.

Angela Rayner spent years as a care worker for Stockport council before she was a politician — the job that took her into a Unison branch office and, eventually, into Parliament. It gives a certain edge to her current fight with her own government over how long foreign care workers should have to wait for the right to stay in Britain.

Rayner, now Deputy Prime Minister, has told The Times she stands by her earlier verdict on Home Office plans to lengthen settlement waits: "un-British." The reforms, driven by Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, would roughly double the standard wait for permanent residency from five years to ten. Workers who arrived on health and social care visas would face fifteen years before they could settle; anyone claiming benefits for more than twelve months in that window would wait twenty. Crucially, the change would apply retrospectively — to people who moved to Britain, in many cases in 2021 or after, on the understanding that five years' residence would be enough.

About 180,000 foreign care workers arrived in Britain between 2022 and 2024, most of them recruited under the last Conservative government to plug gaps in a social care system that has struggled for a decade to hold on to staff. Under existing rules, many would qualify for settlement from 2027. Mahmood's proposal would push that date out by another decade for anyone on a care visa.

"Rip up the rules halfway through"

Rayner has made the retrospective element her central objection, telling a Unison rally that "care workers helped us through the darkest days of the pandemic" and that changing the terms on people already living and paying tax in Britain amounts to moving the goalposts. "We shouldn't rip up the rules halfway through — taking retrospective action on people who are taxpayers," she said, distinguishing that from ordinary border control, which she called "completely different."

She is not a lone voice. More than 100 Labour MPs and a clutch of trade unions now oppose the plan, up from around 40 who signed a letter earlier in the year branding it "un-British" and accusatory of "moving the goalposts." That is a large slice of the parliamentary party willing to defy a Home Office policy that Mahmood has told the Commons home affairs committee she intends to finalise "very soon."

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who took over from Keir Starmer in July, inherited the row along with the job. He reportedly sided with Rayner's concerns back in March, before shifting toward Mahmood's "broad thrust" during the Makerfield by-election campaign — a wobble that has done little to settle nerves on his backbenches. He is now said to be weighing an exemption for care workers already in the country, a compromise that would blunt the retrospective sting without abandoning the headline policy.

The dispute lands just as Labour gathers for its autumn conference, an awkward backdrop for a government barely two months into Burnham's leadership. Mahmood's promised decision, whenever it lands, will be the real test of whether more than 100 restless MPs were making noise or making a threat.
