BEYOND THE TAPE · VATICAN · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-09-27 · 3 min read

Pope arrives in Lourdes to meet abuse survivors and EU leaders

Pope Leo XIV, whose four-day France tour has taken him from record Paris crowds to a private meeting with abuse survivors in Lourdes. — Edgar Beltrán / The Pillar, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Pope Leo XIV met seven French abuse survivors in Lourdes and heads to Metz to address EU leaders, capping a France tour that drew 700,000-plus to Paris.

There is a particular kind of silence that settles over a papal meeting with abuse survivors — no cameras inside the room, no transcript, just a Vatican statement afterward confirming it happened. That silence fell again on Sunday in Lourdes, where Pope Leo XIV sat privately with seven survivors of clergy sexual abuse, the most sensitive stop on a four-day tour of France that began with one of the largest crowds any pope has drawn in years.

The contrast is the story. On Saturday, an estimated 700,000 to 800,000 people packed the Place de la Concorde in Paris for an open-air Mass, having watched the American-born pope ride the length of the Champs-Élysées in his popemobile the day before. He had already made history simply by walking into Notre-Dame — the first pope to set foot in the cathedral since fire gutted it in 2019 — to lead vespers before the crowds arrived. Twenty-four hours later, in the grotto town where Bernadette Soubirous said she saw visions of the Virgin Mary in 1858, the tone turned inward and considerably more difficult.

A meeting nobody agreed on

The seven survivors, drawn from Catholic schools, parishes and religious congregations across France, said beforehand that they did not claim to speak for anyone but themselves. "Engaging in dialogue does not mean endorsing," they said in a joint statement. "Being received does not mean abandoning our demands." That caveat mattered, because the meeting was contested before it even took place. Marie Derain de Vaucresson, who heads INIRR, the body set up to compensate French abuse victims, called it "a missed opportunity," arguing the church's message should have been delivered more collectively. Other survivors wrote an open letter on the day Leo landed in Paris, objecting that the seven had been chosen by bishops and church institutions rather than by victims' own organisations.

The underlying numbers explain why the selection process draws such scrutiny. A 2021 independent commission found that some 330,000 children in France had been sexually abused over seven decades by priests, clergy and church volunteers — a reckoning the French church is still absorbing. Leo held a similar closed-door session with six survivors in Madrid in June, and has said he intends to weigh their suggestions on reform, though he has offered no specific policy commitments from either meeting.

Diplomacy after the reckoning

From Lourdes, Leo moves on to Metz on Monday for the trip's final and more overtly political stop: an interreligious gathering followed by an address titled "To the Roots of Europe, for Peace and Unity," delivered at the Robert Schuman Convention Center in the city where European integration's founding figure was born. President Emmanuel Macron has invited leaders from all 27 EU states, with Ukraine's Volodymyr Zelensky among those reportedly weighing attendance — a reminder that Leo's tour is also an exercise in the Vatican's diplomatic reach at a moment of renewed war anxiety on the continent.

None of this moves markets, and it isn't meant to. What it tests is whether a church still working through the largest institutional trauma in its modern history can hold both threads at once — packed boulevards on one day, a quiet room with seven people who were harmed by it on the next — without either overshadowing the other. The next marker will be whether the French bishops' conference responds to Sunday's criticism with anything more concrete than acknowledgement, and whether Metz produces anything beyond a speech.
