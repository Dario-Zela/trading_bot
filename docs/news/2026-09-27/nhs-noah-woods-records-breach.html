NHS trust apologises after staff snooped on murdered toddler's medical file

Colchester General Hospital, run by East Suffolk and North Essex NHS Foundation Trust, which apologised after staff accessed a child patient's file without authorisation. — Elshad / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Ten ESNEFT staff were removed from duty for snooping on three-year-old Noah Woods' medical file — two months after NHS England's own zero-tolerance crackdown on the practice.

Noah Woods was three years old. On 15 September he slipped through a gap in a fence at a playground in Merriam Close, Brantham, Suffolk, and became separated from a relative. More than 1,300 people joined the search that followed. His body was recovered from Decoy Pond the next day by the Metropolitan Police dive team. Suffolk Constabulary has said the death is not being treated as suspicious — a distinction worth stating plainly, since coverage of the case has at times slid toward calling it a killing. An inquest has opened and remains ongoing.[^1] What is established, and confirmed directly by the trust involved, is the second part of this story: while Noah's family grieved under national media attention, ten employees of East Suffolk and North Essex NHS Foundation Trust (ESNEFT) looked at his medical file without authorisation to do so.

The trust disclosed the breach on 26 September, saying the ten staff have been removed from duty while an internal investigation runs. Dr Martin Mansfield, ESNEFT's deputy chief medical officer and Caldicott Guardian — the senior clinician responsible for safeguarding patient confidentiality — called the access "completely unacceptable" and said the trust understood "the significant distress it can cause patients and their families."[^2] ESNEFT has apologised "unreservedly" to Noah's family, secured the record against further access, reported the incident to the Information Commissioner's Office, and said disciplinary action will follow where the investigation supports it.

A pattern the sackings haven't broken

The case lands two months after NHS England issued what it billed as a zero-tolerance crackdown on record-snooping. In July, chief executive Sir Jim Mackey warned staff they faced dismissal or prosecution for looking at patient data out of curiosity, calling it "wholly unacceptable, a disgraceful breach of patient trust and against the law." That campaign followed its own trigger cases: staff dismissed for accessing the records of victims of the 2023 Nottingham attacks, and up to 40 employees reportedly viewing the file of a child injured in a crocodile enclosure.[^3]

The enforcement record behind these warnings is thin. Section 170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 makes unlawful access a criminal offence, but prosecutions have typically ended in fines of a few hundred to a couple of thousand pounds — a GP practice manager was fined £1,345 in 2013 for illegally viewing records of nearly 2,000 patients.[^4] A comparable case at University Hospitals Bristol this year found 38 people had viewed a deceased 18-year-old patient's file 637 times since 2016, with one doctor self-referring to the General Medical Council.[^5] Kate Middleton's 2024 treatment at the London Clinic produced a police referral after staff reportedly tried to access her notes, though no outcome has been made public.

What recurs across these cases is not the absence of policy — access logs, audit trails and disciplinary frameworks already exist — but the gap between a high-profile file being flagged as sensitive and staff choosing to look anyway. ESNEFT's investigation, and any ICO enforcement that follows, will test whether this incident ends in more than removals from duty.
