Vučić resigns presidency to seek power as PM in snap October election

Aleksandar Vučić, who resigned Serbia's presidency to run for prime minister in the October 25 snap election. — Duma.gov.ru / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Vučić resigned Serbia's presidency to dodge term limits, aiming to return as prime minister in an October 25 election shadowed by two years of protests.

Aleksandar Vučić resigned as president of Serbia on Sunday, ending a rule that began before most of the students now marching against him were born. He did not go quietly, and he did not go far. Within hours his party had confirmed what protesters had predicted for months: he intends to return to power next month as prime minister, running at the head of a list bluntly named "Aleksandar Vučić — United Serbia" in a snap parliamentary election he has called for October 25.

The timing is not coincidental. Serbia's constitution limits presidents to two terms, and Vučić — in office since 2017, and effectively running the country as prime minister for three years before that — has exhausted his. Rather than hand over the office to a successor of his own choosing and retire from front-line politics, he is switching seats. The presidency, once occupied, will pass to the speaker of the National Assembly on a caretaker basis until a new presidential vote, which must happen by 1 May 2027. The premiership, if his party wins in October, would pass to him.

A resignation with an asterisk

Serbian officials have been careful to frame the move as Vučić bowing to public pressure. It follows close to two years of street protests — the largest since the fall of Slobodan Milošević in 2000 — triggered by the collapse of a railway station canopy in Novi Sad on 1 November 2024, which killed 16 people. What began as student-led grief over apparent corruption in a Chinese-linked renovation contract widened into a broader indictment of Vučić's governing style: media capture, patronage networks, and a security apparatus protesters accuse of beating and detaining demonstrators. A rally in Belgrade last December drew more than 100,000 people, by some estimates the largest gathering in the country's modern history.

Vučić first promised to resign back in June, and protesters did not believe him then either. "Whilst in the past, he has used elections to re-legitimize himself and the SNS, this time, the party is not certain that it can actually win," said Markus Kaiser of the Friedrich Naumann Foundation, who has called the resignation-and-re-election maneuver a "charade." Political scientist Dušan Spasojević put the mechanics more plainly: "His possible move to the position of prime minister would simply place him, constitutionally, where he already is — the country's key political figure."

The presidency in Serbia is largely ceremonial on paper but has functioned, under Vučić, as the command center of the state — chairing the security council, dominating foreign policy, and serving as final word inside his Serbian Progressive Party (SNS). The premiership carries the formal executive authority he would need to keep governing without the term-limit problem. Analysts across the political spectrum read the sequence the same way: not a concession, but a reshuffle designed to look like one.

The students on the ballot

What makes October's vote different from the parliamentary elections Vučić has won comfortably since 2012 is who is standing against him. The movement that grew out of the Novi Sad protests has formalized into an electoral bloc — the Student List — and its members marched into the race in Belgrade with the kind of unguarded relief that comes from finally getting the fight they'd been demanding. A Faktor Plus poll taken 16–20 September put the SNS list at 48.6% against the Student List's 37.1%, a real contest by the standards of an SNS that has typically run up margins closer to two-to-one.

The backdrop has not helped Vučić's case that Serbia is moving toward, rather than away from, European norms. Only weeks before his resignation, senior Serbian officials attended the funeral of Ratko Mladić, the Bosnian Serb general convicted of genocide for the Srebrenica massacre, who was given military honors and buried as a national hero. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President Antonio Costa issued near-identical statements calling the scenes "shocking." Brussels' annual progress report on Serbia's candidacy — due in late October, days after the election — will land against that backdrop.

What the vote settles, and what it doesn't

Serbia has been an EU candidate since 2012 with little to show for it; accession talks have stalled repeatedly over media freedom, judicial independence, and, now, the government's tolerance for glorifying a convicted war criminal. A Vučić-led parliament returning him to the premiership would not, by itself, change any of that — it would confirm to Brussels that the man they have been negotiating with for over a decade intends to keep negotiating, from a different chair, indefinitely.

For the protest movement, the immediate test is turnout on 25 October, not the presidency Vučić is vacating. The caretaker succession — the National Assembly speaker holding the office until a presidential vote due by May 2027 — is widely expected to go to a Vučić loyalist; names floated inside SNS include former prime minister Ana Brnabić and Miloš Vučević. Whether that arrangement holds depends less on constitutional mechanics than on whether the numbers in the Faktor Plus poll — a 37% opposition bloc that didn't exist in this form eighteen months ago — translate into an actual parliamentary majority against him. If they don't, Vučić exits one office to occupy the one he wanted more, having spent a resignation to buy back a decade.
