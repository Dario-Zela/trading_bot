Heathrow warns third runway could slip to 2039

Heathrow Airport's Terminal 3, seen from the air — the airport says its third runway now won't open until 2039. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Heathrow tells the government its £33bn third runway won't open until 2039, four years later than the 2035 deadline set last year.

Heathrow has told the government that the third runway it promised for 2035 will not be ready until at least 2039 — a four-year slip on a project already decades in the making, and a fresh test of how much faith markets should place in the delivery dates attached to Britain's biggest infrastructure schemes.

The airport's board has informed Prime Minister Andy Burnham that the 2035 target, set by Rachel Reeves as chancellor last January, is not achievable. Heathrow's revised "core scenario" now aims for planning permission by 2029, with the runway opening "within a decade" of that approval — pushing the realistic completion date to the end of the next decade, assuming no legal challenges along the way. A Department for Transport spokesperson acknowledged the original timeline had "always been ambitious" and said the government would keep working with stakeholders "at pace," a form of words that concedes the slippage without quite owning it.

A £33bn runway inside a £49bn programme

The third runway itself is costed at roughly £33 billion, sitting inside a wider £49 billion Heathrow expansion that also covers a new terminal and a rerouted M25 through a tunnel between junctions 14 and 15. Those figures have moved before and will likely move again — mega-project budgets tend to swell rather than shrink — and a four-year delay on the headline date is the kind of early warning sign that has preceded cost overruns on comparable UK schemes, from HS2 to Hinkley Point C.

Heathrow is also using the delay as leverage. The airport has warned it will "pull the plug" on the whole expansion if the Civil Aviation Authority does not approve higher passenger charges to help fund it — a rise from an average £26.57 per passenger to £33.80 between 2027 and 2031, well above the roughly £22.27 average at comparable European hubs. The regulator is reportedly leaning toward approving the increase alongside safeguards against cost overruns, which would shift more of the project's financial risk onto airlines and, ultimately, passengers rather than Heathrow's owners.

Why traders should care

For construction firms with UK infrastructure exposure — Balfour Beatty and Kier among them — a later start date means revenue recognition on any eventual Heathrow work pushes further out, even before contracts are formally awarded through the project's logistics-hub supply chain. For gilt markets and government-spending credibility more broadly, this is another data point alongside HS2's cost blowouts: British mega-projects keep landing later and pricier than announced, which erodes confidence in headline delivery dates used to justify capital spending. Watch for the CAA's ruling on Heathrow's fee request, the Department for Transport's draft Airports National Policy Statement, and Parliament's expected decision this autumn on the planning framework — each is a concrete point where the 2039 date could harden or slip again.
