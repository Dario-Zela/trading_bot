UK gilt yields hold near 19-year highs ahead of autumn Budget

Chancellor John Healey, who has called UK borrowing costs "historic highs" ahead of the 28 October Budget. — Richard Townshend / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
UK gilt yields near 19-year highs are eating Chancellor Healey's Budget headroom before he even stands up on 28 October.

Britain is borrowing at prices last seen when the iPhone was new. The 10-year gilt yield stood at 5.35% on Friday and the 30-year at 5.90% — both hovering near levels not touched since 2007 — and the persistence is the story as much as the level. This is not a one-day spike that reverses by lunchtime; it is a market that has spent weeks marking UK debt down and shows no urgency to mark it back up.

The proximate trigger was global, not domestic: Brent crude pushed above $100 a barrel in early September as the naval standoff around the Strait of Hormuz dragged on, and bond markets from London to Tokyo repriced for stickier inflation. But gilts have underperformed peers even within that selloff, which points to a UK-specific worry sitting on top of the global one — a Treasury that already runs one of the highest debt loads in the G7, close to 100% of GDP, walking into a Budget with less room than it had in the spring.

The arithmetic tightens

Chancellor John Healey, delivering his first major speech in the role in Coventry on 7 September, called UK borrowing costs "historic highs" and declined to rule out tax rises beyond the manifesto commitments on income tax, VAT and National Insurance. "If I respond to those questions, I'm only going to fuel more speculation," he told reporters — a standard dodge, but one that markets are reading as confirmation rather than denial.

The reason for the caution is mechanical. Every basis point added to the gilt curve raises the government's projected debt-interest bill and eats into the fiscal headroom the Office for Budget Responsibility will certify against Healey's own rules. The Resolution Foundation puts that headroom as low as £7-10 billion, down from the roughly £23 billion Healey inherited; other estimates land nearer £13 billion. Either way, the cushion has roughly halved since midsummer, driven almost entirely by the bond market rather than any policy choice.

Lord Jim O'Neill, a former Treasury minister, described one single-day, quarter-point jump earlier this month as something markets "had not had... since Liz Truss days" — a comparison every UK Chancellor now flinches at, fairly or not, whenever gilts move sharply.

What the Budget can and can't fix

With yields set largely by global rate expectations and oil, the 28 October Budget cannot simply talk borrowing costs back down. Healey's real choices are narrower: raise taxes, trim spending, or let the deficit run wider and risk feeding the very yield pressure squeezing him. Markets will parse Budget day less for the headline numbers than for whether the OBR's forecast holds up against a gilt curve that has already moved against it — the next test arrives when the OBR locks its yield assumptions in the days before the statement.
