Lagarde says Middle East energy shock delays ECB's 2% inflation return to 2027

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where the Governing Council has raised rates twice since the Iran war began. — Daniel Vorndran (User:DXR) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Lagarde says the Iran war's energy shock pushes the ECB's 2% inflation target out to late 2027, after a second rate hike to 2.5%.

Christine Lagarde has told the European Central Bank's own timeline what markets had already priced in: the war around the Strait of Hormuz has broken the ECB's inflation forecast, and there is no quick fix. In a speech earlier this month, the ECB president said headline eurozone inflation would stay above the bank's 2% target through the first half of 2027, with a return to target only "towards the end of 2027" — a full year later than policymakers were still projecting in the spring.

The culprit, in Lagarde's own framing, is not a domestic wage-price spiral or a credit boom. It is oil and gas, moving through the economy from a conflict the ECB has no lever over.

A shock that keeps compounding

Euro-area headline inflation ran at 3.3% in August, with the energy component alone up 14.3% year-on-year — a number that would have been unthinkable in the ECB's 2024-era forecasts, when the bank was still congratulating itself on taming the post-pandemic surge. The proximate cause is the naval standoff around the Strait of Hormuz, where roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil has to pass. Iranian attacks on tankers this year triggered a US blockade of Iranian ports; Brent crude, which spent much of 2025 in the $70s, has swung as high as $106 a barrel in recent weeks as talks over reopening the strait have stalled.

That stalling has a name attached to it in this edition: Donald Trump's rejection of Tehran's latest offer to reopen Hormuz shipping lanes, which keeps the blockade — and the price pressure — in place rather than easing it. Lagarde has also pointed to a second, less-discussed driver: strikes on Russian refining infrastructure, which have curtailed diesel and refined product exports on top of the crude disruption.

Her description of the combination has been blunt. "The current shock is longer-lasting," she said, contrasting it with the sharper but shorter energy spikes the ECB dealt with in 2022. Downside risks to the outlook, she added, now run through "the Middle East war" and Russia's war on Ukraine simultaneously — two fronts, one transmission channel.

The numbers behind the 2027 call

The ECB's own scenario analysis shows why Lagarde is hedging rather than committing to a date. In its baseline, 2026 inflation lands around 3.0%, easing to roughly 2.5% in 2027 and closer to 2.1% by 2028 — a slow-motion glide back to target rather than a clean landing. In a more adverse scenario, in which Hormuz disruptions run longer and deeper, the ECB has modelled headline inflation as high as 3.5% in 2026; in a severe scenario, where energy prices stay elevated for an extended period, that figure rises to 4.4%.

Those brackets matter because they show the ECB treating the war as a genuine unknown rather than a shock it can forecast through. "The energy shock could intensify further," Lagarde warned, noting it could feed into core inflation and wages with more force than the bank currently assumes — the mechanism by which an external, geopolitical price shock stops being temporary and starts looking like the kind of embedded inflation central banks exist to fight.

The policy response so far has been to hike, not cut. The Governing Council raised the deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.5% on 10 September — its second increase since the war began — and Lagarde called the move a "no-brainer" given the inflation trajectory. Bundesbank president Joachim Nagel has gone further, suggesting rates may need to move into "mildly restrictive" territory to keep the shock from bleeding into expectations.

What it means for rates and the euro

The market reaction to all this has been oddly quiet. EUR/USD slipped modestly below 1.1600 on the rate decision before recovering to hold near 1.1610 — a move dwarfed by what the dollar side of the pair is doing around the Federal Reserve's own meetings. That muted response reflects a policy picture that is, for now, coherent rather than confusing: the ECB is hiking into an energy shock while the Fed holds and lets futures markets price in a steeper tightening path of its own, meaning the rate differential that usually drives EUR/USD hasn't moved much even as both central banks reprice their outlooks upward.

Lagarde has been careful to decouple the rate path from the oil price directly. "Interest rates do not move in lockstep with the price of energy," she said after the September decision, arguing the Governing Council weighs the growth and demand effects of expensive energy alongside the inflation effects — a reminder that a further Brent spike does not mechanically produce another hike. The eurozone economy has, so far, proven more resilient than the ECB expected, with unemployment low even as job creation slows, which gives the Council room to keep tightening without immediately triggering a recession call.

For traders positioning around the delayed target, the practical upshot is that eurozone rates have more room to rise, or at least less room to fall, than the pre-war consensus assumed — and that the ECB has explicitly signalled it will tolerate an above-target overshoot into 2027 rather than force disinflation through recession, so long as the overshoot's source remains external and energy-driven.

The next test of that stance comes on 29 October, when the Governing Council meets again in Frankfurt. Whether Lagarde has to revise the 2027 timeline further will depend less on eurozone data than on events in the Gulf — specifically, whether Washington and Tehran can find the phased arrangement over Hormuz access that negotiators have been circling, or whether the blockade Trump just declined to ease drags oil, and the ECB's forecast, further into 2027.
