ShinyHunters hackers leak FBI agents' medical data in extortion-free breach

The J. Edgar Hoover Building, FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C., where the bureau confirmed it is investigating the ShinyHunters breach claims. — ajay_suresh / Georgfotoart, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Hackers who breached FBI recruitment and HR systems are leaking agents' medical and psychiatric records, demanding the bureau retract a May advisory rather than pay a ransom.

Sometime Monday night, a hacking crew found a hole in Oracle's PeopleSoft software — the kind of unglamorous back-office system that runs human-resources databases at thousands of institutions — and used it to walk into the FBI's own recruitment site. By Friday, the bureau had confirmed what the hackers were already boasting about: someone had gotten inside, and they had walked out with medical files belonging to FBI agents.

The group behind it, ShinyHunters, says it took two to three terabytes of data from apply.fbijobs.gov and several internal systems, including one called MedLink that stores agency personnel medical records, plus human-resources and background-investigation databases. Samples shared with reporters, including 404 Media co-founder Joseph Cox, contained doctors' notes on agents' "fitness for work" — conditions ranging from a shellfish allergy to high cholesterol to blood in urine — alongside psychiatric evaluations and personal data on roughly 5,000 bureau employees and applicants: names, home addresses, phone numbers, details about spouses and siblings.

What makes this breach unusual isn't the sophistication — PeopleSoft flaws and cloud lateral movement are well-worn techniques — but the ask. ShinyHunters didn't post a ransom note. It posted a grievance.

A demand with no dollar sign

On May 15, the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center had published a public advisory describing ShinyHunters' playbook against a learning-management platform, Canvas: stolen credentials, abuse of trusted vendor relationships, and — the line that apparently stung — a pattern of harassment, threatening calls, swatting, and sometimes exaggerating how much access it actually had. A companion FLASH bulletin eight days earlier had sketched the group's wider ecosystem in similar terms.

ShinyHunters disputed all of it. When it went public with the FBI intrusion, the group said the breach was retaliation for that characterization and gave the bureau roughly a week to retract the advisory, insisting — despite sitting on agents' psychiatric and medical files — that the operation "is NOT financially motivated." It told the tech outlet The Register it had hacked the FBI "to protect our business."

The FBI's answer, in effect, has been silence. A spokesperson confirmed only that the bureau was "aware of claims regarding unauthorized activity affecting FBIjobs.gov and is currently investigating." It has given no indication it will touch the May advisory. That refusal has its own logic: retracting a threat bulletin because the group it describes objects to the description would hand every extortion crew a template — hack a regulator, demand it rewrite its own warnings. By midweek, ShinyHunters had quietly deleted its ultimatum from its leak site, saying it would not comment on what happens if the FBI doesn't comply.

Who's doing the asking

ShinyHunters is less a fixed group than a brand shared across a loose alliance — sometimes called Scattered Lapsus$ Hunters — that also claims the Scattered Spider and Lapsus$ names, and which Google's threat-intelligence unit tracks under a scatter of internal codenames rather than one organization chart. It's the outfit behind the 2024 Snowflake-linked breaches that hit Ticketmaster, AT&T and Santander, and the crew that leaked Rockstar Games' unreleased Grand Theft Auto VI footage. French police arrested four alleged affiliates in June 2025; researchers said at the time that the arrests barely dented the operation, since the people picked up were affiliates, not the core.

That history matters for reading this breach correctly. A group that has spent two years running financially motivated smash-and-grabs against corporate cloud tenants chose, this time, to skip the ransom demand entirely and go after the reputational and institutional standing of the one federal agency positioned to write the official narrative about it. Medical records are miserable things to leak — deeply personal, hard to explain away, impossible for victims to change like a password — which is precisely why they make effective leverage even without a wire-transfer address attached.

Why a policy demand travels further than a ransom note

Most extortion operations end where the money changes hands or doesn't; the story resolves. A demand for institutional retraction doesn't resolve the same way — there's no invoice to pay, no clean exit. It's aimed instead at reputational cost: making an agency choose between two bad options, cave and invite a wave of copycat demands, or hold firm and watch the leaks continue.

The technique that got ShinyHunters in — an unpatched flaw in enterprise HR software, followed by lateral movement into cloud infrastructure — is not exotic. PeopleSoft and comparable back-office platforms sit inside banks, hospitals, insurers and market regulators, not just federal law enforcement. If a policy-leverage extortion model proves as workable against the FBI as a payment demand has proven against private companies, it travels easily to a securities regulator, a central bank's IT contractor, or a hospital system's records vendor — anywhere an institution has both sensitive data and a public position worth pressuring.

The FBI says its investigation continues, and it has not disclosed a timeline for confirming ShinyHunters' full data set or notifying every affected employee. The advisory stays up. Whether it stays up because the bureau judged the underlying warning accurate, or because backing down looked worse than the leak itself, is the question likely to shape how the next agency responds when someone tries this again.
