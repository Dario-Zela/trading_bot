Saudi Arabia leads seven-nation OPEC+ group in 188,000 b/d output hike

Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman, Saudi Arabia's energy minister, who has steered the kingdom's OPEC+ output strategy through five straight monthly increases. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Seven OPEC+ producers led by Saudi Arabia and Russia will add 188,000 b/d from August, the fifth straight monthly increase even as Hormuz shipping remains disrupted.

Seven of the world's largest oil producers agreed over the weekend to pump another 188,000 barrels a day into the market starting in August, the fifth straight month the group has chosen to open the taps rather than hold the line on output. Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman — a bloc that has spent much of the past decade arguing over quotas — signed off on the increase in a virtual meeting, extending a gradual unwind of the production cuts they first imposed in 2023.

The headline number is identical to the increase approved for July, and to several months before it. That repetition is the point: OPEC+ has settled into a metronomic pace of roughly 188,000 b/d a month, using it as a release valve rather than a lever it yanks around unpredictably.

Who gets what

The increase is parcelled out by formula, not by whim. Saudi Arabia and Russia each add 62,000 b/d — the two largest producers in the group and, not coincidentally, the two that shoulder the largest cuts when the group tightens supply. Iraq adds 26,000 b/d, Kuwait 16,000, Kazakhstan 10,000, Algeria 6,000 and Oman 5,000. The proportions track each country's share of the roughly 1.65 million b/d layer of voluntary cuts these seven producers (plus the United Arab Emirates, which follows its own schedule) imposed in November 2023 to prop up prices.

In a statement, the group said it would "continue to closely monitor and assess market conditions" and reaffirmed "the importance of adopting a cautious approach and retaining full flexibility to increase, pause or reverse" the phase-out — language that has accompanied every one of these monthly increases and functions as an escape hatch should demand falter or prices fall too far.

A market still finding its footing

The timing matters. The decision lands as the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint that carried roughly one-fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas before this year's US-Israel-Iran conflict — is still only fitfully reopening under a ceasefire reached June 17. Tanker traffic through the strait has climbed from a wartime trickle to around 30-plus transits a day, but that remains far short of the 130-plus daily crossings seen before the fighting, and vessels have continued to turn back along the Omani-side route as Iran presses to assert control over which ships pass.

Against that backdrop, adding barrels is as much a statement of confidence as a commercial decision. Brent crude slipped 24 cents to $71.88 a barrel on the news, and West Texas Intermediate eased to $68.58, as traders read the increase as evidence that the group sees enough spare capacity — and expects enough demand — to keep supplying the market even with Gulf shipping routes not fully back to normal. The war premium that had propped up prices through the spring has largely evaporated; OPEC+ adding supply into that environment, rather than pausing to let prices stabilize, suggests the producers are betting the drop reflects fading risk rather than a demand problem.

Why it reaches beyond the oil desk

OPEC+ supply decisions filter into inflation math faster than almost any other commodity input, because energy costs sit near the front of the consumer price basket and feed into everything from airline fares to fertilizer. A seven-country group — Saudi Arabia and Russia chief among them — agreeing in lockstep to add supply, even a modest 188,000 b/d, tends to cap the upside case for oil-driven inflation surprises that central banks would otherwise have to price into rate decisions. It also matters for producer budgets: Saudi Arabia's government finances assume an oil price well above where Brent now sits, and each additional barrel sold at a lower price is a trade-off between market share and revenue that Riyadh has been willing to make repeatedly this year.

The next test comes August 2, when the group reconvenes to decide whether a sixth straight increase follows the same script. Whether it does will hinge on two things that are still unsettled: how durable the Hormuz ceasefire proves, and whether global demand — particularly out of Asia — absorbs the extra barrels without prices sliding much further.
