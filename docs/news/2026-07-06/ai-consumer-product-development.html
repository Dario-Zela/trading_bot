From shampoo to cookies, consumer brands turn to AI for faster product development

L'Oréal's headquarters extension in Clichy, France, where the company has centred much of its AI-driven research push. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
L'Oréal says AI now lets it repurpose skincare molecules into new products four times faster — but rivals are racing to copy the same trick.

A shampoo bottle does not look like a skincare serum, and until recently, the chemists who made one rarely had much to say to the chemists who made the other. Different brands, different labs, often different buildings. That wall is coming down at L'Oréal, and artificial intelligence is the reason.

The French cosmetics group has been using AI to comb through its own library of skincare molecules — compounds it developed and tested for creams and serums — and identify which ones could plausibly work in a shampoo, a conditioner, or a body lotion instead of sitting unused in a database. The company's consumer products division president told Reuters that the approach has let L'Oréal move from idea to shelf about four times faster than under its old process. One of the products to come out of it: a shampoo built around a collagen-related molecule first developed for skincare.

"You can really go much faster by imagining new associations of molecules," the executive said, describing a process that used to depend on individual chemists happening to know, or guess, that an ingredient developed for one product line might work in another. AI turns that guesswork into a search problem — pattern-matching molecular structures and performance data against a much larger set of possible uses than any one scientist would think to check.

Four years in the making

L'Oréal says it began folding AI into research and innovation roughly four years ago, and the payoff has shown up in the numbers it is willing to share: over that period, the number of molecules the company tests has roughly tripled, while the time needed to take a formulation from concept to launch has dropped by a factor of four. The company has since gone further, pairing with Nvidia to run molecular simulations — modelling how a candidate ingredient behaves before it is ever mixed in a real lab — on the Alchemi platform, which L'Oréal and Nvidia say can cut some discovery timelines by a factor of 100 for narrower research questions like sun protection and skin-tone treatments. The four-times figure is the broader, product-line number; the 100-times figure describes a specific simulation task, not the average shelf-to-launch cycle.

The timing is not incidental. L'Oréal's growth cooled through 2025 — its luxury division, the biggest contributor to revenue, posted a slight sales decline in the fourth quarter — and the company responded with what it calls a "beauty stimulus plan" built around faster, more frequent product launches. Sales from new products picked up by about 150 basis points as the plan took hold, and by the first quarter of 2026 group sales growth had accelerated to 6.7% on a like-for-like basis, ahead of the broader beauty market. Whether that is AI paying off, a stronger product slate for other reasons, or some mix of both is hard to separate out from the outside — but the company is clearly betting that speed itself is now a lever it can pull.

Cookies, toothpaste, and a crowded field

L'Oréal is not alone, and the pattern in cosmetics — reuse what you already know faster — shows up with different ingredients elsewhere in the aisle. Mondelez, which owns Oreo and Cadbury, has built an AI system that generates and screens recipe concepts for its biscuits and chocolate, reporting that around 60% of the formulations it produces improve on nutrition, sustainability or cost versus what came before; the tool has been used on projects including gluten-free Golden Oreos and a reformulated Chips Ahoy. The company's digital chief described the effect in similar terms to L'Oréal's — compressing what used to take months into weeks, and years into months. Nestlé and Haleon, the maker of Sensodyne, are running comparable AI-assisted ingredient and recipe work of their own.

That convergence is the real story here, more than any single company's number. Every major consumer-goods player facing slower volume growth is reaching for the same class of tool to squeeze more product launches out of the same research budget. The tools themselves — molecule and recipe databases paired with machine learning models that search them faster than a person could — are not proprietary in any deep sense. Nvidia is licensing Alchemi to more than one customer; the AI vendors selling recipe-optimisation software to food companies are not exclusive to Mondelez.

The moat question

Which raises the uncomfortable question sitting underneath all these efficiency claims: if faster product development becomes something every large consumer brand can buy, does it stay an advantage, or does it just become the new cost of staying in business? A four-times speed-up is a genuine operational win if your competitors are still working the old way. It is a much smaller win — arguably just table stakes — once everyone in the category has adopted a version of the same approach and launch cycles across the industry compress in lockstep.

L'Oréal's own numbers hint at this tension without resolving it. Faster product development has coincided with accelerating sales growth, but a faster launch cadence industry-wide could just as easily mean more products competing for the same shelf space and the same consumer attention, squeezing the pricing power that made a molecule worth discovering in the first place. Nothing in what L'Oréal or Mondelez has disclosed yet distinguishes a durable speed advantage from a temporary one that erodes as rivals catch up.

The next test of that will come in earnings, not press interviews. L'Oréal reports half-year results at the end of July, and investors will be watching whether the new-product sales lift it flagged in the first quarter holds up or fades as Estée Lauder, Unilever and other rivals roll out their own AI-accelerated launches. If speed becomes universal within the category, the advantage that mattered in 2026 may simply be the price of admission by 2027.
