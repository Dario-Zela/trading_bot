Shell exits Jiffy Lube, nears $1bn South Africa fuel retail sale

A Shell petrol station in the UK — the retail forecourt business Shell is steadily exiting worldwide — Phil Nash (Rodhullandemu) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Shell completed its $1.3bn Jiffy Lube sale and is finalising a roughly $1bn exit from South African fuel retail, both signs of capital rotating away from consumer forecourts.

Shell has quietly closed one chapter of American car culture and opened talks on another continent's fuel retail map. On July 1, the company completed the $1.3 billion sale of Jiffy Lube International and Premium Velocity Auto to private equity firm Monomoy Capital Partners, ending three decades of ownership of the quick-lube chain. Days earlier, Bloomberg reported Shell was closing in on a roughly $1 billion deal to hand its South African fuel station network to the retail arm of Abu Dhabi's state oil company. Neither transaction moves the needle much on Shell's roughly $190 billion market capitalisation. Together, though, they sketch the shape of a strategy: sell the pumps, keep the barrels.

"Portfolio high-grading by monetising a non-core Lubricants asset" — Shell's own description of the Jiffy Lube sale, in materials accompanying the deal.

An American icon changes hands

Jiffy Lube is the kind of business most drivers know without knowing who owns it — over 2,000 franchised and company-run service centres across the US and Canada, built on the promise of a ten-minute oil change. Shell picked up the brand in the 1990s as its Pennzoil Quaker State subsidiary absorbed the market, and by this year the unit accounted for roughly 6.5% of Shell's US and Canada lubricants volume, according to the company's disclosures.

That volume, and the retail overhead that came with it, no longer fits Shell's model. The sale to Monomoy includes Premium Velocity Auto, Jiffy Lube's second-largest franchisee with more than 360 outlets across 20 states, plus Canadian licensees. Shell keeps the parts of the business with better margins and less storefront exposure: the Pennzoil, Quaker State and Rotella lubricant brands, and the manufacturing, marketing and distribution operations behind them. A long-term supply agreement means Monomoy's Jiffy Lube centres will keep buying Shell-branded oil — Shell exits the shopfront, not the shelf.

The bigger prize: South Africa

The more consequential deal is still being finalised. Shell announced in 2024 that it intended to sell its majority stake in Shell Downstream South Africa, and talks with commodities trader Gunvor Group broke down earlier this year. Abu Dhabi National Oil Company's listed retail unit, ADNOC Distribution, has since emerged as the preferred buyer, and Bloomberg reported on June 30 that the two sides were preparing to announce a definitive agreement within days.

The stakes are larger than the roughly $1 billion price tag suggests. The deal covers close to 600 service stations — about 10% of the retail fuel market in Africa's most industrialised economy — and would be one of ADNOC's largest moves into sub-Saharan Africa as the Gulf producer builds out a downstream footprint far from home. It is not without friction. The transaction still needs sign-off from South Africa's Competition Commission and must satisfy the country's Black Economic Empowerment ownership rules, a standing feature of any change of control in the sector. BP has separately exercised a pre-emption right over a shared fuel storage terminal in Durban, a wrinkle that could force ADNOC into additional logistics investment regardless of how the headline deal closes.

Reading the pattern

Neither sale is isolated. Under chief executive Wael Sawan, who took over in January 2023, Shell has methodically shed businesses that tie up capital without matching the returns of its upstream, LNG and trading operations. The company has sold European onshore renewables assets and its Indian renewables platform Sprng Energy, is reportedly weighing a sale of its offshore wind portfolio worth more than $1 billion, and has been in discussions with ADNOC separately over its stake in an Australian LNG project valued at as much as $24 billion. Consumer fuel retail and quick-lube franchises — capital-intensive, low-margin, exposed to local regulation and currency swings — sit near the bottom of that hierarchy.

The market has not fully rewarded the discipline. Shell shares have traded around 31% below the average analyst price target in recent weeks and are down roughly 10% over the past month, with the stock's price-to-earnings ratio of about 11.5 lagging the sector average near 17.8. Part of that gap reflects investor unease about earnings that increasingly lean on the trading division — a business that is lucrative but harder to forecast than steady retail cash flow, and one where Shell has just navigated a leadership transition. Selling Jiffy Lube and South African forecourts raises cash and simplifies the balance sheet; it also means less of Shell's income comes from businesses investors can model with a ruler rather than a trading desk.

For buyers, the calculus runs the other way. Monomoy gets a scale automotive-services franchise without the burden of an oil major's capital discipline. ADNOC gets a beachhead in a large African fuel market as it builds out Gulf-funded downstream operations far beyond its home base — its fourth continent of retail exposure in as many years. What Shell is selling, in both cases, is exactly what a national or private buyer with a longer horizon might want: predictable, unglamorous cash flow from millions of small transactions at the pump and in the service bay.

The near-term catalyst is formal confirmation of the ADNOC deal, expected within days of Bloomberg's report and likely to land before Shell's second-quarter results are due at the end of July. Regulatory clearance in Pretoria, not the announcement itself, will determine how quickly Shell can book the proceeds — and how much further the retail exit has to run.
