Asia's loan market stays weak as war fallout saps lender confidence

Singapore's Central Business District, a hub for Asia's syndicated loan market, at dusk. — Basile Morin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Asia's loan market was supposed to recover in 2026, but bankers say fallout from the Iran war has frozen deal-making instead.

Asia's loan bankers went into 2026 expecting the year to get easier. It hasn't. Six months in, the region's syndicated loan market is on track for its weakest first half in 16 years, and bankers say the reason has less to do with economic fundamentals than with a war that has nothing to do with Asia directly — but has left everyone touching the market too nervous to commit.

Loan issuance in dollars, euros and yen across Asia Pacific excluding Japan fell 15% from a year earlier to $69 billion in the first six months of 2026, according to data cited by bankers tracking the market. That's the softest first-half tally since 2010. The expected catalysts for a rebound — a pickup in M&A financing, a thaw in rate-driven borrowing delays — simply didn't show up.

A war knocking on the wrong door

The Iran war is not an Asian conflict, but its effects have traveled. The closure of the Strait of Hormuz sent ripples through global energy and shipping costs, and — more relevant to loan desks — through the confidence of both lenders and borrowers who suddenly have one more unpriced risk to worry about. Middle Eastern banks, a meaningful source of liquidity into Asian deals in recent years, have pulled back to shore up home markets first.

"With increased uncertainty, there is a reduction in corporate confidence," said Andrew Ashman, a loan banker at Barclays. That reluctance shows up less as outright refusal and more as delay: deals get pushed, terms get renegotiated, timelines stretch. Birendra Baid at Deutsche Bank expects the squeeze to show up in price too, saying "pricing will compress" as fewer active mandates chase the same pool of lenders.

Old headwinds haven't gone away either

The war compounds a slump that predates it. Loan volumes in G3 currencies across Asia Pacific outside Japan had already fallen 6.7% in 2025 to a two-year low of $189 billion, as tariff uncertainty deterred new investment and high U.S. interest rates pushed borrowers to put off dollar financing. Gavin Chappell at ANZ Bank noted that "loan margins continue to be under pressure" even before the latest shock, as banks compete harder for a shrinking set of deals. Australia has borne a visible share of that pain, with loan volumes there down roughly 10%.

What's kept the market from stalling entirely is a narrower band of activity: structured credit and, especially, data center financing, where demand tied to AI infrastructure has stayed strong regardless of the geopolitical backdrop. DayOne Data Centers closed a roughly $3.5 billion multicurrency facility for its Johor, Malaysia campus, since expanded toward a reported $7 billion, while Blackstone-backed AirTrunk has been arranging around $2.3 billion for a data center project in the same corridor. Bankers describe these as one of the few areas generating fee income worth fighting over.

Confidence, not rates, is the constraint

The distinction bankers keep drawing is between a market that's expensive and one that's simply reluctant. Rate cuts, if and when they arrive, don't automatically fix the latter. As one market analyst put it, even if rates come down, access to credit may still tighten because confidence doesn't show up on a rate sheet. That's the harder problem for a regional loan market that has now had two consecutive down years for different reasons — tariffs and rates in 2025, war-driven risk aversion in 2026.

The next real test comes as banks close out third-quarter mandates and see whether the trickle of data-center and structured-credit deals is joined by anything broader — a sign that corporate borrowers are willing to look past the Gulf and start planning again. Until then, Asia's loan desks are bracing for margins to keep thinning on volumes that keep shrinking.
