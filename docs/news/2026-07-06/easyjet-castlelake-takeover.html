EasyJet agrees in principle to Castlelake's £5.2bn takeover

An easyJet Airbus A320 at Madrid-Barajas Airport — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
EasyJet's board would recommend Castlelake's improved £6.90-a-share, £5.2bn bid — but it's only 'in principle' until a firm offer lands by 3 August.

EasyJet's board has told the private equity firm Castlelake it would recommend a takeover offer of £6.90 a share to shareholders — the fifth price Castlelake has put on the table in six weeks, and the first the airline hasn't rejected outright. The agreement is "in principle" only. Castlelake now has until 5pm on 3 August to convert that into a firm offer under UK takeover rules, or walk away.

At £6.90 a share, the equity value of the deal comes to roughly £5.2 billion, rising to £5.5 billion on a fully diluted basis once options and convertible instruments are counted. It marks a sharp climb from where this started: Castlelake's opening approach in May valued easyJet at 560 pence a share. Four rejections later — 600p, 625p, 650p, each waved away by the board — the fifth attempt cleared the bar.

Why a fifth bid, and why now

The timing traces back to a rough spring for the airline. EasyJet's first-half results, reported in May, showed a total after-tax loss of £377 million, up 27% on the year, as European jet fuel prices climbed more than 80% since late February. The cause was the conflict involving Iran and the disruption it caused to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. EasyJet has put the added fuel cost at around £25 million, layered on top of softer bookings as travellers deferred summer trip decisions. Castlelake disclosed its stake and interest to UK regulators on 29 May, with the shares trading well below where they'd sat before the profit warning — the kind of gap between a depressed share price and a carrier's underlying asset base that private equity buyers are built to notice.

The final £6.90 figure represents a 73% premium to easyJet's closing price on 29 May, the day Castlelake's interest became public. It's a big number on its own, but it says as much about how far the shares had fallen as it does about what Castlelake is willing to pay. Shares closed at 558.2p on the trading day before the announcement — still a discount of close to 20% to the offer, the market's way of pricing in the chance the deal doesn't close as agreed.

The structure behind the numbers

One detail explains why this took five attempts rather than one clean bid: EU competition and ownership rules require carriers operating within the bloc to have significant EU ownership, and easyJet flies extensively out of European bases. Castlelake's vehicle is structured to be 49% owned by the fund itself, with the remaining 51% held by EU nationals — reportedly including Peter Bellew, the former Malaysia Airlines chief executive, and Mark Breen, who runs the Dublin-based Oneiros Aerospace. Brookfield Asset Management, which oversees close to $865 billion globally, was brought in as a co-investor partway through the process, a move that widened the financial firepower behind the bid and likely helped push the price from 650p to 690p in a matter of days.

EasyJet's board, in accepting the terms as a basis for recommendation, said Castlelake had stressed "tremendous respect" for the airline and its staff, along with an intention to support its transformation into what the fund calls a "stronger, more resilient European airline." That language matters to a board weighing a sale to a financial buyer rather than a strategic one — private equity ownership typically means a leaner capital structure, faster decision-making outside public markets, and, eventually, an exit via resale or relisting.

What 'in principle' actually means

Agreement in principle is a milestone, not a signed contract. Three things stand between this deal and completion: due diligence, which Castlelake and Brookfield will need to complete on easyJet's fleet, leases, pension obligations and route economics; regulatory clearance, both competition authorities and the EU-ownership test built into the bid vehicle; and a shareholder vote, since a takeover of this size requires approval under the UK's Takeover Code. The 3 August deadline is the immediate marker — Castlelake either files a firm offer document by then, giving shareholders a binding proposal to vote on, or the clock resets and the bid lapses.

For a business moving from public markets into private hands, the stakes go beyond the headline price. A successful deal would hand control of route planning, fleet orders and capital allocation to Castlelake and its partners, away from the scrutiny that comes with a public listing. Some shareholders have made clear they were holding out for £7 a share or more, which the current offer doesn't quite reach — a gap that could still generate resistance at the vote, even with the board's recommendation.

What to watch

The next hard date is 3 August, when Castlelake must either publish a firm offer or step back under Takeover Panel rules. Between now and then, watch the share price gap to the offer price as a rough gauge of market confidence in completion, and watch for any shareholder pushback given the standing hope for a £7 figure. If the firm offer materialises, the subsequent regulatory review and shareholder vote will set the actual timeline for easyJet's exit from the London market — a process likely to run well into the autumn.
