AI age-assessment tool risks wrongly classifying child refugees as adults

The Home Office headquarters at 2 Marsham Street, London, which is overseeing the facial age estimation rollout. — Steph Gray / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
A children's charity warns a Home Office AI tool for guessing asylum seekers' ages carries a 30-month error margin at the exact boundary it's meant to police — with no published accuracy data.

Migrant children who arrive in Britain without a passport or birth certificate have long faced a crude test: an immigration officer looks at them and guesses whether they are 16 or 26. The Home Office now wants a computer to make that guess instead — and children's charities say the software is likely to get it wrong in exactly the cases that matter most.

Under a three-year, £322,000 contract that began on 1 June, British IT firm Akhter Computers has subcontracted German biometrics specialist Cognitec to supply facial age estimation (FAE) software for asylum processing. The system is being piloted on small-boat arrivals at Dover, with a national rollout pencilled in for 2027. It works by analysing a photograph and returning an estimated age range, which the Home Office says will sit "alongside", not replace, an immigration officer's judgement.

The 30-month blind spot

The trouble sits at exactly the boundary the tool exists to police. By the government's own account, even the strongest FAE systems carry a margin of error of around two and a half years — 30 months — when estimating whether someone is 16, 17 or 18. That is the precise window asylum caseworkers most need to get right, since it determines whether a young person is placed in local authority care or sent into adult accommodation or detention. Trauma, malnutrition and months on the road can add further, unmeasured years to a teenager's apparent age, according to the Refugee and Migrant Children's Consortium, a coalition of more than 100 organisations including the Refugee Council, the Children's Society and UNICEF UK.

The stakes are not hypothetical. Home Office figures cited by the Helen Bamber Foundation show 755 children were wrongly assessed as adults on arrival in 2025 alone, under the current, human-only process. "This is not about protecting children," said Maddie Harris of Humans for Rights Network, one of the charities pressing the government to abandon the plan. "This is about shoring up the decisions that [the authorities] are making."

No numbers, no audit

What's missing from the government's case is a published error rate for the population it will actually be used on. An investigation by Lighthouse Reports, using NIST benchmarking data on Cognitec's system, found it misclassified more than half of 16-year-old West African test subjects as over 18, against fewer than a quarter of 16-year-old Eastern Europeans — a gap the Home Office has acknowledged but not quantified for its own deployment. No Equality Impact Assessment or Data Protection Impact Assessment has been made public. In June, 63 organisations — including Human Rights Watch, Amnesty International, Liberty and Big Brother Watch — wrote to ministers demanding the trial be halted pending independent scrutiny.

The Home Office has not published a response to that letter, and campaigners say the 2027 rollout timeline is proceeding regardless. The next test of the policy is likely to come through the courts: lawyers acting for asylum-seeking children have signalled they will challenge any age decision made with FAE input, which would force the government to disclose, for the first time, exactly how the tool performed in the cases it was used on.
