US to ban new Chinese-made humanoid robots and power inverters

A Unitree G1 humanoid robot on display — Chinese-made humanoid and quadruped robots are now barred from new FCC equipment authorisation. — Sayanesy / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
The FCC banned new Chinese-made humanoid robots and power inverters from US sale, extending China tech restrictions beyond chips to the physical infrastructure of the AI buildout.

For four years, Washington's answer to China's AI ambitions has mostly meant one thing: chips. Block the advanced processors, choke off the fabrication tools, and hope Beijing's AI buildout stalls for want of computing power. This week the strategy widened. On Tuesday the Federal Communications Commission added foreign-made humanoid and quadruped robots, along with the power inverters that feed electricity into the grid, to its "covered list" — the roster of communications equipment the agency considers a national security threat. New models in either category can no longer be authorised for sale in the United States.

The practical effect is narrower than the headline suggests. This is an import gate, not a recall: robots and inverters already installed in American homes, warehouses and solar farms are unaffected, as are models that received FCC authorisation before the listing. What's blocked is anything new. A Chinese-made humanoid robot or grid inverter seeking a first-time equipment certificate now can't get one, because the FCC's authorisation process bars the wireless and cellular connectivity these devices depend on to communicate with the cloud.

Why robots and inverters, specifically

The agency's reasoning for robots rests substantially on one machine: Unitree's Go1 quadruped. Security researchers catalogued a vulnerability, CVE-2025-2894, in which a backdoor baked into the Go1's firmware auto-started on boot and phoned home to a cloud tunnel operated from China, handing anyone with the right API key full remote control of the device. The bug wasn't theoretical — vulnerable units were later found running inside networks at MIT, Princeton, Carnegie Mellon and the University of Waterloo. The FCC's order warns that networked robots "collect data that could be leveraged by malign actors to surveil Americans," and that an attacker could "remotely commandeer" one outright.

Inverters make the list for a related but distinct reason. These are the boxes that sit between solar panels, batteries or data-centre backup power and the electrical grid, converting direct current to the alternating current homes and businesses run on. Increasingly they're networked too, reporting performance data and taking remote commands for grid balancing. A compromised inverter fleet is, in effect, a remote kill switch wired into the grid. China dominates this market: Huawei and Sungrow alone accounted for more than half of global solar inverter shipments in 2024, and nine of the world's top ten inverter vendors are Chinese-headquartered. Sungrow, notably, is also the single leading inverter brand in the US market — the company most directly exposed by Tuesday's order.

"The FCC is effectively banning Chinese — and likely some other foreign-made — new-model power inverters and advanced robots from the US," trade policy analyst Peter Harrell wrote, noting the mechanism is the same one long used against Huawei telecom gear: deny the equipment authorisation, not the product itself.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr framed the move as coordinated policy rather than a standalone FCC initiative, saying the agency acted "in lock step with our national security agencies" to secure the supply chains underneath critical infrastructure.

The AI angle

What makes this a technology story rather than a narrow cybersecurity one is the administration's own framing: officials described the goal as protecting the "US AI buildout," not just blocking spyware. That's a tell about how the White House now thinks about AI competition. Chips get the headlines, but a data centre needs power delivery as much as it needs GPUs — and demand for both is climbing fast. US data centres are projected to consume roughly 12% of the country's electricity by 2028, up from about 4% in 2023, with more than 12,000 projects currently queued for grid interconnection. Humanoid and quadruped robots, meanwhile, are increasingly pitched as the physical labour layer for AI systems — warehouse pickers, factory inspectors, eventually home assistants — making them a second front in the same buildout.

Seen that way, Tuesday's order is less an isolated crackdown than a widening of the same playbook applied to chips: identify a chokepoint in the stack that AI infrastructure depends on, and restrict Chinese suppliers' access to it before the dependency deepens. It also fits a pattern of congressional pressure — senators Rick Scott and Tom Cotton introduced the Blocking CCP Spy Tech Act in May, naming Unitree directly and calling for the kind of national-security review that just concluded. The administration's approach to chips this year has been more transactional than absolute — it cleared Nvidia to sell China its H200 chip in December in exchange for a revenue share — which makes the flat prohibition on robots and inverters notable by contrast: no carve-out, no fee structure, just a closed door for new models.

What it doesn't do, and what comes next

The order leaves plenty untouched. It doesn't cover industrial (non-mobile) robotic arms, doesn't touch federal government purchases already exempted, and doesn't retroactively pull any device already in service. Chinese manufacturers can still sell into the US the models they've already certified, and existing supply agreements — including whatever inverters are already spec'd into US data-centre and solar projects — continue undisturbed for now.

The near-term questions are about scope and enforcement: whether the covered list expands to catch adjacent categories like battery storage systems or grid-forming inverters explicitly, whether US and allied manufacturers — companies like SolarEdge or the emerging crop of American humanoid-robot makers — see a demand bump from the restriction, and whether Beijing responds with reciprocal measures of its own, as it has in past chip-related disputes. Congress's Blocking CCP Spy Tech Act, still pending, would harden much of what the FCC just did into statute; its progress is the next marker to watch.
