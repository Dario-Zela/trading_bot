Rio Tinto H1 profit jumps 28% on copper and aluminium strength

Rio Tinto's Kennecott operation in Utah, home to the Bingham Canyon copper mine, one of the assets behind the company's copper earnings growth. — Eric Prado / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto's free cash flow rose 75%, nearly triple its profit growth, as the miner raised its dividend and held capex near record highs — a bet that copper and aluminium prices are durable, not peaking.

Rio Tinto's half-year numbers, out Tuesday, landed roughly where analysts expected on the headline measures — underlying EBITDA of $14.8bn, up 28% on the same period last year, and net earnings up 47% to $6.7bn. Shares in Sydney rose 4.8% to A$167.11, their highest level since July 7. But the number that should hold a trader's attention longer than the earnings beat is free cash flow: it climbed 75% to $3.8bn, nearly three times the rate of profit growth.

That gap matters. When cash generation outruns earnings by that margin, it usually means a company is converting paper profit into distributable cash faster than it's booking it — through working-capital discipline, lower capex intensity relative to output, or both. Rio Tinto's interim dividend rose 43% to $3.4bn, or 211 US cents a share, comfortably ahead of the 203-cent consensus and up from 148 cents a year earlier. Chief executive Simon Trott, five months into the job after taking over from Jakob Stausholm, framed it as a function of "performance, returns, portfolio quality and disciplined capital allocation."

Copper and aluminium do the heavy lifting

Copper, aluminium and lithium together now account for more than half of group EBITDA, a structural shift from the iron-ore dependence that has defined Rio Tinto for decades. Copper-equivalent production rose 3% in the half, helped by higher copper output and the company's first lithium production from its Argentina project, delivered ahead of schedule. Pilbara iron ore, still the largest single earner, posted its strongest first-half output since 2018 even as its share of the earnings mix shrinks.

Revenue rose 15% to $31bn, but the EBITDA growth outpaced that — evidence that pricing, not just volume, did the work. Copper has traded firm through 2026 on data-centre and grid-related demand, while aluminium has benefited from tight supply. Rio Tinto also booked $870m in productivity gains during the half, on track for an annualised run-rate of $1.8bn by year-end, a cost-discipline layer that management is careful to separate from the commodity-price tailwind in its own disclosures — a distinction worth taking at face value, since it's checkable against unit-cost guidance.

The capital-return signal

The interesting tell is not the beat itself but what Rio Tinto chose to do with the cash. Net debt fell during the half even after $5bn of capital expenditure and a $4.2bn final dividend payment for 2025 — the company paying down leverage, funding growth, and lifting the interim payout simultaneously. Return on capital employed rose to 17% from 14%. Management reiterated capex guidance of up to $11bn in both 2026 and 2027, weighted toward copper and iron-ore replacement projects, with spending only easing toward $10bn from 2028.

A producer that were bracing for a price reversal would typically hoard cash, trim the dividend payout ratio toward the low end of its 40–60% target range, or slow the capex plan. Rio Tinto is doing none of those. The dividend beat consensus, leverage is falling from an already-moderate base of roughly $4bn net debt, and the capex programme is holding at multi-year highs. That combination reads as a bet that current copper and aluminium prices are closer to a new baseline than a cyclical peak — a view other diversified miners' upcoming results, and the copper price itself, will either confirm or contradict.

The next test comes with third-quarter production figures and the broader read-through from Big Tech's capex week, where data-centre demand — one pillar under copper's price — gets a fresh data point from Microsoft and Meta's earnings.
