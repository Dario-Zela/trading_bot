Some Claude conversations found publicly indexed online, BBC investigation finds

Anthropic's logo. The company's Claude chatbot had shared conversations turn up in public search results after share pages were missing standard no-index instructions. — Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
A BBC investigation found hundreds of users' shared Claude conversations sitting in Google, Bing and Brave search results, exposing personal and work data users assumed was private.

A chunk of what people assumed were private conversations with Anthropic's Claude chatbot turned out to be nothing of the sort. Over the weekend of 25-26 July, users on Reddit discovered that typing a simple search command — site:claude.ai/share — into Google pulled up hundreds of other people's chats with the AI, spanning at least 25 pages of results. Some of the conversations were only weeks old. Bing, Brave and DuckDuckGo returned the same material.

The BBC's technology desk picked up the thread and confirmed the scale of it: CVs with names, phone numbers and full employment histories; unpublished drafts of corporate blog posts naming internal cloud-security projects; healthcare-related research; and, in at least one case flagged by other outlets, cryptocurrency wallet material. None of the people involved appear to have understood that clicking "share" on a Claude conversation could end with it sitting in a Google index, readable by anyone who knew — or guessed — the right search terms.

A missing line of code, not a data breach

This wasn't a hack. Claude's share feature works as designed: a user clicks "share," the app generates a public URL at claude.ai/share, and anyone with that link can read the transcript. The problem is what happened next. Pages published to that URL pattern were missing a line of code called a noindex tag — the standard instruction that tells search-engine crawlers not to catalogue a page. Without it, any share link that ended up referenced anywhere a crawler could see — a forum post, a social media mention, even another indexed page linking to it — was fair game to be picked up and served back to anyone who searched for it.

Anthropic's position, relayed to the BBC, is that the system worked exactly as intended: "These shareable links are not guessable or discoverable unless people choose to share them themselves," a spokesperson said, adding that "we do not share chat directories or sitemaps with search engines like Google." That's true as far as it goes — nobody stumbled onto these pages by brute-forcing URLs. But it sidesteps the actual complaint, which is that a page can end up indexed without anyone reading it as intended. Once a share link is posted anywhere a crawler can reach, the missing noindex tag does the rest. "Choosing to share" a conversation with one person on Reddit is not the same thing, in most users' minds, as choosing to publish it to Google.

The same mistake, again

Anthropic is not the first AI company to walk into this. In August 2025, OpenAI ran a near-identical experiment with ChatGPT: a checkbox, tucked into the share dialog, that let users opt a conversation into being "discoverable" in web search. It sounded like informed consent. In practice, nearly 4,500 conversations turned up on a basic Google site search almost immediately, and reporting later put the true figure — including chats archived before removal — above 100,000. OpenAI's chief information security officer pulled the feature within about a day of the story breaking. Elon Musk's Grok chatbot reportedly had a comparable indexing problem of its own last year.

The distinction worth noting is that OpenAI's failure came from a feature that at least nominally asked for consent and still let six-figure numbers of chats leak. Anthropic's came from a default state that never asked at all — the noindex omission meant every shared conversation was exposed to indexing risk unless a user happened to know enough to check. Neither approach protected users in practice. By Sunday, Anthropic had pushed noindex tags and robots.txt changes across the share pages, and the bulk of the exposed conversations had dropped out of Google's live results. What Anthropic cannot undo quickly is the copies already pulled into search engine caches and, per multiple reports following the OpenAI precedent, services like the Internet Archive — delisting those requires separate, slower requests to each platform, and there is no guarantee every mirror gets pulled.

What it says about the industry's priorities

The pattern across two of the largest AI labs in twelve months points to something more structural than a coding oversight repeated twice. Chat-sharing features get built to make it easy to publish a conversation — a good product goal, since AI-generated explainers and code snippets are genuinely useful to pass around. But the privacy engineering that ought to accompany a public-by-default publishing feature — noindex tags, clear warnings at the point of sharing, expiring links — appears to have been an afterthought bolted on after journalists went looking, not a design requirement built in from the start.

That gap matters more for AI chatbots than for most other software, because people increasingly treat them as confidants: pasting in draft contracts, medical questions, code with internal project names attached, even, as Anthropic's own admission to the BBC acknowledged, prompts that generated content violating the company's usage policy. A search box that quietly turns a private disclosure into a public one erodes the basic assumption that makes people willing to use these tools candidly in the first place.

Regulators have been circling AI data practices already, and this episode gives them a concrete case study rather than a hypothetical one. The next thing to watch is whether Anthropic publishes a fuller account of how many conversations were exposed and for how long — something it has not yet done — and whether the EU's AI Omnibus rules, which take effect this year, end up treating incidents like this as the kind of high-risk data-handling failure they are meant to catch.
