Goldman's high-beta momentum basket heads for its worst month on record

Goldman Sachs' headquarters at 200 West Street in lower Manhattan, home to the trading desk behind the High Beta Momentum basket. — Z4dude / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Goldman's high-beta momentum basket is on track for its worst month ever, exceeding 2000 and 2008-09, as crowded AI trades unwind under forced deleveraging.

Goldman Sachs runs a basket of stocks designed to capture one trade: momentum. Buy what's going up, ride it, let leverage do the rest. For most of 2026 it was the best trade on the desk. Now it is unwinding at a pace the bank's own data show has no precedent — not in the dot-com bust, not in the 2008-09 financial crisis.

The gauge, known internally as the High Beta Momentum basket (ticker GSPRHIMO), finished the first half of the year up roughly 57%, a run so lopsided that positioning data put hedge fund exposure to the factor at the 92nd percentile of the past five years. It has spent the back half of July giving much of that back. Hedgeye, citing Goldman's own trading desk, flagged the basket as on track for its worst monthly performance in history — worse than the peak drawdowns of 2000 and 2009, the two reference points every risk manager on the Street uses to size a crisis.

From 500 to 850 and back

The basket's own index history tells the story in three moves. It started the year just above 500, climbed to roughly 850 by late June as speculative technology and semiconductor names ran without pause, and has since round-tripped most of the way back to where it began — inside a single month. A two-session stretch in mid-July did outsized damage on its own: GSPRHIMO fell a cumulative 18%, its steepest two-day drop since the pandemic crash of March 2020, according to trading desk commentary circulated at the time.

Goldman was not entirely blindsided. The desk had flagged momentum as vulnerable to a "July correction" heading into the month, pointing to crowded exposure, quarterly index resets and thinning holiday-week liquidity as conditions that could turn an orderly rotation into something worse. What followed exceeded even that warning. Morgan Stanley's tech momentum index — a separate 27-year-old series tracked by that bank's desk — posted a 17-day rate of change of roughly -36% in mid-July, the steepest reading in the index's history, surpassing the comparable stretches of the dot-com collapse, the 2008-09 crisis and the 2020 Covid selloff.

What broke the trade

Three things arrived close together. Single-stock leveraged ETFs tracking Asian semiconductor names such as SK Hynix had grown large enough that even modest declines in the underlying shares forced the funds to sell to hold their target exposure — a mechanical, self-reinforcing unwind rather than a fundamentals-driven one. Around the same time, the release of a competing AI model out of China, Kimi K3, undercut the market's working assumption that U.S. developers held an unassailable lead, a theme that had underwritten much of the momentum basket's semiconductor and mega-cap tech weighting. And a fresh spike in oil prices tied to renewed Iran tensions reintroduced a macro risk the momentum crowd had largely stopped pricing.

The result was a classic crowding unwind: everyone owned the same trade, so there was no marginal buyer once selling started. Goldman's prime brokerage desk described systematic managers — funds that trade off quantitative signals like momentum and volatility rather than fundamental research — as absorbing the brunt of it, falling 3.6% in their worst drawdown since the summer of 2025 and erasing close to a quarter of their year-to-date gains, which slid from roughly 14.4% in late June to about 10.8%. Fundamental long-short managers fared only modestly better, with Goldman attributing nearly all of their losses to unwound technology and momentum exposure rather than stock-picking, and describing many as "aggressively cutting the AI longs that had generated their entire year-to-date alpha" through late June.

The liquidation math

What makes this drawdown more than a bad month for quant desks is the leverage still sitting underneath it. Goldman's own estimate is that if deleveraging continues at anything like its recent pace, the momentum factor's maximum potential decline could reach 50% from its peak — roughly double what has already been lost. That figure matters because forced selling, unlike a valuation-driven correction, does not necessarily stop at a price level; it stops when the leveraged positions are gone. UBS's cash desk has characterized the current selling as coming mainly from long-term accounts rather than hedge funds turning outright bearish, which points toward deleveraging rather than a broader loss of conviction in the underlying stocks — a distinction that matters for how far, and how fast, the unwind still has to run.

The next test arrives fast. Microsoft and Meta open Big Tech's earnings week with results that will be read directly against the AI-capex thesis the momentum basket was built on, and any sign that hyperscaler spending is slowing — or accelerating — will move the same crowded positions again. Risk-parity and systematic funds rebalancing off elevated volatility readings are a mechanical source of further selling pressure in the near term, independent of what the earnings actually show. For a market that spent the first half of the year treating momentum as a one-way trade, the lesson of July is that crowded positioning cuts both directions, and considerably faster on the way down.
