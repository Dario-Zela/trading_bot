Burnham's Labour draws level with Reform in first poll bounce as PM

Andy Burnham, whose first week as UK prime minister has moved both the polls and the gilt market. — House of Commons
In one sentence
Three polls show Labour level with or ahead of Reform a week into Burnham's premiership, but gilt yields hit G7 highs on his fiscal-flexibility remarks.

A week is supposed to be a long time in politics. For Andy Burnham, it appears to have been long enough to do what Keir Starmer could not: put Labour back within touching distance of Nigel Farage.

Three separate polls released this week show Labour drawing level with, or overtaking, Reform UK — the first such movement since Reform began its run at the top of British polling more than a year ago. Survation put Labour on 26%, ahead of Reform's 24%, the pollster's best Labour number since June 2025 and its first Labour lead since May of that year. More In Common had it wider still, Labour on 28% to Reform's 24%. YouGov had the two tied at 22%, with Labour up six points since May and Reform at its lowest reading since March 2025. YouGov also found more Britons rate Burnham a better prime minister than Farage — a personal-approval gap that, for a leader one week into the job, is doing real work for his party.

The bounce follows a chaotic month in Westminster. Reform's gains in May's local and regional elections spooked Labour badly enough that the party removed Starmer and installed Burnham, the former Greater Manchester mayor, as prime minister on 20 July. His opening moves were retail politics aimed squarely at the cost of living: scrapping a tax on energy bills, capping bus fares, and cutting levies on pubs and bars — an attempt, in his own framing, to give voters "breathing space" before harder decisions arrive in the autumn.

The gilt market has a different question

Voters may be warming to Burnham; bond investors are not yet convinced. His remark that he would stick to the previous government's fiscal rules but use "any flexibility" within them landed badly the day he took office, pushing the 10-year gilt yield up to roughly 5.04% — already the highest among G7 economies — and the 30-year yield to 5.75%, a two-month high. Sterling slipped against the dollar the same day. The appointment of John Healey as chancellor, in place of the widely tipped Shabana Mahmood, added to the sense that markets are still pricing in an unknown.

That is the gap this week's polling doesn't close: a popularity bounce built on tax cuts and fare caps says little about how Burnham intends to pay for them, or what "flexibility within the rules" will mean once it is written down. Traders will not get that answer from a poll. They will get it, if at all, in the October budget — the same fiscal event now being squeezed from the other side by elevated oil prices, according to NIESR's latest warning on the public finances. Until then, expect gilts and the pound to trade on rumour about Healey's intentions rather than on Burnham's approval rating.
