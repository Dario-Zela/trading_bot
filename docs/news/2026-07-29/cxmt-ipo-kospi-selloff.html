CXMT's $8.6bn Shanghai debut sends Kospi into record slide

The Shanghai Stock Exchange building in Pudong, where CXMT's shares surged 466% on their trading debut — 螺钉 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
CXMT's $8.6bn Shanghai IPO surged 466% the same week a Chinese DUV lithography breakthrough sent Samsung and SK Hynix into a 10.8% Kospi rout.

China's chip market delivered two numbers this week that, taken together, tell a story markets have been slow to price: 466% and 10.8%.

The first is how far shares in ChangXin Memory Technologies, known as CXMT, jumped on their Shanghai debut on Monday. The Hefei-based memory chipmaker priced its initial public offering at 8.66 yuan a share, raised 57.92 billion yuan — about $8.6 billion — and by the closing bell was worth roughly 3.3 trillion yuan, or $488 billion. That made it China's most valuable listed company, ahead of Industrial and Commercial Bank of China, and briefly more valuable than Intel. It is the largest semiconductor IPO in mainland Chinese history.

The second number is how far South Korea's Kospi fell a day later. Down 10.8% to 6,023.66, its worst close since April, the index dragged Samsung Electronics down 13.4% — its steepest one-day drop in almost two decades — and SK Hynix down 14.7%. The Korea Exchange triggered sell-side sidecars within minutes of the open and then a full circuit breaker, the eighth of the year. Japan's Nikkei fell 4%, Taiwan's Taiex dropped 4.7% and TSMC slipped 3%. In the US the previous session, AMD had already fallen 5.2%, Nvidia 5% and Micron 2.3%.

Read separately, these look like two unrelated stories — a hot IPO here, a tech selloff there. Read together, they describe a single rotation: capital moving toward Beijing's bet that China can build its own memory-chip industry, and away from the South Korean and American firms that have dominated it for two decades.

A DRAM maker worth more than Intel

CXMT makes DRAM, the workhorse memory chip found in everything from smartphones to AI servers, and its listing had been anticipated for months as a test of investor appetite for Beijing's chip self-sufficiency push. The scale of the debut still surprised the market. Founded in 2016 with backing from the Hefei municipal government's industrial investment fund, CXMT has gone from a standing start to a company Nomura estimates could hold 18% of the global DRAM market by the end of 2028, up from roughly 10% today.

CXMT's own prospectus points to the same tailwind lifting incumbents: AI-driven demand has pushed DRAM prices sharply higher over the past year, and the company said it would direct most of its IPO proceeds toward expanding memory-wafer production. Analysts have been unusually blunt about the implications for the field CXMT is entering. Goldman Sachs, in a note flagged widely after the debut, said the memory industry "has a new problem" — a well-capitalised Chinese entrant arriving just as AI demand has pulled global supply tight. The bank forecasts CXMT's production capacity could double by 2030, enough to draw level with Micron.

Apple is reportedly already testing CXMT's chips as an alternative to the Samsung–SK Hynix–Micron triumvirate that has long set DRAM pricing. That prospect, more than the IPO pop itself, is what sent Micron down 5%, SanDisk down 12% and SK Hynix's US-listed shares down 8% on Monday, even before the Kospi cracked the next day.

The lithography report that broke the Kospi

What turned a sector rotation into a full-blown rout was a second piece of news landing almost simultaneously: a report, initially from The Information and matched by Reuters, that a Shanghai state-backed manufacturer — since named as Shanghai Aishengna Electronic Technology Group — has begun mass-producing China's first home-grown immersion deep-ultraviolet lithography machines. The first five units are due to ship this year to SMIC, Hua Hong Semiconductor and CXMT, with output scaling to around 20 machines in 2027.

The technical detail matters. DUV lithography is the tool China has struggled hardest to replicate domestically after Washington cut off access to Dutch supplier ASML's most advanced machines. The new tools are built for 28-nanometre-class chips, but reports suggest they could reach 7nm — or 5nm at reduced yields — using multi-patterning techniques, and most of their components are sourced domestically, though some critical parts still come from Japan.

That is a long way from matching the extreme-ultraviolet lithography that Samsung and SK Hynix use for their most advanced memory, and the report has not been independently verified against production data. But markets did not wait for confirmation. If China's foundries can genuinely compress the timeline for producing competitive logic and memory chips without ASML's most restricted equipment, it undercuts a central assumption behind Samsung and SK Hynix's margin premium — that the technology gap would hold for years, not quarters. South Korean traders reacted first and asked questions later; the scale of Tuesday's decline, wiping out a reported $430 billion across the region in a single session, reflects a market repricing that assumption in real time rather than digesting a single confirmed fact.

What the divergence signals

Set side by side, the CXMT IPO and the Kospi crash are not two separate stories about Asian tech having a bad week. They are the same story told from opposite sides of the trade. Investors bid CXMT to a $488 billion valuation on the belief that Chinese memory and lithography capacity is arriving faster than consensus expected; South Korean investors sold Samsung and SK Hynix on the same belief. Capital did not leave Asian semiconductors this week so much as reallocate within them, toward the companies seen as beneficiaries of Beijing's self-sufficiency drive and away from the incumbents it is designed to displace.

For algorithmic and systematic traders, that divergence is the more durable signal than either headline number alone. A single-day IPO pop can mean-revert; a single-day index crash can bounce. But if Chinese DRAM output and domestic lithography tooling keep scaling on the timelines analysts are now sketching out — Nomura's 18% DRAM share estimate, Goldman's capacity-doubling forecast — the gap between Chinese chip valuations and their Korean and American counterparts is a trade with a multi-year thesis behind it, not a one-week overreaction.

The next data points will come quickly. CXMT's post-listing lock-up structure and any follow-on filings will show whether Monday's demand was durable or a first-day scramble; Samsung and SK Hynix both report earnings within weeks, and their commentary on DRAM pricing and Chinese competition will be parsed line by line. Washington's response also bears watching — a Capitol Hill probe into CXMT's US customer relationships was already opened following the debut, and any move to restrict Apple or other American firms from qualifying Chinese DRAM would directly test how much of this week's rotation reflects a genuine technology shift versus a valuation overshoot.
