Mexican cartels outsource meth production to Nigerian labs, investigation finds

Crystal methamphetamine — Mexican cartels are increasingly producing it in West African labs rather than at home. — Radspunk / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A BBC investigation finds Mexican cartels increasingly outsourcing meth production to Nigerian labs, chasing cheap labour and weak chemical controls over turf defence at home.

The methamphetamine that shows up on European and West African streets with unusual purity is, increasingly, not being cooked in Sinaloa or Jalisco at all. It is being cooked in the forests of Nigeria, by Mexican chemists flown in to train local labour — because it is cheaper, safer for the cartel, and easier to hide.

A BBC investigation, drawing on interviews with Nigeria's National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) and US Africa Command, maps a pattern that has accelerated sharply since 2023: at least 14 Mexican-linked meth labs raided across Africa, four of them in Nigeria alone in 2026. The largest, dismantled in May in a forest in Ogun state, was industrial in scale — 2.4 tonnes of finished product and precursor chemicals seized, valued at roughly $363 million, with three Mexican nationals and six Nigerian collaborators arrested alongside a local drug baron.

Why Nigeria, why now

The logic is closer to offshoring a factory than fighting a turf war. James Griego, who runs Africom's counter-narcotics operations, told the BBC that Mexican cooks "perfected it, to the point now it is the highest purity, the highest potency version of meth" — and that reputation for quality is exactly what cartels are now replicating on Nigerian soil rather than shipping finished product across an ocean.

NDLEA spokesperson Femi Babafemi framed the appeal in blunter economic terms: West Africa's young, underemployed population offers cheap labour, and a domestic market already primed to absorb output that doesn't make it onto a boat. A 2018 government survey put Nigeria's adult illicit drug use rate at 14.4%, more than double the global average — a captive customer base sitting next to the factory floor.

The regulatory gap matters as much as the wage gap. Nigeria's chemical import controls are weak relative to Mexico's, now under sustained US pressure, and ephedrine and pseudoephedrine — the precursors that give meth its purity — remain more available there than tightly monitored alternatives elsewhere. Add forested, sparsely policed terrain and Atlantic shipping lanes feeding both Europe and Asia, and the arithmetic of where to build a lab starts to look obvious.

Not just Nigeria

The pattern repeats across the continent with local variations. Al Jazeera's reporting on South Africa found four major Mexican-linked meth sites uncovered on remote farms in two years, including one facility near Groblersdal valued at $105–110 million — chemists moved onto isolated farmland to produce closer to consumers and cut both transport costs and border exposure. Kenya and Mozambique have seen smaller versions of the same model. Analysts at the Institute for Security Studies argue Nigeria in particular is shifting from a transit country, ferrying other people's drugs, to a producer and exporter in its own right.

What it signals

Cartels defending Mexican territory against rivals and the state is expensive and increasingly dangerous, with US designations and sanctions tightening the noose on Sinaloa and Jalisco New Generation Cartel financing and logistics at home. Standing up a lab in a Nigerian forest, staffed by a handful of imported cooks and local hires, sidesteps that cost structure almost entirely — it is a bet that margins on synthetic drugs are wide enough to make geography nearly irrelevant, so long as the precursor chemicals and cheap labour are somewhere the enforcement isn't.

That has a narrow read-through for markets: it adds to the case for scrutiny of precursor-chemical export controls out of India and China into West Africa, and it is one more data point for the informal-economy volatility that regional currencies like the naira already absorb. It is not, on its own, a trading signal. But as a case study in how criminal enterprises respond to margin compression — outsource production, don't defend it — it reads like any other industry under cost pressure.

Watch for whether Nigeria's National Assembly moves to tighten precursor import licensing following the Ogun state case now before the courts, and whether similar lab discoveries turn up in Ghana or Ivory Coast, the next logical waypoints on the same coastline.
