GSK beats forecasts, unveils $2.52bn savings plan for drug pipeline

A GSK office building in Brentford, west London, part of the group's UK footprint. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
GSK beat Q2 forecasts and launched a $2.52bn savings plan aimed at funding pipeline trials, not dividends or buybacks.

GSK's second-quarter numbers were never going to be the interesting part. The interesting part is what the company plans to do with the money.

The London-based drugmaker reported turnover of £8.4 billion for the quarter, up 5% at constant currency and ahead of the roughly £8.2 billion analysts had pencilled in. Core earnings per share came in at 50.5p, well clear of the 47.1p consensus, and core operating profit rose 7% to £2.8 billion. Shares gained 4.2% on the day — a reasonable pop for a beat of that size, though the more telling reaction was to what came bundled alongside it.

GSK also confirmed a £1.9 billion (about $2.52 billion) cost-savings programme, targeted for completion by 2029, that will draw on AI adoption, technology upgrades, supply-chain streamlining and a leaner support-function footprint. Getting there won't be free: the restructuring itself is expected to cost £2.4 billion to execute, £2.1 billion of it in cash, before the savings start flowing through.

Where the money is actually going

The detail that separates this from a routine efficiency drive is the destination of the savings. GSK says the funds will bankroll an acceleration of its late-stage pipeline — more than 20 Phase III trial starts expected in 2026, up from a previous plan of around ten, spanning seven assets across 18 indications in oncology, respiratory disease, hepatology and vaccines. Notably absent from the announcement: any increase to the dividend or a new buyback. The £2 billion repurchase programme unveiled at the 2024 full-year results has simply run its course, and the payout stays flat at an expected 70p for the year, following a 17p second-quarter declaration.

That allocation choice has a clear driver: dolutegravir, the compound underpinning GSK's dominant HIV franchise, starts losing patent exclusivity in key markets from 2028 through 2030. Rebuilding the pipeline before that revenue erodes is, in management's telling, the more urgent use of capital than returning it to shareholders now. New chief executive Luke Miels, who took over from Dame Emma Walmsley on 1 January, framed the strategy as delivering "strong operational performance and shareholder returns over the next five years" through what the company calls disciplined capital allocation — a formulation that reads, in practice, as jam tomorrow rather than jam today.

The headline profit picture was muddied by a £1.3 billion impairment tied to camlipixant, a respiratory drug candidate, which dragged total operating profit down 75% to £481 million even as the core, adjusted numbers improved. GSK treated that as noise and left full-year guidance unchanged: turnover growth of 3-5%, core operating profit growth of 7-9%, core EPS growth of 7-9%. It is also holding to a longer-range target of more than £40 billion in annual sales by 2031.

What comes next

The bet only pays off if the trials read out. With 20-plus Phase III starts now scheduled for this year and the dolutegravir cliff still two years out, the next real test of GSK's pivot arrives with Q3 results and any early efficacy data from the newly accelerated oncology and vaccine programmes — the point at which "pipeline investment" either becomes pipeline, or just a bigger cost base.
