Barclays' 'messy' Q2 print sends shares lower despite raised guidance

One Churchill Place in Canary Wharf, Barclays' global headquarters. — Zolfeqar Fatihzadeh / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Barclays raised full-year guidance on strong investment banking growth, but rising costs and thin margin for error sent shares among the FTSE's fallers.

Barclays raised its full-year profit outlook on the back of a roaring second quarter for its investment bank. Investors sold the stock anyway, sending it among the FTSE 100's biggest fallers on Tuesday — a reaction that says as much about the state of European bank valuations as it does about Barclays itself.

The headline numbers were hard to fault. Second-quarter pre-tax profit rose 31% year-on-year to £3.25bn, income climbed 16% to £8.3bn, and return on tangible equity hit 16.1%. Barclays lifted its 2026 group income guidance to around £31.5bn, up from roughly £31bn, and nudged its net interest income forecast above £13.7bn. Chief executive C.S. Venkatakrishnan called it "another strong quarter," and on capital returns he delivered: a £1bn buyback, 20% larger than the market had priced in, plus a 5.9p interim dividend, bringing first-half shareholder distributions to £2.3bn.

Shares fell 5.6% to 500.8p regardless.

Where the guidance raise fell short

The gap between the results and the guidance raise turned out to be the story. Barclays' income upgrade — to £31.5bn and £13.7bn of net interest income — landed close to what analysts already had in their models (£31.2bn and £13.9bn, respectively), leaving little room for the stock to re-rate on the headline print alone. That put the burden of proof on everything else, and everything else was mixed.

Operating costs rose 8.7% to £4.51bn in the quarter, roughly 3% above what analysts had expected, and management flagged a further £450m–£500m of incremental costs for the second half — up to £300m tied to structural cost actions and up to £150m from changes to investment banking pay. Litigation and conduct charges also ticked up, including a further top-up to the FCA's motor finance redress provision. Credit impairments for the half rose to £1.4bn, with the loan loss rate climbing to 62 basis points from 52bps, partly on a £228m single-name charge inside the investment bank. The CET1 capital ratio, at 14.3% before the buyback and set to fall to around 14.0% after it, sits near the top of Barclays' target range, limiting how much further capital return can flex if conditions worsen.

Jefferies summed it up as "a slightly messy set of numbers" — strength in the investment bank, where fee income jumped 30% and equity capital markets revenue nearly doubled, offset by softer net interest income in the UK retail bank and the US consumer business, where a one-off £225m gain from selling the American Airlines card portfolio flattered the headline figure.

The margin-for-error problem

The episode is a reminder of how little slack the market is currently extending to European banks. A guidance raise used to be enough on its own to move a stock; now investors are pricing the composition of the beat, not just its size, and punishing signs that cost growth or credit quality could erode a strong quarter's substance. For a lender whose investment bank is doing much of the heavy lifting, that scrutiny is likely to persist through the second half, when the £450m-plus of flagged costs actually lands. Barclays' third-quarter results, due in late October, will show whether that cost guidance holds — or adds to the list of reasons the market keeps discounting good news out of Barclays' stock.
