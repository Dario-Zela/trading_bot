Wildfires scorch France and Spain as fresh 40C heatwave looms over Europe

A lightning-sparked wildfire burns at Peña Montañesa, Spain, 16 July 2026. — Azahara00 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Wildfires have burned roughly 265,000 hectares across France and Spain in 2026's worst fire season on record, and a fresh 40C-plus heatwave now threatens to reignite them just as it strains Europe's power grids and crops.

France's interior ministry has a running tally it updates most mornings now: hectares burned, fires still active, people moved out of harm's way. On Tuesday the national count passed 115,000 hectares — nearly 285,000 acres — since January, the worst wildfire season French records have shown. Across the border, Spain is not far behind, with roughly 150,000 hectares gone and firefighters in Madrid's regional government calling the blazes around Ávila and the capital's outskirts the worst in the region's history. Between the two countries, more than 250,000 people have been moved from their homes in the past ten days alone.

The fires are not finished, and the weather is not helping. A fresh heat dome is settling over the Iberian peninsula and southwestern France, with AEMET, Spain's meteorological agency, forecasting 42°C in the northeast and Córdoba possibly touching 44°C by the end of the week. It arrives on already-scorched, tinder-dry ground, which is precisely the combination fire chiefs dread most: a landscape primed to burn, meeting the conditions that make it burn faster.

Gironde, Landes, and the fire that would not stay contained

The worst single blaze has been in Gironde, near Bordeaux, where a fire that broke out in late July burned more than 42,000 hectares — an area close to four times the size of Paris — and grew large enough to generate its own weather. Pyrocumulonimbus clouds, the fire-driven thunderstorms that can spawn lightning and start new blazes downwind of the original one, formed over the region, a phenomenon French officials say had never previously been documented in the country. Bordeaux's mayor, Thomas Cazenave, pushed back on reports the city itself might need to evacuate — "that is absolutely not on the agenda," he said — while conceding the exhibition centre had been readied to house up to 10,000 people if the fire's path shifted.

It didn't need to shift far to do damage. Roughly 220,000 people have been evacuated from the Gironde area since the fire began, part of what officials have described as France's largest peacetime evacuation since the Second World War. Ten deaths have been linked to this year's fire season nationwide, and around 240 structures have been destroyed. The French interior minister said this week that crews are now fighting 30 to 40 separate wildfires on a given day, and the EU's Civil Protection Mechanism has brought in aircraft and crews from Croatia, Czechia, Germany, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Sweden and Turkey to help.

Spain's fires have followed a similar arc. Blazes near Los Gallardos in Almería killed 13 people in early July; in the Madrid region, Isabel Díaz Ayuso, the regional president, called the fires there the worst her region has recorded, with roughly 24,000 hectares burned locally and another 21,000 in neighbouring Ávila. Three firefighters have died in Spain this season. By late July, the country had already exceeded its full-year average burn area before the traditional August peak had even arrived.

When rivers get too warm to cool a reactor

The fires are the visible damage. The less visible strain is on the systems that keep the lights on and food on shelves, and this is where the story stops being a European one and starts being a trading one.

France draws roughly 65% of its electricity from nuclear power, much of it cooled by river water — and European rules require utilities to throttle or halt reactors when the water they would discharge back into a river gets too warm for the ecosystem downstream. During the June heatwave, EDF cut close to 6.2 gigawatts of nuclear capacity, nearly 10% of its fleet, as temperatures on the Rhône, Seine and Garonne pushed past safe thresholds; reactors at Golfech, Bugey and Chooz have all been taken offline entirely at points this summer. Golfech sits directly on the Garonne, the same river system the Gironde fires have burned along.

The Rhine has its own version of the problem. Water levels at Kaub, a key chokepoint for barge traffic into southern Germany and Switzerland, have fallen to roughly 28 centimetres — the lowest since 2018, with forecasts pointing toward levels not recorded since data collection began in 1990. Barges are being loaded lighter to keep from grounding, which means more trips at higher cost: tanker-barge rates from Rotterdam to Karlsruhe have risen from around €45 a tonne at the end of June to €120–€125 now. Coal stockpiles at the Amsterdam-Rotterdam-Antwerp hub have swollen roughly 75% because inland barge deliveries have been cut by three-quarters, and Uniper has warned its Staudinger-5 coal plant may run irregularly through mid-September for lack of fuel deliveries. Analysts tracking Spanish power markets expect Q3 prices there to rise around 20%, driven by the same gas-and-cooling dynamics playing out across the continent.

Agriculture is absorbing a parallel hit. Europe's June heatwave alone is estimated to have wiped out roughly 9 million tonnes of wheat, barley, maize and oats — about €2 billion of crop value — and Spain, the world's largest olive oil producer, is heading into harvest with soil moisture low enough that both yield and oil content are expected to fall, a combination that tends to show up quickly in futures prices. Euronext maize contracts have already touched fresh highs on the dry outlook, even as some milling wheat has eased on a stronger Black Sea harvest — a reminder that heat doesn't move every crop the same way, and traders are having to price the divergence rather than a blanket shortfall.

A second summer of the same shape

What makes this year different from a single bad fire season is that it isn't a single bad fire season — it's the second one in a row of roughly the same shape: extreme heat, reactor curtailments, low rivers, evacuated towns, and a scramble in grain and power markets that arrives on schedule each July. Forecasters cited in French and Italian press this year have put the cumulative economic cost of the current heat-and-fire cycle at roughly $240 billion for France, $147 billion for Italy and $120 billion for Spain between 2026 and 2030 if the pattern holds. Whether or not those specific figures prove accurate, the direction of travel — insurers, utilities and commodity desks increasingly modelling a hot, dry, fire-prone European summer as a base case rather than a tail risk — is now hard to dispute.

Across the Channel, Britain is living a smaller version of the same story. The UK Health Security Agency raised an amber heat health alert covering the East Midlands, East of England, London and the South East from Wednesday morning, with temperatures forecast to reach 35°C in the south-east — the fourth heatwave of the UK's summer, after record-breaking heat in May and June. The alert specifically flags elevated mortality risk for people over 65. Britain's grid operator, NESO, issued an overnight call for extra generation capacity as air conditioning and fans pushed demand up just as low wind speeds — a frequent companion of high-pressure heat domes — cut into wind output. It is not, on its own, a market-moving event. But it is one more data point in a summer where nearly every corner of western Europe has now had its heat-and-grid moment.

The immediate question is how much worse the next few days get before the heat breaks. AEMET's forecast has this week's peak arriving Friday; French crews are watching whether the Gironde fire's containment lines hold once temperatures climb again. The longer question, the one now sitting on trading desks rather than just weather desks, is whether next July looks the same — and whether that predictability is itself becoming the thing to hedge.
