Pentagon locks in $22.5bn fixed-price deal for nine attack submarines

USS New Hampshire (SSN 778), a Virginia-class attack submarine, at its 2008 commissioning — the class now moving to its sixth production block. — U.S. Navy photo by Mass Communication Specialist 1st Class Fernando Allen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Pentagon certified a fixed-price deal for nine Block VI submarines, projected to save $2.4bn versus the $22.5bn Block V benchmark, despite delivery delays.

The Pentagon has cleared the way for General Dynamics to build nine more Virginia-class attack submarines under a fixed-price contract — a structure that, for once, puts the cost-overrun risk on the shipbuilder rather than the taxpayer.

Michael Duffey, the Pentagon's undersecretary for acquisition, told the House Armed Services Committee in a letter dated June 29 that the proposed fixed-price deal for nine Block VI boats meets all legal requirements to proceed. Duffey's letter did not put a number on the contract itself, but the reference point in the market is the $22.5bn the Navy paid for the equivalent nine-boat Block V order — and Duffey said the new multi-year structure should save roughly $2.4bn against what a conventional contract would have cost. General Dynamics shares rose 1.2% on the news.

Fixed price, shifted risk

Most major shipbuilding contracts run on a cost-plus basis: the Pentagon pays the contractor's costs plus a fee, and overruns land largely on the government's books. A fixed-price structure flips that — General Dynamics's Electric Boat division agrees to a set price up front and eats any cost growth itself, aside from negotiated adjustments. It is a discipline the Pentagon has reached for only sparingly on programs this large, since contractors typically demand a premium to absorb the extra risk. That the Navy is willing to use it on a submarine program — the most technically demanding and delay-prone shipbuilding work it does — signals confidence that Electric Boat's cost base is now predictable enough to price in advance.

The pace problem hasn't gone away

That confidence exists despite an unresolved production problem. The Navy's target has been two Virginia-class boats delivered a year since 2011; actual output has run closer to 1.2–1.3 a year since 2022, a shortfall the House Armed Services Committee flagged again in May as Block V construction continuing to underperform. General Dynamics chief executive Phebe Novakovic has pointed to late deliveries of major components — reactor plants, castings, valves — from a supplier base that shrank during the low-order years of the 2010s, and the company slowed its build cadence in 2024 to work through the backlog. The Navy still expects the two-a-year rate only in the early 2030s, with Electric Boat and HII's Newport News Shipbuilding sharing the work.

The timing lines up with General Dynamics's own numbers: the company beat Wall Street estimates in results released the same morning, with Marine Systems — the submarine and shipbuilding division — posting double-digit revenue growth off the Virginia- and Columbia-class backlog. The Block VI award itself is not yet final; a formal contract with a disclosed price is expected to follow the legal certification, and that figure, once public, will show whether the $2.4bn savings estimate holds up against the $22.5bn benchmark it was measured against.
