NIESR: Iran-driven oil prices squeeze room for Burnham's autumn budget

Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street, days into a premiership already facing a fiscal squeeze — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (CC BY-NC-ND 2.0)
In one sentence
NIESR says Iran-war inflation has blown a £24bn hole in Burnham's spending plans, leaving tax rises or cuts as the only options before October's budget.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for nine days. The National Institute of Economic and Social Research has already told him he cannot borrow his way out of the hole the Iran war has dug in his budget.

In analysis published this week, NIESR put a number on the squeeze: roughly £24 billion, the estimated hit to public spending power over the course of this parliament as higher energy prices and stickier inflation eat into departmental budgets that were set only last year. The think tank traces the damage to the conflict's energy shock, which it estimates has already cost the UK economy about £15 billion in lost output. Consumer price inflation, NIESR now expects, will peak at 3.8% in February 2027 — higher than previously forecast — and won't return to the Bank of England's 2% target until early 2029, a year later than the think tank assumed before the fighting began. It expects the Bank to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% through 2026 and 2027 to keep it there.

A parliament-long squeeze, not a one-off

The mechanism is straightforward and familiar from the last two years of UK fiscal policy: oil and gas prices pushed up by the conflict around the Strait of Hormuz feed into headline inflation, which erodes the real value of cash-terms spending settlements agreed before the shock hit, while also discouraging the Bank of England from cutting rates that would otherwise ease the government's own borrowing costs. NIESR's point is that this isn't a rounding error to be absorbed quietly — it's large enough to force explicit trade-offs at the autumn budget, now expected in October and reportedly being combined with a full departmental spending review.

Choices, not more borrowing

"There's clearly no scope for increasing borrowing, so it is about choices," said Stephen Millard, NIESR's deputy director for macroeconomics, arguing that additional debt is out given the UK's already-stretched public finances. His recommendation is to close the gap through tax reform or spending reductions rather than raising income tax rates outright — pointing to welfare, the pensions triple lock, council tax and VAT exemptions as the more plausible targets.

That lands awkwardly against Burnham's own pitch. Since taking office on 20 July he has cast his government as a "cost-of-living" administration, promising to zero-rate VAT on domestic electricity from October — worth roughly £45 off the annual price cap — and to reinstate a £2 cap on bus fares outside London from January, alongside a commitment to lift defence spending to 3.5% of GDP by 2035. Those measures are being funded for now by cancelling the previous government's digital ID programme and redirecting climate-finance money, but NIESR's arithmetic suggests that kind of budget-line shuffling won't stretch to cover a £24 billion structural shortfall.

The October budget is where the trade-off gets made explicit. Markets will be watching less for the headline tax-and-spend numbers than for what they imply about the Bank of England's room to cut — a persistent oil shock that keeps inflation elevated narrows that room further, which is also why gilt yields have stayed close to two-month highs into the summer.
