EU's AI Omnibus takes effect, pushing high-risk compliance to 2027

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which drafted the AI Act's Digital Omnibus revisions. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The EU's AI Omnibus took effect July 27, delaying high-risk AI Act rules to December 2027 while Article 50 transparency duties still land August 2026.

The European Union's landmark AI law just got more complicated, not less. On July 27, a package known as the Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force, rewriting parts of the EU AI Act just as its toughest provisions were about to bite. The headline change: obligations for so-called high-risk AI systems — the kind used in credit scoring, hiring, and fraud detection — won't be mandatory until December 2, 2027, an 18-month reprieve from the original August 2026 deadline.

But anyone hoping for a clean delay is out of luck. A separate set of transparency rules, under Article 50 of the Act, stays exactly on schedule: businesses have until August 2, 2026 — six days from the law's entry into force — to disclose when a user is interacting with an AI system, and until December 2 to add machine-readable watermarking to AI-generated content. The result is a rulebook that arrived all at once but takes effect in pieces, leaving compliance teams to sort out which parts apply now and which can wait.

A regulation that arrives in installments

The timeline, as it now stands, has four separate dates. Article 50 transparency obligations land August 2, 2026, largely untouched by the Omnibus. A new ban on AI-generated non-consensual intimate imagery — the so-called "nudifier" provision — carries its own deadline of December 2, 2026, alongside the watermarking grace period for systems already on the market. The core high-risk obligations under Annex III, covering standalone systems in employment, education, law enforcement and financial services, shift to December 2, 2027. And AI embedded inside already-regulated products, such as medical devices or machinery, gets until August 2, 2028.

Henna Virkkunen, the European Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, security and democracy, framed the changes as a balancing act rather than a retreat. "Our businesses and citizens want two things from AI rules," she said when the political agreement was struck in May. "They want to be able to innovate and feel safe. Today's agreement does both. With simpler and innovation-friendly rules, we make it easier to innovate without lowering the bar on safety."

Why fintech is watching closely

For algorithmic trading firms and consumer fintechs, the split calendar creates a specific kind of headache. Credit scoring and creditworthiness assessment sit squarely in Annex III's high-risk category, alongside insurance risk pricing — meaning the heaviest obligations (documented risk-management systems, data governance, human oversight, accuracy and robustness testing) now don't bind until December 2027. Firms building those models get more runway to prepare technical files and bias-testing pipelines.

But that runway doesn't extend to transparency. Any fintech deploying a chatbot, an AI-driven advisory tool, or a system that generates synthetic content for customers still has to meet the August 2 disclosure requirements, on the original timetable. Layer in Europe's existing algorithmic-trading rules under MiFID II — kill-switch requirements, national regulator reporting, trade surveillance — and firms are left reconciling at least three overlapping clocks: securities law, AI Act transparency, and AI Act high-risk compliance, each ticking at a different pace.

Reprieve, or an invitation to ask for more

How the delay gets read depends on who's asking. Industry groups, including DIGITALEUROPE, broadly welcomed the simplification package as proportionate breathing room for a law that outran the pace at which technical standards and enforcement guidance could be written. Critics see something else: a coalition of more than 120 civil society organizations and trade unions had urged the Commission to abandon the Omnibus altogether, arguing it strips protections before they ever take effect. Corporate Europe Observatory and LobbyControl separately found that 69% of the Commission's 2025 meetings on the topic were with business groups, versus 16% with NGOs — fuel for the argument that the rewrite reflects industry wish-lists more than considered policy.

That tension matters for compliance planning. An 18-month delay is not the same as a settled rule; it is a deadline that has already moved once, under sustained lobbying, before it arrived. Firms budgeting for December 2027 compliance are making a bet that the date holds — a bet civil society groups are actively trying to make a bad one.

What comes next

The immediate test is August 2, 2026, when Article 50 disclosure obligations become enforceable regardless of a company's high-risk status — the first real-world check on whether the Commission's staggered approach holds together in practice. After that, watch December 2, 2026, when the nudifier ban and watermarking grace period expire together, and then the harmonized technical standards the AI Office is meant to publish well before the December 2027 high-risk deadline — standards whose delay, more than any political fight, is the likeliest reason this timeline moves again.
