LVMH shares fall as fashion division growth disappoints

Louis Vuitton's flagship store on the Champs-Élysées, the group's largest brand and the biggest drag on its recent results. — Jorge Láscar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
LVMH's fashion division grew for the first time in two years but missed forecasts, sending shares down and deepening doubts about a luxury recovery.

LVMH shares slipped as much as 2.6% in Tuesday trading, closing the session down 1.5%, after the world's largest luxury group reported second-quarter results that showed its core fashion and leather goods division growing again — but not by enough to convince investors the industry's two-year slump is actually over.

The division, home to Louis Vuitton, Dior and Loro Piana, posted organic sales growth of 1% to €8.90 billion ($10.12 billion) in the quarter. That ended seven consecutive quarters of decline, the longest slide in the unit's history. But analysts had penciled in growth of 1.7%, and the miss was enough to send the stock — already down roughly 30% for the year and trading near a six-year low — lower again.

A recovery that isn't quite one yet

Group-wide, LVMH did better: overall organic revenue rose 3% in the quarter to roughly €19.5 billion, or 4% stripping out an estimated 1% drag the company attributed to the Iran war's effect on Gulf mall traffic and European tourism. Watches and jewellery led the way, up 11%; Sephora-led selective retailing grew 6%; wines and spirits added 5%. First-half revenue came to €38.6 billion, up 2% organically, though profit from recurring operations fell 4% to €8.7 billion as margins compressed slightly to 22.5%.

The geography of demand was telling. The US, South Korea and Japan accelerated, with LVMH pointing to AI-linked wealth creation among buyers in those markets. Europe was softer, weighed down by the Iran conflict's tourism effects, while Chinese demand stayed largely flat — the market whose slowdown triggered the original downturn in 2023 has yet to properly turn.

Why the market wasn't satisfied

UBS analysts called the print "decent, but unlikely to change the debate," and RBC, Morgan Stanley and UBS all trimmed price targets afterward. The more pointed critique came from independent luxury strategist Rafael Carlesso, who argued the fashion division's return to growth looked "supported by pricing and cost discipline rather than meaningful improvement in demand" — a distinction that matters for a business whose investment case rests on aspirational buyers coming back, not on price increases doing the work. Morningstar's Jelena Sokolova was more constructive, noting trends were "turning more positive," even as she flagged that LVMH continues to lag its closest rivals: Kering shares are down 17% this year and Hermès down 21%, though both had entered 2026 from a stronger footing.

Dior's improving momentum under new creative director Jonathan Anderson was one of the few unambiguous bright spots cited alongside the results, a reminder that within the fashion division's aggregate numbers sit brand-by-brand stories that don't move in unison.

The read-through

LVMH's results function as a proxy for the health of the world's wealthiest consumers, and Tuesday's reaction suggests the market isn't yet ready to price in a durable turn. A single quarter of modest growth, achieved partly through pricing rather than volume, is a thin basis for calling the luxury downturn over — particularly with Chinese demand still stalled and geopolitical disruption chewing into European and Gulf traffic. The next test comes with third-quarter figures in October, alongside earnings from Kering and Richemont that will show whether LVMH's fractional improvement is an industry inflection or simply the least-bad print among a still-struggling cohort.
