China pivots to consumption-led stimulus as growth slows to 4.3%

The Great Hall of the People in Beijing, where China's Communist Party leadership sets economic policy priorities at its Politburo meetings. — Xiquinho Silva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
China's leaders shifted policy language toward household spending after Q2 growth slowed to 4.3% and factory PMI stayed in contraction for a third month.

China's economy grew at its slowest pace since the tail end of 2022 last quarter, and for the first time this year Beijing's policy language has shifted unmistakably toward getting ordinary households to spend more, rather than leaning again on factories and building sites.

Second-quarter GDP rose 4.3% year-on-year, the National Bureau of Statistics reported, down from a first-quarter pace that helped keep first-half growth at 4.7% — technically inside the government's 4.5%-to-5% full-year target, but the weakest single quarter in three and a half years. Underneath that headline, the report card was uneven enough to worry planners: manufacturing PMI has now spent three straight months below the 50 line that separates expansion from contraction, coming in at 49.4 in July, down from 49.5 in June and 49.something in May, according to NBS data. Factories are still running, in other words, but a shrinking share of purchasing managers say things are getting better rather than worse.

A quarter that missed on the ground, not in the export lane

The unevenness is the story. Exports have been the one unambiguous bright spot — shipments rose 27% year-on-year in June alone, with AI-related hardware imports and exports up 56.6%, and high-tech investment climbing 4.6% even as broader capital spending fell. Fixed-asset investment dropped 5.7% in the first half, driven almost entirely by a property sector that will not stop deflating: real-estate investment fell 18%, and new construction starts were down 23.4% year-on-year, an acceleration from the 22.6% decline recorded through May.

Household spending, meanwhile, is limping rather than collapsing. Retail sales rose 2.7% in the first half and 1.0% year-on-year in June — an improvement on May's outright 0.6% contraction, but still soft by the standards of an economy that used to post consumption growth in the high single digits. The composition told its own story: communication equipment sales jumped 16.5% and cosmetics 12.6%, but car sales slumped 16.1% and home appliances fell 8.7%, a pattern consistent with a household sector trading down and deferring big-ticket purchases. Economists have taken to describing 2026 as a K-shaped economy — semiconductors and AI-linked manufacturing pulling one way, property and traditional consumption pulling the other — and the gap between those two arms widened again last quarter.

The pivot in Beijing's language

What changed at the Communist Party's late-July Politburo meeting was not a stimulus number but a vocabulary. Official statements called for the country to "give play to the fundamental role of consumption in driving economic growth," naming autos, electronics and household goods for support alongside services spending — sports, leisure, culture, tourism — that Beijing has struggled for years to get households to loosen their wallets for. That language followed, rather than preceded, the rollout in mid-July of China's first-ever stand-alone national consumption plan, which sets a 2030 retail-sales target of 60 trillion yuan, nearly 20% above 2025 levels, and pairs it with an "urban and rural household income growth plan" built around minimum-wage increases, expanded tax deductions and higher pension payouts.

That sequencing matters. Beijing has talked about rebalancing toward consumption for the better part of a decade without much to show for it; infrastructure and export credit have historically been the levers policymakers actually pulled when growth wobbled. The decision to name a specific retail-sales target and attach it to household income measures — rather than another round of local-government bond issuance for roads and rail — is the clearest signal yet that officials see the current soft patch as more structural than seasonal.

Why this reads as more than the usual hedging

China's central bank has left its policy rate unchanged for 14 consecutive months, and several analysts still expect the Politburo to favor "making good use of existing policies" and faster fiscal delivery — over 7 trillion yuan earmarked for infrastructure this year — rather than a fresh headline stimulus package, with ballooning local-government debt cited as the constraint. Some China watchers argue consumption is still being treated as a by-product of industrial upgrading and employment rather than a policy lever in its own right, meaning the rhetorical shift could outrun the actual spending.

That tension is precisely why the pivot is notable. A government confident that weak demand was cyclical — a hangover from Iran-related trade disruption, a soft property comparison, a temporary export lull — would not bother rewriting its language around a 2030 retail target and a household-income plan. Doing so implies planners now think the consumption shortfall predates this quarter's PMI readings and will outlast them without a change in how households save, borrow and spend.

The next test comes with the NBS's July activity data due in mid-August, which will show whether retail sales continued June's tentative rebound or slipped back toward contraction, and with the pace of new consumption-plan implementation — vouchers, trade-in subsidies, service-sector spending drives — that Beijing has promised but not yet quantified. Markets exposed to Chinese commodity demand and regional growth, including Rio Tinto's iron-ore and copper volumes and Kospi-linked supply chains rattled by CXMT's Shanghai listing, will be watching whether Beijing's words translate into a demand recovery or simply mark the moment planners admitted the problem is bigger than they'd said.
