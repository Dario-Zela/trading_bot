Barclays now sees Fed hikes in December and January as Warsh gives nothing away

In one sentence
Barclays now forecasts 25bp Fed hikes in both December and January after hotter inflation, while Chair Warsh gave no policy signal at an IMF event in Thailand.

Barclays has moved to a faster Fed tightening path, forecasting two consecutive quarter-point rate rises, in December and again in January. The Fed itself has offered no sign it is heading that way: Chair Kevin Warsh spoke at an IMF event in Thailand and gave nothing away on policy.

The call, reported on Friday, is a sell-side forecast, not a change in policy. It does not alter the Fed's stance, and it will matter to rate-sensitive markets only if other forecasters adopt it.

What Barclays changed

The bank now expects the Fed to raise rates by 25 basis points in December and by another 25 in January. Reuters reported that Barclays had previously expected a single hike, in December, so the change adds a second move rather than pulling one forward. The stated reason was stronger-than-expected inflation readings. The syndicated report gave no figures, and does not say which inflation measure prompted the revision.

The forecast is the latest turn in a sequence. At the end of August, after Warsh's Jackson Hole speech, which Barclays called "notably hawkish", the bank had reversed an earlier no-change view and pencilled in hikes in September and December. Warsh had said policymakers would have "work to do" if inflation did not clearly head back towards 2%. Barclays' latest revision builds on that more hawkish footing rather than starting afresh.

Warsh offers no guidance

At the IMF event in Thailand, Warsh gave no policy signal, according to the Benzinga report. That is consistent with his habit of resisting explicit forward guidance. The gap is the point of the story: a bank is putting dates and sizes on moves the Fed chair has declined to preview.

A forecast that runs ahead of the central bank is not unusual, and it is not evidence the Fed agrees. Barclays' own record in this cycle shows how quickly such calls move: the path has been revised at least twice in about seven weeks, each time in response to data or to Warsh's language rather than to any announced decision.

What would give the call weight

Two things would. The first is other dealers following. A single bank's back-to-back hikes are an outlier until a few others join; if they do, the shift would show up in futures pricing for the December and January meetings. The second is the Fed's own communication: the next FOMC statement, the minutes of the previous meeting and any remarks from Warsh's colleagues are the places where a second hike would first need to be made plausible.

The inflation prints before December are the most direct test. Barclays attributes its change to hotter data; a softer reading would undercut the January leg in particular, since that is the move furthest from any Fed signal. Until then the forecast is untested, and the Fed's silence leaves it that way.
