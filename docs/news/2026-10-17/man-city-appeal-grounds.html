Do Manchester City have any grounds to win their appeal?

In one sentence
City face 114 proven charges and say they will appeal; with the grounds unpublished, the stronger routes look procedural, and the outcome tests the league's rulebook as much as the club.

Manchester City have been found guilty of 114 of the 115 charges the Premier League brought against them, and they say they will appeal. The club has called itself innocent. What matters now is how much room the appeal has to succeed. Appeals against disciplinary panels rarely re-run the evidence. They ask whether the panel applied the rules correctly and fairly. On that test the case's real weakness may be the league's own rulebook, not City's accounts.

Where the case stands

The league charged City in February 2023 over alleged breaches of its financial rules covering roughly 2009 to 2018. The independent commission heard the case in private from 16 September to 6 December 2024. The verdict was reported on 25 September 2026 and the league confirmed it in a statement on 29 September. The panel found City guilty of all 80 charges of financial wrongdoing and 34 of the 35 charges of failing to cooperate with the league's investigation.

The commission has not yet set a sanction. That comes in a separate hearing, and the options run from a fine to a points deduction to expulsion. Reporting on the appeal is thin. The grounds and the filing date have not been confirmed by a major outlet, and the hearing does not appear to have been scheduled. Any assessment of City's chances is therefore an assessment of the type of appeal likely to be available, not of a published argument.

What an appeal can realistically do

Sporting appeal bodies tend to defer to the original panel on findings of fact. A club arguing that the commission misjudged the evidence over nine seasons faces a steep climb. The stronger routes are procedural, and there are three of them:

  • Time. Much of the alleged conduct is more than a decade old. City can argue that the rules or the league's powers did not reach back that far, or that the delay made a fair hearing impossible.
  • Rule validity. If a rule the commission relied on was drafted or applied in a way the league's own constitution does not allow, the finding built on it is exposed.
  • Fairness of the process. This covers the handling of evidence, the failure-to-cooperate charges and the confidentiality of the hearing.

The cooperation charges look like the club's biggest exposure, since 34 of 35 were upheld. They also cut the other way. A panel that found City obstructed an investigation may have been less willing to give the club the benefit of the doubt on the financial charges, and City may argue that this coloured the whole case.

The league's rulebook is on trial too

City have already tested the league's rules in a separate dispute over its associated-party transaction regime, where a tribunal found parts of the rules unlawful. That ruling is why this appeal matters beyond one club. The commission's guilty verdict gives the league its first large-scale enforcement win over club finances. A successful appeal on process grounds would not clear City of the underlying allegations in a sporting sense, but it could show that the league's machinery cannot carry a case this size.

The cost of that outcome is mostly the league's. Other clubs, including those already penalised for financial breaches, would study the reasoning. Future cases would be drafted with an eye to the appeal. A league that wins the case but loses on procedure arguably ends up in a weaker position than one that loses on the evidence.

What to watch

Three things are worth tracking:

  1. Whether the league or the club publishes the grounds of appeal.
  2. The date set for the appeal hearing.
  3. The timing of the sanctions hearing, which will show how the league handles a verdict that is still under challenge.

Until then, City's results are not at issue. What is at issue is whether the league's rules and procedures can survive a challenge from a well-funded opponent.
