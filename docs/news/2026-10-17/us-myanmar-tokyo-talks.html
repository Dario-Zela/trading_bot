US holds secret talks with Myanmar's military-backed rulers in Tokyo

In one sentence
Reuters reports the US opened direct talks with Myanmar's military-backed government, starting with a June Tokyo meeting, but sanctions on its leader remain and details are thin.

The United States has opened direct talks with Myanmar's military-backed government, according to a Reuters exclusive published on 8 October. The report says the contacts reverse years of Washington's policy of isolating the generals who seized power in the 2021 coup. The Tokyo meeting in the headline was an early link in that chain. It took place in June, and the report names no date for it.

The triage framing of "secret talks" in Tokyo undersells what Reuters actually reported. The Tokyo session was one step in a broader channel that has since moved to Naypyitaw, Myanmar's capital.

What Reuters reported

The September talks "build on a June meeting between US and Myanmar officials in Tokyo," which also involved Michael Vance, four people told Reuters. Vance is the State Department's top intelligence official. One of the sources said officials from the Treasury Department, the Department of Homeland Security and the Pentagon took part. Reuters said it was unclear who represented Myanmar.

Vance then joined talks with Min Aung Hlaing on 15–16 September in Naypyitaw. Min Aung Hlaing is the coup leader who is now styled president. Reuters said the aim was to secure the release of Adam Castillo, an American businessman detained at Yangon airport on 11 June. The meeting also opened the door to wider engagement. Castillo has since been freed. He declined to comment beyond crediting President Donald Trump.

The outreach has focused on shutting down scam centres that target Americans. Reuters reported it could extend to other areas, including Myanmar's rare-earth deposits.

What Washington says, and what it has not

The State Department has not disowned the contacts. Spokesperson Tommy Pigott said: "You don't solve problems by ignoring them. You solve problems by addressing them." He said the department engages with all groups in Myanmar and presses for an end to violence and the release of political prisoners. He declined to address Vance's role. The administration, he said, "operates as a team with the president as the lead."

The sanctions picture is mixed. Min Aung Hlaing remains personally under US sanctions, which were imposed over human rights abuses including the 2016–17 expulsion of hundreds of thousands of Rohingya Muslims. Separately, one outlet reported that Treasury removed several junta-linked businesses and their owners from its sanctions list, among them KT Services & Logistics and the MCM Group. The reports I found give no date for that notice and no reason from Treasury. The two moves are not shown to be connected.

Reaction and the open questions

Representative Gregory Meeks, the top Democrat on the House foreign affairs committee, called the shift "contrary to both our values and our interests." He said Secretary of State Marco Rubio was "ceding US leadership and providing this murderous regime greater international legitimacy."

The timing sharpens the criticism. Less than two weeks after Vance's visit, a military airstrike on a crowded market in Rakhine state killed more than 50 people, according to UN human rights officials.

Several points are still unclear. The Myanmar side of the Tokyo meeting is unnamed, and no exact date has been given. Nothing in the coverage I found says Japan hosted the session as a formal mediator. So reading Tokyo as a deliberate back channel is inference from the venue. Most other outlets have carried the same Reuters report, so they are not independent confirmation.

What to watch

The test is whether engagement turns into sanctions action. Watch for further Treasury delistings, any move on Min Aung Hlaing's own designation, and whether the rare-earth discussions become formal. Min Aung Hlaing's reported visit to Malaysia, at Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim's invitation, will show how regional partners are treating the new US posture.
