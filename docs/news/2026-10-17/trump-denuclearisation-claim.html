Trump says Washington is talking to Moscow and Beijing about denuclearisation

In one sentence
Trump told Newsmax that Washington is talking to Moscow and Beijing about denuclearisation, but gave no detail and neither government nor the State Department has confirmed it.

President Donald Trump said in a television interview that Washington is talking to Moscow and Beijing about denuclearisation. He gave no detail, and as of Saturday neither the Kremlin, the Chinese government nor the State Department had confirmed that any such talks exist.

The remark came in an interview with Newsmax, reported by Benzinga. The only parties named were Russia and China. The accounts available give no dates, venue, negotiators or agenda, and nothing suggests the discussions have a formal status. A president's description of a conversation can mean anything from a leader-level phone call to staff contacts to a passing exchange. The remark alone does not show which of these Trump meant.

Why the claim would matter

If real trilateral negotiations were under way, they would be the most significant arms-control development in years. The United States, Russia and China are the three largest nuclear powers, and they have never negotiated limits together. Cold War and post-Cold War treaties were bilateral, between Washington and Moscow. China, with a much smaller arsenal, has consistently refused to join talks on that basis.

The bilateral framework has also lapsed. New START, the last treaty limiting US and Russian strategic forces, expired on 5 February 2026. Before expiry, Vladimir Putin had offered to keep the treaty's numerical limits for a further year if Washington did the same. Axios reported in February that the two sides were working on a way to keep observing the limits, citing anonymous sources, and Russia's foreign minister said Moscow would stay within them as long as the US did. The two governments also agreed to restore high-level military-to-military dialogue and said new arms-control talks were needed. None of those reports described a Chinese role.

A familiar line

The phrasing is not new. In early 2025 Trump said he wanted denuclearisation talks with Russia and China and floated the idea of all three halving their defence budgets. Later he said he had raised the denuclearisation of weapons with China. In neither case did a negotiating process follow in public. Against that record, a new mention is better read as a restatement of an aspiration than as evidence of a process, unless another government says otherwise.

What would settle it

Confirmation would have to come from one of three places. The Kremlin, which has repeatedly said it is open to dialogue, could describe contacts. Beijing's foreign ministry could say whether it has agreed to any discussion; its past position has been that its arsenal is not comparable to the two largest and that it will not join talks on those terms. The State Department could say who is negotiating and what is on the table.

The wider diplomatic calendar crowds the picture. Washington's dealings with Moscow are currently dominated by Ukraine and sanctions, and its dealings with Beijing by trade and market-access disputes. Arms control could be folded into either, or could go unmentioned in both.

The next signal is whether the Kremlin's or Beijing's regular briefings address the remark, and whether the State Department supplies any detail on talks it has so far not described.
