Nandy 'minded to intervene' in Sky's £1.6bn bid for ITV's entertainment arm

In one sentence
Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy says she is minded to launch a public-interest review of Sky's £1.6bn bid for ITV's broadcasting arm, with responses due 20 October.

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy has told Parliament she is "minded to intervene" in Sky's £1.6bn purchase of ITV's Media & Entertainment arm. That is a preliminary step, not a decision. It does start the clock on whether the sale of one of Britain's last large commercial broadcasters to a Comcast-owned group gets a formal public-interest review.

What was announced

On 15 October Nandy set out her thinking in a statement and a letter to ITV. She said she had not made a final decision. Sky and ITV have until Tuesday 20 October to respond, and she will then decide whether to issue a formal intervention notice.

The deal covers ITV's broadcasting and streaming business: the ITV1 channels and the ITVX platform. ITV's studio operations are not included. It is subject to shareholder and regulatory approval, and would create the UK's largest commercial broadcaster. Sky is owned by Comcast and also owns Sky News. The companies have said Sky News and ITV News will remain editorially distinct. ITV has said it will "fully co-operate" with the government's scrutiny.

The grounds Nandy cited are the usual ones under the Enterprise Act 2002:

  • free expression in news media;
  • plurality of views in news markets;
  • plurality of ownership across media enterprises.

She also floated something new. If she intervenes, she would legislate to amend section 58 of the Act so that a further public-interest consideration covers the availability of a wide range of on-demand programming, not just broadcast. That matters because ITVX is part of what Sky is buying.

How the process would run

If Nandy issues the notice, Ofcom would assess the public-interest issues and report to her, while the Competition and Markets Authority looks at competition. She would then decide whether a more detailed CMA investigation is needed. That decision is the point at which ministers could clear the deal, seek remedies, or block it.

The route has precedent, though not much of it. In 2007 an intervention notice was issued over BSkyB's purchase of a 17.5% stake in ITV, and Ofcom flagged concerns about "sufficient plurality of news provision". In 2010 the then Secretary of State issued a notice on News Corp's bid for the rest of Sky and asked Ofcom to report on plurality.

What is at stake

The plurality argument rests on a simple count. Sky News and ITV News are two of the main commercial television news suppliers, and the BBC is the other big one. Common ownership would remove one of the few independent voices in that group, whatever editorial assurances the buyer gives. Sky's side will say the two newsrooms can be kept separate and that ITV's commercial position is weakening. The on-demand limb gives ministers a second route to scrutiny that does not depend on the news-plurality case alone.

One caveat on sourcing: the reporting here comes from the government's published letter and wire and press coverage of the statement. Some outlets quote the deal at $2.1bn, which is the same £1.6bn converted.

What to watch

The first marker is 20 October, when Sky and ITV's responses are due. A formal notice would follow if Nandy decides to proceed, and it would set the timetable for Ofcom's report. Any draft amendment to section 58 would show how far ministers mean to extend media-plurality law to streaming.
