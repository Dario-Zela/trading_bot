Why hasn't the UK had any named storms yet this season?

In one sentence
No named storm yet this season reflects a lack of damaging weather, not a trend; the naming scheme tracks expected impact, and a decade of data is too short to read a pattern.

Autumn has so far passed without a named storm in the UK or Ireland. For many readers that will feel like a small piece of good luck. As a signal about the climate, it carries much less weight than it seems to.

What a name actually means

The naming scheme is run jointly by the Met Office and Met Éireann, with the Dutch service KNMI also taking part, and its season runs from 1 September to 31 August. A name is not handed out when a low-pressure system reaches a certain depth or on a fixed date. Forecasters assign one when they expect a system to cause significant impacts, usually because it is likely to prompt an amber or red weather warning for wind, rain or snow.

By that definition, a season with no names yet is a season with no disruptive systems yet. It does not mean the Atlantic has been empty. Ordinary autumn lows can pass through, bring a wet and blustery day, and never meet the threshold. The scheme measures consequences, which means it is a poor stopwatch for the storm calendar.

Is a late start unusual?

The record is uneven enough that a quiet first half of autumn is not remarkable on its own. Storm Ashley, the first name of the 2024–25 season, arrived on 20 October 2024. Storm Amy, the first of 2025–26, came in early October 2025. Storm Arwen, the first of 2021–22, did not appear until late November, and it was a damaging one.

Those dates sit across a span of roughly two months from the start of the season. Since naming began in 2015, a first name in September has been common, but a first name in October or November has happened more than once. A season that is still unnamed in mid-October is on the late side of that range. It is not outside it, and it has not set a record on the evidence available. Without a long series of first-storm dates, and only a decade of naming, no trend can be read from one year.

What would make it a climate story

Two things could. The first is a clear statistical pattern: first named storms arriving systematically later, or fewer named storms per season over many years. A decade of data under a naming system that has changed its practices is too short to show that. The second is a physical link to the jet stream, the fast current of air that steers Atlantic lows towards Britain. When the jet is strong and tracks over the UK, storms follow. When it weakens, buckles or sits further north or south, the weather can turn settled for weeks.

Research on how climate change affects the jet stream is still contested, and the signal in it is hard to pick out from natural year-to-year swings. A calm autumn here may reflect a northward-shifted storm track, a persistent blocking high, or simple chance. The coverage so far does not say which, and this report has not been able to check the claim against historical records independently.

What to watch

The season has more than ten months left to run. Winter is when the Atlantic usually delivers its strongest systems, and the Met Office's first name of the season will be the next real data point. If it comes after late November, it would put this year among the latest starts since naming began. Whether that points to anything beyond the weather will depend on the seasons that follow.
