Robbins reaches settlement with government over sacking

In one sentence
A reported six-figure settlement ends Sir Olly Robbins's challenge to his sacking over Mandelson's vetting, leaving unanswered whether the dismissal was lawful.

The government has reached a settlement with Sir Olly Robbins, the former Foreign Office permanent secretary it dismissed in April over the vetting of Peter Mandelson, according to reports that put the payment at six figures. The terms have not been published in detail, and the sum has not been confirmed independently. If the figure is right, the state has put a price on its own decision to remove one of its most senior officials.

How the dispute began

Robbins was removed in mid-April, within hours of the Guardian reporting that Mandelson had failed his security vetting in late 2024. Downing Street said he had lost the confidence of both the Prime Minister and the Foreign Secretary. Its case was that ministers had not been told of the failure. Keir Starmer went further, saying Robbins had kept him in the dark.

Robbins told the Foreign Affairs Committee a different story. He described a "dismissive approach" from No 10 to the vetting process. He called the vetting team's case "borderline" and said ministers were not normally told anything about vetting beyond the outcome. He also said the clearance decision was his to take as head of his department. The FDA union, which represents senior civil servants, said the sacking had sent a "chill" through Whitehall.

The legal challenge

In July Robbins sought judicial review. His submission argued that the Prime Minister has no statutory power to dismiss the head of the diplomatic service, and that the dismissal followed no fair procedure. He said at the time that an apology and amends from the Prime Minister would have made a legal case unnecessary.

Those two arguments explain why a payout carries weight. Settling avoids a court ruling on who may dismiss a permanent secretary and by what process. A judgment against the government would have set a precedent. A confidential settlement leaves the question unanswered.

What the payment suggests

A settlement does not amount to an admission of fault, and officials will stress that. The reading it invites is still hard to avoid. Ministers who believed the dismissal was lawful and defensible would have had little reason to pay to avoid a hearing. The more likely explanation is that they judged the procedural arguments risky, or that they wanted to keep ministers' handling of the vetting from being examined in open court. Neither explanation has been confirmed by the government.

The consequences reach beyond one man. Senior civil servants have watched to see whether a minister can sack a permanent secretary in an afternoon, with no process, and face no cost. A six-figure payment suggests there may be a cost.

What to watch

The first test is whether the terms are disclosed. The Foreign Affairs Committee, which heard Robbins's evidence, may press for them, and Treasury rules on the reporting of severance payments may also apply. The second is whether the government now sets out in writing how a permanent secretary can be removed. The FDA has argued that it should. The Mandelson vetting affair itself remains unresolved, and the settlement leaves its central question open: who knew what, and when.
