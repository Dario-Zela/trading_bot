Banks brace for a higher tax bill at the Budget as the IMF urges Healey to look at pensions or welfare instead

In one sentence
UK banks are bracing for a higher bank-profits surcharge at the 28 October Budget, reportedly worth about £2.25bn a year, while the IMF points Healey towards pensions or welfare.

Britain's biggest lenders are preparing for a larger tax bill at the Budget on 28 October. The Chancellor, John Healey, has not ruled out a rise in the levy on bank profits. The IMF has meanwhile pointed him towards pensions and welfare as places to look for money. Bank shareholders are therefore being asked to price a decision that has not been made, and the Treasury says it does not comment on Budget speculation.

The scale reported so far is modest against the sector's profits, but it is large against what the Treasury has to find. This account rests on press reports and the Treasury's silence. The BBC's report that lenders expect an increase was reviewed by headline only.

What is being priced

UK banks pay the main corporation tax rate of 25% and, on top of that, a surcharge on profits above an allowance. The surcharge was cut from 8% to 3% from April 2023, and the allowance was raised from £25m to £100m. The combined rate for the larger lenders is 28%. Smaller groups below the allowance no longer pay the surcharge at all. These are the 2021–22 legislative settings. This piece has not confirmed that they are unchanged in October 2026.

Reports put the Chancellor's ambition at about £2.25bn a year, raised by restoring a higher surcharge. That is a press estimate, not a Treasury figure. Nobody has published the rate or the threshold. The TUC has asked for a surcharge of at least 8%, the pre-2023 level. Even partway back to that level would remove a visible slice of earnings. As a rough illustration only, each percentage point of surcharge on the sector's taxable profits costs lenders on the order of hundreds of millions of pounds a year. The exact figure depends on how the allowance is treated and which entities are caught.

The timing matters for the shares. Analysts usually cut forecasts for net profit as soon as a tax change looks likely, not when it is legislated. A consensus earnings number for FTSE 100 lenders can therefore fall before the Chancellor has spoken. No named bank, rate or revenue estimate came with the original triage. Any quantified claim about a single lender's hit would be conjecture at this point.

The lobbying

The industry has not stayed quiet. Reports say the Chancellor has summoned bank chiefs. UK Finance argues that higher taxes would weaken the competitiveness of UK banking. Jamie Dimon of JP Morgan has warned Healey against the move. A former Chancellor, Jeremy Hunt, has said raising bank taxes will reduce jobs.

These arguments are standard, and they are not empty. A surcharge falls on profits, and profits are what fund lending capacity, dividends and buybacks. The counter-argument is as familiar. Banks earned wide net interest margins as rates rose, and the surcharge is a way to take a share of that. Healey has also reportedly committed to keeping the main corporation tax rate at a maximum of 25%. A bank-specific levy is one of the few ways to raise corporate revenue within that pledge.

The IMF's different advice

The IMF's intervention complicates the picture. The fund praised Britain's progress in cutting the deficit and urged Healey to stay the course. That means balancing day-to-day spending with tax revenue by 2029/30. According to the headline that prompted this piece, the Fund's advice was to look at pensions or welfare rather than banks. The detail of that advice was not available to this desk, so what follows is inference.

The gap with the reported plans is plain enough. Taxing banks is politically easy and raises money quickly. Restraining pensions or welfare is slower and costs the government votes. The Chancellor has reportedly signalled that some welfare savings could come from tackling youth unemployment. The British Chambers of Commerce has suggested scrapping the State Pension triple lock to fund cuts in employer National Insurance. Both are options that sit on the spending side of the ledger.

The IMF is a lender-of-last-resort institution with no say over the Budget. Its view still shapes how gilt investors read it. A Budget that closes the gap mainly through a sector levy may look less durable than one that makes structural spending choices.

What to watch

The Budget on 28 October is the catalyst. Three numbers will decide how much the banks' earnings are marked down: the surcharge rate, the profit allowance, and the start date. A rate of 8% would be a large step. A smaller rise with the £100m allowance intact would hit only the biggest lenders. The Office for Budget Responsibility's costing, published alongside the speech, will show whether the Treasury's own estimate matches the £2.25bn in the reports. Third-quarter results from the FTSE 100 banks, due in the weeks around the Budget, give the lenders a first chance to tell investors how they are treating the risk.
