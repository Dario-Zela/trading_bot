HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-17 · 5 min read

Ship attacks near Hormuz hit a record as Washington taps the oil reserve and tightens Iran sanctions

The oil tanker Al Shaffiah sails off the Omani coast near Musandam, on the Strait of Hormuz, in June. — Reuters
In one sentence
Attacks on ships near Hormuz hit a wartime record, likely undercounted because UKMTO reporting is voluntary, as Gulf exports slump and Washington lends oil and tightens Iran sanctions.

Attacks on commercial ships around the Strait of Hormuz have reached the highest level of the five-month-old Iran war. The official tally probably understates them. The main public record, run by the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre (UKMTO), depends on ship operators choosing to report, and UKMTO says many struck vessels are not doing so. As the attacks have risen, Gulf oil exports have fallen back and Brent crude has touched $110 a barrel. Washington is answering on two fronts: it is lending oil from its emergency stockpile and tightening sanctions on Tehran.

A record, and its limits

The figures behind the word "record" come from several trackers that do not agree. Eleven tankers were attacked in the week ending 4 October, the most since the war began in late February, according to International Maritime Organization data cited by CNBC. In the following week UKMTO counted at least 12 vessels struck, including six in a single day, its highest daily tally of the conflict. CNBC reported on 16 October that at least nine commercial ships had been attacked since Sunday 11 October. US Central Command says Iran sank the container ship GFS Pride. Iran's Revolutionary Guards say a mine did it, and Central Command calls that claim false and says the ship sank while under tow.

The counts are probably low, and the clearest evidence is the delay in the reporting itself. Trade-press accounts of the past three weeks show UKMTO issuing "time-late" warnings. An attack on 16 September was not made public until the 18th. Four warnings covering strikes on 28 and 29 September were published afterwards. On 28 September UKMTO logged a ship hit by a suspected projectile, which started a fire, as "suspicious activity" rather than an attack.

GCaptain reported that the system is voluntary and that its notices often do not name the vessel. That makes company totals hard to reconcile with public ones. ADNOC, the Abu Dhabi state oil company, said in August that 15 of its ships had been targeted since the conflict began, killing one crew member and injuring 20. Some ships also sail with their transponders switched off, so they are missing from tracking data.

The claim that the true number is "far higher" rests on a single newsletter source, and it is an inference, not a measured figure. No independent count exists to set against UKMTO's. The accurate reading is that the record is a floor. Nobody outside the shipping companies and navies knows how far above it the real figure sits.

What it does to the oil

The link to exports is clearer. UBS estimates that Gulf crude and product flows fell to about 9.1 million barrels a day in recent days, from an early-October peak of about 17 million. Before the war, roughly 20 million barrels a day moved through the strait, a figure Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has used. The recovery was fragile. Kpler data put September Middle East crude exports above 16 million barrels a day, with about 9.7 million of that moving through Hormuz. The strait's commercial traffic is nowhere near its prewar scale in ship numbers. GCaptain, citing Kpler and vessel tracking, put daily transits at about 18, against some 125 large commercial vessels before the conflict.

Sources differ on how bad it is. A Vanguard analyst, quoted by AFP, said traffic had recently recovered to about 80% of prewar levels. That gap is part of the problem. Every measure of the strait, whether ship counts, barrels or attacks, captures only part of the picture. Insurers and charterers have to price risk from the same incomplete data, and an undercounted threat is likely to be priced too low until a large loss corrects it.

Washington's two levers

The first lever is the Strategic Petroleum Reserve. Benzinga reports that the US is seeking an exchange of up to 17 million barrels. We could not check that figure against a Department of Energy notice. It would be a loan, not a sale: companies take crude now and return it later with extra barrels as a premium. The terms, return schedule and premium for this round are not in the material available. The mechanism is familiar. The department has run a series of exchanges since March as part of a 172-million-barrel US contribution to a 400-million-barrel International Energy Agency commitment. The most recent solicitation we could find, dated 30 September, was for up to 40 million barrels. The department says the programme will eventually return about 200 million barrels to the reserve.

The second lever is financial. Bessent has led an economic campaign against Iran since August, when he promised the toughest sanctions yet and called the effort an "economic D-Day". He argued that maximum economic pressure makes a renewed large-scale military campaign less likely, "for now". A US naval blockade has not reopened the strait on Tehran's terms, and Iran's foreign ministry calls the measures "economic terrorism". Benzinga's latest item says Bessent is stepping up that pressure again. It gives no new measures, figures or dates, and we have not confirmed them against a Treasury release. The same item said US futures were mixed and the Nasdaq weaker. That is a soft session, and it does not show that sanctions are moving markets.

The two levers pull in different directions on oil. Sanctions are meant to squeeze Iranian revenue, and they do not add barrels to the market. The reserve adds barrels but cannot protect the ships carrying them. With a stockpile already drawn well down, below 300 million barrels by mid-August by one tracker's count, each further loan leaves less cushion for a longer disruption.

What to watch

The next test is whether UKMTO's published figures converge on the tallies from insurers, port states and the navies escorting ships. A revision upward would confirm the underreporting. Beyond that, three signals matter. One is the Energy Department's formal notice and the bids it draws. Another is the weekly Gulf export estimates from Kpler and UBS. The third is whether Treasury publishes the new designations that Benzinga's item implies. If attacks keep rising while reporting lags, the risk to oil supply will be larger than the official numbers show.
