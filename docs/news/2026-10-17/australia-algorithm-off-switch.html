Australia weighs an 'off switch' for recommendation algorithms

In one sentence
Australia is moving a proposal to let users switch off recommendation algorithms from draft towards a bill, but scope, enforcement and passage are unsettled.

Australia's government wants social media users to be able to switch off the recommendation algorithms that decide what appears in their feeds. The idea is still on the way to becoming law and is not law yet. Reports from early September described a draft. A later report says a bill has since been introduced, but the sources disagree on details and none of this has been voted on.

The proposal is part of a wider "digital duty of care" framework. Communications Minister Anika Wells has framed it as a matter of choice. "A lot of Australians will want to choose the algorithm," she has said, arguing that platforms should offer the choice and then respect it. Another account of the plan gives the government's line as: "This is not about giving government control, it is about giving people control."

What is on the table

The core mechanism is a prompt. Platforms would have to tell users they can pick between a recommended feed and a non-algorithmic one, typically a chronological timeline. Reporting on the later bill says the chronological option must be about as easy to use as the default. It also says the platform could not quietly flip a user back to the algorithm, even after they leave the app. One account, under the label "My Feed, My Way", says the right would cover users aged 16 and over. Treat that detail with care, since it comes from a single report.

The design is an opt-out, not an off-by-default rule. Campaigners wanted the algorithm off unless a user turned it on. Reporting says the government is not going that far. An academic commentator writing in The Conversation argues that a chronological default would be the stronger change. She notes that most people never touch settings, so an opt-out may change little in practice. She also says it is unclear which parts of a service like Instagram the switch would actually cover. Reels, suggested accounts and search ranking are all driven by recommenders.

The duty-of-care framework goes further than the switch. It would require platforms to identify and manage risks such as illegal pornography. For under-18s it would add roughly six areas of "psycho-social harm", including body image and bullying.

What is not settled

The published coverage leaves several basic questions open:

  • Scope. Meta is named as an example. No source gives a definitive list of covered platforms or a size threshold.
  • Definition. Nothing reported defines which systems count as a "recommendation algorithm". The word "toxic" appears in headlines, but it is not a legal test in anything described.
  • Enforcement. The sources do not name the regulator. The Conversation piece suggests the eSafety Commissioner and outside researchers could help test compliance, but its author says they have not seen the details.
  • Penalties. Accounts put the maximum fine at more than A$100 million, with one report giving A$109.2 million. The Conversation author calls that small for the largest tech firms and suggests fines tied to a percentage of Australian revenue.

Australia has recent form here. Its under-16 social media minimum age law took effect on 10 December 2025, so Canberra has already shown it will legislate on how platforms operate. The Conversation piece also notes that the new proposal is separate from a bill to toughen enforcement of that ban, and that the same author sees weak disclosure of how companies comply as a flaw in the ban.

Politics and what to watch

Opposition leader Angus Taylor has said he is "deeply sceptical" and that his Coalition had not seen the bill. He has warned of "an attempt by the government to censor social media." The opposition's stance matters for how smoothly any bill gets through Parliament.

The practical effect would fall on platform operators and the engineers who build recommender systems. A legal right to a non-algorithmic feed would mean maintaining a fully supported alternative path, in a market other regulators watch. Other regulators could copy it. The European Union's Digital Services Act already requires very large platforms to offer a feed not based on profiling, so Australia would not be alone. The Conversation author also flags possible friction with the United States, given its approach to platform liability.

The next markers are the bill text itself, any parliamentary committee scrutiny, and whether the final version settles the default, the covered platforms and the regulator. Until then the "off switch" is a policy proposal.
