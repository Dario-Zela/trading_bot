AI trade wobbles: memory and infrastructure names slide as Meta's capex path and TSMC's capacity squeeze come into focus

In one sentence
AI-linked memory and infrastructure stocks reportedly sold off as long Treasuries had their best week since March 2025, a single-source picture that the coming capex guidance will test.

A sell-off in the stocks most closely tied to the artificial-intelligence build-out, together with a sharp rally in long-dated US government bonds, is the pattern reported by Benzinga. If it holds, it points to money moving out of the AI trade and into duration. The figures have not been checked against a second source, and the exact size of the moves is not available. What can be said is how the reported pieces fit together, and where they are weakest.

The sell-off

Per Benzinga, the Nasdaq 100 fell, with Micron, SanDisk and CoreWeave leading the decline. The report attributed the move to worries that AI valuations have run ahead of the earnings that justify them. No named analyst is quoted, and the index and single-stock percentage moves were not available for this article.

The names are informative even without the numbers. Micron and SanDisk make memory, the component in short supply when AI servers are built at scale. CoreWeave rents out data-centre capacity and carries the heavy fixed costs that come with it. They are the parts of the market where AI optimism is most directly priced, and so where doubts about the pace of spending would show first. A sell-off led by them says more about the AI trade than a broad slide in the index would.

The companion move was in bonds. Benzinga reported that long-dated Treasuries had their best week since March 2025. Yields and exact dates were not supplied. A rally of that kind alongside falling growth stocks is the textbook sign of investors reducing risk and buying safety. It is an inference, though, not a finding. A bond rally can also reflect inflation data, Fed expectations or oil, and none of those is established in the source material. Barclays, for one, now expects Fed rate rises in December and January, which sits awkwardly with a simple flight-to-safety reading of long bonds.

The spending question

The bubble argument rests on capital expenditure by the largest buyers of AI hardware. A separate Benzinga item put a number on it: Meta's AI spending could reach $205bn in 2027. That is a projection for a year that has not yet begun, not reported spending or company guidance, and it has not been confirmed against Meta's own statements.

The figure matters because it works both ways. For memory makers and data-centre operators, a hyperscaler budget of that size is revenue. For sceptics, it is the exposure: if returns on the spending disappoint, the budgets are cut and the suppliers' earnings go with them. The same logic applies to the Magnificent 7 exchange-traded funds, which hold Meta alongside the other mega-cap names, and so amount to a wager that spending keeps climbing.

The bottleneck at the foundry

Spending alone does not produce chips. Nvidia, Apple and AMD all rely on TSMC for their most advanced silicon, and capacity at the leading edge is limited. Candidates for the same wafers have to bid for them, which shifts pricing leverage toward the foundry. The triage material supplied no capacity figure, price rise or contract term, so how tight the squeeze is, and what it costs the customers, cannot be quantified here.

The implication runs both ways again. A supplier with pricing power can capture more of the money Meta and its peers plan to spend, leaving less margin for the chip designers. It also caps how fast any hyperscaler can turn a bigger budget into more computing power. A larger cheque does not guarantee more chips.

The bear case, and its limits

Mike Novogratz of Galaxy Digital has said the Nasdaq could fall 30% in an 'AI catastrophe', per the Benzinga headline. The trigger, timing and probability were not given, and the 30% figure is his scenario, not a forecast supported by data in the brief. The more useful part of his view is the other half: he still expects the data-centre build-out to continue. On that reading the danger is a repricing of AI equities, not a halt to construction. That distinction fits the week's pattern, in which the suppliers and the leveraged renters of capacity fell while the underlying spending plans were not reported to have changed.

What to watch

The reported figures need confirming against index and Treasury data before anything firm is built on them. The next hard test is the coming round of quarterly results from the large hyperscalers, where capital-expenditure guidance for 2027 will either support or undercut the $205bn projection. TSMC's next earnings call is the other: any disclosure on leading-edge capacity or pricing would settle whether the foundry's leverage is as strong as the logic suggests. Until then, the one-week pairing of falling AI stocks and rising long bonds is a signal worth noting, not a conclusion.
