Brazil reports record fall in Amazon deforestation as election looms

In one sentence
Brazil says Amazon deforestation fell to its lowest level since 1988, but the gains rest on enforcement policies that the 25 October runoff could change.

Brazil's government said on Friday that deforestation in the Amazon over the past year was the lowest since satellite records began in 1988. The announcement came eight days before a presidential runoff that will decide who runs the policies behind the decline.

Satellite data showed 3,335 square kilometres (about 1,300 square miles) of forest cleared between August 2025 and July 2026, the 12-month window used to calculate the annual rate. The previous low, set in 2011-12, was 4,571 sq km. Environment Minister Marina Silva called the result a "historic achievement". The government says clearing has fallen 71% since 2023, the year President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva returned to office.

A caveat on the numbers: the figure was reported through news coverage of the government announcement, and the year-on-year percentage was not given in the accounts reviewed. The official annual series, PRODES, run by the national space research institute INPE, had last been published for the year to July 2025, when clearing fell 11% to 5,796 sq km. Preliminary PRODES figures for the latest year are normally released in October or November. INPE's faster but less precise DETER alert system points the same way. It logged about 2,874 sq km of alerts for August 2025 to July 2026, a 36% fall and the lowest since the current system began in 2015. IPAM, a Brazilian research group, notes that DETER shows the trend but is not the official rate.

What changed in enforcement

The decline tracks a deliberate reversal of policy. Between 2019 and 2022, under Jair Bolsonaro, deforestation rose by about 60% and more than 45,000 sq km were cleared. Lula's government revived PPCDAm, the federal action plan to prevent and control Amazon deforestation, which rests on four pillars: monitoring and enforcement, a sustainable economy, protected areas and public forests, and regulation and financial incentives.

In practice that meant restoring sanctions on illegal clearing detected by satellite, a tool halted under the previous government. It also meant proposing to bar properties with illegal deforestation from bank loans, sending more money to priority Amazon municipalities that cut forest loss, funding action against environmental crime, and building subsidised credit for sustainable businesses.

That dependence on enforcement is the reason the result is politically fragile. Clearing that falls because inspections are credible and penalties are applied can rise again when inspections slacken. "The formula is always the same, and it works," said Márcio Astrini of the Climate Observatory, a coalition of Brazilian environmental groups.

A runoff with the forest on the ballot

In the first round on 4 October, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro, the former president's eldest son, took 47% of the vote to Lula's 45%. The two meet in a runoff on 25 October.

Flávio Bolsonaro has pledged to end illegal deforestation by 2029, a year ahead of Brazil's current target of 2030. His campaign describes him as a defender of the environment. But he has not detailed how he would protect the Amazon or finance forest conservation. He has called for modernising environmental laws, a phrase many environmentalists read as weakening them. Climate and conservation experts have warned that a Bolsonaro victory could reverse the policies that curbed clearing.

Congress adds a second risk whoever wins. Cristiane Mazzetti of Greenpeace Brazil said the legislature "has worked to weaken, delay and dismantle laws and regulations that help protect the environment". A president can direct agencies and budgets, but statutes and licensing rules sit with lawmakers.

Fires and the limits of the statistic

Two developments qualify the good news. Fire counts across the Amazon were near historic lows this year, but they have tripled since the first round of voting on 4 October. Authorities are investigating whether some fires were set deliberately for political reasons. No findings have been published, and the link remains an open question.

Second, a fall in clear-cutting does not capture the whole of forest damage. Wildfires, selective logging and drought degrade about 40% of the Amazon, and that degradation has in recent years outpaced outright clearing. Mongabay has cautioned that the forest stays vulnerable to damage that does not show up immediately in deforestation statistics. An independent system run by Imazon, a Brazilian research institute, estimated forest loss in calendar 2025 at 228 sq km, near a six-year low, which is consistent with the broad direction but measures a different period and method.

What to watch

The first marker is the runoff on 25 October. The second is the release of preliminary PRODES data, expected in October or November, which will confirm or adjust the 3,335 sq km figure. After that, the test is what the next government does with enforcement budgets and satellite-based sanctions in its first months. A record low is a statistic about the past. Whether it becomes a trend depends on policies that are now on the ballot.
