National security adviser under pressure over wife's Palestine Action post

In one sentence
The Met is assessing an X post by Sarah Helm, wife of UK national security adviser Jonathan Powell, backing proscribed group Palestine Action; the government says her views are her own.

Police in London are assessing a social media post by the wife of Jonathan Powell, the UK national security adviser. The post appears to express support for Palestine Action, a group the British government proscribed as a terrorist organisation in 2025. Whether it becomes a criminal matter is still undecided.

The post

Sarah Helm, a journalist and author who is married to Powell, wrote on X on Thursday, replying to a post about the novelist Sally Rooney. Rooney had said her books would no longer be sold in Britain because she plans to use her royalties to support Palestine Action. Helm's reply read: "God help us authors who support Palestine and Palestine Action."

The Metropolitan Police said the post was "being assessed to determine whether any further police investigation may be required." It has not said whether a formal investigation has been opened. An assessment is an early, preliminary stage. It can end with no action.

The legal backdrop

Since the proscription, expressing support for Palestine Action can be an offence under UK terrorism law. The ban has produced a large number of arrests at protests. Al Jazeera reported that the campaign group Defend Our Juries, which has defended Helm, knows of 36 people arrested over similar social media posts. Police have therefore already treated online expressions of support as a matter for assessment, and sometimes arrest. Helm's post will be judged against that practice.

Reaction

A government spokeswoman said: "These views are held by an individual who does not represent the government in any capacity." The line separates the adviser from his spouse's opinions without commenting on the police assessment.

The Campaign Against Antisemitism called on police to investigate. It acknowledged that spouses are entitled to independent views but said the post raises "extremely serious questions" for Powell and the government.

What is at stake

The case tests two things. One is whether the law is applied evenly: critics of the ban have long argued that ordinary people are arrested for posts, so a close relative of a senior security official will be watched for any difference in treatment. The other is whether the public conduct of a security official's family should be a matter for government at all. Ministers' answer so far is that it should not.

The verified detail is thin. The full text of the post and its context are limited to what has been reported, and there is no indication that Powell has commented. The next marker is the Met's decision on whether the assessment becomes an investigation. If it does, pressure on Powell is likely to grow. If the force closes the matter, the story may fade quickly.
