Russian man linked to London hospitals ransomware attack arrested

In one sentence
German police hold a 28-year-old Russian said to be a core Qilin ransomware member, and are now probing whether he is tied to the 2024 Synnovis attack on London hospitals.

A 28-year-old Russian man described by German police as a core member of the Qilin ransomware gang is in custody in Germany. German detectives are now looking at whether he and his associates were involved in the 2024 attack on Synnovis, the pathology provider that serves several London hospitals. If so, it would be a rare arrest in a case that disrupted NHS care on a large scale.

The detention has not produced a charge over Synnovis. As the BBC reports, the suspect is wanted in Germany over a different hack, and the London attack has been folded into the wider investigation. No UK charge has been confirmed.

What is known about the arrest

German press has named the man as Vladimir K. According to the BBC, he was arrested in Japan in May after travelling there for a holiday. SecurityWeek reports that he was detained in Osaka and handed over to German authorities on 2 October. The reports do not say which Japanese authorities made the arrest, and the exact May date has not been published.

In Germany he is wanted over the hacking of a logistics company in September 2024. The attackers encrypted its data and demanded about $160,000 (£120,000) in cryptocurrency. German police describe him as a core member of Qilin, which they say has hacked more than 4,000 organisations since 2024 and extorted more than $140m.

The link to London is thinner than the headlines suggest. SecurityWeek's account does not tie him to Synnovis at all. It says only that Qilin was blamed for that attack. The BBC says German police are including the Synnovis hack in their inquiry. That is an investigative line, not an allegation tested in court.

What the Synnovis attack cost

Synnovis is a pathology partnership between NHS trusts and the private firm Synlab. On 3 June 2024 Qilin, a Russian-speaking ransomware group, hit its systems. Hospitals that depended on it, including Guy's and St Thomas' and King's College Hospital, lost much of their ability to process blood tests quickly. Staff fell back on manual methods and on sending samples elsewhere.

The figures widely reported from NHS data are large: more than 10,000 outpatient appointments disrupted, 1,710 elective procedures postponed and about 1,100 cancer treatments delayed. These come from secondary reports of NHS figures, not from the arrest coverage.

Qilin also published stolen data, including pathology results. Security firm CaseMatrix estimated that records relating to more than 900,000 patients were leaked. Synnovis has neither confirmed nor disputed that figure, and it has not said how many people were affected even though its forensic review finished in November 2025.

In June 2025 King's College Hospital confirmed that one patient had died unexpectedly during the incident. Its review found that a long wait for a blood test result, caused by the cyberattack, was a contributing factor. The BBC reports that British police are examining whether their own inquiry could lead to an unlawful killing case. Nothing has been charged.

Why a single supplier mattered

The Synnovis case showed how much risk sits in shared suppliers. No single hospital was breached. One contractor was, and several hospitals lost a core service at once. The harm followed from that concentration, whatever the attackers intended.

It also shows what an arrest does and does not mean. Ransomware groups like Qilin operate from countries that tend to ignore them, so their members are rarely reachable at home. This one was reached because he travelled. That points to a practical limit on the tactic: arrests happen when suspects leave safe territory, not because the groups themselves are disrupted. Qilin was not shut down by this detention. SecurityWeek says the group listed around 400 victims on its leak site during 2025, and that it exploited a Check Point VPN flaw in June 2026.

What to watch

The first question is whether German prosecutors, or the UK's National Crime Agency, bring a Synnovis-related charge, and whether the UK seeks to have him tried in Britain. The second is whether the police inquiry into the patient's death leads to any decision from British prosecutors. Until one of those happens, the arrest is a notable result in a case that is still open.
