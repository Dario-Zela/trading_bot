Burnham's early days: devolution offer to Swinney, a probe into Manchester loans, and a Heathrow split with Healey

In one sentence
Prime Minister Burnham has opened devolution talks with Swinney, faces scrutiny over Manchester loans, and is yet to back Healey's Heathrow runway line.

Andy Burnham has been Prime Minister for only a matter of weeks. Three early tests are already showing what his government will look like. He has opened a conversation with Scotland's First Minister about devolution. A question about how he ran Greater Manchester's money has followed him to Downing Street. And he has declined to say whether he backs Heathrow's third runway, which his Chancellor has already described as approved. Each is a small matter today. Together they show a prime minister who wants to be seen as a devolver and a northern voice, and who now has to govern from the centre.

An offer with no detail

The devolution thread is the most substantive, though it is thin. Burnham and John Swinney met in Glasgow, where the Prime Minister had travelled for the opening of the Commonwealth Games. It was their first face-to-face meeting since Burnham took over from Keir Starmer. Swinney called it constructive and said Burnham would "engage" on further devolution to Scotland.

Swinney's account was pointed. He said the Prime Minister "acknowledges and accepts that Westminster has failed many parts of the United Kingdom and has failed Scotland". That is Swinney's characterisation of the exchange, not a quotation from Burnham. Swinney also said Burnham had agreed not to go "over our heads" and bypass the Scottish Government and Parliament. Downing Street's version was more cautious. A spokesperson said Burnham sees deeper devolution as a way to help communities rebuild their economies.

No powers, timetable or mechanism have been announced. Holyrood's own coverage noted how little detail there was on what further devolution could mean. Until a list of powers appears, the offer remains a statement of intent.

The limits are already clear. Downing Street has said a second independence referendum is off limits, on the grounds that it would take focus away from growing the economy. Burnham reportedly told Swinney they would have to "agree to disagree". Swinney says he will keep pressing for a referendum and has a mandate for one. Holyrood also reports that the Scottish Government has grown dissatisfied with the approach of Scottish Secretary Douglas Alexander, whom Burnham reappointed this week.

The arrangement is therefore a trade. Westminster offers more power in exchange for the independence question being set aside. Whether Swinney will accept that, or use devolution talks as a platform for his referendum case, is the real question. Burnham's record as Greater Manchester mayor, where he pressed for more devolved control, makes the offer credible. It also raises expectations that Westminster departments tend to resist.

The Manchester loans

The second thread comes from Burnham's old job, and part of it could not be confirmed. The triage brief described a council scrutiny probe into housing loans made during his mayoralty. Searches found no record of such an inquiry. What exists is a long-running dispute over Greater Manchester Combined Authority loans to the developer Renaker.

The authority's investment director told a scrutiny committee in August that it has lent Renaker £598m since 2016. A March 2024 meeting chaired by Burnham reportedly approved £120m in loans to two Renaker vehicles, and a further £140m was held back. A rival developer, Weis Group, challenged the loans on subsidy-control grounds. The Competition Appeal Tribunal found that the authority had not obtained a statement of Renaker's backer's assets and liabilities before approval. That left public money exposed, the tribunal said. The authority rejects the suggestion that due diligence was lacking and says the tribunal praised its approach. Reports indicate Weis lost at the Court of Appeal and is weighing further action.

It is not yet clear what the councillors' new scrutiny covers, what it will examine or when it will report. The case matters because opponents have already used it. A Reform candidate in the Greater Manchester mayoral race promised to "open the books", and the Greens' Zack Polanski has criticised the lending too. For a Prime Minister who built his reputation on running Manchester well, any adverse finding would be awkward.

Heathrow and the Chancellor

The third thread is the sharpest. Chancellor John Healey has said the government has given the Heathrow expansion the go-ahead, because the economic case stacks up and will bring growth. Burnham has refused to say whether he agrees. "Principally, it's a matter for London and Londoners," he said, adding that it would not be right to "just jump straight in on the back of the consultation". He has also argued that such plans pull investment "away from the North and traps it in London".

Heathrow's chief executive, Thomas Woldbye, has called for clarity: "it would be good to know where Government is on this". He warned that investment could be lost overseas without a firm commitment. The runway is now not expected before 2039, four years later than the original 2035 target, and Heathrow wants planning permission by 2029. Critics say it would overshoot the UK's legally binding climate targets.

The gap between Chancellor and Prime Minister is a difference of emphasis, not yet a rupture. Healey is speaking as the minister responsible for growth, Burnham as a northern politician who does not want to be seen favouring London. But a Prime Minister who stays silent on a decision of this size will not be able to do so for long.

What ties it together

All three threads are about the same thing: how a mayor who spent years arguing for power outside Westminster uses it from the centre. Devolution talks with Swinney will show whether the rhetoric becomes a list of powers. The Manchester loans will show whether his old record holds up under scrutiny. Heathrow will show whether his government can speak with one voice. The next markers are concrete: any published list of powers following the Swinney meeting, the outcome of the Renaker scrutiny and legal challenges, and the end of the Heathrow consultation, which will force a position from the Prime Minister.
