No 10 keeps Chinese EV tariff option open if UK joins EU scheme

In one sentence
Downing Street says it could still impose tariffs on Chinese EVs even if the UK joins the EU's scheme, keeping a lever it has not yet used.

Downing Street is keeping a door open on Chinese electric vehicles as Britain and the EU work through the terms of their post-Brexit reset. According to the Guardian's report, No 10 says the UK could still impose its own tariffs on Chinese-made EVs even if it joins the EU's scheme. The position is a hedge. London wants the benefits of closer alignment with Brussels without giving up a lever it has not yet used.

The detail of what the hedge covers has not been independently confirmed. This account rests on the Guardian's reporting of the government's stance, and no quotes are reproduced here.

What the EU did

The EU imposed countervailing duties on Chinese-built battery electric vehicles from October 2024, after an anti-subsidy investigation concluded that Chinese producers had benefited from state support. The duties sit on top of the standard 10% car import tariff, and the rate varies by manufacturer. At the time of the original decision, BYD faced 17.0%, Geely 18.8% and SAIC 35.3%. Other cooperating producers faced 20.7%, and non-cooperating ones 35.3%. Tesla, which builds cars in Shanghai, was set at 7.8%. Those figures come from the EU's 2024 decision and may have been revised since.

Brussels has also held talks with Beijing on replacing duties with minimum-price arrangements. That matters for the UK question, because an EU scheme is not a fixed object. What Britain would be aligning with could change after any alignment is agreed.

Where Britain stands

The UK has not applied EV-specific duties on Chinese imports. Chinese-built cars arriving in Britain pay the standard 10% tariff, and the government has so far chosen not to follow the EU, the US or Canada in adding a surcharge. The "option" No 10 wants to keep is therefore a policy choice it has not exercised, not a measure it would be giving up.

The reasons for caution on both sides are visible. Chinese brands, BYD and MG among them, have been gaining share in the UK market. Cheap imports help the government's decarbonisation targets under the zero-emission vehicle mandate. Against that, British carmakers and unions argue for protection, and the same worries about subsidised overcapacity that moved Brussels apply in London.

What the reset tests

The significance lies less in the cars than in the principle. Alignment with EU rules usually carries a price in autonomy, and the question for the reset is how much room London keeps to decide its own trade defence. A retained right to tariff Chinese EVs, if Brussels accepts it, would show that closer alignment can leave space for divergence on specific measures. If the EU resists, the line will indicate how far the reset is a negotiation between equals.

For Chinese manufacturers, the immediate effect is uncertainty. A UK that mirrored EU duties would raise costs for firms that have used Britain as a lower-tariff route into Europe. A UK that kept discretion leaves them facing a threat they cannot price.

The next marker is the text of any UK-EU agreement and whether it says anything on trade remedies. That wording, more than the briefing language from Downing Street, will show whether the retained option is real.
