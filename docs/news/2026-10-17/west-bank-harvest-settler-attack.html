Palestinians and a US journalist injured in settler attack during West Bank harvest

In one sentence
Masked settlers attacked olive pickers near Abu Falah, beating Washington Post photographer Heidi Levine, as Washington demanded arrests and Israel's government called the facts unclear.

Masked settlers attacked Palestinian olive pickers near the village of Abu Falah, in the occupied West Bank, on Friday. They beat a Washington Post photojournalist, Heidi Levine, who was working with the harvesters. The attack was one of several settler incidents around Ramallah that day, during the annual harvest.

What happened at Abu Falah

Witnesses told Israeli and international outlets that around 80 settlers, many of them masked and some carrying gas canisters, came down a hill into a terraced grove where villagers were picking olives. Some used slings to throw stones. Villagers threw stones back. Footage reviewed by the Times of Israel showed masked men beating Levine with sticks before they ran off.

Levine, who was wearing a flak jacket and helmet, said she credited that gear with protecting her. "They just kept kicking me and kicking me," she said afterwards, adding that her equipment had been taken. Palestinian colleagues drove the attackers off. She was taken by ambulance to a clinic in Abu Falah and then to a hospital in Ramallah. She is reported to be in stable condition, and the Post's executive editor said she "received treatment and is recovering".

A local official told the BBC that 12 Palestinians were hurt, including four with dual Palestinian-American citizenship. That figure comes from one official and has not been independently confirmed. Palestinian media said the Red Crescent also treated a 63-year-old woman and a 40-year-old man for injuries from beatings.

Witnesses said at least two Israeli army jeeps drove through while the violence was under way and that it continued after they arrived. Soldiers fired tear gas towards Palestinians. The army said it fired warning shots and then shot at a suspect who ran at troops carrying a sharp object. It said the confrontation was dispersed with riot-control means.

Other incidents the same day

In Turmus Ayya, settlers set fire to olive trees and vehicles. The Palestinian news agency WAFA reported three cars burned, one belonging to the Civil Defence, and a Civil Defence member was attacked while trying to put out a fire. In Birzeit, a 70-year-old Palestinian was injured in what Palestinian media described as a settler attack. Police said they were also investigating Turmus Ayya.

Competing accounts

The US embassy condemned the violence and called for "investigations, arrests and convictions of those responsible". Israel's ambassador to Washington, Yechiel Leiter, told CNN that "those perpetrating the violence have to be brought to justice".

The Israeli Prime Minister's Office gave a different picture. It said the facts were "unclear and under investigation" and that Levine "was allegedly assaulted". It cited reports that about 200 Arabs and far-left activists had confronted roughly 40 Israeli youths, and said footage showed violence from both sides. The Binyamin Regional Council called the episode a "planned provocation". The Foreign Press Association in Israel described it as part of "a long line of violent attacks" and criticised Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu for dismissing such assaults as the work of "troubled youths".

The army and police said they "view any manifestation of violence gravely", particularly against journalists, and that all the circumstances would be examined. Investigators were to enter Abu Falah under military escort to take testimony.

Why it matters

The harvest is a regular flashpoint. Olive groves are often close to settlements and outposts, and access to them is often contested. Farmers say attacks are meant to push them off their land. Critics say troops frequently stand by, and that the authorities have more often obstructed the activists and observers who accompany pickers than the attackers themselves.

What distinguishes this incident is who was hurt. Foreign journalists and foreign-passport holders have been caught up in earlier harvest attacks, including one in November 2025 near Beita in which two Reuters staff were injured. But an American press photographer, together with several Palestinian-Americans, draws in Washington in a way that attacks on local farmers have not. The embassy's call for convictions is a sharper public demand than the usual expressions of concern. Whether that has consequences depends on the outcome of the police inquiry, and on whether anyone is arrested for an attack on a group of masked men filmed in daylight.

The next markers are practical ones: whether police name suspects from the footage, and whether the army's promised review addresses why troops on the scene did not stop the beating. The harvest runs for several more weeks.
