STANDING WATCH · HORMUZ · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-18 · 3 min read

Hormuz: ship-attack reports hit a record as oil exports slow

In one sentence
Reported attacks on shipping near Hormuz are at a record and Gulf oil outflows have slipped, keeping Brent around $105–110 on chokepoint risk rather than supply alone.

Attacks on merchant ships in and around the Strait of Hormuz have reached a record, and the oil that normally passes through the strait is starting to thin out. That matters beyond the Gulf. Brent crude is trading around $105–110 a barrel. A price at that level reflects the risk of a blocked chokepoint as well as the supply and demand for oil.

NBC News reported on 17 October that attacks on shipping near Hormuz had surged to a record high and had "already reduced oil flows". The BBC carried a similar account. Neither report, as far as could be established, names a data source or a measurement window for the headline count. The record is therefore a reported one. The direction is plain enough. The size is less certain.

What the numbers show

The better-documented figures come from elsewhere. The International Maritime Organization counted 11 tankers attacked while transiting Hormuz in the week to 4 October, the most since the war began in late February, according to CNBC. Oilprice.com put the October total at 24 attacks so far. The two counts cover different periods and are not directly comparable.

The flow data point the same way. UBS estimates that crude and product flows leaving the Gulf of Oman fell to 9.1 million barrels a day over the past couple of days. The weekly average was 13.5 million, against an early-October peak of 17 million. Oilprice.com separately reports that tanker traffic hit a two-month low.

That is a sharp reversal. In September, Middle East crude exports had recovered to prewar levels, helped by US military escorts along a route hugging Oman's coast. CNBC judged that recovery fragile, and it was right to.

The latest incidents show why. Iran's navy said a container ship sank after hitting a sea mine on 17 October. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said a tanker was on fire in the strait after striking a mine. Iraq, which lacks the storage to sit out a blockade, has halted most of its production. It is making limited exports by truck to Syria and by pipeline to Turkey.

Prices and the chokepoint

Oil trackers put Brent at about $109–110 at the latest settlement. That is up more than 3% in the session, and it follows a day when Oilprice.com reported a rise of around 5% on stepped-up tanker attacks. The US Energy Information Administration expects Brent to average $105 in the fourth quarter and $84 in 2027. In effect, it assumes the disruption fades.

The evidence supports a cautious reading of the price. A fall in flows from 17 million to 13.5 million barrels a day is large, but oil is not short of supply in the usual sense. The barrels exist and the tankers exist. The difficulty is getting insurers, owners and crews to accept the risk of moving them. That is what a chokepoint premium looks like. The price responds to each attack, each mine report and each change in escort policy, as well as to inventories.

One caution applies. Transit figures vary widely between sources. Some aggregators quote only a handful of daily crossings against a prewar baseline in the dozens, while the US military has said most ships are crossing safely. Some of those sources are secondary, so the choice of dataset matters. The UBS and IMO figures are the firmest.

What to watch

The next data points are weekly: IMO and UBS counts of attacked ships and Gulf of Oman outflows, and whether the Oman-coast escort route holds. If flows stay near 9 million barrels a day, the case for a sustained premium strengthens, with consequences for fuel costs and inflation expectations in importing countries. If escorts restore flows toward 17 million, the premium should ease. The wider market backdrop, with oil near $110 colliding with an equity sell-off, is covered in the Markets section.
