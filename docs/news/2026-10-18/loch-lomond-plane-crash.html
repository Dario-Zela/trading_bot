Boy, 16, and man, 80, killed in light aircraft crash near Loch Lomond

In one sentence
A 16-year-old boy and an 80-year-old man died when a light aircraft, reported as a Piper Tomahawk, crashed near Drymen by Loch Lomond; the AAIB is investigating.

A 16-year-old boy and an 80-year-old man died on Friday when a light aircraft crashed north-east of Drymen, close to Loch Lomond. Police Scotland said both were pronounced dead at the scene. Their next of kin have been informed, and neither has been named.

Emergency services were called at about 4.45pm on 16 October after a report of a crash. Police said enquiries were continuing "to establish the full circumstances". The Air Accidents Investigation Branch (AAIB), which investigates civil aviation accidents in the UK, has been told and has sent a team to the site.

What is known about the aircraft

The aircraft has been reported as a Piper Tomahawk II, a two-seat trainer widely used by flying schools. The BBC is the source for that detail, and police have not confirmed it. Police have not said who was flying. Whether either victim was the pilot is unconfirmed, and nothing official has been said about a cause.

Local reports give a timeline for the aircraft's day, but it is not officially confirmed and some accounts differ on detail. They say it took off from Cumbernauld Airport at 11.46am and reached Glasgow Airport about 15 minutes later. It reportedly left Glasgow at about 1pm, flew north-east towards Newport-on-Tay and turned back, then stayed on the ground for roughly an hour. Its final flight is said to have begun at 4.17pm, and signal was lost near Drymen at 4.37pm, about eight minutes before the emergency call. Not all outlets agree that this flight is the one that crashed.

Airspace closed

Temporary airspace restrictions are in force over the Drymen area, including for drones. Police said they are likely to stay in place "for some time" while the AAIB works, and warned that flying in restricted airspace is a criminal offence.

The AAIB's work in such cases normally starts at the wreckage, with the position of the aircraft, the condition of the controls and engine, and any recorded flight data. It usually publishes a special bulletin within weeks, followed by a full report that can take a year or more. It does not apportion blame.

What to watch

The first things to emerge will be the identities of the two people, once families have been informed and agree, and whether police confirm the aircraft type and its flight path. Any early findings from the AAIB would come in its initial bulletin. A safety lesson for light aviation would matter beyond Drymen, but nothing so far points to one.
