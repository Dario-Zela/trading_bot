Poor in-store service is pushing shoppers online, retail survey suggests

In one sentence
A reported retail survey says poor in-store service is pushing UK shoppers online, but its sample, dates and figures could not be verified.

Shoppers who find service in UK stores poor are increasingly taking their spending online, according to a retail survey reported by BBC Business. If the pattern holds, a customer-experience complaint becomes a point about high-street footfall and retailer margins.

The Tribune could not check the underlying survey. The BBC report was not retrievable, and the details that would let a reader weigh it are missing: who ran the poll, how many people answered, when it was fielded, and which retailers or sectors it covered. No percentages are given here because none could be confirmed.

What the claim amounts to

The claim is that store experience is a reason for channel shift, not just price or convenience. That is a narrower and more testable idea than the general drift to online shopping, which has been under way for years. It implies that some of the online spending is avoidable: a customer who leaves a store over queues, thin staffing or unhelpful assistants might have bought there had the visit gone better.

The finding is not new in kind. UK surveys over the past decade, including work by Accenture and Market Force, have reported similar complaints about too few tills open, items out of stock and weak service. They also reported that many shoppers still prefer to buy in a shop. One search turned up a grocery study in which about two-thirds of respondents were dissatisfied with the in-store experience, chiefly over queues and finding products. These are older and differently built surveys, so they are context and not confirmation of the new one.

Why it matters for retailers

The link to margins runs through costs. Online sales usually carry delivery and returns costs that in-store sales do not, particularly in clothing. A shopper pushed online by a bad store visit may therefore be worth less to the retailer than one who buys in person. The survey as reported gives no basis for estimating the size of that effect.

The link to footfall is looser. A stated reason for buying online is not the same as measured behaviour, and shoppers' accounts of their motives are often tidier than the facts. Footfall counts and card-spending data would show whether service-driven switching is large enough to register.

What to watch

The test is whether the survey's authors publish their sample and methodology, and whether retailers' trading updates and footfall data, including the next monthly British Retail Consortium figures, show store visits falling in sectors where service complaints are most common. Until then this is a consumer-sentiment signal and not a measured shift in spending.
