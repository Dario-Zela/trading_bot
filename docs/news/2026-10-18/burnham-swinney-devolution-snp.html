Burnham offers 'deeper devolution'; Swinney says Scotland is 'not like Manchester' as SNP says referendum campaign has begun

In one sentence
Burnham's offer of 'deeper Scottish devolution' met Swinney's retort that Scotland is 'not like Manchester', as the SNP says its referendum campaign has begun.

The Prime Minister has put a devolution offer to Scotland's First Minister, and the reply has turned it into an argument about categories. Andy Burnham invited John Swinney to work with him on what he calls "deeper Scottish devolution". Swinney's answer, as reported by the BBC, was that Scotland is "not like Manchester". The SNP has added that the next independence referendum campaign "has already begun", according to the Guardian's headline.

The terms of the offer have not been made public in the material available. The dispute is therefore about principle and precedent more than about any list of powers.

Why Manchester

The comparison comes from Burnham's own career. Before becoming Prime Minister he was the mayor of Greater Manchester, and his case for devolution has rested on that experience: power moved out of Whitehall to city regions that can shape transport, skills and investment locally. In his first speeches as leader he proposed a "Number 10 North", an outpost of Downing Street in Manchester, to drive that agenda.

To Swinney, that framing is the problem. Reporting from July, when Burnham took office, has the First Minister describing Scotland as a "nation not a region" and warning that "rhetoric alone will not cut it". He said Scotland needs "more job creating powers and the ability to lower energy bills". An SNP MP was blunter, saying Burnham "seems to think Manchester is the north of the UK, when it's barely the north of England".

The objection is constitutional rather than geographical. Scotland already has a parliament with substantial powers over health, education and justice. An offer modelled on English city-region deals, however generous, implies a different sort of body: an administrative partner of Westminster rather than a national government with its own mandate.

The referendum behind the offer

The backdrop is a flat refusal from Downing Street on the larger question. After the two men first met this summer, Burnham's office described a second independence referendum as "off limits". The two agreed to disagree, according to the Press Association's account. Swinney said Burnham would "engage" on where powers should sit and would not bypass the Scottish Government and Parliament. He also said he would continue his independence push, citing a record number of pro-independence MSPs elected in May, though the SNP fell short of a majority.

That is the context for the SNP's claim that the campaign "has already begun". If a referendum cannot be had, the party's strategy is to treat every Westminster concession as a test. Scots can be offered more powers, but the SNP will argue that only a vote settles who decides. Refusing a choice, Swinney said earlier, makes Labour's devolution pledge "ring hollow".

The Scottish Conservatives have pressed from the other side. Their leader, Russell Findlay, said Burnham must say which powers he means and argued that more devolution will never satisfy the SNP. Both criticisms rest on the same gap: so far there is little detail.

What to watch

The offer will be judged on its content. The first signal will be whether Burnham's proposals name specific powers, such as energy, employment or borrowing, that go beyond the English city-region template, or whether Scotland is folded into a UK-wide scheme. A UK-wide scheme would sharpen Swinney's objection.

The second is whether the Scottish Government agrees to take part at all. Joining talks would give Burnham a claim of cooperation. Staying out would let the SNP argue the offer was never meant for a nation. Neither side has yet said what a working group would produce or by when. Until it does, "not like Manchester" is as much a campaign slogan as a policy position.
