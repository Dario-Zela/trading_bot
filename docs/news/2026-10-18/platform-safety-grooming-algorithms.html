Child-safety report says grooming on Snapchat and Instagram is getting easier as Australia targets algorithms

In one sentence
A report says grooming on Snapchat and Instagram is getting easier, adding pressure on UK enforcement as Australia drafts an algorithm opt-out; the report's details are unconfirmed.

A new report says it is becoming easier for adults to groom children on Snapchat and Instagram. The finding, reported by BBC Technology, lands while Britain's Online Safety Act is being enforced and while Australia is drafting rules that would let users switch off recommendation algorithms. The report's author, date, sample and figures could not be confirmed ahead of publication, so this article treats its headline conclusion as a claim, not as established fact.

What the report says

The BBC's account is that grooming on the two platforms is getting 'easier'. That is the word in the headline. The report's name, its method, the number of children or cases it covers and the specific legal duties it says are being missed were not available. Without them, the finding cannot be weighed on its own terms.

It does not arrive without background. The NSPCC, the UK children's charity, has reported that Snapchat was the platform most often named in police-recorded online grooming cases. It appeared in almost half (48%) of the cases where the means of communication was disclosed. Meta's apps appeared far less often in the same data: WhatsApp 12%, Facebook and Messenger 10%, Instagram 6%. Police-recorded grooming offences reached a record 7,062 in one year-end figure. These are reported cases and not the full extent of abuse, and the figures come from different years, so they say nothing direct about whether grooming is getting easier now.

The NSPCC and others have pointed to design as the cause. Disappearing messages leave little evidence behind. Children can be contacted by strangers. Private chats are hard for platforms to scan. One teenager quoted by the charity said vanishing messages make it easier for people to hide things they shouldn't be doing. Snapchat has said it is working on grooming-detection technology and on in-app reporting.

The UK enforcement test

Under the Online Safety Act, the regulator Ofcom can require platforms to assess risks to children and to take steps against them. When the Act passed, Ofcom said its draft codes would make it harder for perpetrators to contact children. The NSPCC has argued that enforcement focuses too much on acting after harm has happened, and has urged the government to tighten the law so that child sexual abuse is disrupted in private messages too.

This is why a report that grooming is getting easier carries weight. If it holds up, it suggests the duties are not yet changing what happens in the most heavily used youth apps. That would raise pressure on Ofcom to show it can enforce the codes, and on the platforms to show they work. The report's lack of published detail is a limit. A reader cannot yet tell whether it measures real-world grooming or tests how easily an adult can reach a child's account.

Australia's different bet

Australia is trying another approach. The Albanese government has released draft legislation that would require platforms to offer users aged 16 and over a choice between an algorithmic feed and a feed of accounts they follow. Platforms would have to notify users and present the choice. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese called it 'sensible, pragmatic, practical reform'. Communications Minister Anika Wells said it would rein in firms she accused of running real-time product testing on Australians. The draft follows Australia's ban on accounts for under-16s, enacted last year.

The EU is the closest precedent. Its Digital Services Act has required large platforms to offer a way to switch off profiling since 2024, but regulators have complained that platforms made opting out too hard. That is a caution for Canberra: a switch only helps if people can find it and use it.

The two stories are linked, but not neatly. An algorithm opt-out targets what feeds push at users. It does not directly address a stranger messaging a child, which is the core of the grooming concern. The Australian draft was not presented as a grooming measure, and whether it would affect contact risks is unproven.

What to watch

The first test is the report itself. Its method and numbers, once published in full, will show whether 'easier' is a measured trend or a judgment. The second is Ofcom's enforcement record on children's safety duties. The third is Australia's draft bill as it goes through parliament, and whether the UK or EU copy it. All three are open questions.
