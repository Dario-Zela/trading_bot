Fifteen NHS staff sacked for accessing records of Valdo Calocane's victims

In one sentence
Nottingham University Hospitals has dismissed 15 staff, up from 11 in May, for improperly viewing records of Valdo Calocane's victims, as its record-monitoring system is still being piloted.

Fifteen staff at Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust (NUH) have now been dismissed for looking at the medical records of three people killed by Valdo Calocane in June 2023, without a legitimate reason. The latest disciplinary outcomes, published on 17 October, raise the number from the 11 dismissals the trust announced in May. They also leave a harder question than whether individuals broke the rules: why did a system that is supposed to log every record access take until 2025 to catch it?

What the trust has disclosed

The trust's latest figures, as reported by the local news site Westbridgford Wire, cover 34 hearing outcomes: 15 dismissals, 15 final written warnings, two first written warnings and two cases with no case to answer. The records belonged to Ian Coates, Grace O'Malley-Kumar and Barnaby Webber, who were killed in the attacks. The staff concerned included doctors, nurses, registered healthcare professionals and other employees.

The May announcement put the count at 11 dismissed and 14 further staff facing action, with the same trust citing two first written warnings and 12 final written warnings at that stage. The rise in dismissals therefore comes from cases that were still open in May. The trust says it began investigating in early 2025. It has not said that the total is final: investigations continue into access to the files of the three survivors, Wayne Birkett, Sharon Miller and Marcin Gawronski.

I have not been able to confirm the October figures against a trust statement or a Guardian or BBC report. They rest on one local outlet's account, which in turn relays the trust's statement. The May figures were reported widely.

The trust is discussing the outcomes with the Nursing and Midwifery Council and the General Medical Council, which can remove clinicians from their registers. It has also informed the Information Commissioner's Office and Nottinghamshire Police. Whether either body has opened its own case has not been reported.

Dr Manjeet Shehmar, the trust's medical director, said: "There is simply no excuse for accessing the records of a patient without a legitimate reason." He added: "I am so sorry that their grief has been further compounded by the actions of these staff members." The trust contacted the families this week with the latest outcomes. No family statement accompanied the October announcement.

The question of scale

In May, Emma Webber, Barnaby's mother, described the conduct as voyeurism. She also said that 150 staff had accessed the records. That figure was reported by one outlet and is far larger than the number of staff sanctioned. The families also disputed the trust's claim that 48 staff had a legitimate reason to access the files. The trust has not, in the material I have seen, reconciled these numbers publicly. They matter because the gap between how many people opened a file, how many were investigated and how many were disciplined is the only outside measure of how well an audit process works.

A caution about reading too much into the sanctions mix. Fifteen dismissals and fifteen final warnings suggest the trust treated the cases differently according to the circumstances. The public record does not say what distinguished them, such as repeated access, seniority or any later disclosure of what was seen.

What a monitoring system is for

The trust's response to the monitoring question is a new system. It is described as continuously monitoring access to patient records, flagging suspicious behaviour, and producing alerts, audits and reports. It is being tested now, with a pilot to follow and a go-live target of early next year. A staff education campaign titled "Your Responsibility, Your Actions, Their Privacy" is already running.

The timeline is the awkward part. The attacks happened in June 2023, and the trust's own investigation began in early 2025. A system that goes live in early 2027 will have been introduced more than three years after the event that made the records among the most sensitive the trust has held. The material I found does not say whether the problem was first raised by internal audit, by a complaint or by the families' representatives. One less established outlet reports that the survivors' records were initially overlooked until a solicitor raised the matter in March 2025. I could not corroborate that. If it is accurate, it would suggest that detection depended on outside prompting rather than routine monitoring.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this episode changes practice. The first is the outcome of the investigations into access to the survivors' records. The second is whether the NMC and GMC bring fitness-to-practise proceedings against the clinicians dismissed. The third is whether the Information Commissioner's Office announces a formal inquiry into the trust. The statutory public inquiry into the Nottingham killings is still running, and the trust's account of how the breach was found, and why it took so long, may come under examination there.
