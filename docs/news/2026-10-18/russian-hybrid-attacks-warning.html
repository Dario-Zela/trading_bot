Ex-national security adviser warns UK must guard against Russian 'hybrid attacks'

In one sentence
A former national security adviser says Britain is under-prepared for Russian sabotage and cyber attacks, echoing an MI5 warning this week, though no new incident or policy was cited.

A former UK national security adviser has warned that Britain is not ready for Russian "hybrid attacks", the sabotage, cyber intrusion and disruption that Moscow carries out below the threshold of open war. The warning was reported by the BBC. The summary of that report available to this desk gives no new incident, policy decision or deadline. The adviser's name and the date of their remarks could not be independently confirmed, so this article does not attribute specific words to them. The warning is best read as one more voice in a debate that has sharpened this month.

What 'hybrid' means

The term covers a range of hostile acts that fall short of armed attack. They include arson and sabotage by proxies, cyber operations against hospitals and utilities, disinformation, and pressure on undersea cables and other infrastructure. The aim is to impose costs and sow disorder while keeping deniability, so that no single act triggers a military response.

The argument over British readiness is not new. A former national security adviser, Lord Sedwill, described Russia as operating in the "grey space" between normal relations and armed conflict, and said the UK had to be able to act effectively there rather than "leave the initiative to our adversaries", according to Yahoo News.

A crowded week of warnings

The latest intervention follows an official one. On 13 October the MI5 director general, Ken McCallum, said Russia remained "aggressor number one" and that the service was devoting more resources to state threats than it did during the Cold War, according to Euronews. Semafor reported that he also pointed to escalating hybrid campaigns from Iran.

Other former officials have been blunter. The former Chief of the Defence Staff, Lord Peach, has said the hybrid war is already under way and that the country is unprepared for scenarios such as widespread power cuts, according to Euromaidan Press. A parliamentary committee appointed in January is due to report in November, a likely next point at which the preparedness question could get a formal answer.

Why it matters beyond the warning

A retired official's warning changes little by itself. It matters if it is followed by evidence of a specific gap: a funding shortfall, a failed exercise, or a named incident. The sectors most exposed are energy, telecoms and health. The ransomware attack on London hospitals, in which a Russian man has now been arrested, shows the kind of disruption involved.

The political backdrop is also shifting. Washington has just waived several Russia sanctions, which may affect how London judges the pressure Moscow is under, and how it expects Moscow to respond.

The November committee report is the next concrete test. If it puts figures or deadlines on resilience, this month's warnings will have had a policy outcome. If not, they will have joined a long list of cautions that did not lead to action.
