Councillors investigate housing loans granted while Burnham was Greater Manchester mayor

In one sentence
A Greater Manchester scrutiny committee is examining over £1bn of housing loans made while Burnham was mayor, but no findings or wrongdoing allegations have been published.

A committee of councillors in Greater Manchester is examining the housing loans handed out while Andy Burnham was the region's mayor. The inquiry matters beyond the city region because Burnham is now prime minister. A question of local governance has become, by accident of career, a question about the occupant of Downing Street.

The probe is being run by the overview and scrutiny committee of the Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA), according to the BBC's reporting, which has been syndicated by other outlets. The committee agreed to launch an inquiry into the loans earlier this year. John Leech, its chair, is now taking the work further. No findings have been published.

What is being examined

The loans came from a fund run by the GMCA. Reports put the total at more than £1bn, lent since 2015 to around 50 property developers. Those include three firms controlled by the Abu Dhabi owners of Manchester City. The money was meant to get stalled housing schemes built.

Councillors have raised concerns about transparency. The reports say they describe the approval process as "out of kilter", and Leech has said it was criticised as opaque in the past. Those are concerns about process. None of the coverage reviewed alleges that any loan was improper.

The GMCA's position, as reported, is that "not a single viable scheme was ever turned down". A spokesman for Manchester Life, one of the City-linked developers, said the company put its own capital into projects before drawing on the loans, and that the loans were repayable debt finance rather than grants.

A familiar argument

The fund has been contested before. A rival developer, Aubrey Weis, brought a legal challenge claiming that loans to the Renaker Group were offered on unduly favourable terms. He lost in July 2025. The tribunal chair rejected the claim of a "cosy relationship" between Renaker and the authority's leadership, saying: "This is not the case." One report of the ruling says the GMCA nonetheless failed to obtain a statement of assets and liabilities from Renaker's backer before the loans were approved. That detail is from a single secondary account and has not been checked against the judgment.

Critics have also pointed to delivery. One report says the fund supported roughly 11,000 homes but only around 500 of them were affordable, under 5% of the total. Burnham has defended the lending, saying it was always done "very rigorously and properly". Green leader Zack Polanski has attacked the record, calling it unacceptable that a Labour mayor loaned millions to private developers.

One point cuts against a personal framing. Reports of the committee's work say the loans were approved collectively by GMCA members, not by Burnham alone. The mayor chaired that body and was its public face, but a decision taken collectively is harder to pin on one person.

What to watch

The inquiry's existence gives opponents a ready line. It does not yet amount to a finding. Nothing published so far gives an amount in question, a named recipient under suspicion or an allegation of wrongdoing. The scrutiny process is also being run by a combined authority committee, which is a different thing from an independent investigation, and its political make-up will shape how its conclusions are read.

The signal to watch is whether the committee publishes a report, and whether it faults the approval process, as the criticism so far suggests it might, or the lending itself. A process finding would be an embarrassment. A finding of improper lending would be a different matter altogether. For now the story is that the scrutiny is under way, not what it will conclude.
