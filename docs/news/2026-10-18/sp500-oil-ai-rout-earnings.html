Oil near $110 and an AI-stock rout collide with solid earnings

In one sentence
US equities are caught between reportedly solid earnings, an AI-stock rout and oil near $110, though October price levels and earnings figures could not be independently confirmed.

US equities are being pulled three ways. Corporate earnings are described as solid. The AI-linked stocks that have carried the index are being sold. And crude oil, reported by Benzinga at around $110 a barrel, is raising the cost of almost everything else. Whether the S&P 500 can keep rising depends on which of these forces wins.

The data for this piece is thin, and the gaps matter. The triage brief gives no benchmark for the $110 oil price, and no date or size for the AI sell-off. It gives no earnings figures at all. A search for primary data found no October 2026 market report confirming those levels. What follows separates what is documented from what is only asserted.

What the record shows

The pairing of expensive oil and wobbling AI stocks is not new this year. On 28 April, Reuters reported Brent crude up 3% to $111.40 a barrel, a three-week high, with US crude up 3.8% at $100. The stall in the Iran conflict and constrained shipping through the Strait of Hormuz were the stated causes. Stocks fell that day on doubts about the AI boom, a day after the S&P 500 had set a record.

By July the two themes had hardened. AP reported weeks of selling in AI chip stocks over high valuations and doubts about whether demand would justify the spending. On 17 July the S&P 500 lost 1% to finish its first losing week in three, while Brent jumped 4.6% to $88.10. A week later, Reuters reported Brent settling above $100 for the first time since May, and the S&P 500 down about 1.2% on the day. Alphabet fell 7% after reporting higher spending plans alongside heavy cash burn.

The July episode shows how the two forces feed each other. Oil moves on geopolitics. The AI sell-off moves on spending and valuation. Both reduce the market's tolerance for disappointment.

Why oil and AI together are awkward

A rise in energy prices works as a tax on companies outside the energy sector. It hits hardest where fuel and power are a large share of costs: airlines, chemicals, freight and manufacturing. Data centres are also large power consumers, so the AI build-out is not immune.

The AI sell-off matters for a different reason, which is concentration. A handful of Nasdaq heavyweights account for a disproportionate share of the S&P 500's gains and its earnings growth. When they fall, strength elsewhere in the index has to be large to offset it. The brief mentions UK-listed AI-exposed names moving in step with the US leaders, but does not name them. That detail is unverified.

Can earnings carry the index?

The bull case is simple. If third-quarter profits come in solidly above expectations, and managements hold their guidance, multiples can compress without the index falling much. The brief offers no figures to test this: no blended growth rate, no sector breakdown, no count of companies beating estimates. The claim of solid earnings remains unquantified.

The July pattern is a warning. A large technology company reported, and the market punished it for what it planned to spend, not for what it had earned. Earnings season therefore tests two things at once: profit levels, and whether investors still believe the AI capital spending will pay off.

What to watch

Three data points will settle the question. The first is the Brent and WTI settlement prices, and whether $110 holds or fades. The second is the capital-spending guidance from the largest AI spenders in their next reports. The third is whether companies outside technology cite fuel costs when they trim margin forecasts. If oil stays near $110 and the AI leaders keep falling, earnings strength elsewhere will have to carry the index. In that case the first cut to margin guidance from a non-energy company will be the most telling sign.
