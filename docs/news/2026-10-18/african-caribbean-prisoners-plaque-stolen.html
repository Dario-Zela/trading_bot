Plaque honouring African-Caribbean prisoners in Britain stolen within days

In one sentence
A plaque at Portchester Castle honouring about 2,500 African-Caribbean prisoners of war was stolen five days after its unveiling, and no recovery has been reported.

A memorial plaque installed at Portchester Castle in Hampshire to honour African-Caribbean people held there as prisoners of war has been stolen, five days after it was unveiled.

The plaque went up on 9 October, 230 years to the day after the arrival in England of the people it commemorates. Castle staff found it missing on Wednesday 14 October. A padlock on one of the castle gates had been removed, according to reports of the discovery.

The plaque recognises about 2,500 African-Caribbean men, women and children brought to Britain as prisoners of war in 1796. More than 2,000 are reported to have survived the voyage and were held at Portchester, the Norman castle on the edge of Portsmouth Harbour, which was used to hold prisoners during the wars with France.

A small marker for a large omission

The plaque was commissioned by St Vincent and the Grenadines 2nd Generation (SV2G), a UK Caribbean arts and heritage organisation. Funding came from the University of Warwick, whose researchers have worked on the history of Caribbean prisoners held in Britain. The organisations behind it have called for its return.

This is a chapter of British history that rarely appears in school curricula or on castle signage. Portchester's better-known stories are Roman, Norman and Napoleonic. A marker naming the Caribbean prisoners was a modest attempt to put a few lines of that omission right, and it lasted less than a working week.

What is not yet known

No arrest or recovery has been reported, and the available accounts do not say whether police have opened an investigation. Nor is it clear whether the thieves were after the plaque for its metal or for what it said. Metal theft from memorials and public art is common in Britain, and it would be wrong to assume the plaque was taken for its message. Equally, other plaques to Black history have been stolen. A separate case in Leeds, involving a plaque to David Oluwale, led to two arrests.

The gate padlock matters here. It suggests the theft was planned, or at least opportunistic with some preparation, rather than a casual lift from a public wall. It also raises a question for whoever manages the site about how a memorial in a monument that is secured at night can be taken.

What comes next

The first test is practical: whether the plaque is found, or whether SV2G and its funders commission a replacement. The second is whether a replacement is fixed more securely, or sited where it is easier to protect. The group has asked for the original to be returned, and the response of the site's custodians and the police will show how seriously the loss is being treated.

For a memorial meant to make a long-neglected history visible, the theft has done the opposite for now: it has made people ask where the plaque went, and a good many more people have now heard of the prisoners it named.
