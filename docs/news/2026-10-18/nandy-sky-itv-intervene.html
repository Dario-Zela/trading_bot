Nandy 'minded to intervene' in Sky's £1.6bn takeover of ITV's entertainment arm

In one sentence
Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy says she is "minded to intervene" in Sky's £1.6bn takeover of ITV's entertainment arm, with responses due from both companies by 20 October.

The Culture Secretary has told MPs she is "minded to intervene" in Sky's proposed £1.6bn purchase of ITV's media and entertainment arm. The statement was reported on Thursday 15 October. It is a signal of intent, not a decision. If she follows through, a deal that was priced and structured as a commercial transaction would instead be examined for what it does to the range of programming and news available to UK audiences.

What is being bought

Sky, owned by Comcast, which also owns NBCUniversal, would take over ITV's free-to-air channels and its streaming service, ITVX. The combined business would be the UK's largest commercial broadcaster. The deal does not include ITV Studios, the production arm that makes shows such as Love Island. That split matters to the argument. What Sky would acquire is the distribution and scheduling side: the channels and the advertising that sits against them, plus the on-demand platform. It would not own the programme-making.

ITV is listed in London, and its shares have fallen more than 37% over the past five years, a decline that continued this year. That record is the commercial case for a sale. ITV chief executive Carolyn McCall has argued that UK broadcasters compete with YouTube and Netflix rather than with one another. The article reporting Nandy's statement gave no figures for the share-price move on the day.

The Culture Secretary's concerns

Nandy said there are reasonable grounds to believe the merger "might affect availability of a wide range of high-quality and varied programming" on on-demand platforms. A letter from her department, which ITV described as a public interest intervention notice letter, also raises competition rules, free expression in news media and the plurality of broadcasting.

There is a legal wrinkle. One report said current law does not cover the effect of a merger on streaming or on-demand programmes, so extending the review to that ground could need new legislation. That is a reported claim, and the legal basis for the intervention is not set out in the coverage seen here. The mechanics should be treated as unconfirmed until the department publishes its reasoning.

If she does intervene, the usual division of labour is that Ofcom reports on the public-interest questions and the Competition and Markets Authority examines the competition effects. Neither regulator was mentioned in the main report consulted for this piece, so their precise roles in this case are an inference from how such reviews have typically worked.

The parties' responses

Sky and ITV have until 20 October to respond to the letters. Sky said it would "carefully consider" the letter and "engage constructively" with the review, adding that it has committed to protecting ITV's identity, its free-to-air services and its public service broadcasting obligations. ITV said it would "look forward to fully cooperating" with the government.

The companies have also pledged to keep ITV News and Sky News separate until 2031. That is a long commitment by the standards of corporate undertakings, but it has an end date, and plurality arguments tend to concern what happens after the end date. Sky News says its core news offer will remain free, although it has begun exploring paid products, among them a defence and military app at £14.99 a month. Critics are likely to use that as evidence that "free" is a commitment with a shelf life.

What to watch

The first marker is 20 October, when both companies' responses are due. After that, the question is whether Nandy issues a formal notice, and on what grounds. Which grounds she cites will show whether the review is confined to news plurality, where the law is established, or reaches into on-demand programming, where it reportedly is not. A narrow review would be a delay and a set of conditions. A wide one would test whether plurality concerns can override the commercial logic of a sale by a company whose shares have lost more than a third of their value in five years.
