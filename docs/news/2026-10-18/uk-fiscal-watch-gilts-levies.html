Gilts near post-2007 highs squeeze Healey's headroom as banks brace for Budget levies

In one sentence
With the 10-year gilt reportedly near 5.4%, Bloomberg Economics estimates about £12bn could vanish from a £23.6bn headroom buffer, as bank, energy and gambling levies are weighed.

Britain's borrowing costs are doing some of the Chancellor's work for her, and not in a helpful direction. Reports put the 10-year gilt yield near 5.4%, described by City AM as close to its highest since 2007. Bloomberg Economics, as relayed in the discovery brief, estimates that the rise alone could remove about £12bn from the government's fiscal headroom. The figures come from secondary reports; the underlying sources could not be checked directly for this piece.

What headroom is, and what the rise does to it

Headroom is the gap between the Treasury's forecast and the limit set by its own fiscal rules. A bigger gap means more room to absorb a bad forecast, or to spend or cut taxes. The starting figure cited is £23.6bn. If the Bloomberg Economics estimate holds, a £12bn hit would cut that by roughly half.

The mechanism is mechanical. Higher yields raise the interest the government pays on new borrowing and on index-linked and maturing debt as it is refinanced. The official forecaster then builds those higher rates into its projections, and the cost shows up as a smaller margin against the rules. None of this requires a policy decision. That is the point of the angle: the Budget's margin for error is now set largely by bond-market pricing, not by the Chancellor's choices.

The estimate also depends on yields staying where they are. The forecast uses market rates at a cut-off date, so a move lower before then would give some of the margin back. A move higher would take more.

The search for revenue

The second thread is who might be asked to fill the gap. Burnham's government is reportedly weighing levies on banks, on oil and gas, and on gambling, with the stated aim of restoring headroom. The brief gives no amounts for any of these, and no Budget date.

The reports sit awkwardly beside an earlier signal. A Bloomberg report dated 25 September 2026 was headlined "Burnham signals reluctance to increase taxes in UK budget". Both can be true: a government can be reluctant to raise broad taxes while still reaching for sector-specific charges that it can describe as targeted. But the two positions will have to be reconciled in the Budget itself, and the reluctance is harder to sustain if headroom has been halved.

The three named sectors share a feature. Each is a concentrated industry with identifiable profits, which makes a levy simpler to design than a change to income tax or VAT. Whether it is enough is a different question. Without figures, it cannot be said whether such levies would raise anything close to £12bn.

What to watch

Two things decide the outcome. The first is the path of the 10-year gilt yield up to the forecast cut-off: a sustained fall below 5.4% would ease the pressure, while a further rise would deepen it. The second is the Budget itself, where the choice between the levies, spending restraint and a looser reading of the fiscal rules will show how much of the shortfall the government expects the market to return and how much it intends to find elsewhere.
