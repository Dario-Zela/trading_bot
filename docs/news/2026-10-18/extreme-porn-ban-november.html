Government to ban porn depicting choking or incest from early November

In one sentence
New UK restrictions on pornography depicting choking or incest are due to take effect in early November, testing how far the law will police lawful-looking adult content.

The UK government is set to make a category of pornography illegal that, until now, has sat in the lawful part of the adult internet: material depicting choking, and material depicting incest. BBC Technology reports that the restrictions take effect in early November.

The exact commencement date and the legal instrument have not been confirmed here, and neither has the wording of the offence. Those details decide who is caught and how, so what follows is limited to what is established and to what the policy puts at stake.

What is changing

The measures target pornography showing choking, meaning strangulation, and pornography depicting incest. The government has framed both as extreme pornography.

That framing matters. Existing UK law already makes it an offence to possess certain extreme images, such as those depicting serious injury or threats to life, and the underlying acts there are illegal or plainly dangerous. The new categories are different in kind. Much of the material is staged by consenting adult performers, and a good deal of the incest category is fictional role-play. The law is moving from policing the record of a crime to policing a theme.

The questions the law has to answer

Three issues will decide how far this reaches.

Who commits the offence. Possession is one thing, distribution another. A rule aimed at publishers and platforms is a different instrument from one that makes private viewing a criminal matter. Whether individuals face prosecution for what they hold is unconfirmed, and it is the point on which the reach of the criminal law most depends.

What counts as depiction. "Incest" in adult content is often a label, not a description. Performers who are unrelated adults play family roles, and titles and tags say more than the footage does. A statute has to say whether the test is the apparent content, the performers' real relationship or the marketing. Choking raises a parallel problem: the line between a hand on a neck and a more ambiguous frame is a judgement call, and prosecutors and regulators will be the ones making it.

Who enforces it. Sites based abroad, which host much of this material, are hard for UK authorities to reach. Online-safety regulation has given Ofcom powers over platforms that serve UK users, and age-verification duties now apply to adult sites. Whether those same powers can be used to remove newly illegal content, and how quickly, is the enforcement gap. A ban on paper is only as strong as the ability to find the material, assign responsibility and impose a penalty.

Why it matters beyond the category

Supporters argue that this content normalises dangerous acts. Critics, including some civil-liberties and sex-worker groups, argue that criminalising lawful-looking adult material on the basis of theme invites inconsistent enforcement and pushes viewers and performers toward unregulated corners. Both views will be tested on the drafting, not the press release.

For operators, the practical effect is likely to be a rush to audit catalogues, tags and search terms before the date. Large sites can do that; small ones may simply block UK visitors.

What to watch

The first marker is publication of the commencement order and the offence wording, which should settle the possession question. The second is whether Ofcom or another body issues guidance on how "choking" and "incest" are to be identified. The first prosecutions or removal notices after early November will show whether the law is aimed at a few producers or at a much wider audience.
