Plan would make firms pay more to train older workers than youth apprentices

In one sentence
Ministers are reportedly drafting a plan for employers to pay 25% of training costs for apprentices aged 25 and over, while 16-to-24s stay fully funded; the plan is not final.

Ministers in England are drafting a change to apprenticeship funding that would make employers pay part of the cost of training workers aged 25 and over, while training for 16-to-24-year-olds stays fully funded. The plan has not been finalised and is not in force. The details below come from the Guardian's report of 17 October and from FE Week and other outlets that picked it up. All of them rely on sources describing the plan, not on a published government document.

What is proposed

Under the draft, employers would cover 25% of eligible training costs for apprentices aged 25 and over. Funding for apprentices aged 16 to 24 would remain at 100%. Reports say the change would form part of a wider overhaul of the growth and skills levy, and that ministers are considering a phased introduction from September 2027.

FE Week reports that the rule would also reach employers that do not pay the levy. The levy applies to firms with an annual pay bill above £3m, so the rule would extend the change to the smaller employers outside it.

The 25% figure is a co-investment share. It is not a 25% rise in the cost to employers, which for those apprentices is currently close to nothing. Where an employer now pays little or nothing towards an older apprentice's training, a quarter of the bill would be new money. The reports give no total for what that would add across the system.

Why now

The reported motivation is youth unemployment. The plan is said to be part of the government's response to Alan Milburn's review of young people not in education, employment or training. The full report has reportedly been delayed until after the Budget. Youth apprenticeship starts have fallen by about 40% over the past decade, according to the reports.

Older workers have taken up much of the slack. The Economics Observatory has noted that people aged 25 and over accounted for nearly half of apprenticeship starts in 2022/23, a pattern it links in part to how the levy operates. The draft would nudge employers the other way by making the younger recruit the cheaper one.

The policy fits a direction already set. A new incentive of up to £2,000 for non-levy employers, typically smaller firms, that take on 16-to-24-year-old apprentices applies to starts from 1 October 2026. England has already moved the same way elsewhere: from January 2026, government funding for level 7 apprenticeships, which are master's-level programmes, is limited to young people. Older applicants are no longer eligible for those programmes.

What is not known

The reports give no consultation timetable, no estimate of how many apprentices or employers would be affected, and no costing. Which programmes would be covered, and whether any would be exempt, is also unclear. Without those figures the scale is unknown.

The likely effect runs through hiring decisions. A firm choosing between a 22-year-old and a 35-year-old for the same apprenticeship would face a bill for the second and not the first. That could reduce training for career-changers and for existing staff who retrain through apprenticeships, particularly at small firms with thin training budgets. Whether it would push employers towards more youth hiring, or simply towards less training, is untested. The government's stated aim is the former, and the cost to employers of the older cohort is the mechanism it relies on.

What to watch

The next marker is the Budget, after which the delayed Milburn report is expected. Any formal consultation on the levy overhaul would be the first sight of the numbers. A September 2027 start would leave employers about a year to adjust.
