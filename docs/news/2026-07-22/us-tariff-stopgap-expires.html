New US tariffs set to bite as stopgap deadline expires

Shipping containers at a port terminal — the physical front line of the tariff stopgap set to expire Friday. — Igor Ovsyannykov / Unsplash via Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Washington's 10% global tariff stopgap expires Friday with no deal in place, and traders are quietly hedging into the gap rather than betting on an outcome.

The mechanism sounds arcane — Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, a balance-of-payments provision written half a century ago and dusted off five months ago. But the number attached to it is not: a flat 10% duty on most goods entering the United States, layered atop existing tariffs, applied to nearly every trading partner Washington has. That duty lapses at midnight Friday, and nobody in the market has a clean read on what replaces it.

Bloomberg reported Tuesday that the administration is racing to have new tariffs in place before the stopgap expires, aiming to avoid any gap in coverage. As of publication, no comprehensive replacement deal was in place — not with the European Union, not with China, not with India, not with the roughly 60 economies caught up in a parallel investigation into forced-labor trade practices. Traders are left pricing a binary event into a market that, on the surface, does not look like it is pricing much at all.

Borrowed authority, hard expiry

The stopgap exists because of a legal defeat. In February the Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act did not give the president authority to impose tariffs, striking down the reciprocal-tariff regime built through 2025 — duties on Mexico, Canada and China tied to fentanyl and immigration, plus the broader "Liberation Day" schedule. Within the same week, the White House pivoted to Section 122, a provision that permits a temporary import surcharge of up to 15% for balance-of-payments emergencies — but caps it at 150 days unless Congress acts. That clock runs out this week.

Unlike the IEEPA tariffs, Section 122 was never going to be permanent — everyone involved, including the trade lawyers who wrote memos about it in February, flagged the July deadline from day one. What was less certain is what would be ready to take its place, and the answer arriving this week is Section 301: a slower, more litigation-resistant tool built on a specific finding rather than an emergency declaration.

The forced-labor route

The Office of the US Trade Representative spent the spring building that finding. In March it opened Section 301 investigations into 60 economies over their enforcement of import bans on goods made with forced labor. By June, USTR had concluded that all 60 fell short in some way, and proposed two tiers of duty: 12.5% on 46 countries — including China, Japan and Brazil — judged to have no meaningful forced-labor import prohibition at all, and 10% on 14 economies plus the EU, including Canada, Mexico, the UK and Taiwan, which had at least a partial regime in place. A public comment period closed July 6; a hearing followed the next day. Raymond James analysts have described the exercise bluntly, as the White House "reverse engineering" its old reciprocal-tariff structure through a legal channel the courts are less likely to touch.

A second 301 track, examining what USTR calls excess structural capacity across China, the EU and 16 other partners, is still open and due to conclude later this year — a reminder that Friday's deadline settles one layer of the tariff stack, not the whole thing. Meanwhile the diplomatic loose ends multiply: Chinese negotiators say Beijing and Washington agreed to a 90-day truce extension, but the US side has not confirmed sign-off; EU officials dispute the terms of their own arrangement; tariff treatment for India remains unresolved; and several African economies are still waiting to hear where they land.

Markets hedge quietly, not loudly

What is striking is how little of this shows up in headline volatility. The VIX has sat around 16-17 in the run-up to Friday, and the S&P 500 has traded near record territory above 7,300, with AI-driven earnings momentum doing most of the work. But options desks describe a market hedging underneath the surface rather than selling into it: longer-dated index put positions in the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq have been building, consistent with portfolio protection carried through the tariff deadline, a Federal Reserve meeting, and a heavy megacap earnings week rather than a directional bet on Friday itself.

The dollar has been the more puzzling instrument. Tariff uncertainty of this kind has historically supported the greenback as a stress hedge; this time it has stayed range-bound in the high 90s against a basket of peers, with some technical readings pointing toward further softening rather than a safe-haven bid. Ten-year Treasury yields, meanwhile, are hovering near the 4.25% level that traders have flagged as the pivot — durable tariffs that keep goods inflation sticky would argue for yields drifting back toward 4.60%, complicating the Fed's rate-cut calculus just as it heads into its own meeting.

There is a mechanical market effect too, separate from positioning: businesses have been front-loading imports ahead of the deadline for months, and that pull-forward is expected to reverse once the new duties land. Trade estimates point to import volumes falling roughly 5% in August and 6% in September against year-earlier levels — a drag that will show up in freight volumes and inventory data well after Friday's headlines fade.

What comes next

Friday itself resolves less than it appears to. If the Section 301 duties take effect on schedule, the practical change for most trading partners is a shift from a flat, expiring 10% levy to tiered, open-ended rates of 10-12.5% — not a retreat from tariffs, but a change in the legal foundation underneath them. The nearer-term catalysts to watch are the Federal Reserve's policy meeting and the next batch of megacap earnings, both landing in the same window traders are already hedging against; further out, the structural-capacity 301 investigation covering China and the EU is due to report later this year, and could add another layer before 2026 is out.
