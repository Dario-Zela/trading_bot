Trump administration pauses $1bn in Medicaid funding from two states

CMS Administrator Dr. Mehmet Oz, who announced the Medicaid payment freeze alongside HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — U.S. Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
CMS is withholding over $1bn in Medicaid payments from California and Minnesota over fraud claims both states call politically motivated.

The Trump administration is withholding more than $1 billion in Medicaid payments from California and Minnesota, escalating a fight over healthcare fraud that state officials say is really about politics.

Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Administrator Mehmet Oz announced Tuesday that CMS would defer roughly $867.5 million in payments to California and about $199 million to Minnesota, pending review of what officials called "high-risk" claims. The two states can still recover the money if they can show the disputed billing meets federal standards — the funding is paused, not formally cancelled.

What the audits found

CMS pointed to two distinct threads of concern. In California, reviewers flagged spending growth in in-home care programs — services that let elderly and disabled beneficiaries stay out of nursing facilities — that outpaced national trends by a wide enough margin to trigger scrutiny. In both states, officials said they found claims billed for patients who had died and instances of providers billing for improbably large numbers of patients seen at the same time.

The amounts announced Tuesday sit on top of funding already frozen: the administration has withheld $1.3 billion from California since May and nearly $260 million from Minnesota, meaning the two states are now collectively locked out of well over $2.5 billion in federal reimbursement.

States cry foul

Both governors rejected the fraud framing outright. Gavin Newsom's office called it "the same recycled political stunt we've seen before," arguing California "isn't being targeted because Trump has evidence of fraud" but because Oz "doesn't understand that we are saving taxpayers money by keeping seniors and people with disabilities out of far more expensive nursing homes."

Minnesota Governor Tim Walz was blunter still. "They're not punishing fraudsters, they're punishing children, seniors, working families, and people with disabilities," he said, calling the freeze "a campaign of retribution against Minnesota" and noting the administration "is cutting more money in healthcare than they've prosecuted for fraud."

Why it matters beyond two states

Medicaid is a joint federal-state program, and Washington's reimbursement share is the load-bearing wall of most state health budgets — for in-home and long-term care in particular, where margins are thin and providers depend on prompt payment to make payroll. A freeze of this size, layered on top of funding already in limbo since May, forces both states to either front the cash themselves, delay provider payments, or cut services while the dispute plays out.

It also fits a pattern CMS itself has acknowledged: officials described the fraud crackdown as part of a broader push that has so far concentrated on Democratic-led states. Whether that reflects where the spending anomalies actually cluster, or where the administration has chosen to look hardest, is precisely the question Newsom and Walz are raising — and one a court challenge, should either state file one, would need to test.

The near-term marker is procedural: both states now have a window to submit documentation defending the flagged claims before CMS decides whether to release, further reduce, or permanently withhold the money. How that review plays out — and whether other states face similar holds — will show whether this is a genuine anti-fraud enforcement effort or a template for using Medicaid reimbursement as leverage in unrelated policy disputes.
