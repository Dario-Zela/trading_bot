Russian warship conducts live-fire exercise off Plymouth coast

The Russian frigate Neustrashimy, the vessel that conducted Monday's live-fire drill, pictured during a 2008 Baltic exercise alongside a US Navy cruiser. — U.S. Navy photo by Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Mike Banzhaf / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A Russian frigate fired live rounds 40 miles off Plymouth on Burnham's first day as PM; the MoD tracked it, calling it provocative but lawful.

A Russian frigate opened fire with its deck guns some 40 nautical miles south of Plymouth on Monday, in international waters but well within sight of instruments along England's southwest coast — a live-fire drill that the Ministry of Defence says it tracked from start to finish, and that the new government has called a deliberate provocation.

The vessel involved was the Neustrashimy, lead ship of Russia's two-strong Neustrashimy-class frigates and a veteran of the Baltic Fleet commissioned back in 1993. Before firing, it did what maritime protocol requires: it radioed HMS Tyne, the Royal Navy offshore patrol vessel shadowing it, and asked the British ship to move to a safe distance. HMS Tyne complied. The exercise itself lasted about 30 minutes. A French military aircraft also made radio contact mid-drill, asking the Russian crew to clarify their intentions.

A pointed piece of timing

What gives the episode its edge is less the drill itself — Russian warships transiting the Channel is nothing new — than its timing. The live fire came on the first full day of Andy Burnham's premiership, as the new prime minister was reaffirming British support for Kyiv. New Defence Secretary Wes Streeting did not mince words, calling the exercise "performative and irresponsible." "It's not the first time that Russia has behaved like this," he told reporters, "and frankly, it's the tip of the iceberg of the daily threats that this country faces and our allies face."

The Kremlin's response ran the other way. Spokesman Dmitry Peskov told the BBC that "all exercises and all actions undertaken by our warships are carried out in strict compliance with international and maritime law" — a formulation that concedes nothing while inviting scrutiny of exactly where the ship was and what rules applied. Forty miles offshore is comfortably outside Britain's 12-mile territorial waters, which is precisely the point: Moscow gets to stage a show of force while staying, technically, within its rights.

Part of a longer pattern

The Ministry of Defence says it "continues to track the vessel's activity closely, and stands ready to protect UK national security" — language that is calibrated to signal vigilance without suggesting alarm. That calibration matters, because this is not an isolated sighting. UK officials have said Russian naval activity near British waters has risen roughly 30% over the past two years, and in April a Royal Navy surveillance operation spanning more than 450 hours tracked an Akula-class submarine and two specialist deep-sea vessels loitering near undersea cable and pipeline routes in the North Atlantic for over a month. Britain and Norway have since stood up joint patrols, and a new AI-assisted monitoring effort — Nordic Warden — went live in January to flag suspicious vessel behaviour near sensitive infrastructure.

Set against that backdrop, Monday's gunnery drill reads less as an isolated incident than as the visible tip of a steadier campaign of naval pressure — submarines mapping cables out of sight, a frigate firing live rounds in full view. Both serve the same purpose: reminding NATO members that Russia can operate close to their coastlines more or less at will.

Nothing about the exercise breached international law, and nothing in the British response suggests an escalation is imminent — no diplomatic protest has been lodged, no NATO consultation triggered. The question worth watching is whether that restraint holds if the pattern repeats. Streeting's "tip of the iceberg" framing suggests the government expects it to.
