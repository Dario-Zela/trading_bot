UK inflation falls to 2.6%, a boost for Burnham's cost-of-living push

In one sentence
UK inflation eased to 2.6% in June — in line with forecasts — handing new PM Burnham a political win just as the Bank of England's rate-setters remain split on whether to cut, hold, or hike.

Britain's inflation rate eased to 2.6% in the year to June, the Office for National Statistics said Wednesday, down from 2.8% in both April and May and the lowest reading since the Iran war rattled energy markets in the spring. For a government two days old, it landed as close to a gift as economic data gets.

Andy Burnham took office as prime minister on Monday, succeeding Keir Starmer, and by Wednesday morning could point to the first clean piece of good news his administration had not had to manufacture itself. The figure arrived within a whisker of what economists had pencilled in — Pantheon Macroeconomics had called 2.6% exactly, Deutsche Bank 2.7% — which matters for how the reading should be read. This was not a surprise so much as a confirmation: the disinflation trend already under way simply continued.

What actually cooled

ONS chief economist Grant Fitzner attributed the fall chiefly to fuel: a drop in motor fuel prices, and diesel in particular, which fell 10.7 pence a litre month-on-month. Food inflation slid to 1.7%, its lowest since August 2024, with cheaper chocolate, margarine and beef doing the work, and clothing prices fell as summer sales cut deeper than a year earlier. The broader CPIH measure, which folds in owner-occupiers' housing costs, dropped to 2.8% from 3.0%.

Beneath the headline, the picture is stickier. Core inflation — stripping out food and energy — held flat at 2.6%. Services inflation, the measure the Bank of England watches most closely as a gauge of domestically generated price pressure, was unchanged at 3.6%. Owner-occupiers' housing costs stayed at 3.3%, and housing and household services have now been the largest contributor to CPIH inflation for 24 consecutive months. Restaurants and hotels actually accelerated, to 4.4%. The headline fell; the parts of inflation the Bank treats as more persistent did not move.

A win Burnham didn't have to build

The timing suits a government that had already staked its opening days on the cost of living. Burnham used his acceptance speech to ask "how can we have control over inflation, public spending, and the rest of the economy" without "sufficient public control over the cost of the essentials," and followed it within days with an £850m cut to VAT on energy bills, from 5% to zero, funded in part by shelving a planned digital ID scheme. New chancellor John Healey — a surprise pick, having spent the past two years as defence secretary rather than anywhere near the Treasury — inherits a set of internal government forecasts that reportedly put inflation back up near 3.2% by the fourth quarter, a reminder that Wednesday's number is a low point passing through, not a new plateau.

That gap — a cooling headline rate against a warmer internal forecast — is the honest way to read the politics here. Burnham inherited a data print that was already heading down before he took office; the VAT cut, whose energy-bill effect is expected to shave only around 0.1 percentage points off CPI once it feeds through, had barely had time to register in June's basket. The government gets the framing of the win. It does not yet get the credit for the mechanism.

The Bank's harder calculation

For the Bank of England, the more interesting number is not the headline but the vote it will inform. At its June meeting the Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% by 7–2, and the dissent ran in the hawkish direction: two members wanted a rise to 4%, not a cut, citing volatility in global energy markets tied to the continuing Middle East conflict. Gilt yields touched roughly 5% in that period — territory not seen since the financial crisis — before easing back toward 4.8%. Sterling has spent recent sessions rangebound around $1.34–1.35, with traders weighing Healey's appointment and Wednesday's data in the same breath as renewed oil-price risk.

A softer CPI print gives the MPC's more dovish members ammunition, but it does not resolve the underlying disagreement. Core and services inflation sitting still, oil supply risk from the Gulf, and a government forecast that expects inflation to reaccelerate all argue for caution. Markets currently price the Bank holding at 3.75% through the rest of the year, with only a minority wagering on a cut before one on a hike. Wednesday's data nudges the odds; it does not settle them.

What's next

The Bank's next scheduled rate decision falls on 30 July, eight days after this reading and ten days into Healey's tenure at the Treasury — the first real test of whether a 2.6% headline print is enough to shift a committee that just split 7–2 the other way. Mortgage holders hoping for cheaper fixed rates are watching the same date. And the number to watch after that is whether the government's own forecast of inflation climbing back toward 3.2% by the fourth quarter proves right, which would make Wednesday's reading less a turning point than a pause on the way back up.
