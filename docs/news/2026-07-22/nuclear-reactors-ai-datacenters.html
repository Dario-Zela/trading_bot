Oklo, X-Energy join White House push to fast-track nuclear reactors for AI data centres

The White House, where the Trump administration is coordinating a federal push to speed nuclear reactor deployment for AI data centers. — Martin Falbisoner / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oklo and X-Energy are joining a $200m White House-backed push to fast-track nuclear reactors for AI data centers — though neither firm has a reactor running yet.

The Trump administration is preparing to put federal money and regulatory muscle behind a bet that has, until now, mostly lived in slide decks and press releases: that advanced nuclear reactors can be built fast enough to keep America's data centers lit.

On Monday, Bloomberg reported that reactor developers Oklo and X-Energy are joining a $200 million federal push, alongside Microsoft and Nvidia, aimed at speeding the design, licensing and construction of new nuclear plants built specifically to power artificial intelligence infrastructure. Details are expected as soon as Wednesday at an AI energy summit convened by the Department of Energy. Both companies' shares jumped in after-hours trading on the news — X-Energy as much as 12%, Oklo nearly 10% — a reminder that this remains, for now, as much a story about investor sentiment as about kilowatts.

"If the United States backs away or drags our feet on data centers, China wins."

That's Energy Secretary Chris Wright, laying out the administration's underlying logic earlier this month: AI is now a geopolitical contest, and electricity is the bottleneck.

What the money actually buys

The $200 million isn't going toward pouring concrete. According to Bloomberg's reporting, several Department of Energy national laboratories and the University of Texas at Austin will share $60 million of it over three years, with the rest directed at the underlying problem slowing every advanced reactor project in the country: the gap between a design on paper and a plant with a Nuclear Regulatory Commission license. The stated goals are blunt — cut the time it takes to design, license and build new reactors, and cut the number of staff needed to run them once they're operating.

That framing matters because it's a tacit admission of where the industry actually stands. Oklo, chaired by OpenAI's Sam Altman since its 2024 SPAC merger, is building its first Aurora powerhouse — a 75-megawatt liquid-metal-cooled reactor — at Idaho National Laboratory, where the NRC accepted a key design filing for review in 15 days rather than the usual 30 to 60. X-Energy, which went public in April, is working toward its first four-unit Xe-100 plant at Dow's Seadrift chemical site on the Texas Gulf Coast, backed by a $500 million investment from Amazon and a $700 million Series D round closed last November. Neither company has a reactor operating commercially yet.

The power problem this is meant to solve

The policy push is a direct response to a genuine constraint. Data centers built to train and run AI models are colliding with a grid that wasn't built for them — developers now expect real power shortages by 2027-28, and more than 2,500 gigawatts of projects worldwide are stuck in grid-connection queues. Hyperscalers have responded by trying to buy their own power outright: Microsoft, Amazon and Google are between them planning to spend well over $1 trillion on data center build-outs through 2026, and nuclear is the option that offers round-the-clock output without the emissions of gas.

The trouble is scale and speed. Industry analysts note that every announced hyperscaler nuclear deal to date — combined — would add only around 13 gigawatts of capacity, translating to roughly 100 terawatt-hours a year, and not until the mid-2030s at the earliest. That's a rounding error against the scale of AI's projected electricity appetite, which some estimates put in the range of 1,050 terawatt-hours a year by 2026 for data centers generally.

Reasons for skepticism

The gap between announcement and electron is where this story has repeatedly stalled before. TerraPower's Natrium project, one of the furthest along in the sector, has already seen its target for power-on-grid slip to 2031, and carries a cost estimate near $9.4 billion for a plant rated at just 345 megawatts — roughly double the cost per megawatt of a conventional large reactor, which is itself about three times pricier than solar or wind. NuScale's Idaho project was cancelled outright after utilities balked at a $9.3 billion price tag. No small modular reactor is currently under construction in the United States.

Seen against that backdrop, Amazon's $500 million and Terrapower's roughly $650 million in outside fundraising look less like plant financing and more like feasibility-stage bets — Amazon's own agreement reportedly commits it only to funding an initial feasibility phase, with an option, not an obligation, to buy the power later. Oklo itself remains pre-revenue, posted a $73.6 million net loss last year, and has seen its stock fall from a 2025 peak above $190 to under $50 — still valued near $8 billion by markets betting on a reactor that hasn't yet produced a watt of commercial electricity.

None of that makes the federal push meaningless. Shaving regulatory years off a licensing process that has historically taken a decade or more is a real lever, and pairing it with DOE lab expertise and university research money is a more concrete mechanism than an executive order alone. But $200 million spread across research support, against price tags in the billions for a single plant, is not itself construction money — it is an attempt to clear the runway, not fuel to make the plane fly faster than physics and concrete pours allow.

What to watch

Wednesday's DOE summit should clarify whether this is a funding commitment or a coordination exercise — watch for whether specific reactor sites, licensing deadlines, or offtake agreements with Microsoft and Nvidia are named, versus vaguer language about "partnership" and "acceleration." The more durable test is further out: whether the NRC's compressed review timelines for Oklo's Aurora design hold once the agency moves from paper review to construction inspection, and whether X-Energy's Seadrift project breaks ground on the schedule Dow and the DOE have set. Until a reactor built under this initiative actually reaches criticality, the honest read is that Washington has bought itself a faster regulatory process — not yet a faster power grid.
