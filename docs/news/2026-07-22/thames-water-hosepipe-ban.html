Thames Water imposes hosepipe ban on 10 million customers

Wraysbury Reservoir, part of the network supplying London, as Thames Water imposes its broadest-ever use restrictions — Mx. Granger / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Thames Water's widest-ever hosepipe ban hits 10.1 million customers as heat-driven demand outstrips a network already strained by debt and underinvestment.

Thames Water will impose the widest hosepipe ban in its history from 00.01 BST on Thursday, restricting non-essential water use for the 10.1 million customers it supplies across London, the Thames Valley and the Home Counties. It is the clearest sign yet that England's largest water utility, already fighting for financial survival, is also struggling to keep pace with the physical strain of a hotter, drier climate.

The company says water use in the Thames Valley and Home Counties is currently running about 10% above normal, with London around 7% above normal — together adding roughly 100 million litres a day to the network, which Thames Water likens to connecting every home in Manchester to its pipes overnight. The trigger has been a run of three official heatwaves and the driest spring on record for England and Wales, compounding pressure that had already been building through the year.

"We would not be taking this step unless it was necessary to protect water supplies for everyone and the environment," said Nevil Muncaster, Thames Water's director of strategic water resources, in the company's announcement.

What the ban actually covers

The Temporary Use Ban — the formal name for what is popularly called a hosepipe ban — bars customers from using hosepipes to water gardens or allotments, wash cars, clean patios and windows, fill or top up paddling pools and hot tubs, or run mains-connected sprinklers. Buckets and watering cans remain permitted, as does water use for health and safety reasons or by customers with severe mobility problems. Breaching the restriction is a criminal offence under the Water Industry Act, though enforcement in practice tends to rely on public compliance rather than prosecutions.

Thames Water points to work under way to shore up supply: more than 750 leaks repaired weekly, over 100km of new mains laid since April 2025, 1.3 million smart meters installed and 70,000 acoustic sensors deployed to detect leaks earlier, framed by the company as the largest upgrade to its network in 150 years. Critics will note that leakage and demand management have been persistent weak points for the company for years, and that a ban of this scale — covering roughly a quarter of Britain's population once other regional restrictions are counted — suggests the upgrades have not yet caught up with demand.

A company already under strain

The timing compounds an already difficult year for Thames Water. The utility is carrying a debt load of roughly £19–20bn and has said it has funding secured only through the fourth quarter of 2026, with talks ongoing among creditors, regulators and government over a recapitalisation that could avert the company being placed into special administration — a form of temporary public ownership reserved for insolvent water companies. A creditor consortium's rescue proposal reportedly includes a commitment to take no dividends for a decade. That history is not incidental: Ofwat fined Thames Water £123m in May 2025 for what it called systemic failures in managing wastewater assets, including £18.2m tied to unauthorised dividend payments, and campaigners have long linked the company's underinvestment to the dividend-heavy ownership period under Macquarie between 2006 and 2017.

A hosepipe ban does not directly touch the balance sheet, but it lands at a moment when regulators, creditors and the government are all scrutinising whether Thames Water's infrastructure spending matches the scale of the risks it faces. Demand shocks driven by heat are exactly the kind of stress that rate-base investment is supposed to absorb; a ban this broad signals the buffer has been thin.

What to watch

The ban has no fixed end date and Thames Water says it will stay in place until supplies and the environment are no longer under pressure — a decision likely tied to rainfall over the coming weeks rather than a calendar date. The more consequential clock is the one running toward Thames Water's Q4 funding cliff, when the company's recapitalisation talks with creditors and ministers will need to produce a durable settlement, or hand the government a decision on special administration it has so far tried to avoid.
