Baby puffin spotted on Dorset coast for first time in three decades

An Atlantic puffin, the species clinging to a last mainland-England foothold on Dorset's Jurassic Coast. — Andreas Trepte / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
A puffin chick has been confirmed at Dorset's last mainland colony for the first time in 30 years — though whether it survives to fledge won't be known until late summer.

Somewhere in the crevices of a Jurassic Coast cliff called Dancing Ledge, a puffin chick appears to have hatched and survived long enough to be counted. Conservationists monitoring England's last mainland puffin colony describe it as the first confirmed chick on the Dorset coast in three decades — a rare piece of good news for a colony that has spent most of that time dying out in slow motion.

The numbers explain why the sighting matters. Some 85 puffins were counted at Purbeck in 1958; by 1975 that had fallen to 23. Today the colony clinging to the limestone ledges near Swanage numbers three breeding pairs, watched over every spring by National Trust rangers and volunteers from the Purbeck Natural History Forum. No chick is known to have fledged there in around a decade — 2023 came closest, when adults were seen carrying fish into a nesting fissure for about 25 days before the deliveries stopped abruptly, suggesting the chicks died just short of flying age. In 2024 and 2025, there were no fish deliveries at all, meaning the eggs likely never hatched.

What's actually confirmed

This year's six adult puffins returned to the cliffs between late March and mid-April, and by May all three pairs had been seen displaying courtship and nest-building behaviour. To work out why chicks keep failing, rangers led by the ecologist Dr Richard Caldow installed improved solar-powered, motion-sensor cameras in the nesting crevices — an upgrade on a 2024 system that captured 70,000 images and still found no predators, no clear culprit, nothing. Because the cameras don't livestream, nobody learns what happened in a given season until the equipment is retrieved and reviewed later in the summer.

That detail matters for how much weight to put on this week's news. A confirmed chick sighting, if it holds up, would be the clearest sign yet that whatever has been going wrong — food shortages, disturbance, something in the crevice itself — didn't happen this time. But it is one data point from three pairs at a single site, not evidence that puffins are recovering more broadly on the south coast, and it says nothing yet about whether the chick will fledge. The most recent confirmed near-miss, in 2023, also looked promising for close to a month before it wasn't.

Why one nest carries so much weight

Dancing Ledge is the last place on mainland England's south coast where puffins still try to breed at all, which is why a single successful hatch reads as a milestone rather than a footnote. Atlantic puffins nationally are listed as vulnerable to extinction, squeezed by warming seas that shift the sandeels they depend on, and by predation and disturbance at colonies from Shetland to the Farnes. A three-pair colony in Dorset was never going to move that needle on its own — its value has always been more symbolic than demographic, a marker of whether this stretch of coastline can still support a species that thrived here within living memory.

What happens next is straightforward, if slow: the chick needs to survive roughly six weeks in the crevice before fledging, and the camera footage recording that process won't be reviewed until the equipment comes down later this summer. Only then will rangers know whether 2026 breaks the run of failed seasons — or simply extends it by one more near-miss.
