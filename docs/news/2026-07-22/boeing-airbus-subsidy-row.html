Boeing presses Washington over EU's record loan to Airbus

A Boeing 737 MAX at its roll-out ceremony in Renton, Washington. — Aka The Beav from Seattle, Washington / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Boeing asked the US Trade Representative to press the EU for transparency on a record €3bn EIB loan to Airbus, days after a tariff truce extension.

Boeing has written to the office of the US Trade Representative asking Washington to press the European Union for a full accounting of a €3 billion ($3.43 billion) loan package the European Investment Bank has extended to Airbus — the EIB's largest-ever corporate loan. The letter, addressed to USTR Jamieson Greer and reported by Reuters, lands just days after the US and EU agreed to extend the truce that has kept a two-decade subsidy war between the two planemakers in check.

The timing is the crux of Boeing's objection. The EIB announced the loan on June 29, only four days after the EU formally adopted its decision to extend the 2021 standstill agreement — the deal that ended years of retaliatory tariffs stemming from parallel World Trade Organization rulings against both companies for illegal state aid. An initial €1 billion tranche has already been signed, with the rest of the €3 billion earmarked for Airbus research and development through 2030.

"At a minimum, the timing of this loan is surprising," Boeing said in the letter. It asked the USTR to request a "full accounting of the terms of this loan" and to establish whether it is compatible with the standstill agreement, which committed both sides to an "open and transparent process" on future government support for their aircraft programmes.

A dispute that never really closed

The 2021 truce was meant to draw a line under 17 years of litigation at the WTO, in which both Boeing and Airbus were found to have received illegal subsidies — European launch aid in Airbus's case, US tax breaks and defence contracts in Boeing's. That finding is what makes the current spat awkward for both sides: neither wants a full re-adjudication of who owes whom, which is presumably why Washington has been cautious in public. The USTR has not officially confirmed extending its own tariff truce tied to the dispute, though people familiar with the talks say both governments have effectively shelved the WTO fight rather than resolve it.

The EIB has pushed back hard on the idea that anything irregular occurred. "This is a normal loan, carrying interest, part of the EIB's overall financing activity," the bank said, noting it finances thousands of companies annually. Airbus's chief financial officer, Thomas Toepfer, told reporters in London the financing had been taken out "completely at market level." Airbus has also flagged the loan as part of Europe's push for aerospace and defence "strategic autonomy" — language EIB president Nadia Calviño has used publicly, which is unlikely to reassure a Boeing government-affairs team already primed to read industrial-policy signalling into any European state-adjacent financing.

Why it matters beyond the letter

None of this amounts to a reopened trade war — no new tariffs have been proposed, and Boeing's ask is narrower than the sweeping retaliatory duties both sides traded through the early 2020s. But the letter is a useful data point for anyone pricing transatlantic aerospace risk: barely a week after the truce extension was announced as a diplomatic win, the underlying grievance — who gets to subsidise national-champion planemakers, and how quietly — is back on the table. The truce has proven durable enough to prevent tariffs from snapping back, but not durable enough to stop either side from immediately testing its edges.

For Boeing specifically, the complaint arrives at an awkward moment competitively. Airbus used last week's Farnborough Airshow to lift its medium-term delivery outlook and announce a share buyback, underscoring a production lead it has held over Boeing since the 737 MAX's grounding years. A three-billion-euro war chest for next-generation R&D, even at market rates, sharpens that gap in exactly the area — new aircraft programmes — where Boeing can least afford to fall further behind.

The next marker to watch is whether the USTR responds publicly to Boeing's request, or handles it quietly through diplomatic channels as it has with previous subsidy complaints under the standstill framework. A formal US response, or an EU refusal to disclose the loan's terms, would be the clearest signal yet of whether the truce holds through 2026 or starts fraying at the edges.
