US strikes on Iran continue as Tehran hits Gulf allies, killing US troops

A US Navy sailor stands watch aboard the guided-missile destroyer USS Paul Hamilton during a transit of the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint at the centre of the widening US-Iran conflict. — Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Elliot Schaudt / US Navy
In one sentence
US strikes on Iran hit an 11th night as Iranian retaliation kills US troops in Jordan and Iraq and Hegseth seeks $67bn more to fund the war.

Eleven nights ago, the United States began bombing Iran. It has not stopped since. Overnight Tuesday, US Central Command flew its eleventh consecutive round of strikes against Iranian targets — military command centres, aircraft hangars, drone depots, the logistics chain that keeps Tehran's forces fighting — and President Trump said Washington would probably soon hit Pickaxe Mountain, a fortified underground facility tied to Iran's nuclear programme. Iran has not absorbed the campaign quietly. Over the past week its retaliatory strikes have reached into Jordan, Bahrain and Kuwait — three of the Gulf's most important US-aligned states — killing American troops and setting energy infrastructure ablaze. The war that many hoped a June ceasefire framework might contain is instead widening, and its costs are now showing up in two places at once: the Pentagon's ledger and the price of moving oil through the world's most important chokepoint.

Eleven nights, and no exit

The conflict traces back to February 28, when the US first struck Iranian nuclear and military sites. A June 17 memorandum of understanding had briefly pointed toward a 60-day negotiating window between Washington and Tehran. That window is now effectively void. CENTCOM says the renewed campaign is meant to strip Iran of its "ability to threaten commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz," a formulation that itself concedes the strait is the point. Explosions have been reported across Tehran, in Tabriz to the northwest, and across the south of the country. Earlier in the war the US massed three aircraft carrier strike groups in the region — the USS Gerald R. Ford, USS Abraham Lincoln and USS George H.W. Bush — the largest concentration of American naval power in the Middle East since 2003, before drawing back to two as the June truce briefly held.

The Gulf pays the price

Iran's response has landed squarely on the American basing network that rings the Gulf. In Jordan, air defences intercepted several incoming missiles, but a strike still killed two US service members and forced the evacuation of four more; a further American died in northern Iraq during the controlled detonation of a downed Iranian drone. In Bahrain, Iran's Revolutionary Guard said it hit weapons-support warehouses, a satellite communications centre and a building housing US forces at the Juffair base. In Kuwait, drone strikes have twice in two days hit a power and desalination plant, alongside a Patriot air-defence battery, fuel tanks and an ammunition depot at a facility housing American forces. The Pentagon says 16 US service members have now been killed and more than 430 wounded since the war began — a toll that has hardened political pressure in Washington for further retaliation even as it raises the question of how much more the basing arrangements that underpin US Gulf strategy can absorb. Two UAE-flagged tankers were also struck in the strait itself, killing one crew member, a reminder that the shipping lanes are not collateral to this war but one of its objects.

The bill comes due

The financial strain is now surfacing in public testimony. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth told the Senate Appropriations Committee on Tuesday that the war has cost the Pentagon $37.5 billion to date — up from a $29 billion estimate in May — and asked Congress for $67.1 billion in emergency supplemental funding, citing munitions consumption, fuel shortfalls and equipment losses from sustained operations. "The military requires a generational investment to deliver on peace through strength," Hegseth said, describing the request as "a new request based on new realities." Air Force officials have separately warned that training will be curtailed without the money. Some Navy and Air Force operating budgets are on track to run out this month. Not everyone in Congress is convinced: Senator Patty Murray, the Appropriations Committee's ranking Democrat, said the panel would not "rubber stamp" the request given a base defence budget already above $1 trillion a year. CNN has reported that the Pentagon's internal, all-in cost estimate — including infrastructure repair — runs as high as $80 billion to $100 billion, well above the public $37.5 billion figure, underscoring how a campaign originally sold as a short, sharp operation has become an open-ended drain on reserves.

Markets already pricing the risk

The Strait of Hormuz carries roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and gas, and the market has not waited for the war to end before repricing it. War-risk insurance for vessels transiting the strait has surged to between 3% and 10% of hull value, up from a pre-war norm of roughly 0.25% — meaning a $100 million tanker now carries a war-risk premium of $3 million to $10 million for a single transit, against roughly $250,000 before hostilities began. Crude prices have eased back toward $70 a barrel from their spring peaks, but analysts caution that the market may be underpricing the risk of a prolonged blockade and the cost of rebuilding inventories once it lifts. Roughly 6,000 seafarers are currently stuck in the region as the International Maritime Organisation works to route vessels around the worst of the strikes. For markets, the read-through is less about a single spike than persistence: a geopolitical risk premium that had partly faded during the June truce is being rebuilt in real time, in tanker rates, in options markets, and in the growing gap between the Pentagon's public and private cost estimates.

The next concrete markers are near-term. The Senate Appropriations Committee must act on the $67.1 billion request, and Murray's resistance suggests it will not pass untouched. Trump's threat to strike Pickaxe Mountain, if carried out, would mark a further escalation against Iran's nuclear infrastructure specifically, with unpredictable knock-on effects for both the shipping lanes and any residual chance of reviving the June negotiating framework. Until one side de-escalates, both the human toll in the Gulf and the financial toll in Washington look set to keep climbing together.
