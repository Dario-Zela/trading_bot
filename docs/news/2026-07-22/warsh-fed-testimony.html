Fed chair Warsh testifies to Congress amid policy-disconnect concerns

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair on May 22, 2026, in his official Board of Governors portrait. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors
In one sentence
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh talked tough on inflation to Congress, but by dropping forward guidance he's left markets pricing rate hikes for 2027 even as bank economists still expect cuts.

Kevin Warsh spent two days on Capitol Hill this past week doing what new Fed chairs are supposed to do — deliver the central bank's semiannual report, take questions, project resolve. He was unambiguous on inflation. He was far less clear on where interest rates go next. And in the week since, the gap between how his Fed is talking and how markets are pricing has only widened.

The testimony, on July 14 before the House Financial Services Committee and July 15 before the Senate Banking Committee, was Warsh's first appearance before Congress since he was sworn in on May 22, succeeding Jerome Powell. It landed hours after a surprisingly soft June inflation report — consumer prices fell 0.4% on the month, the sharpest drop in six years, pulling annual inflation down to 3.5% from May's three-year high of 4.2%. Economists had expected a smaller decline. Warsh didn't take the win.

"I'm not going to show up here and say mission accomplished," he told the House committee. "There's plenty of work to do."

"A tax on the American people"

Warsh's framing of inflation was pointed rather than technical. "The 63 months of inflation above target has been an unfair burden and has been a tax on the American people and businesses," he said, calling for a "regime change in policy." He also fielded the question every new Fed chair gets asked in some form — whether he takes orders from the White House that appointed him. Rep. Nydia Velázquez asked directly if he worked for President Trump. "We're an independent central bank," Warsh replied. Sen. Elizabeth Warren pressed harder, tying questions about a reported $100 million payment Warsh received before taking office to the Fed's credibility, saying the tone he'd set "seems to invite corruption." Trump, who nominated Warsh in January partly on the expectation of lower rates, has since said he wants him to "be totally independent" — while continuing to call publicly for cuts.

A guidance vacuum

The more consequential move came a month earlier, at Warsh's first FOMC meeting as chair on June 16-17, and it's still working through markets. The committee held its target range at 3.50%-3.75%, unremarkable on its own. What changed was the statement itself — trimmed to a fraction of its usual 300-to-400-word length — and Warsh's explicit refusal to offer forward guidance, a practice the Fed has used in some form since 2003. Asked at his press conference about the case for patience, he turned the question back on the room: "Your question sounded like an encouragement for me to give forward guidance."

The committee itself is split on where rates go from here. Of the policymakers submitting projections at that meeting, roughly half favored higher rates by year-end, most of the rest wanted to hold, and just one penciled in a cut. Analysts read the statement's brevity as deliberate. "Warsh's first FOMC statement left the clear impression that there is a new chair in town," said Ian Lyngen, BMO's head of U.S. rates strategy. Bespoke's George Pearkes was more pointed about the cost: forward guidance, he noted, "has served to suppress volatility and anchor market expectations, and that has led to lower borrowing rates, relative to alternatives." Treasury yields moved higher in the weeks that followed as traders repriced for a Fed that wasn't going to tell them what it planned to do.

Markets and economists are betting on opposite things

With the Fed declining to signal, market pricing and professional forecasters have gone their separate ways. In the near term they roughly agree — CME FedWatch data show traders assigning an 86% probability the Fed holds rates at its next meeting, July 28-29. Beyond that, they diverge sharply. Those same futures markets imply a 98% probability the federal funds rate sits at 4.00%-4.25% by June 2027 — two hikes from today's range. A SIFMA survey of economists at more than two dozen major banks, including Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan and Morgan Stanley, points the other way: most still expect one to two cuts in 2027.

The two camps aren't disagreeing about inflation — 89% of the surveyed economists see inflation expectations as anchored, and both groups broadly pencil in core PCE easing from around 3.2% at the end of this year to 2.5% in 2027. They disagree about how the Fed will respond to that same data, which is precisely the kind of ambiguity a guidance-free Fed leaves on the table. Tellingly, nearly two-thirds of the SIFMA respondents named a Fed hike that ends the equity rally as a bigger risk than inflation reaccelerating — a rally Warsh himself has linked to AI-driven data-center investment, which he called "the most striking feature of the economy right now" in his testimony. Higher long-end rates raise the cost of financing exactly the kind of capital spending that has pushed indices like the Nasdaq 100 through fresh highs this year.

The next data point arrives fast: the FOMC meets July 28-29, a week after Warsh's testimony and with June's cooler inflation print in hand. A hold is priced in almost unanimously. What happens to the 2027 curve — whether it drifts back toward the economists' cuts or hardens further around the market's hikes — will say more about whether Warsh's silence is a deliberate strategy or a communications problem he'll eventually have to fix.
