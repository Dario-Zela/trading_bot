3M turns to AI tools to fuel its turnaround push

In one sentence
3M is spending $250m over three years automating factory work as CEO Bill Brown bets AI-branded efficiency can succeed where 2023's mass layoffs didn't.

3M raised its full-year profit forecast on Tuesday and its shares jumped roughly 8-10 percent, the latest data point in a two-year effort by chief executive Bill Brown to convince investors that the Post-it and Scotch Tape maker can grow again. Increasingly, the pitch runs through artificial intelligence — not the chatbot kind, but AI folded into the factory floor.

The company now expects adjusted earnings of $8.80 to $8.95 a share for 2026, up from a prior range of $8.50 to $8.70, on second-quarter sales of $6.5 billion and organic growth of 5.4 percent. Operating margin reached 24.9 percent, up 40 basis points. Brown told investors the quarter delivered "robust operating margins" alongside "double-digit EPS growth."

What the automation actually does

3M is spending more than $250 million over three years automating tasks it says are repetitive enough to standardize across its network: an estimated 7,000 material handlers moving goods through warehouses, roughly 600 workers doing manual visual inspection for defects, and about 500 manual slitting operations that cut large rolls of material to size. At a plant in Nevada, automating the slitting line lifted productivity by 30 percent per square yard per hour; the company says its cost of poor-quality output fell about 100 basis points year over year, on-time-in-full delivery held above 90 percent, and inventory levels dropped three days' worth. Brown has described the broader push — replacing manual handling and inspection with machine-vision and automated material flow — as embedding an "AI-first mentality" while 3M shifts from a loose holding-company structure to a more centrally run operating company.

A second, less obvious AI angle: 3M is also selling into the boom rather than just adopting it. Its Expanded Beam Optics fiber connectors, which cut data-center cable installation time by about 85 percent, landed a deal with Microsoft this year as the first named hyperscaler customer. 3M expects $40-50 million in EBO revenue in 2026, a figure it says could grow four- or fivefold as hyperscalers build out AI infrastructure — a welcome adjacency for an industrial conglomerate that spent the last decade better known for lawsuits than growth.

Old cost-cutting, new label

The skepticism, where it exists, isn't about whether the automation works — the Nevada numbers are real — but about whether it adds up to more than 3M has tried before. The company cut 2,500 manufacturing jobs in January 2023 and another 6,000 positions that April under previous CEO Mike Roman, promising $700-900 million in pretax savings; the stock kept sliding anyway, weighed down by PFAS litigation and an earplug liability settlement tied to its Aearo subsidiary. 3M spun off its health-care unit as Solventum in April 2024 and brought in Brown, a L3Harris veteran and outsider, the following month. Shares have since risen about 69 percent, and 2025 brought a 200-basis-point expansion in adjusted operating margin to 23.4 percent.

But CFRA analyst Jonathan Sakraida, describing investor sentiment around the current quarter, said shareholders are "still waiting to see that this is a consistent growth story," wary that recent gains reflect pricing actions and easy margin comparisons more than durable transformation. Two-year average organic growth has run around 2.1 percent, and Wall Street's own forecast for the next twelve months, at roughly 2.9 percent, isn't much better. Net profit margin over the trailing year sits at 11.9 percent, down from 16 percent a year earlier once one-off charges are counted in — a reminder that the adjusted-earnings story and the GAAP one still diverge.

The honest read is that 3M's AI-branded automation is doing something previous restructuring rounds didn't: producing measurable, repeatable plant-level gains rather than just headcount reductions. Whether that scales past pilot lines to the rest of a 25,000-supplier network — and shows up as top-line growth rather than margin arithmetic — is the question third-quarter results, due in October, will start to answer.
