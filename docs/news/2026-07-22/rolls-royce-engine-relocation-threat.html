Rolls-Royce threatens to build £3bn jet engine programme abroad without Burnham backing

A Rolls-Royce Trent turbofan — the family of engines the company hopes UltraFan will one day join. — Jorge Láscar / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Rolls-Royce is pressing Andy Burnham's week-old government for £100-200m of a £3bn UK engine programme, threatening to build it in Germany or the US instead.

Rolls-Royce has told the government it needs an answer, and soon. The Derby-headquartered engine maker is pressing Sir Andy Burnham's fortnight-old administration to commit public money to its £3 billion UltraFan 30 programme — or watch the work, and the jobs behind it, go to Germany or the United States instead.

The ultimatum lands at an awkward moment. Burnham became prime minister on 20 July, two days before this piece was filed, after Keir Starmer's resignation triggered the fastest Labour leadership contest in a generation. He has barely had time to find the Downing Street light switches. Now one of Britain's most totemic industrial names is asking him, in effect, to prove that a government built partly on the promise of regional renewal can also hold the line on national industrial policy — and to do it fast.

What Rolls-Royce is asking for

The UltraFan 30 is Rolls-Royce's bid to re-enter the narrowbody engine market — the workhorse single-aisle jets that make up the bulk of global short-haul fleets — more than a decade after it exited the segment. A 30,000lb-thrust geared turbofan with a fan pushing towards 90 inches and a bypass ratio of nearly 15:1, it promises up to 20% better fuel burn than engines currently in service and compatibility with 100% sustainable aviation fuel. Rolls-Royce completed a concept review in June and wants to build a demonstrator through 2028, with flight tests following towards the end of the decade.

None of that happens without money. Having already sunk more than £500 million into UltraFan technology, the company is seeking an initial £100 million to £200 million from the government to fund development and testing this year, as a first tranche of the wider £3 billion programme. Chief executive Tufan Erginbilgiç has been in talks with Business Secretary Peter Kyle over how that might be structured — through the Aerospace Technology Institute, the National Wealth Fund, or traditional launch aid.

Erginbilgiç's argument is comparative rather than sentimental. He has pointed to the scale of state backing available to rivals — GE Aerospace and Pratt & Whitney in the US, Safran in France — as the yardstick Britain is failing to meet. "Our competitors get two or three times what we get," he has said. "It is a competitive world and you need to think about that." It is a company that has learned to speak fluently in shareholder logic: since Erginbilgiç took over in 2023, Rolls-Royce shares have risen roughly 115% from their 2025 trough, underlying operating profit guidance for 2026 sits at £4.0–4.2 billion, and the stock trades on a price-to-earnings ratio of 46 — rich, but a long way down from the 66 it carried at the start of the year. This is not a company begging for a lifeline. It is a company negotiating from strength.

Why the threat carries weight

Rolls-Royce says a UK-based UltraFan programme would support up to 40,000 jobs across the company and its supply chain and generate more than £100 billion in economic value over its lifetime — figures that are, inevitably, the company's own, and which it has every incentive to inflate ahead of a funding decision. But the underlying strategic logic is real. Airbus and Boeing are expected to commit to next-generation single-aisle designs around the turn of the next decade, and whichever engine maker is ready with a certified, flying alternative to the CFM International–Pratt & Whitney duopoly stands to capture a slice of a market Rolls-Royce has put at roughly £1.6 trillion in lifetime value. Missing that window would lock the company out of the segment for another generation.

That is what gives the relocation threat its teeth. Rolls-Royce would, by its own account, prefer to keep the programme in Britain — Erginbilgiç has said as much — but the company says it must soon decide where future engineering and manufacturing work will actually sit, and it is being courted actively by officials in Berlin and Washington, who can point to deeper state-aid pots and, in America's case, a domestic aerospace-industrial complex hungry for the work.

A test for the new government

The politics are pointed. Burnham spent nine years as mayor of Greater Manchester building a reputation as the politician most fluent in the language of regional economic renewal — devolved transport, local industrial strategy, the pitch that power and investment could be pulled out of Whitehall and pushed towards the regions. He is now the person a £3 billion national aerospace decision lands on, within days of taking office, with a company whose supply chain runs through Derby, Bristol and Lancashire rather than through any single devolved authority he used to run.

That is the uncomfortable symmetry in this story. A prime minister who built his career on the argument that regions need more fiscal muscle to compete is now the one being asked, at national level, for exactly the kind of committed public capital that critics say regional governments have never had enough of to hold onto employers like this on their own. Whether Number 10 finds £100–200 million for a first tranche will say something about how Burnham intends to run industrial policy differently from his predecessor — and whether "levelling up" rhetoric survives contact with a company that can simply take the jobs to Ohio or Bavaria instead.

No formal deadline has been set publicly, but Rolls-Royce has been pushing for a decision before it locks in siting choices for the demonstrator build, which is due to run through 2028. The next marker to watch is any statement from Kyle's department or from Number 10 on aerospace funding in the coming weeks — and, separately, Rolls-Royce's next trading update, which will show whether the market is pricing in a UK win or a UK loss.
