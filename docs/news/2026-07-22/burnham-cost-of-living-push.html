Burnham orders cost-of-living push as VAT cut confirmed and household strain deepens

Andy Burnham, who became UK prime minister this week and used his first cabinet meeting to order a cost-of-living push. — UK Parliament / House of Commons, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham's October VAT cut saves households £45 a year — about a fifth of the £221 bill rise from July's price cap increase.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for two days. His first act of substance, delivered to cabinet on Tuesday, was less a policy than an instruction: find something, anything, that takes money off people's bills.

"We need to be a cost-of-living Government, getting that cost of living down," Burnham told ministers at his first full cabinet meeting in Downing Street, according to those present. "What can we do just to take that little bit of pressure off people's shoulders, just to give them that little extra sense that help is coming."

The one concrete answer arrived alongside the directive: VAT on domestic electricity bills will fall from 5% to zero from October 1, for six months, through to the end of the financial year. The Treasury puts the saving at roughly £45 a year for a typical household, at a cost to the exchequer of £850 million — funded, in part, by scrapping the digital ID scheme inherited from Sir Keir Starmer, which had been costed at around £600 million a year over three years.

A government defined by its in-tray

Burnham is the seventh prime minister in a decade, and he inherits a cabinet room where the problems are familiar even if the faces have changed. Chancellor John Healey, moved from defence, sat alongside Cabinet Secretary Dame Antonia Romeo and Education Secretary Lucy Powell as Burnham framed the government's purpose almost entirely around household finances rather than growth, trade or foreign policy — a signal of where he judges the political danger to sit.

The reaction split along predictable lines. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds called the electricity cut "a statement of priorities." Darren Jones, a former Treasury minister now outside cabinet, was blunter, describing the measure as unfunded — a charge Burnham pre-empted in the same meeting, telling ministers he would "always show how we're going to pay" for whatever comes next. Rent controls, a fuel-bill support scheme and a cap on bus fares were all raised as options still under review, with no timeline attached.

The arithmetic doesn't flatter the announcement

The difficulty for Burnham is that the VAT cut lands after, not before, the bigger number. Ofgem's energy price cap rose 13% on July 1, pushing the typical dual-fuel direct debit bill to roughly £1,862 a year, up around £221 on the spring cap — driven by wholesale gas prices that spiked in March as the Middle East conflict widened and have stayed elevated since. Gas alone rose closer to 24% in that reset; electricity, the only fuel the VAT cut touches, rose by a comparatively modest 5%.

Set the two numbers side by side and the October relief covers about a fifth of what households lost to the July price cap rise, and none of the gas inflation that drove most of it. VAT at 5% was already a reduced rate; zeroing it out only ever recovers that last slice, which is why the saving is counted in tens of pounds rather than hundreds. The measure is real, and it will show up on bills from October — it just isn't sized to the problem that prompted it.

The scale behind the politics

That problem was quantified the same week by the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, whose cost-of-living tracker found 7.4 million low-income households — up from 7.1 million a year earlier — unable to afford at least one essential item, such as food, heating or transport, in the past six months. It is the highest figure since the foundation began the survey in 2021. The data, drawn from 4,121 households in the bottom 40% of the income distribution surveyed between May 7 and June 5, also found that 53% of struggling families were not on means-tested benefits at all — a detail that undercuts any assumption that the existing benefits system is reaching the households now falling behind.

What the measure signals for the economy

For markets, the more useful reading of Tuesday's announcement is not the £45 but the choice to intervene at all, and so early. A government moving on energy VAT within its first week is one that judges household energy costs to be a source of sustained inflationary and political pressure, not a transitory spike — consistent with UK headline inflation easing to 2.6% even as the JRF numbers worsen, a divergence that points to cost pressure concentrated in essentials rather than the basket as a whole. The VAT cut buys Burnham a headline before October; it does nothing about the wholesale gas prices that actually set the bill. That leaves the next Ofgem price cap review, due in the autumn, as the more consequential number — it will show whether the Middle East-driven gas premium is fading or embedding itself in UK household costs for the winter.
