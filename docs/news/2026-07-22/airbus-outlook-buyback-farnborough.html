Airbus lifts mid-term outlook, unveils buyback at Farnborough

An Airbus A350-1000, the type Airbus is flying at this year's Farnborough Airshow, pictured at the Paris Air Show. — New York-air / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Airbus shares jumped up to 3.4% after it targeted near-doubling profit by 2029 and launched a €5bn buyback, even as 2026 delivery guidance held steady.

Airbus shares climbed as much as 3.4% on Tuesday after the planemaker told investors it expects to nearly double core earnings by 2029 and unveiled a €5 billion share buyback — a pair of announcements timed to land in the same week as the Farnborough International Airshow, where Airbus and Boeing are chasing tens of billions of dollars in new aircraft orders a few miles from the briefing room.

The numbers, laid out by chief executive Guillaume Faury and his finance team at an investor day in London, mark the company's first formal mid-term guidance since the supply-chain crunch that has throttled jet production industry-wide for the past three years. Airbus is now targeting adjusted operating profit of €12-13 billion in 2029, up from €7.13 billion last year, with the commercial aircraft division — the unit hit hardest by parts shortages — expected to roughly double its own earnings to around €10 billion.

Visibility, not just volume

What moved the stock was less the headline profit number than the language around it. "We have a level of visibility we've never had before," Faury told investors. "We've put behind us a lot of challenges, and we've cleared the skies to move forward." It is a notably more confident register than the one Airbus has used since 2023, when engine-maker shortfalls — chiefly from Pratt & Whitney on the A320neo family — forced repeated delivery downgrades and left the company perpetually explaining why targets kept slipping.

That caution has not entirely lifted. Airbus left its 2026 guidance unchanged: adjusted earnings of €7.5 billion and a 10% rise in commercial deliveries to 870 aircraft, a target built on first-half handovers that were already up 15% year-on-year. Faury described the supply chain as "in a much better place" while acknowledging "issues here and there that will probably continue as we continue to go higher." Airbus is negotiating higher Pratt & Whitney volumes for 2027 and expects the A320neo engine shortage to be fully resolved only by 2028 — a reminder that the recovery being priced in this week is still, in large part, a forecast rather than a fact on the ground.

The buyback signal

The €5 billion buyback, to be executed over three years subject to continued board approval, is the more unambiguous part of Tuesday's message. Companies rarely commit capital to repurchasing stock unless management is reasonably confident that free cash flow will hold up under the mid-term plan — in Airbus's case, a cash-conversion ratio of roughly 1.0 sustained across the five-year outlook, built on a euro-dollar assumption of 1.22. Pairing a buyback with a profit upgrade, rather than announcing either alone, is the kind of capital-allocation signal that tends to draw generalist and income-focused investors who had stayed on the sidelines through the supply-chain years, on top of the aerospace specialists already in the stock.

The announcement landed deliberately close to Farnborough, which opened Monday and runs through Friday in what organisers are billing as the biggest edition in the show's 78-year history. Order activity has been brisk if short of the most bullish forecasts of 800-plus aircraft: day one alone produced 234 firm orders worth a combined $28.6 billion at list prices, and Boeing has for now out-paced Airbus on headline deal count. But airshow orders are lumpy and easily gamed for headlines — a strong showing at Farnborough says little about whether either manufacturer can actually build and deliver the jets it has sold. Tuesday's guidance and buyback, by contrast, are commitments to shareholders about production and cash generation over years, not a week of dealmaking.

A parallel trade fight

The upbeat mid-term framing arrives alongside a less comfortable subplot. Boeing has written to the US Trade Representative asking Washington to press the European Union over a €3 billion European Investment Bank loan to Airbus, disclosed just days after the two sides extended their truce in the long-running transatlantic aircraft-subsidy dispute. Boeing's letter noted the loan's timing coincided with Airbus executives discussing a potential new aircraft launch, and questioned both the size and intent of the assistance. Airbus has not detailed how the EIB financing feeds into its 2029 targets, but the episode is a reminder that even as the company tells investors the skies have cleared operationally, the regulatory and trade backdrop remains contested.

The next test of whether Tuesday's confidence is durable comes with Airbus's half-year results, expected in the coming weeks, which will show whether the 15% rise in first-half deliveries is translating into the cash generation the buyback assumes. Farnborough itself runs through Friday, with further order announcements likely before the show closes — though, as this week's guidance made clear, Airbus would rather be judged on 2029 than on this week's order book.
