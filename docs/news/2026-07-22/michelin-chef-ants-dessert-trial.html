Michelin-starred restaurateur faces jail over ant-garnished dessert

A red weaver ant (Oecophylla smaragdina) — a species prized as a delicacy garnish in parts of Southeast Asia, and at the centre of a Seoul food-safety prosecution. — Basile Morin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A two-Michelin-starred Seoul chef faces a year in prison after prosecutors said his restaurant served ant-garnished sorbet 12,000 times using an unapproved, and reportedly contaminated, ingredient.

The dish sounds like the kind of thing food-television producers dream up: a sikhye sorbet — the sweet, malty Korean rice punch, frozen and dressed with green tea syrup — finished with a scatter of live-picked ants for a citric, almost formic tang. At Evett, the two-Michelin-starred Seoul restaurant of Tasmanian chef Joseph Lidgerwood, it was served roughly 12,000 times over nearly four years. Now it has landed him in Seoul Western District Court, with prosecutors asking for a year in prison.

The charge is unglamorous: violating South Korea's Food Sanitation Act, which permits only ten insect species for human consumption. Ants are not one of them. Prosecutors say Lidgerwood and his restaurant company imported dried ants from the United States and Thailand from April 2021, generating about 120 million won (roughly $87,000) in sorbet sales and using an estimated 49,000 ants along the way. They're seeking a one-year prison term for Lidgerwood and a 20 million won fine ($13,500) for the company. A verdict is due September 2.

A forager's tale complicates the defence

What makes the case more than a bureaucratic footnote is a wrinkle that has nothing to do with insects being novel food. South Korean health officials testing the ants found heavy-metal levels reportedly up to 55 times higher than those found in species that are cleared for sale — the kind of detail that turns a permitting dispute into an actual public-health argument. It hasn't helped that Lidgerwood previously described hand-foraging his ants from the mountains around Seoul in press interviews, before switching, once regulators started asking questions, to insisting they were commercially imported and food-grade. Prosecutors and diners alike have noticed the inconsistency.

Lidgerwood's defence leans on the argument every avant-garde chef eventually reaches for: ants are eaten, without incident, in tasting menus across Denmark, Australia and the UK — Noma's Nordic Food Lab has spent over a decade researching insects as a serious ingredient, and Lidgerwood himself trained there. His lawyers also dispute the scale of the prosecution's numbers, arguing only around 60 percent of diners actually opted for the ant-topped course, since guests could ask for a substitute. None of that changes the underlying fact the defence has conceded: the ants were never cleared for sale under Korean law, whatever their pedigree elsewhere.

A test case for culinary daring

Lidgerwood is not some anonymous operator. He came up through the French Laundry and the Ledbury before settling in Seoul, won the Michelin Guide's Young Chef award in 2021, and appeared on Netflix's Culinary Class Wars — the kind of resume that makes prosecutors' choice to pursue an actual jail term, rather than a quiet fine, feel pointed. Regulatory regimes for food safety were not built with tasting menus in mind; they were built around mass catering and supply chains, where an unapproved ingredient is a shortcut, not a statement. When a chef with Michelin backing runs headlong into that framework anyway, the test isn't really about ants — it's about whether high-end kitchens get treated differently than a corner restaurant cutting corners.

The outcome due in September will answer that, at least for Korea. A custodial sentence for a two-star chef over a garnish would be a sharp signal to any restaurant treating novel ingredients as fair game ahead of the paperwork; a fine, on the other hand, would read as regulators drawing a line without trying to make an example of someone. Either way, kitchens experimenting with foraged or imported ingredients elsewhere in Asia will be watching Seoul Western District Court as closely as any diner watches the tasting menu.
