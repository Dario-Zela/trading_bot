Trump directs US carriers to resume direct flights to Lebanon

Joseph Aoun, elected President of Lebanon in January 2025, made the first White House visit by a Lebanese leader since 2009. — Republic of Lebanon / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Trump says he'll let US airlines fly direct to Lebanon for the first time since 1985, but no route, airline, or security clearance has been set.

President Trump said on Tuesday that he is directing the U.S. government to allow American airlines to fly directly to Lebanon for the first time since 1985, capping a White House meeting with Lebanese President Joseph Aoun that produced warm words but few operational details.

"After meeting with the President of Lebanon, Joseph Aoun, who has done a remarkable job working to transform his Country, I am hereby directing my Administration to allow all U.S. airline carriers to fly directly to Lebanon so that Americans can easily visit this beautiful land," Trump wrote on social media following the meeting. Aoun's visit was the first by a sitting Lebanese president to the White House since 2009.

A four-decade-old ban

The route has been closed since the Reagan administration grounded it in 1985, after Hezbollah-linked hijackers seized TWA Flight 847 and murdered U.S. Navy diver Robert Stethem. Since then, Americans traveling to Beirut have had to connect through European or Gulf hubs, and no U.S. carrier has scheduled service to Rafic Hariri International Airport.

Lifting that ban is not simply a matter of a presidential post. U.S. officials would first need to conduct a security review of Beirut's airport and certify it under FAA and TSA standards — a process that has no announced timeline and was not addressed in Trump's statement. No airline has committed to a route, and none was named.

The wider bargain

The flights announcement came hours after a substantive meeting in which Trump and Aoun discussed Israel's phased withdrawal from southern Lebanon and the disarmament of Hezbollah, following a framework agreement reached between Israel and Lebanon in late June. Trump said Washington has "concrete plans" to help the Lebanese army take over security responsibilities in the south as Israeli troops pull back from so-called pilot areas.

Hezbollah has rejected the disarmament framework outright, and Israel has said it will not complete its withdrawal until satisfied the group no longer poses a threat — an impasse that leaves the practical path to normalized U.S.-Lebanon air travel entangled with a much harder security negotiation.

Symbolism over substance, for now

For an airline industry still parsing tariff shocks and fuel costs, Tuesday's announcement changes nothing on any carrier's near-term schedule. It is, instead, a diplomatic signal: an endorsement of Aoun's government and a marker of goodwill toward a country the U.S. has spent years trying to pry out of Hezbollah's shadow. Whether it becomes more than that depends on decisions well outside Trump's social media feed — an FAA security certification, an airline's commercial calculus, and a ceasefire framework that Hezbollah has yet to accept.

The next concrete marker to watch is Israel's promised withdrawal from the pilot areas in southern Lebanon and whether the Lebanese army follows through on disarmament — the precondition Israel has set before it will consider the broader security picture, including air travel, genuinely settled.
