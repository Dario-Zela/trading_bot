Wildfires force mass evacuations across southern Europe as heatwave breaks records

Aftermath of the Los Gallardos wildfire near Bédar, Almería province, Spain — the deadliest fire in Andalusian history, July 2026. — Urci dream / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A record European heatwave has fuelled wildfires across France, Spain, Portugal and Greece, with France's burned area already double last year's pace.

The air over Andalusia has carried the smell of burnt pine for two weeks. In Los Gallardos, in Spain's Almería province, the wildfire that tore through the hills this month left 13 people dead — seven of them British holidaymakers — making it the deadliest fire in Andalusian history and the third-deadliest ever recorded in Spain. It is one blaze among dozens now burning across southern Europe, in a fire season that, by the numbers, is arriving faster and hitting harder than any in recent memory.

In France, the country's civil security service puts the year-to-date burned area at roughly 320 square kilometres — nearly double the pace recorded at the same point in 2025, according to Insurance Journal's reporting from the ground. That figure captures a summer in which fire has become almost background weather: a blaze near Trévillach, in the Pyrénées-Orientales, forced the evacuation of 10,000 people in a single weekend; another consumed some 900 hectares straddling the Aude and Hérault departments; and a third burned through 800 hectares of the Fontainebleau forest, close enough to Paris that smoke was visible from the capital's southern suburbs.

A heatwave that keeps setting records

The fires are a symptom, not the disease. Europe has now lived through three distinct heatwaves since late May — the first beginning May 24, the second and most severe from mid-June, and a third that reached the UK in early July. Western Europe recorded its hottest June on record, driven partly by the warmest sea-surface temperatures ever measured for the month, according to the World Meteorological Organization. The records have kept falling since: Barcelona's Fabra Observatory hit 40.5°C on July 8, Pissos in southwest France reached 44.3°C in June, and on July 18 the Sicilian town of Casteltermini recorded 48.4°C — among the highest temperatures ever documented in Europe.

One French wire report tracking the June heatwave alone counted more than 2,000 excess deaths in France during that spell, on top of roughly 300 during a hot stretch in late May. Soil that would normally hold some moisture into midsummer has instead dried out early, scientists note, stripping away the evaporative cooling that used to blunt these events — and leaving tinder-dry ground exactly where fires are now breaking out.

Four countries, one fire line

The damage is not confined to France. In Spain, wildfire authorities count 14 major fires that have together burned around 50,000 hectares this season, including the 15,400-hectare Orés blaze in Aragón — the largest in the country this year — and a Costa Brava fire that tore through more than 2,200 hectares in 48 hours, prompting evacuation or shelter-in-place orders for close to 50,000 people.

Portugal has lost roughly 30,000 hectares to thousands of separate ignitions, the largest a blaze near Vouzela in the north that consumed some 13,000 hectares before 1,200 firefighters, backed by nearly 400 vehicles and 15 aircraft, brought it 80% under control. In Greece, a fire that engulfed a recycling plant near Thessaloniki sent toxic smoke over the city, triggering evacuations and stay-indoors warnings even as authorities battled separate blazes elsewhere in the country. Spain and Italy have sent reinforcement crews to Portugal; France has called in aircraft from neighbouring states. The scale has outstripped any one country's firefighting capacity, which is itself becoming a recurring feature of the European summer.

The bill comes due

For markets, the fires intersect with two slower-moving stories: food commodities and insurance.

Spain and Portugal together produce close to half the world's olive oil, and last year's wildfire season already did the bulk of Europe's crop damage — two-thirds of it in those two countries alone, by industry estimates. Olive oil prices spent late 2025 and early 2026 tripling before easing back this spring, and Spanish output is still running as much as 30% below normal, according to market trackers, with this year's fires and a second consecutive drought now weighing on the 2026/27 harvest before it has even been picked. Wine regions in southern France and northern Spain face a related, quieter risk: smoke taint, which can ruin a vintage without the vines themselves ever burning — an exposure insurers in wildfire-prone wine country have only recently begun pricing properly.

Insurers, meanwhile, are confronting a structural problem that predates this fire season but is being sharpened by it. The EU's insurance regulator, EIOPA, estimates that 75% of Europe's economic losses from natural catastrophes have historically gone uninsured — a gap Brussels wants to close to around 10% through a proposed public-private reinsurance pool, though that would require up to €65 billion in public backstop capacity to cover extreme-loss years. France's own CatNat disaster fund has run a deficit since 2016 and has already forced the government to raise the mandatory surcharge on property insurance from 12% to 20%. Germany's insurance association has separately warned that premiums there could double within a decade on climate-driven claims. Wildfire, in other words, is no longer just a wildland problem; it is becoming a line item on household bills.

The immediate question is how much worse this season gets before the Mediterranean's autumn rains arrive — typically not before late September. Until then, France's civil security agency and the EU's Copernicus satellite service will keep publishing weekly burned-area updates, and each one will be measured against the same grim yardstick: whether 2026 closes out ahead of, or merely level with, the record-breaking fires of 2025.
