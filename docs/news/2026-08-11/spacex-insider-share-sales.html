SpaceX insiders cash in as private valuation climbs

A SpaceX Falcon 9 lifts off — the company priced the largest IPO in Wall Street history at $135 a share in June 2026. — Charles Boyer / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
SpaceX's first post-IPO lockup expired on August 6, freeing roughly $101bn in insider shares — and the stock rallied anyway.

For most of the past decade, cashing out of SpaceX meant waiting for one of Elon Musk's periodic tender offers — twice-yearly windows in which employees and early backers could sell shares back to the company at whatever price the board decided the business was worth that quarter. That price climbed steadily: $210 in mid-2024, $350bn as a company valuation by December 2024, $400bn by July 2025, reportedly north of $800bn in secondary talks by December. Then, on June 12 2026, the arrangement changed entirely. SpaceX priced the largest IPO in Wall Street history at $135 a share, opened at $150, and closed its first day at $161 — a valuation north of $2 trillion and, by most reckonings, the moment Musk became the world's first trillionaire.

What's happening now is the hangover from that party. On August 6, the first tranche of insider lockups expired, freeing roughly 911.5 million shares — about 20% of the company, worth some $101bn at prevailing prices — for employees and early investors to sell for the first time since the listing. It is, as one report put it, one of the largest single releases of stock into a public market in memory.

A staggered unwind, not a cliff

Unlike the standard single 180-day lockup most IPOs use, SpaceX built a nine-stage release schedule: 20% of eligible shares two trading days after the first quarterly report (the trigger for Thursday's unlock), another 7% at regular intervals through the autumn, 28% after the second-quarter report in November, and the remainder — including the more than six billion shares held by Musk himself — not free until June 2027, a full year after the debut.

The design was meant to avoid a single flood of selling, and largely it worked. Roughly 7 million shares changed hands on the first day of eligibility, around $750m worth, hardly a stampede given the size of the newly eligible pool. The stock, which had already slid from $152 in early July to around $107 in the run-up to the unlock — more than 50% off its post-debut high — actually rallied about 6% once the selling failed to materialise. Gil Luria at D.A. Davidson argued the pre-unlock decline had already priced in the anticipated selling; Ivan Feinseth of Tigress Financial Partners called the event "a largely technical event that accelerates the transition of ownership from insiders to a deeper, more stable public float."

Not everyone's first payday

Among those cashing in: Jessie Bates III, a safety for the Atlanta Falcons, who told CNBC he intends to sell his entire stake — bought for $150,000 in 2022, when SpaceX was valued at roughly $127bn, now worth well into seven figures. But one former employee's observation cuts against the idea that this is a windfall catching insiders by surprise: those long-tenured staff already had liquidity through the company's decade of semiannual buybacks, meaning, as they put it, they "didn't need an IPO to get paid." The real news in the unlock is less that insiders are selling — they always have been — than that the public market, rather than Musk's own tender pricing, is now the mechanism setting the price they sell at.

The next test comes quickly: a further 319 million shares become eligible on August 12, with the far larger 28% tranche following the November earnings report. How the stock absorbs those releases, against a backdrop where SpaceX still lost $4.9bn last year despite $18.7bn in revenue, will say more about whether $1.77 trillion was a fair opening price than Thursday's calm did.
