UK pushes allies on Ukraine air defence as deep strike kills 13 in Russia

Trypilska thermal power plant, Kyiv Oblast, after a Russian missile strike destroyed it in April 2024 — a preview of the grid damage both sides now risk repeating. — State Emergency Service of Ukraine / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Britain is pressing allies to speed up air-defence deliveries as Russia plans a fresh Kyiv grid assault and a Ukrainian strike killed 13 deep in Russia.

Britain's defence secretary returned from Kyiv this week with a blunt message for Europe's capitals: the interceptors are running out, and Moscow knows it. Wes Streeting's first visit to Ukraine since taking the post coincided with one of the war's grimmer nights — a Russian missile barrage on August 5 that killed 17 people and wounded more than 44 in the Kyiv region — and it ended with him warning that Vladimir Putin intends to make the coming winter itself a weapon.

"Winter in Ukraine is no longer a season. It is a weapon," Streeting wrote in the Sunday Times. "I return from Kyiv with a clear understanding of what Ukraine needs to weather this winter."

The warning was underscored, almost immediately, from the other direction. On Monday a Ukrainian drone strike tore through an oil refining hub deep inside Russia, killing 13 people in one of the deadliest single attacks either side has carried out since the invasion began. The two events are not the same story, but they are the same war — a mutual, accelerating campaign against the energy infrastructure that keeps both economies running, playing out just as Europe starts totting up what a third wartime winter might cost it.

A weapon called winter

Ukraine's difficulty is straightforward and increasingly acute: it is running short of the missiles that stop other missiles. The Patriot system is the only weapon in Ukraine's arsenal capable of shooting down Russian ballistic missiles, and interceptor deliveries have fallen by roughly two-thirds this year, with US stocks diverted toward the Middle East after the conflict with Iran. President Volodymyr Zelensky has said Ukraine is now receiving nearly three times fewer anti-ballistic interceptors than it was at the start of 2026 — a shortfall Russia appears to be exploiting deliberately. Ukrainian officials, citing US intelligence, say Moscow is preparing a major strike campaign against Kyiv's energy infrastructure timed for the run-up to Ukraine's Independence Day on August 24, calculated to hit while air defences are thinnest and before Ukraine can finish repairing what was destroyed last winter.

That repair job is enormous. Ukraine's energy minister, Denys Shmyhal, has said Russian strikes over the 2025-2026 winter damaged or destroyed roughly 80 percent of the country's energy system; national generating capacity fell from around 17.5 gigawatts to about 11.5 gigawatts by February. Millions of Ukrainians spent January without reliable heat or power as temperatures dropped to minus 20°C. Streeting's pitch — delivered alongside his German counterpart Boris Pistorius, with whom he co-chairs the roughly 50-nation Ukraine Defence Contact Group — is to convert allied sympathy into hardware before that damage repeats itself. "Where the UK can provide that resource, we will," he wrote. "And where we cannot, it is my job to mobilise allies to find that capability." The group's next session, expected in September, is likely to make interceptor supply the central item on the agenda — a deadline that sits awkwardly close to the strike window Ukrainian intelligence is already warning about.

Deepest strike of the war

The other half of this picture sits nearly 1,200 kilometres to the east. Ukraine's drone strike on Nizhnekamsk, in Russia's Tatarstan region, hit the Taneco refinery complex — one of Russia's most advanced post-Soviet refining facilities — and set it ablaze. Local authorities said 13 people were killed and 78 wounded; nine of the deaths came when a single drone struck a workers' hostel, killing citizens of Uzbekistan and Kyrgyzstan among others, and a child. At roughly 750 miles from the Ukrainian border, it is one of the furthest strikes Kyiv has managed to land inside Russia, and among the deadliest of the war's four years.

Zelensky did not disguise the intent behind it. "We do deliver entirely justified responses, and every Russian strike will be met with our response," he said. Ukraine has been striking Russian refineries at a near-daily tempo in recent months, aiming to squeeze the fuel supplies and export revenue that finance Moscow's war effort — the mirror image of Russia's own campaign against Ukraine's grid. Both governments are, in effect, trying to freeze or starve the other into exhaustion before ground operations, which have barely moved in months, force any resolution of their own.

A race with a market at the end of it

The strategic logic runs in both directions, but the economic exposure does not. Ukraine losing large stretches of grid capacity forces it to lean harder on emergency imports and diesel generation, a cost the state and its allies absorb directly. A dented Russian refining sector chips away at Kremlin export revenue but also feeds into the same tight global product-fuel picture already unsettling European energy traders this year. Either direction, the practical effect converges on the same place: a market already nervous about supply heading into the northern winter, watching two combatants attack each other's energy infrastructure faster than diplomacy or logistics can repair it.

What happens next depends less on rhetoric than on delivery schedules. If Russia's anticipated strike campaign on Kyiv lands before Ukraine's repair crews and Western interceptors catch up, the coming winter could reproduce — or worsen — the blackouts of the last one, with knock-on effects for regional energy prices already primed by tight supply elsewhere. The September meeting of the Ukraine Defence Contact Group, and whether Russia's assault begins before or after Ukraine's August 24 Independence Day, are the two dates worth watching to see which side wins that race.
