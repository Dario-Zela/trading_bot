Treasury 'materially contributed' to death of personal assistant, coroner finds

HM Treasury's building at 1 Horse Guards Road, Whitehall, where Chloe Moffat worked as a personal assistant. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A coroner found HM Treasury's mishandling of a disciplinary complaint materially contributed to the suicide of a 26-year-old personal assistant.

A coroner has ruled that HM Treasury "materially contributed" to the death of a 26-year-old personal assistant who took her own life the day after being confronted with allegations that she had leaked confidential information about colleagues — a finding that lands government departments squarely inside the same duty-of-care scrutiny usually reserved for corporate employers.

Chloe Moffat worked as personal assistant to a Treasury director in London and, by all accounts, had an unblemished record and a job she enjoyed. On 19 May last year she was called into a meeting with her line manager and a more senior manager, who told her that colleagues had accused her of sharing sensitive information about them. She was visibly upset. When she asked whether she would lose her job, she was told managers "could not predict the outcome at this stage." The next morning, 20 May, she died.

What the coroner found

Assistant Coroner Anna Crawford, sitting at Surrey Coroner's Court, recorded a conclusion of suicide and found that the Treasury's handling of the complaint breached both its own internal HR policy and Acas guidance on disciplinary procedure. The department's written policy made clear that, even if the allegations against Moffat were proven, the likely sanction was a written warning — not dismissal. Nobody told her that. Left to sit with the meeting's ambiguity overnight, she was, in the coroner's assessment, "frightened, isolated and convinced that her career" was over, in the words her family later used to describe her state of mind.

Crawford went further than the individual case. She found the fact-finding meeting that preceded Moffat's death followed a format that is standard practice not just at the Treasury but across other Whitehall departments — and called that a "matter of concern" in its own right, since it means the same gap between what policy promises and what staff are actually told could recur elsewhere in the civil service. She will issue a Prevention of Future Deaths report, a formal mechanism coroners use to compel a response, to the Treasury, the head of the civil service, and Acas.

The response

Moffat's family said that at the moment she most needed "support, reassurance and fair treatment," she instead was left to fear the worst about a process nobody had properly explained to her. The Treasury said it was "considering the findings carefully" and pointed to changes already made to its guidance and staff wellbeing arrangements since her death.

The ruling carries no market consequence and no ministerial resignation is on the table. Its significance is narrower but still pointed: a coroner has put in writing that a government department's own procedural sloppiness — not merely the stress inherent in any disciplinary process — contributed to a young employee's death, and has flagged that the same sloppiness may be systemic across Whitehall. What happens next depends on how the Treasury, the Cabinet Office and Acas respond to the Prevention of Future Deaths report, which by law requires a formal reply within 56 days.
