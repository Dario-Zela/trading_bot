Colombia earthquake death toll passes 130 in country's worst tremor this century

Hospital patients are evacuated in Cali, Colombia, after the magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck on August 10, 2026. — Santiago Saldarriaga/AP Photo
In one sentence
A magnitude 7.4 earthquake killed at least 132 people in western Colombia, forcing a president three days into office to declare a national disaster with little catastrophe insurance in place.

The earthquake struck at 7:34 a.m. on a Monday, while western Colombia was still waking up. A magnitude 7.4 rupture some 107 kilometres beneath San José del Palmar, a Chocó department town of fewer than 5,000 people, sent shaking as far as Bogotá, 240 kilometres to the east, and across the border into Ecuador. By nightfall the country's president was calling it a national disaster, and by Tuesday morning the confirmed death toll stood at more than 132, with hundreds still unaccounted for beneath collapsed buildings in Cali, Pereira and Manizales.

Colombia's Geological Service described it as the strongest earthquake to originate in the country in at least a decade — possibly the worst of the current century, surpassing anything since the 1999 Quindío quake that killed nearly 1,900 people in the country's coffee belt. That comparison matters less for the death toll, which so far remains a fraction of 1999's, than for what it signals about the scale of physical destruction: this is the kind of event that reshapes a national budget.

A president three days into the job

Abelardo de la Espriella was inaugurated as Colombia's 36th president on Friday, August 7. On Monday, he was standing in the crisis room of the national disaster agency, UNGRD, declaring a state of national emergency and announcing he would relocate to Chocó to personally oversee the response. "I have assumed direct leadership of the response to the emergency in San José del Palmar," he wrote on X, ordering the agency's director to detail exactly what resources the affected departments would need.

The damage sprawls across five departments. In Cali, home to Colombia's third-largest metropolitan area, the pediatric and neonatal wards of a hospital collapsed and rescue teams pulled a woman from the rubble of a flattened building live on camera; authorities there reported dozens dead and thousands of structures damaged. In Pereira, roughly 60 people died and Matecaña International Airport's terminal partially caved in. In Manizales, the tower of the city's cathedral basilica fell into the nave. Seven or more regional airports suspended operations entirely, complicating the movement of aid and rescue crews into a mountainous interior that was never easy to reach on a good day.

Who pays for it

This is where the story moves from human tragedy to fiscal arithmetic, and Colombia's arithmetic is unusually exposed. The country carries an insurance penetration rate of roughly 3.2% of GDP — thin even by Latin American standards — and an estimated 86% of its population lives in zones of recognised seismic hazard. Globally, insurers have covered only about a fifth of the roughly $535 billion in earthquake losses recorded worldwide over the past decade; in a market like Colombia's, that ratio is almost certainly worse. Loss adjusters were already warning this week that distinguishing cosmetic cracking from structural failure — the difference between a paid claim and a denied one — will take weeks in areas rescue teams have not yet reached.

Colombia has tried to hedge this kind of risk before. In 2018 the World Bank arranged a $1.36 billion multi-country catastrophe bond covering Chile, Colombia, Mexico and Peru against earthquake losses, with Colombia's slice worth $400 million and payouts triggered automatically by U.S. Geological Survey shake data rather than slow loss assessments. That instrument, however, was written with a three-year term and lapsed around 2021; there is no public record of Colombia renewing a sovereign cat bond since. That leaves reconstruction financing to fall back on the older, blunter tools: the national budget, multilateral loans, and bilateral aid. Washington has pledged $15.5 million in relief so far — a figure dwarfed by the roughly $1.86 billion in damage the 1999 Quindío earthquake caused, equivalent then to about 2.2% of GDP, of which international assistance covered barely $40 million.

Markets registered the shock without panicking. Colombia's benchmark COLCAP equity index slipped roughly half a percent and dollar-denominated sovereign bonds softened, but the peso actually firmed by about 1% against the dollar, a move traders attributed more to regional currency flows than to any earthquake-specific repricing. That muted reaction is itself informative: investors are not yet pricing a fiscal crisis, but insurers and reinsurers with Andean exposure will spend the coming weeks quietly repricing catastrophe layers across the region, a process that tends to show up in renewal pricing months before it shows up in headlines.

What happens next

De la Espriella inherits a rebuilding bill before he has named a full cabinet, and how he finances it — austerity-minded budget reallocation, fresh multilateral borrowing, or a belated return to catastrophe-bond markets — will be an early test of the technocratic instincts he campaigned on. Rescue operations in Chocó's harder-to-reach terrain are expected to continue for days, meaning the death toll is very likely to keep climbing before it stabilises. The more durable story is structural: a country where the overwhelming majority of the population sits in a hazard zone, but where formal catastrophe risk transfer has quietly lapsed, is now relying on ad hoc aid and treasury reserves to rebuild hospitals, an airport terminal and a cathedral. The next marker to watch is UNGRD's first formal damage assessment, expected within the week, which will start to put a dollar figure on a bill Colombia is still working out how to pay.
