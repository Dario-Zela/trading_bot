Meta ordered to pay $567m and overhaul product design in child-safety verdict

In one sentence
A New Mexico judge ordered Meta to pay $567m and ruled its platforms a 'public nuisance,' forcing product changes like hiding Like counts and capping teen usage.

The fine is the smaller number. Meta has absorbed nine-figure penalties before and kept its engagement metrics climbing regardless. What makes the New Mexico ruling handed down this week different is the other half of the order: a state judge telling the world's largest social media company, in binding legal terms, how to redesign its products for anyone under 18.

On August 6, State District Judge Bryan Biedscheid ordered Meta to pay $567 million into a fund for treating and preventing youth mental health harms in New Mexico, on top of the $375 million a jury had already assessed against the company in March. The combined $942 million is, by most tallies, the largest child-safety judgment a court has levied against a social media company. But the number that will worry Meta's product and growth teams more is zero — as in, the number of public Like counts Instagram and Facebook will now be allowed to show anyone under 18 in New Mexico without a guardian's sign-off.

From damages to design specs

Courts suing tech companies over child safety have mostly stuck to writing checks. New Mexico's order does that too — $420 million of the fund goes to treatment services, $90 million to screening and assessment, $33 million to prevention campaigns, $15 million to referral and care coordination, and $9 million to oversight, spread over five years. But Judge Biedscheid went further, in a bench-trial phase that ran three weeks with no jury, examining whether Meta's platforms amounted to a “public nuisance” under state law. He ruled that they did.

The operational fallout is specific. Meta must set Instagram accounts for under-18 users to private by default and, on Facebook, limit a minor's friend list to other minors unless a parent changes the setting. It must mute push notifications to underage accounts between 10pm and 7am. It must cap minors' monthly usage at roughly 90 hours — about three hours a day, on average, before the app has to start pushing them elsewhere. And it must hide public Like counts and other engagement metrics from anyone under 18 unless a parent explicitly opts back in.

Each of those levers touches something Meta's business depends on: time on app, the dopamine loop of visible engagement, the network effects of an open friend graph. A fine, however large, is a line item. A mandated redesign of the Like button is a constraint on how the product is allowed to work.

How New Mexico got here

The case dates to 2023, when Attorney General Raúl Torrez's office ran an undercover investigation, setting up decoy accounts posing as 13- and 14-year-olds. According to the state's complaint, Meta's ad-targeting systems correctly identified the fabricated user as a young teenager — and served age-appropriate advertising — while the platform's safety systems failed to flag or restrict the same account from contact by adult strangers. The lawsuit accused Meta and Mark Zuckerberg personally of running what it called a “breeding ground” for predators and of misrepresenting how safe its products were for children.

A jury sided with the state in March, finding Meta had violated New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act and awarding $375 million. The nuisance phase that followed, decided by the judge alone, produced this week's order. “Meta is paying for that choice,” Torrez said of the company's product decisions. “This judgment holds the company accountable for the damage it caused to our children, our families, and our schools.” Meta's communications chief said the company disagrees with the ruling and will appeal, while a spokesperson maintained the platforms have been transparent about the difficulty of removing bad actors.

Why this ruling travels

New Mexico is not litigating alone. A coalition of roughly 33 state attorneys general has a parallel case consolidated before a federal judge in Oakland, California, with more than 1,700 individual suits folded into the same proceeding, and states including Tennessee are pursuing their own claims. On August 10 — four days after the New Mexico order — a federal appeals court cleared the way for that broader wave of social-media addiction lawsuits to proceed, rejecting Meta's bid to have them thrown out on free-speech grounds.

Taken together, the two rulings arrive within a week of each other and point the same direction: courts are increasingly willing to treat platform design choices, not just content moderation failures, as the legal harm. If the New Mexico order survives appeal, it becomes a template other states can cite — not just for how much to fine Meta, but for what to make it change. For a company whose stock price tracks engagement and ad-targeting precision as closely as revenue, a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction patchwork of mandated product features is a harder problem to manage than any single check.

Meta's appeal will likely take months to resolve, and until it does, the New Mexico-specific product changes are not yet in force. The next marker to watch is the Oakland multidistrict litigation, where the same underlying question — whether addictive design constitutes a legal harm rather than a business choice — will be tested against a much larger set of states and, this time, a jury.
