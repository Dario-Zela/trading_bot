Thames Water's £1m signing bonus for finance chief sparks nationalisation fears

Thames Water's headquarters; the utility carries more than £20bn in debt and is barred from paying performance bonuses over environmental failures. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Thames Water paid finance chief Steve Buck a £1m signing bonus from emergency lender funds as the utility teeters toward nationalisation, prompting government fury.

Thames Water is Britain's largest water utility, serving 16 million customers across London and the Thames Valley, and it is, by most measures available to a market observer, close to broke. It carries more than £20bn in debt, has warned it could run out of cash before the year is out, and remains one of at least eight water companies barred from paying performance-related bonuses because of chronic environmental failures — sewage spills, leakage, missed targets. Against that backdrop, the company has just paid its finance chief a £1m signing-on fee, funded from the emergency lending facility that creditors extended to keep it afloat.

The payment to chief financial officer Steve Buck was disclosed not by Thames Water proactively but in a letter from its chairman, Sir Adrian Montague, to the House of Commons' Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (EFRA) Select Committee last week. Buck joined the company in April 2025. The money landed in his account at the end of July 2026 — fifteen months after he started, and drawn directly from the super-senior emergency facility that lenders arranged specifically to keep Thames Water solvent while it negotiates its future with the government.

A bonus by another name

Montague's defence, laid out in the letter to EFRA chair Alistair Carmichael, was that the payment was "a necessary incentive" to secure Buck when he was hired — a sum agreed at the point of appointment but deferred while a wider retention scheme for other executives was put on hold amid the political backlash over bonuses. Buck's substantive pay is separately disclosed: a £500,000 salary, a pension allowance worth 12% of that, and a £12,500 car allowance. The £1m is additional, and — company officials say — followed legal advice on Thames Water's contractual obligations to him.

The company has previous form here. Chief executive Chris Weston's total pay has climbed to £1.2m, and Thames Water has paid out more than £4m in bonuses and retention payments across its senior team even as it sought government-backed rescue financing, prompting a rebuke from the then environment secretary, Emma Reynolds, who called the payouts "outrageous." The EFRA Committee has spent months trying to establish, in Carmichael's words, "whether these payments fall within the remit of the Government's ban and will be recouped" — a question complicated by Thames Water's habit of calling these sums retention payments rather than bonuses, a distinction that keeps them technically outside the ban Ofwat imposed.

Whose money is it anyway

The detail that has generated most anger in Whitehall is the funding source. This was not paid from operating cash or shareholder equity — there is effectively none of the latter left, after Thames Water's original owners walked away rather than inject fresh capital. It came from the emergency super-senior facility, money lenders advanced on the understanding it would keep the taps running and the company stable while a rescue is negotiated, not fund executive compensation. By 30 September last year, the company had already drawn £1.426bn of the initial £1.5bn facility, and liquidity had fallen to roughly £0.9bn.

A spokesperson for Prime Minister Andy Burnham was blunt: "It's unacceptable that one of the worst-performing water companies is handing out huge payments to its executives when it should be focusing on improving performance and rebuilding public trust." Reynolds went further: "It flies in the face of basic fairness, and the British public are right to be furious." For a government trying to hold the line on water-sector reform while keeping Thames Water out of formal state control, the optics are difficult to overstate — taxpayers and billpayers are the backstop for a firm that just found seven figures for one executive's signing fee.

The nationalisation calculus

Thames Water has avoided the special administration regime — a form of temporary nationalisation last used on failed energy supplier Bulb in 2021 — largely because the government prefers a negotiated private rescue to the fiscal and legal complexity of taking on the company directly. The leading proposal, from the London & Valley Water consortium of creditors including Elliott Management and Apollo Global Management, envisions writing off roughly £4bn of senior debt and around £1bn held by class B bondholders, in exchange for an equity injection of £3-4bn and looser regulatory terms from Ofwat. That deal has already been revised four times amid objections over customer costs and investment delays, and Ofwat has said it shares the government's concerns about its current shape.

Every disclosure like the Buck payment makes that negotiation harder politically. It hands ammunition to MPs and campaigners who argue a company this reliant on emergency support and this willing to reward executives should simply be taken into special administration, where compensation would fall under tighter statutory constraints. It also raises a narrower but sharper question for creditors and regulators alike: if the emergency facility can be tapped for pay awards once, what stops it happening again before a rescue deal closes.

What comes next

The EFRA Committee has already recalled Thames Water executives once this year to answer for retention payments and is expected to press Montague and Buck further on the signing-on fee specifically — likely the next public flashpoint. Separately, the London & Valley Water consortium's revised rescue package needs sign-off from Ofwat and the government before the company's cash position, already tight, deteriorates further. Whichever comes first — a negotiated private rescue or a slide into special administration — the £1m payment has become a shorthand for a broader complaint: that the people managing Thames Water's crisis have shown more fluency in protecting their own terms than in protecting the infrastructure, or the public money, underwriting them.
