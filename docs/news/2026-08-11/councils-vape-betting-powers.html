Councils win new powers to block vape and betting shop clusters

A William Hill betting shop on a UK high street — the kind of premises councils will gain new powers to restrict under the planning overhaul. — Betty Longbottom / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Councils will gain power to block new vape and betting shops and cap their concentration on high streets, layering fresh headwinds onto an already-shrinking sector.

Councils in England are about to get a lever they have wanted for the better part of a decade: the power to say no. Under plans unveiled by Prime Minister Andy Burnham on August 10th, local authorities will be able to block new vape and betting shops from opening — and, crucially, stop them clustering on the same stretch of high street — through a rewrite of planning and gambling law rather than a new statute of its own.

The mechanics matter more than the headline. Vape retailers currently sit in the same planning use class as ordinary convenience stores, meaning a shop can open without so much as a nod from the local authority. The reform carves out a distinct planning class for vape shops, requiring permission to trade, and tightens the legal definition of what counts as one — closing a loophole that let some outlets register as general retailers to dodge scrutiny. Adult gaming centres, the 24-hour slot-machine venues that have proliferated on discount high streets, get the same treatment.

For betting shops, the government is going after the underlying statute. The "aim to permit" duty in the Gambling Act 2005, which has obliged councils to approve licence applications that meet basic criteria regardless of local opposition, is to be scrapped. In its place, councils gain the right to run cumulative impact assessments — weighing how many gambling premises already sit in an area before approving another — giving them, for the first time, a density lever rather than a case-by-case one. Closure orders for premises linked to organised crime, meanwhile, double from six months to twelve.

"For many people, the high streets they grew up with have been hollowed out. The rise of vape shops, betting shops and rogue operators have replaced the shops, services, and community spaces," Burnham said. Housing Secretary Angela Rayner added that councils are being given "the power to say no."

The changes are aimed at a real trend: since 2016 Britain has lost nearly 1,800 pubs and bars while vape and tobacco shops have grown to almost 2,200 locations, according to Centre for Social Justice figures cited alongside the announcement. Both sets of changes are subject to consultation and are expected to take effect from next year, folded into a wider £150m High Streets Strategy due later in 2026.

A sector already in retreat

The planning reform lands on a gambling industry that is already restructuring around a heavier tax burden, which makes the timing awkward for operators rather than existential. Online gaming duty rises to 40% from April 2026 and a new 25% remote betting duty follows in April 2027, and the high-street estate is already shrinking in response: Betfred is closing 132 shops, more than a tenth of its total, with roughly 600 job losses, while Entain has cut 500 staff. Flutter Entertainment delisted from the London Stock Exchange on August 3rd to concentrate its listing on the NYSE, citing a persistent UK valuation discount, and Evoke's roughly £243m sale to Bally's/Intralot will leave just Entain, Rank Group and Playtech as the significant London-listed names in the sector. Ministers have also floated higher business rates on vape and gambling premises from next year, to help fund a 20% business rate cut for pubs and live-music venues from April 2027 — redirecting the tax burden rather than lightening it.

Vape retailers, for their part, are not uniformly opposed. Gillian Golden of the Independent British Vape Trade Association welcomed the closure-order extension as a tool against "rogue traders on the high street" undercutting the legitimate sector, even as her members absorb a new planning hurdle.

The practical effect for national operators is patchwork risk: an expansion plan that clears licensing in one borough may stall in the next once cumulative impact assessments come into force, complicating site-selection models built on uniform national rules. The consultation period, and the detail of the High Streets Strategy due later this year, will determine how much discretion individual councils actually end up with.
