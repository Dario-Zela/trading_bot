Burnham orders justice secretary to keep PC Harper's killers in jail

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has ordered his justice secretary to find a way to keep PC Harper's killers in custody. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Andy Burnham has told his justice secretary to find a way to stop PC Andrew Harper's killers being freed early, after his widow called their looming release a "betrayal."

Andy Burnham has ordered his justice secretary to draw up plans that would keep two of PC Andrew Harper's killers behind bars, after a week of mounting anger over their scheduled release from prison this autumn.

Jessie Cole and Albert Bowers were sentenced to 13 years each for the manslaughter of the Thames Valley Police officer, who died in August 2019 after being dragged along a country lane in Berkshire by a getaway car driven by Henry Long. Long, who pleaded guilty to manslaughter, was jailed for 16 years. Under standard release rules the pair become eligible for parole after serving half their term — putting their release as early as October.

A carve-out that didn't cover manslaughter

The row stems from the government's wider push to ease prison overcrowding by releasing some offenders earlier. Burnham had already agreed to exempt rapists and those convicted of serious child sexual offences and grooming from the scheme. Manslaughter was not on that list, which meant Cole and Bowers — despite killing a police officer in the course of his duty — remained on track for early release.

Justice Secretary Alex Norris confirmed the gap last week, telling reporters the government had wanted to go further. "We couldn't draw the exemptions wide enough to include manslaughter as well," he said. "I am deeply sorry about that, but we've made the decisions we've made to make sure that we have a justice system that stands up in the future." Norris argued that narrowing the early-release scheme further would not, on its own, resolve the capacity crisis driving it.

Harper's widow leads the backlash

The response from Lissie Harper, Andrew Harper's widow, was immediate and unsparing. She called the situation "an utter betrayal" and said she was "beyond appalled and bristling with anger" at the prospect of her husband's killers walking free, adding that neither man had shown remorse. Harper has campaigned since her husband's death for tougher sentencing of those who kill emergency workers — a campaign that led to the cross-party Harper's Law, which raised minimum terms for such killings. She argues the early-release scheme now undercuts that legislation in substance, even if it leaves it untouched on paper.

The case has become a flashpoint for the broader early-release policy, which was already contentious before it reaches implementation, delayed from September to October in part to give probation services more time and to let victims register their views on release conditions.

What Burnham is asking for

Downing Street said Burnham was "increasingly confident" a solution could be found and has instructed Norris to explore ways to keep "a greater number of the most serious offenders" in custody without unpicking the release scheme altogether — likely through a narrower, case-specific mechanism rather than a blanket manslaughter exemption, which the Ministry of Justice has already said would be difficult to draft without capturing a far wider group of offenders than intended.

That balancing act — freeing enough prisoners to relieve overcrowding while shielding cases that provoke public outrage — is the same one that produced the original exemptions, and critics on both sides say a piecemeal fix risks looking arbitrary. Opposition MPs have seized on the episode as evidence the early-release scheme was rushed; Burnham's allies counter that the prison estate leaves little room for manoeuvre.

Norris is expected to report back to Downing Street within weeks, ahead of the scheme's October start date — the deadline against which any change to Cole and Bowers' status will have to be finalised.
