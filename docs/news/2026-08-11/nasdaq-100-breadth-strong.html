Nasdaq 100 breadth hits strongest level in over a year

In one sentence
More than 70% of Nasdaq 100 stocks now sit above their 200-day moving average, the best breadth reading in over a year, as the rally broadens beyond the Magnificent Seven.

More than 70% of Nasdaq 100 constituents are now trading above their 200-day moving average, according to data highlighted by Barchart on August 8 — the strongest reading on that breadth measure in over a year. For an index that spent much of 2025 and early 2026 being dragged around by a handful of trillion-dollar names, that is a meaningful shift in composition, if not yet in headline.

The 200-day average is a blunt but widely watched gauge: it smooths out short-term noise and shows whether a stock is in a durable uptrend or still digging out of one. When seven in ten members of a 100-stock index clear that bar simultaneously, it means the rally has stopped being a story about Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Tesla, and started being a story about the other ninety-plus names too.

From concentration to rotation

The backdrop makes the breadth reading easier to parse. The Magnificent Seven cohort broke below its own 200-day moving average in mid-February, and by March the group was collectively underperforming the broader market for the first time since the pandemic recovery. An equal-weight basket of the Magnificent Seven has fallen more than 8% since its June peak, while the equal-weighted S&P 500 is up over 13% for the year against roughly 8.5% for the standard, cap-weighted index — a gap that only opens up when money is moving into the names that were previously left behind.

Strategists have been describing this as a rotation rather than a retreat. "There's a rotation that has been going on out of the momentum-based trades like technology, and has shifted, especially since the end of the quarter, to other areas of the market that are perceived as less expensive or having underperformed," Robert Pavlik, senior portfolio manager at Dakota Wealth Management, said in July. Jack Janasiewicz, lead portfolio strategist at Natixis Investment Managers Solutions, put it more simply: "We're just seeing money move from one area of the market to another. That's what a bull market is based on."

What the number does and doesn't tell you

Breadth strengthening after a period of concentration is generally read as constructive — it implies more of the index's advance is being carried by earnings and fundamentals across a wider set of companies, rather than by a few names absorbing the bulk of investor flows. The Nasdaq-100 Equal Weighted Index has outperformed its cap-weighted counterpart so far this year, evidence that participation has genuinely broadened rather than merely rotated within the mega-caps themselves.

But a single breadth reading is a snapshot, not a forecast. It says which stocks are in uptrends today; it says nothing on its own about earnings quality, valuation, or how a name got there. King Lip, chief strategist at BakerAvenue Wealth Management, framed the caveat well: "Leadership is broadening beneath the surface, even though that strength is not fully reflected in the headline index." The Nasdaq 100's own level — it touched a record above 30,660 in early August after a 9.3% four-day surge tied to AI-driven earnings — is still very much a mega-cap story even as the breadth beneath it improves.

The read-through for investors is less "buy everything" than "the rally has more legs holding it up than it did a year ago." Whether that holds depends on whether upcoming earnings from the names outside the Magnificent Seven keep confirming it, or whether the next wobble in AI-linked mega-caps drags the rest of the index down with it, the way it typically has.

The next test comes with the broader run of second-quarter results still trickling in and the next Federal Reserve rate decision on the calendar — both of which will show whether the breadth improvement reflects durable earnings strength across the index or simply calmer waters at the top.
