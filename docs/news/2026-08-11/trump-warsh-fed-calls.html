Trump discloses periodic calls with Fed Chair Warsh, denies rate pressure

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair at the White House on May 22, 2026. — Federal Reserve / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump confirmed periodic calls with Fed Chair Kevin Warsh since his May swearing-in but denied pressuring him on rates, deepening doubts about Fed independence.

President Donald Trump acknowledged on Monday that he has spoken periodically by phone with Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh since Warsh was sworn in as the central bank's 17th chairman in May, while insisting he has not leaned on him to move faster on interest rates.

"They made it sound like I live and breathe — you know, I speak to Kevin all the time, every time," Trump told reporters. "I spoke to him one time, briefly, a few days ago." The remark was a rebuttal to a Wall Street Journal report describing more frequent contact between the two men, and it followed a Bloomberg dispatch on Thursday, sourced to people familiar with the calls, that described them as a recurring feature of Warsh's tenure.

The two accounts do not sit easily together. Kevin Hassett, director of the National Economic Council, told Bloomberg Television four days before Trump's denial that the president and the Fed chair "have a very close, long-term relationship from New York City, from Florida, and they talk about the economy all the time." Hassett said he does not sit in on the calls but is "sure" Trump does not pressure Warsh on rates.

An unusual arrangement

Fed chairs have historically kept the White House at arm's length precisely to avoid the appearance that monetary policy bends to political convenience. Trump nominated Warsh, a former Fed governor and Morgan Stanley banker, in March; the Senate confirmed him 54-45 on May 13 — the narrowest confirmation vote for a Fed chair on record — and he took the oath at the White House on May 22, the first Fed chair sworn in there since Alan Greenspan in 1987. At that ceremony, Trump publicly told Warsh to "be totally independent... don't look at me, don't look at anybody, just do your own thing and do a great job."

The calls, whatever their frequency, complicate that instruction. Warsh, testifying before the Senate Banking Committee in July, sidestepped questions about the substance of his conversations with the president: "I just don't want to be in the business of sharing discussions that the president and I have," he said, adding that he considers himself "an independent guy" and that receiving a call from Trump does not make him uncomfortable. He has said separately that he is happy to hear from the president or anyone else on rates, but will decide policy on his own.

Pressure has ebbed, not vanished

Trump spent much of the spring publicly pushing for rapid rate cuts, but the campaign cooled after inflation climbed above 4% in May — driven partly by an oil-price spike tied to the Iran war — giving Warsh what several administration officials described as a political grace period as the Fed held rates steady. Warsh himself has signaled he would lean toward raising rates, not cutting them, if price pressures stiffen further, a position at odds with the president's stated preference.

The White House has tried to have it both ways on the independence question. Spokesman Kush Desai said Trump "has repeatedly stressed that he is giving Chairman Warsh the space he needs," while also asserting the president's "First Amendment right as an American citizen and duty as Commander in Chief to voice his thoughts about the Fed." That framing — free to opine, careful not to instruct — is the same ambiguity running through Monday's denial: admitting the calls happen closes off one question and opens another, namely what, if anything, gets said on them.

The next test lands with the Fed's next policy meeting and the inflation data due beforehand, which will show whether Warsh's rate path continues to diverge from the one Trump has publicly wanted since the start of his term.
