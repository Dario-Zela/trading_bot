BEYOND THE TAPE · TRAVEL · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-11 · 3 min read

Canada flight cancelled after child refuses to wear seat belt

A Porter Airlines Embraer E195-E2, the aircraft type flown on the Victoria–Toronto route where the cancellation occurred. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Porter Airlines flight from Victoria to Toronto was scrubbed after a toddler wouldn't stay seatbelted, stranding the whole cabin overnight.

A Porter Airlines flight from Victoria to Toronto never made it off the tarmac last Thursday night, undone not by weather or mechanical fault but by one small, immovable passenger who would not sit down and buckle up.

Flight PD444 had pushed back from the gate and was taxiing toward the runway when cabin crew realised a young child was standing on their seat and refusing to be strapped in. Porter said in a statement that both the accompanying parent and crew made repeated attempts to secure the child, without success. Every time an adult got the belt across, the child apparently found a way out of it.

"They just kept hiding under their seat," passenger Aryeh Kozuch told the CBC. "If they got forced into being strapped in, they just kind of slid out underneath." Kozuch was careful to note the child wasn't being defiant so much as frightened — this was a scared toddler, not a tantrum for its own sake — but the effect on the rest of the cabin was the same either way.

A one-child, one-hundred-passenger problem

Under federal aviation rules, a Canadian jet cannot take off with any passenger unrestrained, regardless of age or intent. That left the crew of the 132-seat Embraer E195-E2 no real choice: the aircraft turned back to the gate, and everyone on board — not just the family in question — was made to disembark so the child's luggage could be pulled from the hold and the flight plan refiled.

The timing turned an awkward delay into a full cancellation. Victoria International Airport closes its runway to departures at 12:30am, and by the time ground staff had finished paperwork and rechecked bags, the window had closed. "It took forever," fellow passenger Hillary Hertzberg said of the roughly hour-long process between the aircraft's return to the gate and the family's removal. The rest of the passengers were rebooked onto a flight the following afternoon; Porter apologised "for the effect this had on other passengers" but has not said whether it will cover the cost of the stranded night.

It is a small story next to the week's market news, but it captures something true about air travel's safety architecture: for all the layers of regulation, checklists and crew training built around a flight, the entire operation can still be held hostage by one very small person who has decided, for reasons entirely their own, that the seatbelt is not happening tonight. Porter has not said whether the family will face any restriction on booking future flights, which is likely to be the next question asked if the incident repeats itself on another route.
