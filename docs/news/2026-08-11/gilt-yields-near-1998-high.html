UK 30-year gilt yields near 1998 highs as fiscal pressure builds on Reeves

The Bank of England in the City of London, whose interest-rate path is being watched as closely as the Treasury's fiscal plans. — Diliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields hit their highest since 1998 as a global bond sell-off collides with a £22bn fiscal gap facing new Chancellor John Healey ahead of the 28 October budget.

The yield on Britain's 30-year gilt touched 5.75% this week, a level last seen when Tony Blair was a year into his first term and the Bank of England had only just been handed independence. For a trading desk, the number matters less as trivia than as a signal: long-dated UK government debt is now pricing in serious doubt about the country's fiscal trajectory, just eleven weeks before Chancellor John Healey delivers his first Budget.

The move isn't happening in isolation. Bond markets from Tokyo to Frankfurt have sold off hard this year — Japan's 30-year JGB yield has hit its highest level since the maturity was first issued in 1999, and the German long end is at its loftiest in fifteen years as investors reassess how much debt-issuing governments can carry as populations age and defence budgets swell. But Britain's move has gone further and faster than most of its peers. UK gilts already carried the highest yields in the G7 before Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July; the political transition has piled a domestic risk premium on top of a global one.

A global sell-off with a British accent

Strip out the UK-specific noise and the backdrop is a worldwide repricing of long-duration debt. In the US, the 30-year Treasury yield has climbed to its highest since 2007, above 5.2%, after the Federal Reserve held rates steady on 29 July with three officials dissenting in favour of a hike. Fed chair Kevin Warsh, who inherited an inflation rate that has run above target since 2021, has pledged the central bank "will not hesitate to act" — hawkish language that has kept long-end yields elevated even as headline US inflation eased to 3.5% before oil prices jumped again on the back of Middle East tensions.

Japan's Ministry of Finance has watched its own long bonds come under pressure as the Bank of Japan continues normalising policy after decades near zero. Germany's 10-year Bund yield has pushed past 3.2%. The common thread is a market that has stopped assuming governments can issue duration indefinitely at low cost — inflation is stickier than central banks promised, and the bill for a decade of pandemic and energy-crisis borrowing is coming due at the long end of curves everywhere.

Britain's problem is that it entered this global repricing already exposed. The UK carries one of the highest debt-to-GDP ratios in the G7 alongside one of the longest average debt maturities — a structure the Debt Management Office built up after the financial crisis specifically to reduce refinancing risk, but which now means a larger share of the stock resets to whatever the market decides long yields should be. Roughly a third of gilts are held by overseas investors, who have fewer reasons than domestic pension funds to look past political volatility.

The Burnham premium

That political volatility has a name traders have started using: the "Burnham premium." When Burnham took office pledging to seek "flexibility" within Labour's fiscal rules — code, markets read it, for more borrowing — 30-year yields jumped to a two-month high near 5.75% and sterling slid to its weakest level against the dollar since decimalisation in 1971. The reaction eased only once Burnham named John Healey, a Treasury veteran from the Blair-Brown years, as chancellor and had him publicly commit to sticking with the fiscal mandate while working its margins.

"The new administration is already starting to splash the cash, gearing up for another blockbuster tax-and-spend Budget," said Julian Jessop, an independent economist tracking the government's early spending signals.

The arithmetic explains the market's nerves. Rachel Reeves, Healey's predecessor, left roughly £20bn of fiscal headroom against the government's rule that day-to-day spending be funded by revenue by 2029-30. Burnham's early pledges — scrapping VAT on energy bills, an extra £9bn for defence, funding to end rough sleeping, and a junior doctors' pay settlement inherited from the Starmer government — add up to some £22bn in new commitments. Combined with a higher debt-interest bill from the very yields this dynamic is producing, independent analysts including Panmure Liberum's Simon French now regard further tax rises as close to unavoidable, manifesto pledges against touching income tax, VAT or employee National Insurance notwithstanding.

"The more things change, the more they stay the same," said Helen Thomas, chief executive of Blonde Money. "Rachel Reeves said she inherited a black hole, now she passes one on."

What it costs, and who's watching

The mechanics are unforgiving. A one-percentage-point rise in 10-year gilt yields adds an estimated £12-15bn to the government's annual debt-interest bill — a moving target that eats directly into whatever headroom the chancellor tries to bank. The DMO has already responded by tilting its 2026-27 issuance programme away from the long end: just £8bn of long-dated conventional gilts are planned for sale this financial year, roughly a quarter less than 2025-26 and the lowest long-dated total since 2005-06, with the balance shifted toward shorter and medium maturities where demand has held up better. Chief executive Jessica Pulay described the shift as driven by "our assessment of cost and risk," alongside investor feedback that some gilt-edged market makers wanted continued caution on long issuance specifically.

That is a rational response to weak demand at the long end, but it is also a tacit admission: the government is choosing to borrow shorter and more often rather than lock in today's 30-year rate, a bet that yields will be lower when those shorter bonds need refinancing. It is the same trade every over-extended borrower eventually makes, and it works only if the fiscal picture improves rather than deteriorates in the meantime.

What's next

Healey has already commissioned the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecast for 28 October, when the scale of tax rises — and any further guidance on how far the government intends to push its "flexibility" on borrowing — will become concrete rather than speculative. Between now and then, the more immediate catalysts are the Fed's next moves on US long-end yields, given how tightly gilts have tracked Treasuries this year, and any fresh UK borrowing or inflation data that could move the OBR's pre-Budget arithmetic. Markets have already shown they will price a chancellor's credibility before a single fiscal event is announced; Healey's task over the next eleven weeks is to convince them the number they're pricing is wrong.
