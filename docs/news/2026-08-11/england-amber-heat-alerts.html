Amber heat-health alerts cover almost all of England as fifth heatwave builds

Scorched grass in a London park during a summer heatwave, with the city skyline beyond. — Alisdare Hickson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UKHSA amber heat-health alerts now cover almost all of England ahead of a forecast 36°C on Thursday — the fifth heatwave of a summer already blamed for 2,877 excess deaths.

England is bracing for the broadest heat-health alert of the summer, with amber warnings from the UK Health Security Agency now covering eight of the country's nine regions as forecasters point to 36°C by Thursday. Only the North East sits under the lower-tier yellow alert. It is a marker of how relentless this year has been: this is the fifth heatwave of summer 2026, arriving on top of a country that is already 71.3% in drought.

The alerts, issued jointly by UKHSA and the Met Office, run from 9am on Tuesday to 9pm on Friday across the East Midlands, East of England, Yorkshire and the Humber, London, the North West, South East, South West and West Midlands. An amber alert is not a forecast of discomfort — it is a formal warning to the health system. UKHSA's guidance describes "significant impacts... likely across health and social care services," including "a rise in deaths, particularly among those aged 65 or over," and increased demand across the board. Polly Ashmore, UKHSA's acting consultant in public health, put the advice in blunter terms: check on elderly neighbours, stay out of the sun between 11am and 3pm, and never leave anyone in a parked car.

A summer that keeps breaking its own records

What makes this alert land differently from the amber warnings of previous summers is the arithmetic behind it. Britain has already recorded 33 days above 30°C this year, within touching distance of the 34-day record set in 1995. May delivered the hottest day of that month since 1922, at 35.1°C in Kew Gardens. June went further, hitting 38°C in Lingwood, Norfolk — the UK's warmest June day on record — and prompted the Met Office to issue red weather warnings on three consecutive days, something it had never done before. UKHSA's own reckoning puts the human cost of just the May and June episodes at 2,877 excess deaths, which would make 2026 the deadliest heat year on record, surpassing the 2,985 associated with 2022.

The strain has shown up most starkly in emergency care. The London Ambulance Service declared a critical incident on 26 June after logging its busiest day ever — 8,869 calls and 688 category-1 responses, the tier reserved for cardiac arrests and other life-threatening emergencies. The South Western Ambulance Service separately recorded 4,350 incidents in a single stretch. Health Secretary Yvette Cooper has acknowledged a "big increase" in A&E attendances tied to the heat, and several NHS trusts have declared their own critical incidents as hospital equipment — air conditioning units, linear accelerators, MRI scanners — failed under the load. The NHS has since been directed to plan for heat the way it already plans for winter, treating extreme temperature as a recurring operational risk rather than a one-off event.

The grid under evening pressure

Heat does not only strain hospitals; it strains the machinery that keeps them running. The National Energy System Operator issued its first-ever summer electricity margin notice on 9 July, warning of a possible 1.2-gigawatt shortfall during the Thursday evening peak — the same window, 6.30pm to 10.30pm, when air conditioning and fans push demand up just as solar generation is falling away. NESO stressed the notice was a routine call for extra generation rather than a sign of imminent blackout, but the underlying vulnerability is structural: heatwaves tend to arrive with high pressure and still air, which cuts output from Britain's wind fleet at precisely the moment demand rises. Days earlier, on 23 June, operators had already cut electricity exports to the Netherlands and made an emergency generation call after grid frequency slipped below its normal operating band for close to two hours. Whether a fresh margin notice follows this week's peak was not yet confirmed at time of writing, but the pattern — record heat colliding with a grid built around variable renewables — is now a recurring feature of the British summer rather than an anomaly.

Water, farms and a drought with no end date

The fifth heatwave is landing on ground that has had no chance to recover. July was provisionally England and Wales's driest month on record, with just 7% of average rainfall in some southern areas. The Environment Agency has now declared drought across swathes of England, and reservoir stocks stand at 69% of capacity — well below normal for August — while only 14% of rivers are running at typical levels. More than 27 million people are living under hosepipe restrictions, including a first-in-27-years ban from Yorkshire Water. The National Farmers' Union has warned of a squeeze on winter feed stocks and "very, very poor" cereal yields; the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit estimates the shortfall could cost farmers around £390 million this year.

What the pattern signals

None of these threads — hospital strain, grid margins, drought — is new in isolation. What is new is their concurrence, five times over, in a single season. A single amber alert is weather. Five, layered onto a drought that started in July and a grid that needed emergency intervention in June, reads more like a stress test of systems designed around an assumption of occasional extremes rather than a recurring one. The next data points are close at hand: Thursday's peak will show whether temperatures reach the forecast 36°C, and whether NESO follows July's precedent with another margin notice as the evening demand peak collides with a fading breeze. UKHSA's alerts lift on Friday evening — but with four heatwaves already behind this summer, the more relevant question is not whether this is the last, but how the health service and the grid perform when the sixth arrives.
