Embraer raises outlook after record revenue, outpacing Boeing and Airbus

An Embraer E195-E2, part of the E-Jet family driving the company's record order backlog. — Clemens Vasters / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Embraer's Q2 profit jumped 38% and its backlog hit a record $34.5bn, prompting a guidance raise that outshone a loss-making Boeing and a strong but larger Airbus.

Embraer posted the strongest second quarter in its history on Monday, and raised its full-year profit and cash-flow targets for the third time this year — a rare piece of unambiguously good news from an aerospace industry still picking through supply-chain wreckage.

The Brazilian planemaker reported $2.24 billion in second-quarter revenue, up 23% from a year earlier, alongside adjusted net income of $218.6 million, up 38%. Adjusted EBIT margin came in at 13.3%, and free cash flow — excluding its eVTOL unit Eve — reached $401 million, a sharp reversal from the cash burn Embraer was still working through a year ago.

"We delivered the strongest second-quarter revenue in our history," chief executive Francisco Gomes Neto said, pointing to a backlog that has now set a record for seven consecutive quarters.

That backlog stands at $34.5 billion, up 16% year-on-year, built on demand for the E-Jet E2 family from regional carriers and a business-jet line that Embraer says is running near capacity. The company delivered 65 aircraft in the quarter — 20 commercial jets and 45 executive jets — its best April-to-June tally in 16 years.

The guidance raise

Management now expects a full-year adjusted EBIT margin of 10.0%–10.6%, up from 8.7%–9.3% previously, and free cash flow of at least $400 million, doubling the prior floor of $200 million. Embraer credited a tax credit, tariff exemptions and what it called an improved business outlook, while holding its delivery targets unchanged at 80–85 commercial jets and 160–170 executive jets, and revenue guidance of $8.2 billion–$8.5 billion for the year.

A different kind of recovery

The contrast with Embraer's much larger rivals is instructive, if not quite the clean underdog story it might look like on a headline chart. Airbus also had a strong quarter — revenue up 28% to €20.53 billion and adjusted EBIT up 54% to €2.43 billion, a margin of roughly 11.8% — as deliveries jumped nearly 40% to 237 aircraft. Boeing's recovery is messier: commercial deliveries rose 14% to 171 aircraft and revenue climbed 8% to $24.56 billion, but the company posted a net loss of $428 million after a $280 million charge on the Air Force One program, and its Defense, Space & Security unit swung to an operating loss.

What sets Embraer apart is less the growth rate — Airbus, in percentage terms, actually grew revenue faster this quarter — than the combination of scale and margin. At 13.3%, Embraer's quarterly EBIT margin beat Airbus's and dwarfed Boeing's negative return, despite Embraer generating roughly a tenth of either rival's revenue. For a company that spent much of the past decade rebuilding after the collapsed Boeing tie-up and a pandemic-era order drought, that is the more meaningful data point: operating leverage is showing up fastest at the manufacturer with the newest, leanest cost base, not the ones with the biggest backlogs in absolute dollars.

What it means for positioning

The read for markets is less "Embraer beats Boeing" than a signal about where margin is being captured as the industry works through years of engine and fuselage bottlenecks. Regional and business-jet demand has proven more resilient to supply disruption than wide-body narrow-body programs tangled in Pratt & Whitney and Spirit AeroSystems delays, and Embraer's smaller, more homogenous supply chain has let it convert backlog into cash faster than its larger peers. Whether that gap persists depends on execution through the second half: Embraer still has to hit its unchanged delivery targets, and Boeing's cash generation — $631 million free cash flow this quarter, a swing from last year's burn — suggests its own turnaround is gathering pace, however messily.

Embraer reports third-quarter results in early November, alongside updated delivery figures that will show whether the backlog is converting on schedule. Boeing's next test comes sooner: an FAA production-rate decision on the 737 Max that will determine how quickly it can close the delivery gap with Airbus and, by extension, with the momentum Embraer has just banked.
