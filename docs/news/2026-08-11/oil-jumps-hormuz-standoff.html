Oil rallies toward $82 as Hormuz reopening hopes fade, rippling across stocks and shipping

A crude oil supertanker at sea — the Strait of Hormuz once carried roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG flows before the blockade. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Oil neared $82 a barrel as Trump and Iran traded reparations demands over the Strait of Hormuz, pushing Treasury yields up and hitting tech stocks and FTSE consumer names.

Crude oil extended a third straight session of gains on Monday, with West Texas Intermediate climbing roughly 3% toward $82 a barrel and Brent crude pushing above $86, as hopes for a near-term reopening of the Strait of Hormuz curdled into a familiar standoff. The proximate cause was a familiar one for anyone who has followed the six-month-old conflict between the United States and Iran: a list of demands, met with a counter-list, with a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade sitting in the middle.

The compensation standoff

Iran's Supreme National Security Council laid out six conditions over the weekend for allowing free navigation through the strait: an end to threats and insults, a permanent halt to hostilities against Iran and its allies in Lebanon, Yemen and Gaza, withdrawal of US naval and air forces from the region, compensation for war damages, an end to sanctions, and release of frozen Iranian assets. President Trump's answer, delivered from the White House on Monday, was to raise the stakes rather than meet them. He said his administration would seek its own compensation from Tehran — for "50 years" of damage he attributed to Iran, and for the toll of "roadside bombs and many conflicts" in Lebanon, Syria, Yemen and Gaza. He separately claimed "100% control" over the strait itself.

"We're going to ask for money... Iran should be responsible for the damages and death caused to the people of Lebanon, Syria, Yemen, and Gaza."

The exchange matters because it is the clearest sign yet that the June 17 memorandum of understanding — which briefly promised to restore commercial shipping through Hormuz before collapsing over which sea lanes tankers could use — is not coming back on the table soon. The strait carried roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG flows before the war; it has moved at a trickle since, and Monday's rhetoric pushed any resumption further out.

Yields climb, tech pays the bill

The oil move did not stay contained to energy desks. The 10-year Treasury yield rose more than four basis points to 4.705%, its highest level this month, as traders priced in the inflationary drag of pricier crude ahead of Wednesday's US CPI print. That in turn hit the most rate-sensitive corner of the equity market: long-duration tech and software names, where valuations lean hardest on the assumption of eventually lower discount rates, absorbed the bulk of Monday's selling even as broader indices held up better.

It is the kind of transmission mechanism that shows up in textbooks more often than in practice — a chokepoint thousands of miles from Wall Street moving the price of unprofitable growth stocks by way of the bond market. But the Hormuz closure has now run long enough, and the compensation dispute injected enough fresh uncertainty, that the linkage looks less like noise and more like a standing risk factor traders are having to price on a rolling basis.

Energy majors gain, consumer staples pay

That divergence showed up starkly within London's own benchmark. The FTSE 100 closed roughly 0.4% lower, dragged down by consumer-facing names exposed to higher input costs — Unilever and Diageo were among the session's laggards, while tobacco stocks fared worse still, with Imperial Brands and British American Tobacco each falling more than 4%. Set against that, BP rose about 2% to 492.7p and Shell added close to 1% to just over 3,064p, both benefiting directly from the crude they pump. Shell has also been buying back its own shares through the rally, a mechanical tailwind on top of the commodity gain. Miners including Fresnillo and Glencore climbed too, riding the same broad commodity updraft that punished the staples names.

The result is a single index pulling in two directions at once — proof that "oil goes up" is not a single trade so much as a redistribution, with energy and materials capturing the windfall that consumer and travel-exposed names hand back in higher costs.

What comes next

Washington's own hedge against the disruption came in the form of a narrower Jones Act waiver, extended by the White House for another 90 days but restricted this time to vessels hauling specific energy cargoes, a tighter scope than the broader 60-day waiver Trump first granted in March. The narrowing suggests the administration is trying to keep US refiners supplied without fully suspending a century-old shipping-protection law it would rather not touch again ahead of the midterms — a signal that officials expect the disruption to run for months, not weeks.

The next hard data point is Wednesday's US inflation print, which will show how much of the crude move has already fed through to consumer prices and will shape how the Federal Reserve reads the rest of the year. Beyond that, the market has effectively stopped waiting for a Hormuz breakthrough and started treating the strait as a standing variable — one that will keep nudging yields, tech valuations and the FTSE's internal spread for as long as Washington and Tehran keep trading compensation demands instead of signatures.
