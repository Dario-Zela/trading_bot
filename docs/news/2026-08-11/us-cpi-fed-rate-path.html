US inflation print looms as Fed's Hammack defends hawkish dissent

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the central bank. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Wednesday's CPI report is the last major data point before the Fed decides whether Warsh can cut or Hammack's hawkish dissent prevails.

Wednesday brings the July consumer price index, and for once the number genuinely could go either way at the Federal Reserve. Economists polled ahead of the release expect headline CPI to rise 0.2% on the month, cooling the annual rate to 3.4% from 3.5%. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, is forecast to rise 0.2% as well, nudging the yearly figure down to 2.5%. Small moves, in other words — but small moves are exactly what will decide whether Kevin Warsh, five months into his chairmanship, gets any room to cut rates this year.

That question looked settled a few months ago, when markets assumed a Trump-appointed, cut-friendly Fed chair would move quickly to lower borrowing costs. It looks considerably less settled now. The Federal Open Market Committee has held its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% for five straight meetings, and at the last one, on July 29, three regional bank presidents dissented in favor of raising it — the first time since September 2016 that three FOMC members have broken ranks in the same hawkish direction. CME's FedWatch tool put the odds of no change at the September 16 meeting near 45% this week, with the rest split between a cut and, improbably for a Fed that was expected to be easing by now, a hike. Inflation has sat above the Fed's 2% target for more than five years, and Wednesday's print is the last major data point before officials have to decide what to do about it.

A rare three-way hawkish revolt

The dissent at the July meeting came from Cleveland Fed president Beth Hammack, Minneapolis Fed president Neel Kashkari and Dallas Fed president Lorie Logan, each of whom wanted a quarter-point increase rather than a hold. Hammack has been the most vocal of the three. She first broke with the committee in April, dissenting against language in the post-meeting statement that signaled an easing bias, and she has kept up the pressure since.

"Several hikes may be needed. But I don't want to prejudge exactly what that number is," Hammack said in remarks this week, arguing that a single quarter-point move "probably won't have much impact on the economy." Her central claim is that policy at 3.50%–3.75% simply isn't tight enough to do the job — businesses she talks to report no sign of investment being restrained by borrowing costs, and she sees no strain in the labor market that would argue for easing. Left unaddressed, she has said, elevated inflation only gets harder to dislodge the longer it persists. It is a pointed rebuttal to the idea, popular on trading desks earlier this year, that the Fed's current stance is already restrictive enough to bring inflation to heel — Jerome Powell had described policy in almost those terms before he left the chair.

Warsh's balancing act

Kevin Warsh took the oath as the Fed's 17th chairman on May 22, arriving with a mandate — implicit in his appointment, more explicit in Trump's periodic calls with him — to bring rates down. He has been careful not to promise a timeline. In his first congressional testimony in mid-July, Warsh told lawmakers that recent improvement in inflation data was not "mission accomplished" and pledged to "deliver price stability," while declining to say whether further hikes might still be needed. He has also signaled he wants to move away from the kind of explicit forward guidance his predecessors relied on, preferring to let incoming data speak for itself — a posture that gives him flexibility but also means Wednesday's CPI print carries outsized weight in the absence of any other signal from the top of the building.

That leaves Warsh navigating between two pressures that pull in opposite directions. Markets and, by his own account, the president want lower rates. A trio of regional presidents — with Hammack as their most articulate spokesperson — want the opposite, and are prepared to keep dissenting to make the point. A soft CPI print on Wednesday would hand Warsh cover to resume cutting as soon as September without directly overruling his hawks. A hot one would do the reverse, validating Hammack's argument and making a September hold, or worse for rate-cut bets, look like the more defensible outcome.

What the number needs to show

The bar for a rate cut to survive the July dissenters' scrutiny is now higher than markets had priced going into the summer. A headline print in line with the 3.4% consensus would be read as continued, if grudging, disinflation — enough, probably, to keep a September cut alive without silencing the hawks. Anything closer to 3.6% or above, particularly if core inflation surprises to the upside, would strengthen Hammack's hand considerably and could tip FedWatch's probabilities decisively toward a hold, or reopen talk of a hike that seemed unthinkable as recently as the spring.

Either way, the reaction will be fast. Rate futures typically reprice within minutes of the 8:30 a.m. release, and equity and bond markets have both shown outsized sensitivity to CPI surprises this year given how finely balanced the committee now is. The September 15–16 FOMC meeting is the next hard deadline, but the July payrolls revisions and August's Producer Price Index, due before then, will also feed into whichever side of the argument — Warsh's cutting ambitions or Hammack's hawkish holdout — ends up carrying the day.
