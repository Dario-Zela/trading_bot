Burnham three weeks in: Labour edges ahead of Reform as approval is tested

Andy Burnham's official portrait as prime minister, taken shortly after he entered Downing Street in July 2026. — Wikimedia Commons / official portrait
In one sentence
Labour's narrow Ipsos lead over Reform UK is within the margin of error, and Burnham's own approval still trails Starmer's at the same three-week mark.

Three weeks after Andy Burnham walked into Downing Street, the polling picture that greets him is less a verdict than a shrug. The latest Ipsos tracker, fielded 30 July–4 August and released this week, puts Labour on 28% of the vote to Reform UK's 25% — the first time in months that the poll of record has shown Labour ahead of Nigel Farage's party. It is also, on Ipsos's own numbers, not a result anyone should lean on. With a sample of 1,033 adults, the pollster's stated margin means there is only a two-in-three chance the true gap is within 2 points either way — comfortably inside the range where a 3-point lead and a 3-point deficit are statistically indistinguishable.

A lead, or just noise settling

The context matters more than the topline. Reform has led most voting-intention polls since the spring, a run that survived Keir Starmer's resignation in June and Burnham's installation as prime minister on 20 July. What has moved is the trend line, not necessarily the underlying state of the race: Labour has gained four points since June, Reform has slipped one, and the two parties have been trading the lead across different pollsters — DeltaPoll and Survation have shown Labour narrowly ahead in the past fortnight, YouGov has shown them level, and Ipsos itself had Reform slightly in front as recently as three weeks ago. A single poll crossing zero is a coin landing on the line, not a call. The question this piece can't answer, because no one poll can, is whether Reform's spring surge is genuinely reversing or simply stalling while both parties tread water in the high twenties.

The approval numbers are more equivocal than the vote share

Burnham's personal ratings, gathered in the same Ipsos survey, complicate the story rather than resolving it. Just 29% of respondents say they are satisfied with his performance as prime minister, against 30% dissatisfied and 41% who have no view yet — a rating that trails Starmer's own three-week mark of 37% by eight points, and sits well below the honeymoon numbers of David Cameron (57%) and Tony Blair (65%) at the same stage of their premierships. Seven in ten say they are dissatisfied with how the government as a whole is running the country.

Set against that, Burnham comfortably out-polls his rivals on every head-to-head measure. Fifty percent view him favourably against 33% for Kemi Badenoch, and 36% pick him as best-placed to run the country compared with 15% for Farage. PollCheck's tracker puts him as the only party leader with a positive net rating at all, against Farage on -17, Badenoch on -18 and Starmer, now out of office, on -38. Read together, the picture is of a prime minister who is more liked than trusted yet — voters rate him kindly next to the alternatives without yet being sold on the job he's doing.

The honest reading is that both threads point the same way: a stabilisation, not a breakthrough. Labour's poll lead is too thin to call a reversal of Reform's momentum, and Burnham's approval is positive only by comparison with rivals people like even less. What would change that is repetition — two or three more trackers confirming the same direction, or Burnham's own satisfaction numbers closing the gap with Starmer's early honeymoon. Until then, sterling and gilt markets, which have spent the past three weeks reacting far more to Burnham's fiscal signalling than to his poll rating, have little reason to treat this as new information. The next Ipsos and YouGov readings, due in the coming weeks, are what will show whether this was the start of something or a single data point regressing to the mean.
