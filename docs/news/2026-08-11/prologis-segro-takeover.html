Prologis to buy UK's Segro for £14bn in major logistics property deal

Slough Trading Estate, established in 1920, is the flagship property in Segro's UK portfolio and the largest privately owned business park in Europe. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Prologis has agreed to buy Britain's largest REIT, Segro, for up to £14.3bn after a four-bid pursuit — one of the biggest foreign takeovers of a UK-listed company on record.

Prologis, the world's largest owner of warehouses and distribution sheds, has agreed to buy Segro, Britain's biggest listed landlord, for as much as £14.3bn — a deal that will erase one of the London market's oldest industrial names and hand a US giant control of roughly 110 million square feet of UK and European logistics space.

The boards of both companies confirmed terms on August 4th after a pursuit that ran through four separate proposals over two months. Segro shareholders will receive 0.0920 new Prologis shares for every share they hold, with an option to take up to a quarter of the payout in cash, capped at a £3.5bn pool. Including a potential 2026 final dividend that Segro investors are entitled to retain, the offer values the company at up to 1,031.7p a share — a premium of more than 39% to where the stock traded before Prologis's interest became public in June.

A reluctant seller comes round

Segro did not want this deal, at least not at the price first offered. Its board rejected three earlier approaches from Prologis, including a £13.5bn proposal turned down in July, before shareholder pressure and an improved offer brought the two sides to a recommended agreement. Under UK takeover rules, sustained resistance of that kind typically forces a bidder to either walk away or keep raising — Prologis chose the latter, and added a concession that appears to have tipped the balance: a commitment to pursue a secondary listing for the combined business on the London Stock Exchange, giving Segro investors a route to keep trading UK-listed shares in the enlarged group.

"Prologis shares our conviction in the long-term structural drivers underpinning demand for modern logistics and data centre infrastructure," said Segro chief executive David Sleath, whose company traces its roots back more than a century to the Slough Trading Estate — established in 1920 and still the largest privately owned business park in Europe.

Prologis chief executive Dan Letter framed the deal in terms of scale and expertise rather than price. "This deal brings together Segro's exceptional portfolio and customer relationships with Prologis' global platform, operating expertise and financial strength," he said, adding that the two leadership teams' engagement "has reinforced our confidence in the opportunity ahead." The combination is expected to expand Prologis's European footprint by 47%, and on completion Segro shareholders would hold roughly 8.9% of the enlarged company, assuming full take-up of the cash alternative.

Why the price makes sense to Prologis

The logic is straightforward even if the price tag is not small. Prologis already commands roughly 1.3 billion square feet across 20 countries and a market capitalisation near $119bn, built on the bet that e-commerce and, increasingly, data-centre demand will keep pushing rents higher at warehouses positioned near population centres and ports. Segro's book — 10.3 million square metres, concentrated in the UK's south-east, London's urban fringe, and continental European logistics corridors — fills gaps in exactly that footprint, and does so through an asset class where new supply is constrained by planning rules and land scarcity rather than capital.

That scarcity argument matters more than it might have five years ago. UK commercial property values fell sharply through 2022 and 2023 as interest rates rose, and industrial rents have only recently stabilised. A US buyer willing to pay a near-40% premium for the UK's largest REIT is a signal that, whatever the state of gilt yields or the broader economy, institutional capital still sees structurally scarce, well-let logistics space as worth a premium — a different read from the caution showing up elsewhere in UK property and rate-sensitive sectors.

The London exodus widens

The deal is also the latest data point in a trend that has been building for several years: large, well-established UK companies leaving the London Stock Exchange through takeover rather than choosing to list or relist there. By LSEG's count, cited by Reuters, eleven UK-listed companies valued above £1bn have now exited the market this year through acquisitions, and the Segro deal ranks among the largest foreign takeovers of a UK-listed company on record. For a market that has struggled to attract new listings even as it loses established names, losing its biggest REIT — one whose history predates the LSE's own modern form — adds to a narrative UK policymakers have been trying, with limited success, to reverse.

The transaction still needs Segro shareholder approval, UK court sanction, and regulatory clearances, along with admission of Prologis shares to trading in London — the mechanics that typically stretch a deal of this size out over many months. Completion is targeted for the first half of 2027. Between now and then, the shareholder vote will be the first real test of whether investors judge the improved terms, and the London-listing concession, sufficient compensation for handing Britain's largest industrial landlord to an American owner.
