EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules across the bloc

Henna Virkkunen, the European Commission's executive vice-president for technological sovereignty, who announced the start of AI Act enforcement. — European Union, 2024 — Source: European Parliament
In one sentence
Brussels began enforcing AI Act transparency rules on 2 August, forcing chatbots and deepfakes to disclose themselves or face fines up to €15m or 3% of turnover.

Since 2 August, any chatbot, virtual assistant or AI-generated video shown to someone in the European Union has had to say so. That is the practical effect of a new set of rules under the bloc's AI Act that the European Commission began enforcing this month — and it applies whether the company behind the system is based in Paris, Palo Alto or Shenzhen.

The obligations, set out in Article 50 of the Act, are narrower than they sound but broader in reach than most previous tech regulation. Customer-service bots, AI avatars and virtual assistants now have to make clear, on first contact, that a user is talking to a machine rather than a person. Deepfakes — AI-altered images, audio or video of real people, places or events — need a label. And content generated or substantially edited by AI must carry a machine-readable mark so it can be detected downstream, even after it has been copied, cropped or reposted.

"As enforcement begins, we are taking an important step towards AI that people and businesses can understand and trust, and whose benefits are shared widely across our society," said Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for technological sovereignty, security and democracy, in the announcement on 2 August.

Who enforces it, and with what teeth

Enforcement is split between two layers. National market-surveillance authorities in each of the 27 member states police most day-to-day compliance. The EU's AI Office, a unit inside the Commission that has grown to roughly 38 staff with the power to interview company employees, demand technical documentation and revoke market access, steps in for a narrower set of cases — chiefly where a company both builds a general-purpose AI model and the product running on top of it, or where the system sits inside a platform already designated under the Digital Services Act, such as a major search engine or social network.

The penalties are the same regardless of who enforces them: up to €15 million or 3% of a company's global annual turnover, whichever is higher — a formula borrowed from the EU's playbook on antitrust and data-protection fines, where percentage-of-turnover penalties have proven far more painful to large firms than flat sums. For a company the size of Google or Meta, 3% of global revenue would run into the billions.

Brussels has also built in a soft landing. A voluntary Code of Practice on transparency, finalised in July, had drawn roughly 190 signatories by the end of that month — 83 of them AI providers, including Google, Microsoft, OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Mistral, and 152 deployers such as Lufthansa and Getty Images. Signing isn't mandatory, but it flips the burden of proof: a signatory found in breach is presumed to have acted in good faith, leaving regulators to prove otherwise, rather than the company having to prove compliance from scratch.

The compliance gap that's opening up

What makes this enforcement action different from a routine regulatory update is the asymmetry it creates. A US company running a support chatbot for European customers, or a marketing team publishing AI-generated video to a European audience, now carries the same disclosure duties as an EU-based firm — even when the underlying AI model belongs to a third-party vendor. There is no equivalent federal law in the United States requiring AI systems to identify themselves or label synthetic content; the closest thing is a patchwork of state rules in California, Colorado and New York that don't add up to a single national standard.

That gap cuts two ways commercially. Large US and Chinese AI labs, many of which already operate at a global scale, now face the added cost of building EU-specific labelling and disclosure into every product line — audits that legal advisers estimate can run into the tens of thousands of euros per AI system once certification and documentation costs are included. Smaller EU-based AI startups, by contrast, can often design compliance in from the start rather than retrofitting it onto an existing global product, turning what looks like a burden into a market-entry advantage for firms with less to unwind. It is the same dynamic that played out after GDPR came into force in 2018, when large advertising platforms absorbed the compliance cost while some smaller competitors struggled — except this time the early signal points toward EU-native AI firms benefiting rather than being squeezed out.

What Article 50 doesn't yet cover

The rules that took effect this month are, by the Act's own design, the easier half of the job. The AI Act's toughest provisions — obligations on "high-risk" systems used in hiring, education, biometric identification and migration and border control — were due in 2026 but have been pushed back to December 2027. The Commission says it needs more time to finalise technical standards and guidance before holding companies to that bar. Civil-liberties groups have criticised the delay, arguing it leaves some of the most consequential uses of AI — including systems that could affect asylum decisions — without the Act's strongest safeguards for another sixteen months, while general privacy law such as GDPR was not built to catch every risk posed by an opaque or discriminatory algorithm.

That phased rollout — prohibited practices in February 2025, general-purpose AI model rules in August 2025, transparency obligations now, high-risk rules in December 2027 — reflects a deliberate strategy in Brussels: get disclosure requirements running first, since they are relatively cheap to implement and immediately visible to users, before tackling the harder question of how to audit AI systems making consequential decisions about people's lives.

What to watch

The first real test of the new regime will be whether the AI Office issues a public fine, and against whom — a large US platform would send a very different signal than a mid-sized EU firm. Machine-readable content marking for AI tools already on the market before 2 August carries a grace period running to December 2026, so full compliance across the bloc's existing AI deployments won't be visible until then. Beyond Europe, the more consequential question is whether other jurisdictions follow Brussels' lead on disclosure the way many followed its lead on data protection after GDPR — a pattern regulators call the "Brussels effect," in which EU rules become the de facto global baseline because it's cheaper for multinational firms to build one compliant product than several.
