China's exports beat forecasts even as imports underscore a two-speed economy

Container cranes at the Yangshan deep-water port near Shanghai, a hub for China's export trade. — Alex Needham / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's exports and imports both beat forecasts in July, but retail sales, property investment and factory data show the domestic economy still stalling.

China's customs bureau published July trade figures on August 7th that, on their face, tell a story of unbroken strength: exports up 23.9 per cent year-on-year, imports up 27.5 per cent, both numbers beating what economists had penciled in. Read only the headline, and China's export machine looks as durable as ever, riding an AI-hardware boom and a rush of shipments timed to outrun the next round of American tariffs.

Read past the headline, and the picture splits in two. The trade sector is sprinting. The domestic economy — retail counters, construction sites, factory floors — is barely walking. That gap, not a single soft data point, is the story July's numbers tell most clearly.

The export engine, still running hot

Chinese exports reached $397.85 billion in July, comfortably ahead of the roughly 22 per cent growth forecasters had expected, according to customs data reported by the South China Morning Post. That is a deceleration from June, when exports jumped 27 per cent — the fastest pace since October 2021 — so July's reading is best described as strong momentum easing slightly rather than a fresh peak.

The composition matters as much as the headline number. High-tech exports climbed roughly 40 per cent from a year earlier, and semiconductor exports nearly doubled in dollar value despite volumes rising less than 2 per cent — a sign that chip prices, not just chip demand, are doing the heavy lifting. That tracks with the broader AI infrastructure buildout occupying data-centre operators from Virginia to Guangdong, and with a parallel rush by Chinese manufacturers to get goods onto US-bound vessels before Washington's tariff posture hardens again.

That front-loading has a shelf life. The US and China are currently operating under a truce that holds tariffs at 30 per cent on Chinese goods entering America and 10 per cent the other way, extended in October 2025 to run until November 10th this year. Every month between now and then, exporters have an incentive to ship early rather than risk a snapback — which means some of July's strength is borrowed from months not yet arrived.

One export category moved in the opposite direction. Rare-earth shipments totalled 34,706 tonnes over the first seven months of the year, down 10 per cent from 2025, as Beijing's export-control regime — widened to cover additional US and EU firms in June and July — continued to constrain the one commodity category where China holds genuine pricing power over the rest of the world.

Imports rose too, but not where it counts

Here the July data complicates the tidier narrative: imports rose 27.5 per cent, also ahead of forecast, though slower than June's 36 per cent surge. On paper that looks like the beginning of a demand recovery. It is not. Import growth of that scale in an economy running a $112.5 billion monthly trade surplus is overwhelmingly a story of factories restocking components and commodities to feed export production — not households buying more.

The divergence shows up cleanly in the domestic data released around the same window. Second-quarter GDP growth came in at 4.3 per cent, the weakest pace since the final quarter of 2022 and short of the 4.5 per cent economists expected. Retail sales fell outright in May, the first year-on-year decline since December 2022. Fixed-asset investment was down 5.7 per cent in the first half, and property investment — still the sector dragging hardest on household wealth — plunged 18 per cent. July's consumer price index rose just 0.5 per cent year-on-year, undershooting forecasts and slower than June, while factory-gate prices stayed in deflation. China's manufacturing PMI unexpectedly contracted in July, a casualty of both soft orders and a run of typhoons that disrupted output.

Put plainly: the same economy exporting semiconductors at a near-doubling in value is also one where a household walking into a shop is spending less than it did a year ago, and where a construction crew is more likely to be laid off than hired.

What it means for markets

For traders, the split cuts in more than one direction. Export strength is a tailwind for commodity demand tied to Chinese manufacturing — industrial metals, energy inputs, shipping capacity — and it has kept Asian equity indices supported through a summer otherwise dominated by rate-path anxiety in Washington. Tencent's share rally and the broader tech-led bid in Hong Kong owe something to a trade sector that keeps outperforming.

But a growth model this dependent on exports carries its own fragility. Beijing has limited room to let the yuan strengthen without eroding the price advantage keeping shipments competitive, which caps how far CNY can appreciate even as the trade surplus balloons. And a $112.5 billion monthly surplus sustained mostly by front-run shipments and chip-price inflation is not the same as a surplus built on a healthy, demand-driven economy — it is closer to a economy exporting its way around a domestic hole it has not yet filled.

The next real test arrives well before the tariff truce does. China's August trade data lands in early September, followed by August activity figures — retail sales, investment, industrial output — in the middle of that month, which will show whether the mild improvement in deflation readings is durable or a one-month wobble. Between now and November 10th, expect export numbers to keep looking better than the economy underneath them.
