Netanyahu rejects Trump's Gaza plan sequencing, demands Hamas disarm first

Donald Trump greets Benjamin Netanyahu at the White House in February 2025, months before their public rift over Gaza sequencing. — Dan Scavino / White House (public domain, via Wikimedia Commons)
In one sentence
Netanyahu publicly rejected Trump's phased Gaza disarmament plan, demanding Hamas disarm before any Israeli withdrawal — a rift Washington calls election posturing.

Benjamin Netanyahu told his cabinet on Sunday that Israel is done pretending to agree with Washington on how the Gaza war ends. "Israel rejects the 15-point document," he said, ruling out any troop withdrawal until Hamas has undergone what he called "real disarmament, not fictitious disarmament." It was the most direct public break Netanyahu has staged with Donald Trump's Gaza diplomacy since the plan was unveiled — and it landed eleven weeks before an Israeli election that will decide whether he still has a government to run foreign policy with at all.

The disagreement is not, on its face, about whether Hamas should give up its weapons. Both sides agree it should. The fight is about order of operations — and in a conflict this brittle, sequencing is not a technicality. It is the whole deal.

A plan built on trust neither side has

Trump's 15-point framework, built around a Gaza "Board of Peace" and endorsed by Hamas in July, links three things together: Hamas disarmament, an Israeli military pullback, and a new security architecture featuring an International Stabilization Force and a retrained Palestinian police force. Crucially, it treats these as parallel, phased steps rather than a strict handoff — Israeli forces would begin drawing down as disarmament proceeds, not wait for it to finish.

Netanyahu wants the opposite order: full Hamas disarmament first, an Israeli withdrawal second. "The IDF will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is disarmed," he told ministers. Hamas, for its part, has said the reverse — that it will surrender heavy weapons only after a complete Israeli withdrawal and halt to hostilities, with lighter arms addressed later and tied to progress toward Palestinian statehood. Netanyahu closed that door too, repeating his standing pledge that "as long as I am prime minister, no Palestinian state will arise."

Each side, in other words, wants the other to move first in a war where neither trusts the other not to simply stop once its own demand is met. That is the mechanical reason the plan has stalled, not any dispute over Hamas's ultimate fate.

Washington's mixed signal

The administration's response has been telling. Only days before Netanyahu's cabinet statement, Trump had leaned on him hard in a round of calls, telling him bluntly to "take it or leave it" and warning that a "yes, but" answer meant "we walk away from you," according to reporting on the exchange. Special envoy Steve Witkoff said publicly that "phase two is absolutely going to begin," and Secretary of State Marco Rubio, opening a Middle East trip, reaffirmed that Hamas "must be eradicated" — rhetoric squarely in line with Netanyahu's own.

Yet when Netanyahu went ahead and rejected the document anyway, the White House barely blinked. A senior US official, quoted by Axios's Barak Ravid, said Washington was "not bothered" by the statement: "We understand Bibi's political needs. We have no problem with it as long as he continues to do what we ask — especially regarding restraining attacks in Gaza." Axios also reported that Netanyahu had privately told Jared Kushner, Trump's son-in-law and Gaza point man, that Israel would give the plan "a chance" — a pledge that arrived after, not before, the public rejection.

That contradiction is the real story here: a prime minister saying no in public while his own aides tell Washington the no isn't really a no.

An election-shaped rift

Israel's coalition has set October 27 as election day — the last legal date available, and the first time in roughly four decades that an Israeli government will have served a full term before facing voters. Netanyahu's hardline stance on disarmament sequencing plays directly to the settler and nationalist-right voters he needs to hold his coalition together, against a backdrop of near-daily incidents in the occupied West Bank that have hardened the political mood further.

That gives the rift a shelf life tied to the ballot box rather than to the substance of the plan. Washington's calculation, on the available evidence, is that Netanyahu's public defiance is theatre for a domestic audience, tolerable so long as Israel keeps restraining operations in Gaza and the broader ceasefire architecture holds. If that reading is right, the alliance strain is cosmetic. If Netanyahu's coalition partners force him to harden the position further to survive the vote, or if a stalled disarmament timeline gives Hamas room to rearm, the disagreement stops being rhetorical and starts affecting whether the ceasefire holds at all.

What that means beyond the region

For markets watching the Middle East as a risk-premium input rather than a diplomatic story, the distinction matters. Electoral positioning that resolves after October 27 is noise. A genuine breakdown in US-Israel coordination over troop withdrawal and disarmament enforcement is the kind of event that has historically moved oil futures and defense-sector equities on fears of renewed conflict spreading toward shipping lanes and neighboring states. The current calm in Gaza is a truce, not a settlement, and it is being held together by an American administration publicly patient with an ally publicly defying it.

The next marker is procedural but consequential: whether the International Stabilization Force and Palestinian police elements of Trump's plan begin actually standing up in the coming weeks, regardless of what Netanyahu says in cabinet. If deployment proceeds on Washington's phased terms despite Israeli objections, the rejection will look like exactly what US officials are already calling it — an election-season noise. If it stalls, the sequencing argument will have won by default, and the ceasefire's foundations will be that much thinner heading into an Israeli campaign already fought partly on the war's unfinished business.
