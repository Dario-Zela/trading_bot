Burnham brings forward ban on subscription traps by three months

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government is accelerating the UK's subscription-trap crackdown to January 2027. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham is moving the UK's subscription-trap ban forward to January 2027, giving SaaS and subscription firms three fewer months to rebuild renewal and cancellation systems.

Britain's new subscription rules will arrive three months earlier than planned, Downing Street confirmed this week, tightening the runway for streaming services, software subscriptions and delivery boxes to overhaul how they sign up, renew and lose customers.

The measures — clearer terms before sign-up, mandatory reminders before contracts auto-renew, a straightforward online cancellation route and a 14-day cooling-off period after a free trial converts or a long contract rolls over — were due to take effect in spring 2027. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has now brought that forward to January 2027, folding the crackdown into a wider cost-of-living push alongside a parallel ban on "pretend prices," the practice of inflating a reference price before advertising a discount off it.

"I'm determined to pull every single lever we can to provide people with some room to breathe on the cost of living," Burnham said, framing the acceleration as part of what his office has taken to calling a "cost of living government." Consumer group Which? welcomed the move; policy director Sue Davies said the rules should be introduced "swiftly," pointing to the group's own record of exposing subscription practices that trap customers into payments they no longer want.

The numbers behind the push

The Department for Business and Trade estimates roughly 10 million of the UK's 155 million active subscriptions are unwanted — including 3.5 million people quietly rolled from free trials into paid contracts, and 1.3 million caught out by auto-renewals they didn't track. Government modelling puts the collective saving at around £400 million a year, or about £14 a month for every subscription a household manages to shed.

The legal basis is the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, which received royal assent under the previous government and already carries teeth: the Competition and Markets Authority can fine non-compliant firms up to 10% of global annual turnover, or £300,000, whichever is greater. That enforcement regime has already been delayed twice — first from spring 2026, then again — which makes Burnham's move to pull the date forward, rather than push it back a third time, the more notable reversal.

A compressed build for subscription businesses

Three months is not a long runway for the back-end work involved. Renewal-reminder logic, refund handling for mid-cycle cancellations and cooling-off windows all touch billing systems that SaaS providers, streaming platforms and subscription-box retailers have often left untouched for years. Law firms tracking the DMCC Act have been advising clients to map existing renewal and cancellation flows against the incoming requirements well before now; those that were pacing that work to a spring 2027 deadline have lost a quarter's worth of slack.

The practical exposure sits with retention economics rather than headline revenue — reminder notices and one-click cancellation routes tend to lift churn in the quarter they land, a pattern regulators in the EU and US have already produced with similar "click-to-cancel" rules. UK-listed subscription businesses reporting Q4 numbers in early 2027 will be the first to show whether the compressed timeline dents retention metrics before firms have fully rebuilt their sign-up and renewal journeys around it.

What happens next is procedural but consequential: the CMA still has to publish final guidance on how the cooling-off and reminder provisions will be enforced, and businesses will be watching for that detail as closely as the date itself.
