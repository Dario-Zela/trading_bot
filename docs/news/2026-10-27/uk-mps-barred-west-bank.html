British MPs among delegates refused entry to the West Bank

In one sentence
A 23-strong international delegation including British politicians was stopped at Allenby crossing and refused entry to the West Bank, with no official Israeli reason reported.

A delegation of 23 people, including British politicians, was stopped at the Allenby Border Crossing between Jordan and the occupied West Bank on 27 October and refused entry, according to a Guardian report relayed by the Times of Israel. The group was on its way to the Second Ramallah Congress, a gathering organised by Progressive International and Birzeit University. It was to meet Palestinian academics and civil-society groups.

The delegates came from France, Spain, Luxembourg, Switzerland, Greece, the Netherlands, Ireland and the United States as well as Britain. The Times of Israel account says they were held for nine hours. The Guardian's reporting, as summarised there, names no Israeli authority as the source of the refusal and records no formal grounds. Which British officeholders were in the party, and in what capacity, is not settled in the material available. Reports differ on the status of Sam Tarry, who spoke to the Guardian. Nothing here should be read as a full list of the delegation.

What has been said

Tarry described the decision as political. Israel, he said, "didn't want people to witness what's been going on in the West Bank", a reference in particular to the run-up to elections. That is one participant's reading of events, not a finding. The Guardian approached the Israeli embassy in London, and no response is recorded. No British government statement had been reported at the time of writing.

A familiar pattern

The episode follows earlier refusals involving British parliamentarians. In April 2025 the Labour MPs Yuan Yang and Abtisam Mohamed were stopped at Ben Gurion airport. The Israeli side said they were suspected of intending to document its security forces and to stir hostility toward Israel. The then foreign secretary, David Lammy, called the treatment unacceptable and said he had raised it with Israeli counterparts.

In September 2025 two more Labour MPs, the doctors Simon Opher and Peter Prinsley, were turned back at the crossing from Jordan. They were on a visit organised by the Council for Arab-British Understanding. Health Secretary Wes Streeting called their treatment shameful, and the Middle East minister, Hamish Falconer, called the decision unacceptable.

The pattern matters more than any single case. Israel controls entry to the West Bank, including at the Jordan crossings, and foreign visitors have no right of access. Refusals are rarely accompanied by detailed reasons. Each one therefore leaves the reader weighing the account of the people turned away against the silence, or the brief justification, of the authority that turned them away.

Why access is the story

Politically, a row between London and Jerusalem over a handful of travellers is containable. The larger question is who gets to see the territory. Foreign legislators, journalists and aid workers are among the few outside witnesses to conditions there, and a border that admits some visitors and not others shapes what is known. Israel's stated position in earlier cases has been that entry is a sovereign decision and that some delegations intend to provoke. Critics, including the British ministers quoted above, have called the refusals unjustified.

What to watch

Two things would clarify the matter. One is whether Israel gives an official reason for the refusal. The other is whether the Foreign Office responds publicly, as it did in 2025, or leaves it to the individuals concerned. A formal complaint from Westminster would signal that the government sees this as a repeat of those cases and not a one-off.
