UK shop price inflation at highest since 2024

In one sentence
UK shop price inflation jumped to 2.1% in October from 1.4%, a two-and-a-half-year high, as retailers began passing on costs they had absorbed for two years.

Prices in British shops rose faster in October than at any point in about two and a half years. Retailers that spent two years absorbing higher costs have begun to hand some of them to shoppers.

The British Retail Consortium's shop price measure showed annual inflation of 2.1% in October, up from 1.4% in September, according to figures reported by the Guardian. It is the highest reading since early 2024. The jump of 0.7 percentage points in a month is large for a series that has spent most of the past year drifting between roughly 1% and 1.5%.

What moved

Both halves of the index accelerated:

  • Food: inflation rose to 2.9% from 2.5% in September.
  • Non-food: inflation rose to 1.7% from 0.8%, more than doubling. Clothing and footwear are included in this category.

The non-food swing accounts for most of the headline move. Food was already running above the overall rate, and the rise there is the smaller change.

The BRC's account is that retailers had largely absorbed higher raw-material costs because demand was weak, and began passing some of them on in October. Energy and food-ingredient costs are the ones cited. Helen Dickinson, the BRC's chief executive, was reported as saying retailers have absorbed a significant amount of extra cost over two years, and that supermarkets are finding it harder to hold down prices of canned and packaged goods. Pre-Halloween discounting helped attract shoppers but did not fully contain shelf prices.

Margins or prices?

The two-year absorption is the interesting part. A rise in shelf prices that follows a long period of eating costs is partly a catch-up. It is not necessarily a sign of fresh pressure in the economy. If retailers have been funding cost increases out of margin, October's figure may reflect a partial rebuilding of margin rather than new demand-driven inflation.

That reading is an inference. The reported material does not give retailer profit data, and the BRC index measures prices, not margins. The BRC has previously put the added burden on retailers from employment costs, packaging taxes and other measures at about £10bn a year over two years, which is consistent with limited room to keep absorbing.

For the Bank of England, the distinction matters. Pass-through of earlier cost shocks is a one-off adjustment to the price level. Persistent increases that continue after the catch-up would suggest inflation is embedding. One month cannot separate the two. The Guardian's report, as syndicated, does not mention the Bank, so any link to the rate path here is analysis rather than reported fact. Nor is the BRC index the Bank's target measure: official CPI covers services and a wider basket, and shop prices are only one input.

The household end

The squeeze on households is already visible. Ruth Patrick, professor of social and public policy at the University of Glasgow, runs the Changing Realities project, which found that 73% of participants bought from reduced-price sections in the past month, 62% changed their food basket and 63% did not turn on heating despite cold weather. Patrick also pointed to the burden of high petrol and diesel prices on families who need a car for children's school and medical journeys and on workers who drive to work. The Guardian also cited surveys showing consumer confidence has fallen sharply since the summer, though it did not name them.

That combination, weaker confidence and rising shelf prices, is what makes retailers' position awkward. Raising prices into soft demand risks volumes, and holding them risks margins.

What to watch

The next BRC index, due in late November, will show whether October was a step or a spike, particularly in non-food. The Office for National Statistics' October CPI release will test how much of it reaches the official measure. Both will land ahead of the Bank's next rate decision.
