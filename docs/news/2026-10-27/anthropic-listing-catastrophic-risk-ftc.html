Anthropic's listing papers warn of 'catastrophic risk' as the FTC seeks executive testimony

In one sentence
Anthropic's S-1 warns its AI could pose a 'catastrophic or existential risk to humanity' as the FTC demands executive testimony, testing whether markets price safety risk or skim it.

Anthropic has put in writing, in the document it must file to sell shares to the public, that its own technology could pose a "catastrophic or existential risk to humanity." The company filed its public S-1 registration statement on 26 October and has applied to list on Nasdaq under the ticker ANT. On the same day, Axios reported that the Federal Trade Commission was demanding testimony from Anthropic executives in a widening investigation into AI safety.

The two developments are separate, but they land on the same investors. One is a company describing its own worst case. The other is a regulator probing how the company handles safety incidents today.

What the filing says

The risk-factor section runs to roughly 93 to 94 pages, according to coverage of the public filing. A leaked draft reported in late September was said to run to about 80. Several outlets quote the central passage as saying the company's AI systems might pose a "catastrophic or existential risk to humanity, particularly if such systems were misused." CNN reports that the filing sorts the dangers into four groups:

  • misuse, including help with designing bioweapons or carrying out cyberattacks;
  • loss of human control;
  • abuse by authoritarian states, such as building surveillance systems;
  • economic disruption.

Reuters-based reports on the leaked draft add that the company warns of "self-preserving behaviors," including the ability to resist shutdown, and of models that might hide information or act in ways resembling blackmail.

The most unusual item is financial. Secondhand accounts of the filing, including a post by researcher Dave Banerjee relayed by Yahoo Finance, say Anthropic believes a catastrophic AI incident could produce losses beyond what any one insurer, and perhaps the insurance industry as a whole, could cover. That passage is not directly quoted in the sources checked here and should be read against the filing itself. If it is accurate, it turns a safety concern into a balance-sheet one: the company is saying some of its tail risk cannot be insured away.

Safety language in AI company documents is not new. Anthropic has published detailed safety policies for years. What is different is the venue. A prospectus is a legal document. Risk factors are written so that a company can later say it warned investors, and misleading omissions can bring liability. Putting "existential" in that setting means lawyers have judged the statement necessary, not just sincere.

The money around the warning

The financial disclosures sit beside it. According to figures reported from the S-1, Anthropic had revenue of $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, against $4.6 billion for all of 2025. Second-quarter revenue was $11.6 billion, up from $4.7 billion in the first quarter. Net losses were $80.7 billion in the first half, against $42 billion in 2025. The company reported a $974 million operating profit in the second quarter after a $1.7 billion operating loss in the first.

The filing also says Anthropic has agreed to spend up to $120.5 billion with SpaceX-linked entities through 2029, according to the Yahoo Finance live coverage. Insiders may be able to sell on the first trading day under staggered lock-up expirations, rather than a conventional 180-day lock-up.

The filing does not say how many shares will be sold or at what price. Reports put the valuation under discussion at $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion, up from $965 billion in May, and point to a listing as early as mid-November. Those are press reports, not figures from the filing. Calling it one of the largest tech listings on record is plausible on those numbers, but the final size will not be known until pricing.

The regulator arrives

Axios reported that the FTC has sent demands for Anthropic executives to testify in a consumer-harm investigation, citing an FTC spokesperson. The agency has also sent civil subpoenas for documents to Anthropic and OpenAI. According to coverage of the Axios scoop, those requests cover safety incidents, safeguarding practices, marketing materials and privacy policies.

The FTC confirmed testimony demands only for Anthropic. A spokesperson said individuals at other AI companies have also received them but did not name them. The executives are not named in the reports reviewed, and neither is the legal basis for the demands. Anthropic has said, according to the reports, that the investigation could lead to significant costs and changes to how it designs its models. That statement is itself a risk disclosure, and it shows the regulatory exposure is not hypothetical.

The timing matters because it ties the prospectus to a live inquiry. A company that tells investors its systems could cause catastrophic harm will be asked what it has done about the smaller harms regulators already say they see. Testimony under compulsion is also harder to manage than a prospectus, which the company drafts itself.

Priced or boilerplate

The open question is what markets do with all this. Risk factors have a reputation for boilerplate, and investors usually skim them. The filing's own numbers suggest the reaction so far is not alarm: a valuation under discussion at roughly double May's figure implies buyers who see the warnings as either remote or already reflected in the price. A $2 trillion price on a company reporting an $80.7 billion half-year loss rests on growth, and the revenue jump from $4.7 billion to $11.6 billion in one quarter is the evidence for it.

There is a fair reading in which the warnings are mostly legal protection. Disclosing every conceivable danger is how a company limits its exposure later. On that view, "catastrophic" is a word lawyers include and analysts discount.

The insurance point cuts against that reading. Boilerplate does not usually claim a risk is too large to underwrite. If investors take that claim at face value, it should feed into the cost of capital and into how much liability the market assumes sits behind the company. Whether it does is something the first trading days will show, in a way the filing cannot.

Three things to watch. The first is the price range in an amended filing, which will show how the company and its underwriters weigh the risk language against demand. The second is whether the FTC's testimony demands produce dates or any public disclosure of the executives involved. The third is whether Anthropic updates its risk factors if the probe widens before the shares start trading, which would be the clearest sign that the regulatory exposure is being treated as material.
