Labour split over Rosebank deepens

In one sentence
Labour is split over approving Rosebank, with Energy Secretary Fahnbulleh reportedly leaning against and the Chancellor for, in a decision expected before the end of 2026.

A dispute inside Labour over whether to allow the Rosebank oilfield west of Shetland has sharpened in the past week. It has become the clearest test yet of whether the government's net-zero pledges can sit alongside new North Sea oil production. The decision belongs formally to the Energy Secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh. No ruling has been issued, and Downing Street has not confirmed reports that the Prime Minister, Andy Burnham, and the Chancellor, John Healey, favour approval. Several outlets report that Fahnbulleh is leaning towards refusal.

Why the consent is being decided twice

Rosebank, one of the largest undeveloped fields on the UK continental shelf, was first approved in 2023 under the Conservatives. In January 2025 the Court of Session in Edinburgh quashed that consent. The challenge was brought by campaigners, including Uplift and Greenpeace UK, who argued that the environmental assessment had ignored the emissions from burning the oil once it is extracted — so-called scope 3 emissions. The ruling followed the Supreme Court's June 2024 judgment in Finch v Surrey County Council, which held that planning decisions on fossil fuel projects must assess those downstream emissions.

The court allowed preparatory work to continue but not extraction. The government then published guidance on assessing scope 3 emissions and said each project would be judged case by case, weighing climate effects against economic and other considerations. Uplift has estimated Rosebank's lifetime downstream emissions at more than 200 million tonnes of CO2. Scope 3 typically accounts for the overwhelming share of an oil company's footprint, which is why the legal point matters so much.

The fresh assessment is now the battleground. The law requires the emissions to be counted. It does not say what weight they carry against jobs, tax revenue and investment.

Jackdaw as the template, and the dividing line

The first test came last week, when Fahnbulleh approved the Jackdaw gas field, which had also lost its original consent in court. Jackdaw is expected to produce about 36 million tonnes of carbon emissions over its life and to supply mainly the domestic market. Fahnbulleh acknowledged it would not lower household bills but argued it would support energy security amid conflict in the Middle East.

She also drew a line between the two projects. "One is a gasfield that caters for the domestic market, while the other is an oilfield that is mainly for export," she said. Campaigners put Rosebank's emissions at about seven times Jackdaw's. Oil is priced internationally, so opponents argue the field would add little to British energy security. Supporters answer that it holds an estimated 300 to 500 million barrels, roughly 8% of UK oil production, and would protect jobs and generate tax receipts.

The reported cabinet alignments are as follows, and most rest on unattributed briefing:

  • Likely to favour approval: Healey and the Business Secretary, Jonathan Reynolds. Burnham is also reported to lean that way, which Downing Street has not confirmed. Burnham has said Labour backs renewables but still needs oil and gas for decades, and that the government is taking "a pragmatic approach to the North Sea".
  • Opposed or sceptical: Fahnbulleh, reportedly, and Ed Miliband, now Foreign Secretary and previously in charge of energy, who is reported to be firmly against new drilling.
  • Backbench critics: Catherine West, a former minister, and Afzal Khan, who chairs the all-party group on climate change, have criticised further investment. Richard Burgon and Alex Sobel criticised the Jackdaw approval.
  • Outside pressure to approve: The GMB's Gary Smith and Unite's Sharon Graham have both backed the field. So has the Scottish Labour leader, Michael Marra, and the Conservatives' Andrew Bowie.

Labour's 2024 manifesto promised no new licences for North Sea oil and gas. Rosebank is already licensed, which is why ministers describe it as a consent question rather than a licensing one. Critics doubt that distinction will satisfy voters or backbenchers.

The politics of a vote

The pressure is not only inside cabinet. Some Labour MPs are reported to be discussing ways to force a parliamentary vote if Rosebank is approved, which could produce a rebellion against the Prime Minister. Anders Opedal, chief executive of Equinor, the field's operator, has warned that failed approvals would be a serious setback for investment in the sector.

Money adds a complication. The Budget is due on Wednesday 28 October, and Healey is expected to give more detail on a planned energy wealth fund, reportedly with an initial £2bn. How far the fund relies on future North Sea development is unclear, and it does not depend on Rosebank alone. Its backers will nonetheless be watched for any link between revenue and new drilling.

What to watch

A Rosebank decision is expected before the end of 2026, though no date has been set. Whichever way it goes, the reasoning will matter as much as the outcome. Approval would show how much weight ministers give to scope 3 emissions when set against jobs and fiscal receipts. Refusal, following Jackdaw, would suggest the test turns on whether output serves the domestic market. The wording of the decision letter, and any attempt by MPs to force a Commons vote, will show how the government intends to reconcile its climate targets with its energy strategy.

This report draws on press accounts from 22 and 26 October. Much of the cabinet detail rests on unnamed sources and could change.
