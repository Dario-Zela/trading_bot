King travels to the Caribbean as Commonwealth leaders press for a full slavery apology

In one sentence
King Charles tours the Bahamas, Guyana and Antigua until 4 November; the palace rules out a formal apology while the Commonwealth's chief and Caribbean states press for one, plus reparations.

King Charles begins a nine-day tour of the Caribbean today. It will be shaped as much by what he is asked to say about slavery as by anything on the itinerary. Buckingham Palace has said he will not issue a formal apology. The Commonwealth's own Secretary-General has said he should. Caribbean and African governments are pressing the case for reparations, and the question will follow him from Nassau to the Commonwealth summit in Antigua.

The itinerary

The tour runs from 27 October to 4 November. The King and Queen Camilla start in the Bahamas, where Charles is head of state. It is his first visit as monarch. He last went in 1973, as Prince of Wales. A State Visit to Guyana follows, a republic within the Commonwealth. The tour ends in Antigua and Barbuda, where he is due to give a speech at the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM), which runs from 1 to 4 November. The Queen joins him for that leg. Reports note that he is still receiving treatment for cancer.

The three stops carry different weight. In the Bahamas he visits as King of the Bahamas, so what he says there is said in a constitutional role. Guyana is a republic. In Antigua he addresses 56 governments at once, and reparations are expected to be one of the summit's most contested topics.

What the palace has said

In early October, Buckingham Palace set out its position in advance. The King, it said, would not apologise formally, but the trip would not shy away from "the darkest days of our past" while concentrating mainly on each country's present and future. The palace said that both an apology and reparations are matters for government rather than the monarch.

That division follows convention. A King acts on ministerial advice, and the UK government has ruled out reparations. Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband is reported to have said: "I don't think reparations are the answer." The palace line therefore puts the decision with a government that has already given its answer.

Charles has gone further in words than any recent predecessor. He has spoken of his "personal sorrow" at the suffering of slavery and called it an "atrocity which forever stains our history". Those remarks were expressions of regret. They were not a state apology, and they carried no commitment to pay anything.

The pressure from the Commonwealth

The Commonwealth Secretary-General has said publicly that the King should apologise. That intervention matters because it comes from the organisation's own senior official, not from a single critical government. The King is its head, and the summit he will address is hosted by the body that is now asking him to act.

Reparations are likely to dominate CHOGM. Caribbean states have long argued that slavery and colonial rule left lasting economic damage, and that Britain and other former colonial powers should pay for it. African governments have pressed the same case, and the clamour has grown in recent years.

Why it matters

A formal apology would be a significant break. It would mean a monarch, or a government acting through him, accepting legal and moral responsibility for the slave trade and for British colonial slavery. It would also raise the question of what should follow. That is why ministers prefer to keep apology and reparations apart from the King's travels.

The present arrangement leaves the King in a narrow space. He can acknowledge the history in his own words, and he has done so before. He cannot commit the British state. Hosts and fellow leaders may find that distinction unsatisfying, and the speech in Antigua is where it will be tested.

What to watch

Three things will show how far the visit moves the debate. The first is the wording of the King's remarks in Nassau and Georgetown, and whether he goes beyond his earlier expressions of sorrow. The second is the speech at CHOGM from 1 November. The third is whether the summit's final communiqué mentions reparations, and whether the UK government accepts that language. Until then, the palace's position stands: regret from the King, and a decision on money and apology left to ministers.
