Machado reported back in Venezuela after exile

In one sentence
María Corina Machado returned to Venezuela on 25 October after ten months abroad, raising pressure on acting president Delcy Rodríguez to schedule the election she has yet to call.

María Corina Machado, Venezuela's opposition leader, has returned to the country after about ten months abroad. Her return ends an exile that began last December, when she travelled to Norway to receive the Nobel Peace Prize. It comes at an awkward moment for the government in Caracas and for Washington.

Machado announced she was back on Sunday, 25 October, with a short post on X: "I'm here, with you." Her team released images of her at a church in the Plaza del Pueblo de Santa Rosa, in the central-western state of Lara. She has not said where she is staying or how she crossed the border. The New York Times, citing two anonymous government officials, reported that she entered from Colombia. That detail comes from a single outlet's anonymous sources and has not been independently confirmed.

A long road home

The return was not a surprise to those following her. In March she told Bloomberg she would come back "in a few weeks", and in May she said she aimed to be home before the end of 2026. Reports differ on how many earlier attempts were blocked. The Hill says she was turned back three times over 24 hours in September. Latin Times counts seven attempts.

Before leaving, she had spent 11 months in hiding. Her allies, including Edmundo González, the opposition's 2024 presidential candidate, have said they will follow her. González said on X that he would soon join her.

The political setting

The country she returns to differs from the one she left. US forces captured Nicolás Maduro in January. Acting President Delcy Rodríguez has governed since then without scheduling the presidential election that the constitution requires. The Trump administration has praised Rodríguez and has played down talk of a vote.

Washington's reaction has been mixed. Secretary of State Marco Rubio publicly welcomed Machado's return. President Trump said he had no advance notice and hoped she was safe. Some outlets report unease in the White House that her presence could provoke a political crisis. That reading rests on unnamed sources, and the administration has not stated it on the record.

Machado has pressed Rodríguez to hold an election and has said she will run. She has said a vote held under democratic conditions would need seven to nine months of preparation. She has also criticised Spain's support for lifting sanctions on Venezuela as "a great mistake". She argues that pressure on Rodríguez should continue until democracy is restored.

What to watch

The oil and sanctions angle is not yet backed by any specific policy or deal in the coverage reviewed. It matters only because the US has a stake in the stability of the post-Maduro arrangement, and an election timetable could change it.

Two things will show how far Machado's return shifts the balance. One is whether Rodríguez's government moves to set an election date. The other is whether it lets Machado move and campaign freely. González's arrival, which he says is imminent, will be an early test of the second.
