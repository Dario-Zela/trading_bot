Radio and tinned-food sales rise after call to stock up

In one sentence
Retailers say radio and tinned-food sales have risen after Wes Streeting's 14 October call to keep emergency supplies, but no figures or named retailers have been given.

A government suggestion that households keep a few tins and a torch to hand appears to have sent some shoppers to the shelves. Retailers have reported a rise in sales of radios and tinned food, according to BBC reporting. The BBC's report has not been independently verified, and it comes without numbers.

The call

The advice came on 14 October from Wes Streeting, who is named as Defence Secretary in the Grocery Gazette's account. He spoke ahead of the Labour Party conference. Households, he said, should keep a supply of bottled water indoors, along with some tinned goods that can be eaten if a power cut leaves them unable to cook.

The guidance sits within the government's wider "Prepare" advice. It covers disruptions from power cuts and flooding to wildfires, cyber attacks and the possibility of conflict. Tinned meat, fruit and vegetables feature on the suggested list. Nutrition experts also point to oats, rice and pasta.

Streeting framed the message as "alert not alarmed", and the government has said households do not need to make large purchases. That makes the reported response a mildly ironic one: a call for modest, sensible preparation has apparently produced a measurable retail bump, though how measurable is the open question.

What is known about the rise

Very little, in numbers. The BBC report, as relayed to this desk, says retailers have seen sales of radios and tinned goods climb. It names no retailer, gives no percentage, and does not say over what period the increase was recorded. The Grocery Gazette piece from the day of the speech carried no retailer comment or sales data; its observation that suppliers could face higher demand for longer-life products was the reporter's own analysis rather than a trade statement.

The pairing is plausible on its face. A battery or wind-up radio is the classic answer to a power cut, when phones and broadband go down, and a tin is the classic food that needs no cooking. Both items track the advice closely, which supports the idea that the rise is a response to the call rather than coincidence. But that is inference. A seasonal pattern, a storm-driven spike in demand or a promotional cycle could produce a similar bump, and without figures the cause cannot be pinned down.

Online commentary has also muddied the water. Some coverage has treated the advice as official stockpiling instructions, which it is not, and at least one outlet noted that social media versions of the guidance had drifted from what was actually said. If shoppers have acted on the exaggerated version, the sales effect could be larger than the official message warrants, and shorter-lived.

What to watch

The test is persistence. Panic-buying episodes, such as pasta and tins during the spring of 2020, tend to spike and then fade, sometimes leaving a hangover of weak sales afterwards. A durable change would show up in retailer trading updates and in the official grocery-market data in the coming weeks. For now, the story is a plausible response to an official nudge, with no evidence yet of its size.
