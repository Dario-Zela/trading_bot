Budget cost-of-living package: tariff cuts on staples and an end to the carers' earnings cliff-edge

In one sentence
The Budget reportedly cuts some staple-goods tariffs and ends the Carer's Allowance cliff-edge, but neither measure has confirmed figures yet.

The Chancellor's cost-of-living package is reported to contain two measures. One cuts import tariffs on some staple goods. The other removes the earnings "cliff-edge" that catches people on Carer's Allowance. Only the second has any numbers behind it so far. The tariff measure is, for now, a headline without a schedule.

The tariff measure: what is and isn't known

The tariff report comes from BBC UK. It says the Chancellor will lower import duties on some staple goods as part of the Budget's help for households. The report as summarised names no products, rates or estimated savings, and none of those could be checked independently. A search of published material found no Budget document, Treasury release or independent costing that carries the measure.

That gap matters because the policy's effect depends on details that are still missing:

  • Which goods. "Staples" could mean a handful of food lines or a broad basket.
  • Which tariffs. The UK's most-favoured-nation duties apply to imports from countries without a trade deal. Many staples already arrive duty-free or at a preferential rate under existing agreements, which would limit the effect.
  • Whether the saving reaches shelves. A duty cut can lower prices, widen retailer margins, or do some of each. Retailers' own pricing decisions settle that split, and the Budget cannot.

The inflation context is hard to read. The same edition reports UK shop price inflation at its highest since 2024, so a cut that is small relative to the basket would barely register in that figure. Until the Budget documents give a product list and an estimated cost to the Exchequer, the effect on retailers and on the inflation outlook cannot be sized. The cost would show up in the Office for Budget Responsibility's costings of the package if the measure is in the final text.

Carers: the cliff-edge

The second measure is better documented. The Guardian reports that under the Budget changes carers will be able to earn above the weekly limit without losing their benefit. The paper calls the present rule a "cliff-edge", a description that is its own but matches how the benefit works. Carer's Allowance has no taper. A carer who earns a penny over the limit loses the whole payment, not a fraction of it.

The limit was last reset in April 2025 to the equivalent of 16 hours a week at the National Living Wage, which put it at £196 a week, up from £151, according to Carers UK. The charity estimated that about 60,000 more carers would gain access to the benefit as a result. The benefit itself is the lowest of its type, at £81.90 a week in 2024-25. Those figures predate this Budget. The new limit, the form the replacement takes (a taper, a higher threshold or something else) and its start date were not in the material available and need to come from the Treasury's document.

The case for change has been made for some time. An independent review of Carer's Allowance overpayments, led by Liz Sayce, found that the cliff-edge discouraged carers who wanted to combine care with some paid work. The Government has also run a call for evidence on modernising the earnings limit, aimed at reducing the cliff-edge's effect and making payments more predictable for carers whose income fluctuates. Consumer campaigner Martin Lewis has argued for a taper, as in most other means-tested benefits.

What ties the two together

Both measures are small by Budget standards. They are also hard to value without a costing. A tariff cut on a narrow list of goods may lower the Exchequer's income by little and household bills by less. Removing a cliff-edge costs more or less depending on how many carers earn near the limit and what the replacement is. In both cases the question is whether the announcement comes with a figure.

The documents to watch are the Treasury's Budget book and the OBR's costings table. The first should settle the product list, the rates and the start date for the tariff cut, and the new earnings rule for carers. The second will show whether either measure moves the inflation forecast or just rearranges household budgets at the margin.
