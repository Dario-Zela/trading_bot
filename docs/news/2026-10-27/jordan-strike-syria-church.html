Jordan reportedly strikes Syria after church attack

In one sentence
Jordan says it struck armed groups in southern Syria after nine people were killed at a Karak church funeral; Damascus denies being consulted and has closed the border crossing.

Jordan has carried out air strikes in southern Syria, saying they were a response to a deadly attack on a church inside its own borders. Syria's government says Amman did not tell it beforehand. The two accounts of the same night differ on the central diplomatic question, and the casualty figures are still moving.

The attack at Karak

On Friday, gunmen opened fire on a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in Hammoud, in Karak governorate, killing nine people. Jordan's interior minister said the attackers were all Jordanians, and that they had holed up in a building in a village near the church. Security forces arrested two brothers and their brother-in-law after a shootout at an abandoned house, according to reports carried by ABC News and others.

That detail matters for what followed. If the gunmen were Jordanian, the case for striking across the border rests on where Amman says they were supported or sheltered, and Jordan has not yet set that out in public.

The strikes

Jordan's military said the operation hit "hideouts" of armed groups in south-eastern Syria. It did not name the groups or give exact locations. It also said the strikes were launched "in coordination with the Syrian government".

Damascus has said otherwise. Syria's government said Jordan did not coordinate with it before the strikes. It then closed its main crossing with Jordan, which suggests the dispute is more than a matter of wording. Syrian state media, citing a local health official, said the strikes across Daraa province killed eight civilians, including women and children. Earlier reports gave five or seven dead. Jordan has not commented on civilian deaths, and none of the figures has been independently confirmed.

Context

Cross-border action is not new in this corner of the region. Jordan has struck targets in southern Syria before, including raids on Islamic State positions in 2017 and a suspected Jordanian air strike in January 2024 that killed at least nine people. Those operations were aimed at smuggling networks and militants near a long, porous frontier. What is unusual here is the stated trigger: a mass-casualty attack on a Christian congregation on Jordanian soil, in a country that prizes its record of sectarian calm.

The disagreement over coordination is the point to watch. Strikes carried out with Damascus's consent would be a security arrangement between neighbours. Strikes carried out without it would be a violation of Syrian sovereignty, and the closed crossing would then have costs for trade and for the movement of people on both sides.

What to watch

Three things will settle the picture. The first is whether Jordan names the groups it targeted and shows a link to the Karak gunmen. The second is whether the toll of civilian deaths in Daraa is verified. The third is whether the crossing reopens, which would suggest that both governments want the row contained.
