Nike's World Cup bet backfires as favoured teams flame out

In one sentence
Nike's sponsored teams missed the World Cup final and it still ran out of Team USA jerseys mid-tournament — a forecasting problem with earnings implications.

Nike went into the 2026 World Cup with the biggest roster in football, sponsoring twelve national teams including Brazil, France and England. By kickoff of Sunday's final in New Jersey, all twelve were home watching, and the two teams left standing — Argentina and Spain — both wear the three stripes. Adidas, Nike's old rival, dressed 23 of the players on the pitch for the sport's showcase match; Nike dressed none.

That is bad luck, mostly. Tournament brackets are not a supply chain, and no amount of forecasting discipline would have kept England out of a semi-final loss to Argentina. But Nike's second World Cup problem was self-inflicted, and it is the more interesting one for anyone pricing the stock.

Stars, stripes, empty shelves

As the United States men's team — a Nike-sponsored side playing on home soil for the first time since 1994 — advanced through the group stage by winning two of three matches, its jerseys vanished. By late June, adult home and away shirts were sold out or reduced to odd sizes at Nike.com, Dick's Sporting Goods, Fanatics, Soccer.com and the official U.S. Soccer store. The federation's own shop reportedly sold twice the jersey volume it had moved over the equivalent stretch of the 2022 tournament, and one retail executive warned stock could run out entirely within days.

Fans were not shy about the irony: Nike, they noted online, had four years' notice that the U.S. would host and that demand for the host nation's kit would spike. A Nike spokesperson confirmed to The Athletic that new stock was being manufactured and prioritized for markets hosting matches, with replenishment arriving as the U.S. entered the knockout rounds — a fix, but a late one, arriving after the peak commercial window of group-stage enthusiasm had already passed.

The forecasting problem has two ends

What makes the miss worth a trader's attention rather than a fan's grumble is where it sits next to Nike's other inventory story. Under chief executive Elliott Hill's "Win Now" turnaround, the company has spent much of fiscal 2026 trying to clear a glut of unsold product in Greater China, where revenue fell 17% to $1.42 billion in the fourth quarter as local consumers shifted to domestic brands. So the same company that overstocked one major market found itself unable to keep a foreseeable, single-event surge in stock in another. That is not a single bad guess — it looks like a forecasting apparatus struggling in both directions at once, and it lands on a stock already down roughly 44% from last year's high.

What's next

Nike does not report earnings again until late September, when fiscal first-quarter results will show whether the restocked USMNT gear, and the broader marketing spend around the tournament, translated into a sales bump or just a headline. Adidas, fresh off dressing both finalists, reports in early August and will be the more direct read on how much World Cup exposure is actually worth. Hill has already told investors the turnaround will not fully show up until 2027; a supply chain that cannot meet demand for a shirt with a known release date on a known day gives little reason to move that timeline up.
