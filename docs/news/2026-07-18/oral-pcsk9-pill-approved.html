First oral cholesterol pill wins FDA approval, reshuffling pharma winners

A Merck research facility. The company's Lipfendra, approved July 16, is the first oral PCSK9 inhibitor for high cholesterol. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The FDA approved Merck's Lipfendra, the first oral cholesterol pill — but insurers, not the drugmaker, may capture the biggest financial upside.

The Food and Drug Administration approved Merck's Lipfendra on July 16th, the first pill that does what, until now, only needles could do: block a protein called PCSK9 and drive down LDL cholesterol by more than half. For the roughly 86 million American adults with elevated LDL — the "bad" cholesterol that drives heart attacks and strokes — the news means a once-daily tablet, taken at room temperature, may soon replace a subcutaneous injection kept in the refrigerator.

The approval also set off a scramble to work out which companies gain and which lose, because Lipfendra doesn't just add a new drug to the shelf — it threatens to rearrange how an entire cholesterol-drug market gets priced and prescribed. Merck's stock jumped on the news, opening at $124.33 and closing at $127.63 the day of approval, roughly a 3.6% move.¹ But several analysts and industry trackers argue the company writing the biggest check afterward may not be the one that invented the pill.

What the pill actually does

Lipfendra — generic name enlicitide decanoate — is a macrocyclic peptide that blocks PCSK9, a liver protein that normally marks LDL receptors for destruction. Block PCSK9, and the liver keeps more receptors around to clear LDL cholesterol from the blood. It's the same mechanism as Amgen's Repatha and Regeneron/Sanofi's Praluent, both injectables, except Lipfendra comes as a tablet.

In the Phase 3 CORALreef trial program, patients on Lipfendra saw LDL-C fall 56% versus placebo by week 24 in the general high-cholesterol population, and 59% in patients with inherited (heterozygous familial) high cholesterol — figures that, per Merck's trial data, roughly match injectable PCSK9 inhibitors.² The drug also cut lipoprotein(a), a stubborn genetic risk factor, by about 25%. Side effects were mostly mild: diarrhea in 7% of familial-hypercholesterolemia patients versus 2% on placebo, dizziness in 9% versus 4%, with no meaningful jump in trial discontinuations.

Merck priced it at $315 for a 30-day supply — about $3,780 a year at list price, undercutting the $500-to-$600-a-month range that injectable PCSK9 drugs have historically carried.³ That pricing decision, more than the chemistry, is what's rearranging the competitive map — cardiologists Mohammed Alo and Abeer Berry, quoted in coverage of the approval, both framed the drug as a meaningful step toward more aggressive LDL control, though neither claims it yet proves it prevents heart attacks.⁴ That distinction matters: Lipfendra's approval rests on cholesterol-lowering data alone. It has not yet shown, the way Repatha has, that it actually reduces heart attacks and strokes — Merck's outcomes trial, enrolling more than 14,500 patients, is still running.

Merck's hedge against a shrinking cancer franchise

Merck didn't get this drug through the FDA's normal review clock. It used a priority review voucher tied to a Trump-administration program, established under former commissioner Martin Makary, that promises one-to-two-month reviews for medicines judged to serve "national interests." Merck secured the voucher in December after agreeing to separate drug-pricing arrangements with the White House, according to trade-press reporting on the approval.⁵

The urgency lines up with Merck's balance sheet problem. Keytruda, its cancer immunotherapy and the best-selling drug in the world, loses U.S. patent protection in 2028, and investors have spent two years pricing in the resulting revenue cliff. Merck's stock is still up roughly 24% year-to-date and 60% over the past year, and Guggenheim reiterated a Buy rating with a $145 price target after the approval, with other analyst targets running as high as $155.⁶ Some estimates put Lipfendra's peak annual sales above $5 billion — a real number, but not one that fully offsets a Keytruda franchise generating tens of billions.

Amgen, AstraZeneca and the also-rans

Amgen's Repatha isn't going anywhere immediately. It posted $876 million in first-quarter 2026 sales, up 34% year-over-year, and its Vesalius-CV outcomes trial showed a 25% reduction in major cardiovascular events — hard evidence Lipfendra doesn't have yet.⁷ That gap gives Amgen a defensible niche among higher-risk patients until Merck's outcomes data reads out.

AstraZeneca's own oral PCSK9 candidate, AZD0780, remains behind. Its Phase 3 cardiovascular-outcomes trial, targeting more than 15,000 patients, began recruiting in June 2025 and is event-driven, meaning its finish line depends on how many heart attacks and strokes accumulate in the study population rather than a fixed date.⁸ Merck's approval effectively hands it a multi-year head start. Novo Nordisk, building its own oral cardiometabolic pipeline around obesity and diabetes drugs, has no direct PCSK9 program and is exposed mostly by analogy — a reminder that the pill-versus-injection shift Novo has already ridden with Ozempic and Rybelsus is now playing out in a second disease category.

The quiet winner: insurers

The stock less obviously connected to the cholesterol pill may be the one with the most to gain: UnitedHealth. Its pharmacy-benefit arm, Optum Rx, generated $5.8 billion in profit last year — nearly a fifth of UnitedHealth's total operating earnings — largely by negotiating drug prices and rebates on behalf of health plans.⁹ A credible oral alternative at roughly $3,780 a year, versus $6,000-plus for injectables, gives Optum Rx and its peers new leverage: either push patients toward the cheaper pill, or use its existence as a bargaining chip to squeeze injectable-drug pricing down. Optum Rx has already said it will drop prior-authorization requirements for roughly 80 drugs, including cholesterol medications, a move that eases the path for whichever option costs the plan less.

That dynamic is easy to miss because it shows up in a payer's margin line, not a drugmaker's pipeline slide. But it's the same pattern insurers have exploited before: a cheaper new entrant doesn't just win share on its own merits — it becomes a lever against everything already on the shelf.

What to watch

Three things will decide how durable this reshuffling is. First, Merck's cardiovascular-outcomes trial, still enrolling past 14,500 patients, needs to confirm Lipfendra actually prevents heart attacks and strokes, not just LDL numbers on a lab report — until then, doctors treating higher-risk patients have reason to stick with Repatha's proven track record. Second, insurers' formulary decisions, still being finalized as of this approval, will determine how much of that price gap patients actually see. Third, AstraZeneca's AZD0780 outcomes trial, with no fixed completion date, will eventually test whether Merck's head start becomes a lasting monopoly on oral PCSK9 inhibition or just a two-to-three-year lead.
