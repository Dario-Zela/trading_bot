US oil rig count rises by seven, topping estimates

A horizontal drilling rig operating on a US shale pad. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US oil rigs rose to 452, a third straight weekly gain, but the move tracks an Iran-driven price spike more than a lasting shift in drilling plans.

The number of rigs drilling for oil in the United States rose by seven last week to 452, Baker Hughes reported Friday, beating the 446 that analysts had pencilled in and marking the third consecutive weekly increase. Rigs targeting natural gas held flat at 126. Add the two together, along with a handful of miscellaneous units, and the nationwide total reached 588, up from 581 the week before.

On its own, a seven-rig weekly move is noise — Baker Hughes counts bounce around for reasons as mundane as a crew finishing a pad early. But this one arrives on top of two other above-average gains: the count jumped 10 in the week to June 26, the largest single-week increase in four years, after adding seven more in the week before that. Three good weeks in a row is a different signal than one.

A slump that had further to run

Context matters here, because the rig count has done almost nothing but fall since late 2022. The domestic oil-and-gas count dropped from 750 rigs that December to roughly 517 by last October, according to the Energy Information Administration, a decline of 20% in 2023 alone, followed by further drops in 2024 and 2025. Producers kept output climbing anyway — Lower 48 crude hit a monthly record above 11.4 million barrels a day last July — by drilling longer laterals and concentrating rigs on their best acreage rather than by drilling more wells. A rising rig count, in other words, has been the exception this cycle, not the rule.

The recovery is also uneven. Nationally, oil-directed rigs are now above where they stood a year ago (422 then versus 452 now). But the Permian Basin, the country's largest shale field, has kept shrinking: its rig count was down roughly a fifth year-over-year as of January, and the region has shed drilling crews even as national totals stabilised. The jobs data tell a similar story — oil and gas production employment nationally is down about 4,000 since January, and oilfield-services headcount has fallen 39% from its 2016 peak.

Prices are doing the heavy lifting

The more immediate driver is geopolitics rather than a strategic pivot by drillers. Crude has jumped more than 14% over the past week as Israel-Iran hostilities entered a seventh night and the US moved to reinforce a naval presence near the Strait of Hormuz, sending West Texas Intermediate above $82 a barrel and Brent close to $85. That is a sharp premium over the price producers are actually planning around: the EIA's baseline forecast has WTI averaging just $51 in 2026, a level that would keep a lid on new drilling. Rig additions authorised now reflect decisions made when prices were already elevated — a lag of some weeks between price signal and rig deployment is normal — which makes it too early to say whether this is the start of a sustained build or a short-lived response to a war premium that could reverse as quickly as it appeared.

The next Baker Hughes count lands Friday, July 24, and will show whether the gain extends to a fourth straight week. Whether Permian activity turns up alongside the national count — or keeps lagging it — will say more about producer conviction than any single week's headline number.
