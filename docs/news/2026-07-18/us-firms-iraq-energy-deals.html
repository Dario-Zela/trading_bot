ConocoPhillips leads $60bn US energy push into Iraq, but risks loom

The Baba Gurgur gas flame near Kirkuk, part of the oilfield complex ConocoPhillips is buying a 42% stake in from BP. — Chad.r.hill / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
ConocoPhillips led $60bn in US-Iraq deals by buying into BP's Kirkuk oilfields, a bet Washington has tied to a September 30 militia-disarmament deadline.

Dozens of American companies signed more than $60 billion in deals with Iraq on Friday, and the headline transaction came from ConocoPhillips: a agreement to buy 42% of BP's Kirkuk operations, putting the Houston-based producer back into one of the oldest oilfields in the Middle East for the first time in over a decade. The stock rose roughly 2% on the news, a notable gain on a day the S&P 500 slipped about 1%.

The deal signing, at a U.S. Chamber of Commerce summit timed to Iraqi Prime Minister Ali al-Zaidi's first visit to Washington, was less a single transaction than a coordinated rollout — more than 50 agreements spanning energy, healthcare, finance, technology and infrastructure, arriving the same week Iraq and Syria signed a deal to rebuild a pipeline that would let Iraqi crude bypass the Strait of Hormuz entirely. Read individually, these are corporate press releases. Read together, they describe an American attempt to buy influence in a country that has spent two decades drifting between Washington and Tehran.

The Kirkuk trade

ConocoPhillips' stake covers BP Energy Company of Kirkuk Limited, which holds the development contract for the Baba and Avanah domes of the Kirkuk field along with three adjacent fields — Bai Hassan, Jambur and Khabbaz. Combined, the assets hold more than 3 billion barrels of oil equivalent in gross recoverable resources, according to the company, with further exploration upside. CEO Ryan Lance called it access to "a material, high-quality and long-life resource base" that fits the company's capital-allocation criteria — corporate-speak for a large, already-producing field that doesn't require years of exploration risk before it pays out.

The transaction is expected to close by year-end, pending regulatory approval, and no purchase price has been disclosed. What is public is the ambition: partners in the venture are targeting a redevelopment effort reportedly running into the tens of billions of dollars to rehabilitate fields that have suffered decades of underinvestment, war damage and, more recently, Islamic State disruption. Chevron is separately negotiating exclusive rights to West Qurna 2, a southern field already producing 480,000 barrels a day — evidence that ConocoPhillips isn't the only major placing a bet that Iraq's oil patch, not just its politics, is investable again.

Deals as diplomacy

The commercial signings followed a White House meeting three days earlier at which Trump told al-Zaidi "we're going to be doing a lot of deals" and predicted the 41-year-old prime minister — Iraq's youngest — would become an influential regional figure "beyond Iraq." Al-Zaidi, for his part, framed the summit as "not just transactions but strategic co-operation." Behind the diplomatic language sits a concrete infrastructure play: the revived Kirkuk–Baniyas pipeline to Syria's Mediterranean coast, dormant since it was damaged during the 2003 invasion, would give Iraq roughly 2 million barrels a day of export capacity that never has to transit the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint Iran has repeatedly threatened to close.

Rebuilding an 800-kilometre pipeline that has sat idle for two decades is not simply an engineering exercise. Estimates for the work range from $4.5 billion to $8 billion, with a two-to-three-year timeline, and security along the route is thin — ISIS cells remain active in parts of the corridor the pipeline would cross.

What could go wrong

The deals arrive with an explicit political condition attached, and it is the one that matters most for how durable any of this proves to be. Washington has tied its economic re-engagement to Iraq disarming Iran-backed militias by September 30 — the same date the last U.S. troops are due to leave the country. "On the 30th of September the U.S. forces will leave Iraq while American companies will enter Iraq," al-Zaidi said, explicitly linking the two timelines. Analysts covering the militias are skeptical the deadline is achievable: these groups, organized under the Popular Mobilization Forces umbrella, have previously moved to derail foreign contracts they viewed as working against Tehran's interests, including an earlier attempt to torpedo a South Korean-awarded deal in favor of a Chinese bidder.

There is a narrower, more immediate risk too. The Kirkuk fields sit inside a decades-old jurisdictional dispute between Baghdad and the Kurdistan Regional Government over revenue-sharing and control of export infrastructure — the reason Baghdad is reviving a direct Kirkuk–Ceyhan link that bypasses KRG territory rather than negotiating through it. And ConocoPhillips is entering as a minority partner in someone else's operation: it holds 42%, not control, and how the working relationship with BP unfolds in practice — an operational detail no analyst can yet answer — will shape whether the resource translates into barrels on schedule.

What to watch

The two dates that matter sit five days apart at the end of September: the U.S. troop withdrawal and Iraq's militia-disarmament deadline. If Baghdad visibly fails to meet the latter, the political cover underpinning this week's $60 billion in pledges gets harder to sustain, regardless of how attractive the geology looks on a resource map. Closer to home for investors, the Kirkuk transaction's expected year-end close is the next hard checkpoint — and the first test of whether Friday's number translates into actual barrels rather than another round of memoranda of understanding.
