NeoGenomics wins FDA nod for prostate cancer companion test

In one sentence
NeoGenomics won FDA clearance to run a companion test for AstraZeneca's TRUQAP, but the widely cited 'undervalued' claim rests on a 3% fair-value gap, not the approval itself.

NeoGenomics said on July 9 that it had launched PTEN IHC CDx, a companion diagnostic that flags which prostate cancer patients qualify for AstraZeneca's newly approved drug TRUQAP. The test itself is real, the approval is real, and the clinical need it fills is narrow but genuine. What is less clear is why that should make NEO shares a bargain — a claim now circulating in financial media that the underlying facts don't quite support.

The diagnostic detects loss of the PTEN protein in prostate adenocarcinoma tissue, using the Ventana PTEN (SP218) RxDx Assay, and returns results in one to two days as a standalone order or bundled into NeoGenomics' broader NEO PanTracer Pro panel. PTEN loss shows up in roughly one in four men with metastatic, hormone-sensitive prostate cancer — a population NeoGenomics estimates at about 8,750 patients a year in the US — and it marks a more aggressive form of the disease with historically poor outcomes.

What the FDA actually approved

The regulatory chain here matters. On June 12, the FDA approved capivasertib (TRUQAP), AstraZeneca's AKT inhibitor, in combination with abiraterone and prednisone for PTEN-deficient prostate cancer that has not yet progressed on hormone therapy — the first targeted option for that specific subgroup. The approval rested on the CAPItello-281 trial, which followed 1,012 patients and showed the combination outperformed abiraterone alone. Because the drug label requires confirming PTEN-deficient status, the FDA authorized the Ventana assay as the companion diagnostic at the same time. NeoGenomics did not invent the test or win a novel FDA nod on its own; it built the commercial and laboratory infrastructure — national lab network, fast turnaround, pathologist review — to run an already-authorized assay at scale, then folded it into its existing oncology testing menu.

The undervaluation claim doesn't hold up

A Simply Wall St model, circulated by Yahoo Finance, put NeoGenomics' fair value at $15.06 a share against a recent close of $14.61 — a gap of roughly 3%, essentially in line with the average analyst target rather than meaningfully below it. On a different valuation cut, the same publisher found NEO trading at 2.6 times sales, versus a healthcare-industry average of 1.5 times and a peer average of 1.1 times, and noted the stock currently fails all six of Simply Wall St's own valuation checks. Shares have already risen 134% over the past year and 82% in the past 90 days alone, well ahead of the July 9 announcement — momentum that looks driven by broader enthusiasm for NeoGenomics' next-generation sequencing volumes and improving margins, not by this one diagnostic launch.

None of the coverage of the PTEN test offers a revenue estimate for it, a reimbursement rate, or a read on competing labs that could run the same FDA-authorized assay. NeoGenomics' own press materials are silent on dollar figures. An FDA approval that expands a testing menu is not nothing — companion diagnostics tend to carry durable, high-margin volume once adopted into clinical guidelines — but treating it as evidence the stock is cheap skips the steps that would actually establish that: how many of the roughly 8,750 eligible men a year get tested through NeoGenomics rather than a hospital's in-house lab or a rival reference lab, and at what price.

NeoGenomics reports second-quarter results after markets close on July 28, which will be the first chance to see whether PanTracer and companion-diagnostic volumes are translating into the revenue growth its share price already assumes. The company guided in April to full-year revenue of $797 million to $803 million, up from $187 million in the first quarter alone; that report, not the diagnostic launch, is the number that will tell shareholders whether the current multiple is earned.
