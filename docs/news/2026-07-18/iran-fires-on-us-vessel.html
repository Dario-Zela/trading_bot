IRAN-ISRAEL ESCALATION · By C. Tanaka · 2026-07-18 · 6 min read

Iran fires on US vessel as strikes on Iran continue into a seventh night

USS Abraham Lincoln underway; a U.S. carrier group has been operating in the wider Indian Ocean theatre amid the escalating conflict. — U.S. Navy / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Iran says it fired a cruise missile at a US vessel in the Indian Ocean as US strikes hit Iran for a 7th straight night and Washington resupplies tankers to Israel.

The war between the United States and Iran, which most Washington officials insisted only weeks ago was winding toward a ceasefire, is now in its second week of nightly bombardment with no sign of an off-ramp. On Friday, Iran's army said its navy had fired a shore-to-sea cruise missile at a "hostile" American vessel far out in the northern Indian Ocean — even as U.S. Central Command confirmed it had struck Iranian territory for a seventh consecutive night. Behind both moves, a quieter signal has emerged from Axios: Washington is sending dozens more refueling tankers to Israel, restoring the logistics footprint it had at the war's outset. Taken together, the three threads point the same direction — this is no longer a spasm of retaliatory strikes. It is a sustained campaign that both sides are now provisioning for.

A missile fired at the edge of the map

Iran's Army Public Relations office said Thursday that naval forces had launched a coast-to-sea cruise missile at an American vessel operating in the northern Indian Ocean, describing it as part of the thirteenth phase of an operation Tehran has branded "Sa'eqeh," or Lightning. According to state media, the strike "forced the hostile vessel to move beyond the engagement range" of Iranian forces and sowed "fear and panic" among U.S. personnel — a characterization that could not be independently verified and that CENTCOM did not confirm. The U.S. military has previously declined to confirm or deny Iranian claims of missile fire toward its ships in the region, including similar claimed attacks on the carrier USS Abraham Lincoln earlier this year, while insisting its vessels remain able to carry out operations.

What makes the northern Indian Ocean strike notable is geography. It sits well beyond the Strait of Hormuz and the Gulf waters where most of the fighting has concentrated, suggesting the reach of Iran's coastal missile batteries — or Tehran's willingness to claim that reach — is expanding rather than narrowing under the pressure of a week of American bombing. The claim came alongside a wider burst of Iranian retaliation the same night: the IRGC said it struck Al Udeid Air Base in Qatar in the sixteenth wave of a separate campaign it calls Operation Nasr 2, hit U.S. positions in Kuwait, targeted American aircraft at bases in Jordan, and struck radar sites in Oman and Syria, where it claimed — without evidence CENTCOM would corroborate — to have captured U.S. personnel near the Al-Tanf garrison. CENTCOM's own statement was blunt: "No U.S. troops in the region have recently been killed or captured."

Seven nights and counting

While Iran was framing its own retaliation, U.S. Central Command posted on X at 3 p.m. ET Friday that it had launched a fresh round of strikes against Iran — the seventh consecutive night of bombing. CENTCOM said the strikes were "designed to continue degrading Iranian military capabilities at the Commander in Chief's direction," and that this round hit surveillance sites, military logistics infrastructure, underground weapons storage and maritime capabilities using fighter jets, drones and warships. Targets reportedly included Jask, Sirik, Bushehr, Bandar Abbas, Qeshm Island, Ahvaz and Yazd — a spread that runs from the Strait of Hormuz coastline deep into Iran's interior.

The run of consecutive nights matters more than any single night's target list. A ceasefire brokered after an earlier round of U.S.-Israeli strikes broke down roughly ten days ago, after Iran began attacking merchant shipping trying to transit Hormuz and Washington accused Tehran of violating the truce. President Trump has since called further talks "a waste of time" and reinstated an oil blockade with a 20 percent toll on cargo through the strait. What began as a return to hostilities has since calcified into something closer to routine: a nightly bombing schedule, an Iranian retaliatory wave, and a fresh CENTCOM statement each afternoon confirming it happened again. Ship traffic through Hormuz — normally 18 to 22 vessels a day — has fallen to single digits on the worst nights, and Brent crude has climbed above $85 a barrel, its highest level since mid-June, as traders price in a conflict that shows no sign of a natural endpoint.

Washington restocks for a longer fight

The clearest evidence that U.S. planners expect this to run longer still comes from logistics rather than ordnance. Axios reported Thursday that the Trump administration has told Israel it will send dozens more aerial refueling tankers to the country, according to U.S. and Israeli officials, ahead of what several described as a possible expansion of the campaign. The U.S. currently has roughly 30 KC-135 and KC-46 tankers at Ben Gurion International Airport near Tel Aviv and a similar number at Ramon Airport in the south; the new deployment would restore tanker numbers to where they stood at the war's outset, when the total ran closer to 60 to 70 aircraft.

Tankers are not a subtle signal. They exist to extend the range and loiter time of strike aircraft, and massing dozens of them at Israeli airfields is the kind of preparation that precedes sustained or widened air campaigns, not a one-off raid. Axios reported that Trump was presented with several expanded military options in a Situation Room meeting this week, including strikes on Iranian power infrastructure and further attacks aimed at burying Iran's enriched uranium stockpile more deeply underground — options that would require exactly the kind of extended sortie generation the tanker buildup enables. The timing has domestic friction in Israel, too: the aircraft parked at Ben Gurion for months earlier this year had already clogged the airport, and officials have warned a fresh wave could force cancellations just as Israeli summer travel picks up.

Reading the three signals together

Each piece of Friday's news is, on its own, a data point. Together they describe a war settling into a shape rather than escalating toward a climax or fading toward a truce. Iran is demonstrating reach far outside the Gulf and threatening to widen its target set to include the regional assets of any company with American shareholders if strikes on infrastructure continue. The U.S. is treating its own bombing campaign as an open-ended degradation effort rather than a punitive burst, evidenced by the plain fact that CENTCOM is now numbering the nights. And Washington's own force posture — restocking tankers to wartime levels — suggests military planners are not betting on this ending soon.

For markets, the practical question is whether a geopolitical risk premium built for a short, sharp conflict is adequate for one entering its second week with an expanding logistics tail behind it. Brent's climb past $85 has already begun to answer that question, but the tanker buildup implies the strait's shipping disruption — and the associated price support — is likelier to persist than resolve in the coming days. The next marker to watch is whether Trump acts on the wider strike options reportedly discussed this week; a move against Iranian power infrastructure or deeper underground nuclear sites would mark the campaign's next real escalation, rather than its eighth repetition.
