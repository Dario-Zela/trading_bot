Gilead's HIV franchise faces rising competition as Biktarvy patents near expiry

Gilead Sciences' headquarters campus in Foster City, California. — Suiren2022 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Gilead bought Biktarvy three more years of U.S. patent protection, but Merck's newly approved rival pill and its own unproven pipeline will decide what happens after.

Gilead Sciences built its market value on a single molecule combination: Biktarvy, the once-daily HIV pill that has anchored the company's earnings since 2018. For years, the bull case was straightforward — protected exclusivity, minimal switching, near-monopoly pricing power in a chronic-disease market where patients rarely change what works. That protection now looks less permanent than investors assumed, even as Gilead has spent the past year buying itself more time and building the arsenal to replace what it eventually loses.

Biktarvy generated $14.3 billion in sales in 2025, up 7% on the year, and accounted for roughly two-thirds of Gilead's $20.8 billion in total HIV product revenue — itself about 70% of the company's $29.4 billion in total revenue.[^1] That concentration is the whole story: when a drug this size faces a patent cliff, it doesn't dent an income statement, it reshapes one.

The cliff, delayed but not gone

Gilead had projected Biktarvy's core U.S. exclusivity would lapse around December 2033. Over the past year the company reached settlements with three generic manufacturers — Lupin, Cipla and Laurus Labs — that push back the earliest possible U.S. generic entry to April 2036, according to Wells Fargo's read of the filings.[^2] European exclusivity still runs out earlier, around 2033, meaning Gilead's European Biktarvy revenue will likely erode first while the much larger U.S. franchise holds longer.

Settlements of this kind are standard pharma practice — trading a guaranteed, dated entry window for the drugmaker's promise not to keep litigating — but they only delay the mechanics of the cliff. Generic manufacturers still get a fixed date, and Wall Street models still discount future Biktarvy cash flows against it. The extension buys Gilead roughly three additional years to prove its next act works, not indefinite protection.

Competition is arriving before the patent does

The bigger near-term threat isn't generics — it's branded rivals. Merck won FDA approval in April for Idvynso, a combination of doravirine and islatravir, which became available in pharmacies in May.[^3] Clinical trial data presented at the CROI 2026 conference showed the pill was non-inferior to Biktarvy in previously untreated patients, with no reported cases of the lactic acidosis or hepatic steatosis occasionally linked to Biktarvy-class regimens — a tolerability pitch aimed squarely at patients weighing whether to switch.[^4] GlobalData projects Idvynso could reach roughly $1.8 billion in sales by 2033, positioning it against both Biktarvy and GSK's ViiV-marketed Dovato.[^4] Separately, ViiV's maturation inhibitor GSK3640254 remains in Phase II development, aimed at the same suppressed-patient switch market.

The pattern across the field is a shift toward two-drug and metabolically gentler regimens — a direct answer to the three-drug, once-daily formula that made Biktarvy dominant in the first place.

Gilead's answer: lenacapavir and a faster pipeline

Gilead's counter is to obsolete its own franchise before rivals do it for them. The FDA granted priority review in April to a new once-daily combination of bictegravir and lenacapavir, with a decision due August 27, 2026; Phase 3 data from the ARTISTRY trials showed efficacy comparable to Biktarvy in patients switching regimens.[^5] More significant is Yeztugo, Gilead's twice-yearly injectable for HIV prevention, which grew sales 72% sequentially in the first quarter and which the company expects to generate $1 billion in 2026 — a figure that would make it one of the fastest drug launches in company history.[^5][^6] An oral, once-weekly version of Yeztugo is also under FDA review, with a decision expected in February 2027.

HSBC upgraded Gilead to Buy on July 6, citing a more constructive view of the HIV outlook after the stock's pullback; shares were changing hands near $134 on July 17, up modestly on the year but still trading around 15 times forward earnings — below the company's five-year average, according to analysts who argue the market hasn't yet priced in the pipeline replacing Biktarvy's eventual decline.[^6]

What to watch

The August 27 PDUFA decision on the bictegravir-lenacapavir combination is the next concrete catalyst: approval would give Gilead a next-generation product to defend its suppressed-patient base before Merck's Idvynso or ViiV's pipeline pulls meaningful share. Longer term, the real test is whether Yeztugo's prevention revenue and lenacapavir-based treatment regimens can outgrow Biktarvy's decline before 2036 arrives. For now, Gilead has purchased time with its generic settlements; whether it has purchased enough depends on execution the market has not yet fully rewarded.
