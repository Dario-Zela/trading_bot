Sainsbury's banking retreat underlines UK lenders' grip on the high street

In one sentence
Sainsbury's has surrendered its banking licence for a NatWest partnership, following Tesco-Barclays and M&S-HSBC into the same retailer-plus-incumbent model.

Sainsbury's has formally given up its UK banking licence. As of 1 July 2026, the company's financial arm trades as Sainsbury's Money — a brand sitting on top of products built, funded and risk-managed by someone else. That someone else is NatWest, which bought Sainsbury's Bank's credit card, personal loan and deposit portfolios last year and, on 1 April 2026, agreed to extend the relationship with a new NatWest-branded Nectar credit card, instant-access savings accounts and unsecured personal loans, due to launch in the second half of this year.

It is not an isolated retreat. Tesco sold its banking business to Barclays for roughly £600m in a deal that completed in November 2024, alongside a ten-year exclusive agreement to keep credit cards, loans and deposits under the Tesco Bank brand while Barclays runs the infrastructure. Marks & Spencer struck a seven-year arrangement with HSBC in 2024, with the legal transfer of M&S Bank completing on 1 June 2026. Three of Britain's biggest supermarket-adjacent banking ventures have now converged on the same structure inside 20 months: retailer keeps the shopfront and the loyalty points, incumbent bank keeps the balance sheet.

Why the model stopped paying

Sainsbury's took its first banking licence in 1997, launched as a joint venture with Bank of Scotland, then bought the business outright from Lloyds Banking Group for £248m in 2014. That full-service ambition has been unwound in stages since a phased exit was announced in January 2024 — ATM operations went to NoteMachine, Argos card portfolios to NewDay, travel money to Fexco, and now the core banking book to NatWest.

Retail Banker International's analysis of the wind-down points to the obvious culprits: tighter capital and regulatory requirements have made standalone balance-sheet banking an expensive undertaking for a business whose actual profit engine is groceries, and competition from the established players has eroded any margin case for keeping it in-house. Solange Chamberlain, NatWest's retail banking chief, framed the new deal as a distribution play — NatWest already serves more than 20 million customers, or roughly one in three UK families, and gets access to Sainsbury's near-600 supermarkets and 800-plus convenience stores through the arrangement. Sainsbury's financial services managing director Patrick Short called NatWest's "deep expertise" the reason to extend rather than exit the relationship entirely.

The competitive read

The pattern cuts against the post-2008 narrative that supermarket banks and fintech challengers would erode incumbent market share. Instead, Barclays, HSBC and NatWest have absorbed the deposit books and, more importantly, locked in long-dated exclusive distribution through loyalty schemes that any standalone challenger would need years to replicate. RBI's analysts flag the downstream effect: as more retail brands sign over their banking infrastructure to the majors, digital challengers face a shrinking pool of scale opportunities to compete for.

The next marker to watch is whether Sainsbury's Money's NatWest products launch on schedule in the second half of 2026, and whether any remaining independent retail banking ventures — Marks & Spencer's now-settled HSBC tie-up aside — follow the same script before the year is out.
