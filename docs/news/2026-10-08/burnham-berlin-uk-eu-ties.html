Burnham holds security talks in Berlin as a French minister urges the UK to 'come all the way' back into the EU

In one sentence
Burnham is due in Berlin for security talks with Merz while a French minister urges Britain back into the EU, but no outcome has yet been published for either.

Andy Burnham is due in Berlin for security talks with German Chancellor Friedrich Merz. The visit comes as Gulf tensions and the Russia talks crowd the European agenda. At the same time, a French finance minister has said Britain should "come all the way" back into the European Union, according to BBC UK. The two items are separate, but they feed one question: how quickly is London's relationship with the continent changing under its new prime minister?

The Berlin visit

Burnham took office on 20 July, and the Berlin trip is reported to be his first sit-down with Merz as prime minister. At the time of writing, no readout, joint statement or agreed list of topics had been published. The date and length of the meeting have not been confirmed, and nothing has been announced on the Gulf or Russia. This is therefore a preview rather than a report on a result.

The talks sit on firm ground. In July 2025 London and Berlin signed the Kensington Treaty, which set out plans for closer cooperation on defence and security, the economy, transport and migration. Burnham inherits that framework rather than having to build one. The test of this visit is whether it adds anything concrete, such as a shared line on Russia, coordinated steps on Gulf shipping and energy security, or new defence-industrial work. A warm photograph and a statement of "shared values" would show little.

The Gulf matters because of what is happening there. Reported strikes on a tanker and on Saudi airports have raised the stakes for both governments, and the EU has said it is sticking to the International Energy Agency framework on emergency stock releases.

Paris and the 'all the way' remark

The French comment is harder to weigh. The BBC headline attributes it to France's finance minister, but the triage notes do not give the minister's name, the setting or the date, and the underlying article could not be checked. It is unclear whether this was a considered policy position or an aside in an interview. French officials have usually been cautious on the idea that Britain could rejoin the single market or the EU. They have generally treated any such move as a matter for London to initiate.

Burnham has already signalled which way he leans. In late August he told European Council President Antonio Costa that Britain "had to be bolder" in seeking closer ties with the bloc, according to a Downing Street spokeswoman. The two agreed that a UK-EU summit later this year would be a good platform for that work. A French call to go "all the way" is a long way beyond that cautious framing, and Downing Street has so far set no goal of rejoining.

What to watch

The first marker is whether Downing Street and the German government publish a readout, and whether it names Russia or the Gulf specifically. The second is the UK-EU summit, where talk of a closer relationship would have to turn into agreed text on defence, trade or both. Until then, the French remark counts as encouragement rather than commitment.
