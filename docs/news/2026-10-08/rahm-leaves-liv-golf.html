Rahm to leave LIV Golf over 'unacceptable' terms

Jon Rahm at the 2025 Ryder Cup. — Bryan Berlin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Jon Rahm's lawyer told a US bankruptcy court that LIV 2.0's terms are 'unacceptable', a blow to a Saudi-backed league already in Chapter 11 and losing its stars.

Jon Rahm, the Spaniard who became LIV Golf's biggest individual signing, will not take part in the league's planned relaunch. His lawyer told a US bankruptcy court that the terms on offer are "unacceptable". It is a departure from a league that no longer has the Saudi money to make such exits look like a choice.

What was said in court

Attorney John Beck made the statement at a bankruptcy hearing on Wednesday. Rahm, he said, "has independently reviewed the proposed terms of LIV 2.0 … and has determined that those terms are unacceptable to him, and he will not be participating going forward in LIV 2.0."

The statement came from counsel in open court, not from Rahm himself, and the specific objections have not been made public. The only detail of the relaunch model reported so far is that players would become equity owners in both LIV Golf and its teams. Whether Rahm objects to the equity structure, the money or something else is not known.

The exit is also not yet final. Beck said Rahm and LIV are working on a separation agreement, with 15 October reported as the target date and a court hearing on 5 November the hope for approval. Until that is signed, "leaving" describes intent more than a completed fact. Rahm, 31, has said he is willing to honour his existing contract, and his plans beyond it are unclear.

A league in Chapter 11

The backdrop explains why this matters more than a usual player-contract spat. Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund said in April that it would end its backing after the 2026 season, having put in more than $5bn over four years. LIV scaled back, laid off most of its staff and filed for Chapter 11 protection in New Jersey in September. The PIF has agreed to provide $49.6m in financing during the bankruptcy, and LIV said this week it had secured up to $300m from BC Partners to help it emerge and prepare for 2027, subject to court approval.

Rahm joined in late 2023, about eight months after winning the Masters, in a deal reported at figures from $300m to more than $400m. The figures were never official. He went on to win three consecutive season-long individual titles. Reports put what he is owed for the rest of the contract at up to $150m, though that number is reported rather than confirmed. Separately, his $7.5m claim as an unsecured creditor is the largest among the players listed.

He was not freed in the way others were. Sergio Garcia was among the players who persuaded the court to lift the automatic stay on their contracts, and LIV has agreed to terminate his deal. Attorneys for Bryson DeChambeau, Cameron Smith, An Byeong-hun, Marc Leishman, Cameron Tringale and Matthew Wolff have asked the judge for permission to terminate theirs. Those requests do not necessarily rule out joining LIV 2.0, and sources say participation talks remain on track.

What it means for the standoff

For two years the question hanging over golf's divide has been whether LIV's money could hold its best players. The money has now been withdrawn, and the argument has changed. LIV's claim to be a serious rival to the PGA Tour rested on a roster of stars who had been paid enough to stay away. Losing Rahm, one of its two biggest names alongside DeChambeau, would remove one of the pillars of that claim, whatever the equity-ownership pitch for the relaunch.

Rahm has not rushed to the Tour. He declined the PGA Tour's Returning Member Program, which was also offered to Smith, DeChambeau and Brooks Koepka; Koepka was the only one to accept. Taking the route would have meant a mandatory one-year suspension, and Rahm could not have played on the PGA Tour until late August next year. In May he settled his dispute with the DP World Tour, paying about £2.21m in fines and securing conditional releases for 2026, which kept him eligible for the Ryder Cup. His options are therefore open on the European side and narrower in America.

The next markers are concrete. The separation agreement is due by 15 October, and the bankruptcy court is due to consider it and the players' termination requests on 5 November. Those filings should show what Rahm is giving up, what he is paid and whether the other stars follow him out. They should also show whether LIV 2.0 has anyone left to sell the equity to.
