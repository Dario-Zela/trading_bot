Two men serving indefinite IPP sentences freed after two decades

In one sentence
Two men held about two decades on indefinite IPP sentences, despite minimum terms of two years or less, were freed after a Crown Court replaced their sentences on a CCRC referral.

Two men who have each spent about two decades in custody under indefinite sentences for public protection have been freed after a court cancelled those sentences. Each had been given a minimum term of two years or less.

The Guardian reported on 7 October that the two, named as Bennett and Ings, are both 38. They were 17 when they committed the offences that led to their sentences, which were imposed in 2007 and 2006. Their minimum terms were 24 months and 21 months. A high court judge and a magistrate sitting at Wolverhampton crown court replaced the indeterminate sentences. Relatives of the men were reported to have hugged in the public gallery as the judge said the sentences had been "cancelled".

A sentence with no end date

Imprisonment for Public Protection was created by the Criminal Justice Act 2003 and took effect in April 2005. It allowed judges to impose an open-ended sentence on offenders judged dangerous, with a minimum term, known as the tariff, after which release depended on the Parole Board. In practice, many prisoners with short tariffs could not show they were safe enough to be released. Many could not complete the courses and assessments that would have supported that case.

Parliament abolished the sentence for new cases in 2012, through the Legal Aid, Sentencing and Punishment of Offenders Act. Abolition was not retrospective. Everyone already sentenced stayed under IPP rules, so a sentence scrapped 14 years ago is still being served.

The replacement terms

The case reached the Crown Court on a referral from the Criminal Cases Review Commission, which sent the sentences of three men back to be reconsidered. The original judges sat in Wolverhampton, Leicester and Reading.

The court set replacement terms of four years and 264 days for Bennett, and 42 months with a 30-month extended licence for Ings. A third man, Ward, was released in 2017 after serving a decade beyond his one-year minimum, and was later recalled to prison. His replacement term was two years. He was in the public gallery to see his original sentence replaced.

The replacement terms show how far the original sentences ran past what a determinate sentence would have meant. Neither man's new term is as long as the time already served.

The wider picture

The cases are unusual in how they were resolved. Most IPP prisoners have no such route and depend on the Parole Board. The Commission has made other referrals of this kind. In one, it noted two men given minimum terms of four and a half and two and a half years two decades ago, one of whom is still in custody.

Official figures show the population is shrinking only slowly. Lord Timpson, the justice minister, told the Justice Committee in May that 2,329 people serving IPP sentences were in prison on 31 March 2026. Of those, 896 had never been released, down 11% on a year earlier. The other 1,433 had been recalled after release, down 6%. Later Ministry of Justice figures cited in an August report put the number at 856 on 30 June, with 1,415 recalled. That report does not make clear whether the 856 is the never-released group, so the two sets of figures are not directly comparable.

Recall is a feature of the system, not an exception. A large share of those in prison under IPP are people who were released and sent back, often for breaching licence conditions rather than for a new offence.

What to watch

The next test is whether other referrals produce similar outcomes. The Commission is still reviewing IPP cases, and each one that reaches the Crown Court tests whether short tariffs can be reopened. The next Ministry of Justice offender management statistics will show whether the never-released population keeps falling at roughly a tenth a year.
