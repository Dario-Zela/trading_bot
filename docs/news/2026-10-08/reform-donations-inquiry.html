Reform's internal inquiry clears the party over donations while police inquiries continue

In one sentence
Reform's own inquiry says the party broke no donation law, but police inquiries into the same matter continue, leaving the review's independence in question.

A political party has examined its own conduct over a donations controversy and concluded that it did nothing wrong. Police have not reached that conclusion. Reform's internal inquiry has found that the party did not break donation law, clearing its own officials over the donations sting, according to reports from the Guardian and the BBC. Police inquiries into the same matter are continuing.

The two processes answer different questions and carry different weight. The party's review is a private exercise. Whoever runs it, it is commissioned by the organisation under scrutiny and answers to it. A police inquiry is a statutory process with powers to compel evidence, and it ends only when officers and prosecutors decide it should. A clean bill of health from the first does not settle the second.

What the inquiry found

The reported outcome is simple: the party, and the officials caught up in the donations episode, did not breach donation law. The detail behind it is thin in the public record. The published accounts reviewed for this article do not set out the inquiry's terms of reference, who conducted it, what evidence it examined or whether anyone outside the party took part. Those omissions matter because the credibility of a self-run review rests almost entirely on its design. A review led by an independent figure with access to documents and the freedom to publish what they find is a different thing from one led by party insiders reporting to the leadership.

The finding also lands before the police work is done. Closing an internal file while a criminal inquiry is open invites an obvious question about timing, and about whether the party is seeking to shape the public reading of the case before investigators have spoken. Nothing in the reporting establishes that this was the intention. The sequence nonetheless leaves the party open to the charge.

Why the order of events matters

Donation rules in Britain are policed by more than one body. The Electoral Commission regulates party finance, and the police can investigate suspected criminal offences. A party that declares itself in the clear is making a claim about law that others are charged with testing. If investigators later reach a different view, the party will have to explain a public exoneration that did not survive contact with the evidence. If they reach the same view, the internal inquiry will look redundant at best.

There is a wider point as well. Parties facing donation questions have an obvious incentive to commission their own reviews, since these are quick, controllable and can be presented as accountability. How much weight regulators and prosecutors give such reviews, and whether they treat them as evidence, context or noise, will shape how parties behave in future cases. A review that is seen to have pre-empted an open inquiry makes the case for requiring independence as the price of credibility.

What to watch

Three things will show how far the finding holds. The first is whether Reform publishes the inquiry's terms of reference and its conclusions in full, rather than a summary of the result. The second is whether the police say anything about the status of their own inquiries, including whether they have received or requested the party's material. The third is whether the Electoral Commission comments on the matter. Until at least one of these happens, the party's clearance is an assertion by the party about itself, set against an investigation that remains open.
