Badenoch pledges to scrap inheritance tax on family homes as Tories seek to turn unity into votes

In one sentence
Badenoch's pledge to scrap inheritance tax on family homes courts core voters, but with gilt yields high before the Budget it invites hard questions about cost and funding.

The Conservative Party has promised to scrap inheritance tax on family homes. Kemi Badenoch, its leader, set out the pledge as the party tries to win back the core voters it lost, according to BBC Politics and Guardian Politics. The announcement comes in the weeks before a Budget, with long-dated government borrowing costs already elevated. That timing gives the policy a second audience: gilt investors as well as voters.

A pledge aimed at the base

Inheritance tax is among the most disliked levies in British politics, and the Conservatives have long cast themselves as the party that would cut it. Targeting the family home speaks directly to older, property-owning voters in the shires and suburbs, the group that has drifted towards Reform UK since the last general election.

The pledge is as much about positioning as about policy detail. Full costings and the design of any relief were not available at the time of writing. The party has set a direction, and the arithmetic will be argued over later.

Critics say the policy favours wealthier households. That follows from how the tax works: it falls on estates above a threshold, and the families it reaches are disproportionately those holding substantial property or other assets. A cut aimed at the family home would therefore deliver its largest benefit to those households. The argument over who gains is likely to define the early debate. Supporters will frame the measure as relief for ordinary families whose main asset is a house that has risen in value. Opponents will frame it as a giveaway to the already comfortable.

United, but not yet winning

The pledge arrives as the party's internal mood has changed. A BBC Politics analysis describes the Conservatives as more settled under Badenoch, with the open feuding that marked earlier phases of her leadership largely absent. That calm has not yet translated into support, however. The same analysis points to weak Conservative polling against Reform UK. No specific poll figures were available to this reporter, and none are cited here.

That gap explains the choice of policy. A party that is united but still trailing needs a message that cuts through, and a tax cut with a clear beneficiary is a familiar way to offer one. It also carries risk. A policy that appeals to the base but is read by others as aimed at the wealthy can reinforce an old caricature of the party, one its leadership has said it wants to shed.

The gilt market is listening

The fiscal backdrop is what turns a campaign pledge into a test of credibility. Elsewhere in this edition, the 30-year gilt yield is reported to be holding above 6% ahead of the Budget, with the Bank of England facing pressure over a possible November rate rise. Weakness in US Treasuries has added to the strain on global long-dated debt. In that setting, any unfunded or loosely funded tax cut invites scrutiny from investors who price the risk of extra borrowing directly into yields.

A Conservative opposition does not set policy, and its promise has no direct effect on the Treasury's plans. But it shapes the argument the government must win. Ministers are weighing how much room they have to manage the public finances while borrowing costs are high. An opposition offering a large tax cut, without a stated way to pay for it, sharpens the contrast. It also leaves the Conservatives exposed to the question that has followed fiscal events in recent years: where does the money come from?

The party's own history makes that question harder. Many voters and investors associate the Conservatives with the 2022 mini-budget, when unfunded tax cuts were followed by a violent move in gilt markets. Any pledge involving a costly tax reduction invites comparison with that episode. How the party answers will matter as much as the pledge. Credible offsetting savings, or a phased timetable, would blunt the comparison. A bare promise would not.

What to watch

The next test is the Budget itself. Chancellor Rachel Reeves's statement will show how much fiscal headroom the government believes it has, and how the gilt market reacts will indicate how much tolerance there is for borrowing-funded measures of any kind. The Conservatives will then have to say what their inheritance-tax pledge would cost, who would benefit and how it would be paid for. Until they do, it remains an electoral signal rather than a costed policy. Whether it moves voters from Reform, or only reminds the markets of past episodes, will not be clear until the party publishes those details and the polls respond.
