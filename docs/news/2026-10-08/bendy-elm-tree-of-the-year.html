Bendy elm crowned UK tree of the year

In one sentence
A leaning wych elm in Newcastle, County Down, that outlived Dutch elm disease has won the Woodland Trust's UK Tree of the Year, the first from Northern Ireland to do so.

A wych elm that leans out over the seafront at Newcastle, County Down, has been named the Woodland Trust's UK Tree of the Year for 2026. It is known locally, and now nationally, as the Bendy Tree. Its trunk runs almost horizontal, the shape of a tree that has spent a century and a half arguing with the wind off the Irish Sea.

It is the first Northern Ireland tree to win the title, according to reports of the result. It took about 30% of the public vote in a poll that ran from July to September, with tens of thousands of people choosing from a shortlist of ten. This year's theme was "Against all odds".

The last one standing

The tree is more than 150 years old. It was planted in the mid-19th century as part of a row of wych elms along the town's original promenade. Many of its neighbours were felled during redevelopment in the 1980s, leaving it as the last of the line.

The elms that disappeared elsewhere went for a different reason. Dutch elm disease, a fungus spread by bark beetles, reached Britain in a second and far more aggressive outbreak after it was accidentally introduced from North America in the 1960s. It killed most of the UK's roughly 30 million elms, and the tree-lined English lanes and hedgerows the elm once defined largely vanished. The Bendy Tree came through it. The Woodland Trust's shortlist entry says it has resisted the disease, though neither the trust's material nor the coverage explains why it survived. Nothing in the reports ties its survival to disease-resistant breeding or any particular lesson for elm recovery, and it would be a stretch to read one in.

The rest of the field

The competition leaned towards the unlikely. Among the rivals were the Crowhurst Yew in Surrey, which has a Civil War cannonball lodged in its history, and the Culmstock Yew in Devon, which has grown from the top of a church tower for about 200 years. Others were the Balloch Castle ash at Loch Lomond, which has weathered lightning, storms and ash dieback, and the Bollihope Burn birch in County Durham, which clings to a cliff edge above a former industrial landscape.

Another elm was also in the running. The Preston Twin in Brighton, about 400 years old and described as likely the world's oldest English elm, has been protected from Dutch elm disease by years of beetle traps, pruning and root trenches. Brighton's survivor was kept alive by effort; the Bendy Tree seems to have simply been left standing.

What comes next

As UK winner, the Bendy Tree will represent the country in next year's European Tree of the Year competition, where it will face the national winners from other countries.
