New York court hears 9/11 families' lawsuit against Saudi Arabia

In one sentence
An appeals court heard Saudi Arabia's immunity challenge to the 9/11 families' lawsuit under JASTA, focusing on jurisdiction, with no ruling yet.

A federal appeals court in New York heard arguments on Wednesday in the long-running lawsuit brought by families of 9/11 victims against Saudi Arabia. The case turns on whether a foreign government can be made to answer in a US court for its alleged part in the 2001 attacks. No ruling has been issued, and the three-judge panel of the 2nd US Circuit Court of Appeals is expected to decide later.

What the court was asked

The appeal is Saudi Arabia's. A district court in Manhattan declined to dismiss the families' claims, citing what it called reasonable evidence that Saudi officials were involved. The kingdom contends that, as a sovereign state, it is immune from civil suits. The families say a 2016 law removes that protection.

That law is the Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act, known as JASTA. Congress passed it in September 2016 over President Obama's veto. It allows suits against foreign states for alleged support of terrorism on US soil. The Obama administration had warned that it could invite retaliatory suits against American officials abroad.

Press accounts of the hearing say the judges concentrated on jurisdiction, the narrow and technical question of whether the case can proceed against a state, and largely avoided the question of what Saudi Arabia actually did. One family lawyer said afterwards that the judges gave the Saudi attorneys a hard time. That is one side's reading, and the panel has not signalled how it will rule.

A case with a long history

The attacks of 11 September 2001 killed 2,977 people, not counting the 19 hijackers. Fifteen of those hijackers were Saudi nationals. Saudi Arabia has consistently denied supporting them, and the US government's own inquiry into the attacks found no evidence that the Saudi government as an institution financed al-Qaeda.

The families' case rests instead on individuals they say were tied to the Saudi state. The best known is Omar al-Bayoumi, a Saudi national in California whom they describe as a Saudi intelligence asset who helped at least two of the hijackers settle in the United States. Home video of Bayoumi, unsealed in federal court, has featured in their argument. Lawyers for the kingdom have said it fought terrorism and al-Qaeda in the 1990s, as the United States did.

The litigation has gone back and forth for years. Earlier rulings found Saudi Arabia immune from damages claims, and an appeals court later restored the kingdom as a defendant on the strength of a legal exception. The sources consulted give conflicting dates for some of these steps, so the sequence is best read in outline.

Why it matters beyond the courtroom

If the appeals court lets the case continue, it will confirm that JASTA can reach a close US partner, and discovery and eventual trial could follow. Such a result would be a precedent for suing foreign governments in US courts over alleged terrorism links. It would also be an awkward moment for Washington and Riyadh, whose ties span security, energy and investment. A ruling for Saudi Arabia would narrow what the law can do in practice.

The hearing itself settles nothing. It is a procedural stage in a case that could still run for years, including possible further appeals. The next marker is the panel's written decision, for which no date has been set. Whichever side loses can ask the full court to rehear the case or seek review at the Supreme Court.
