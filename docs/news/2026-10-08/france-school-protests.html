France's school protests: what lies behind the anger

In one sentence
French pupils are protesting over teacher shortages and decaying schools, and the unrest has turned violent, leaving a squeezed government under pressure to act by the end of October.

The protests in French schools are not, on the evidence so far, a dispute about one budget line. They began as a complaint about the condition of classrooms and the absence of teachers. They have since become the most violent youth unrest France has seen in years, and they now weigh on a government that has little room to manoeuvre.

From one lycée to a national movement

The movement started in late September, in the Paris region, over staff shortages at a school in Créteil. Pupils blocked entrances and the action spread across the suburbs and then to other cities. The demonstrators are mostly high-school students aged 15 to 17. Parents, teachers and union members have joined them.

The grievances are specific. A 17-year-old from Val-de-Marne told reporters of classrooms without enough desks and chairs, and of toilets with no doors, soap or paper. Others point to unfilled teaching posts, long timetables and buildings that struggle in hot weather. A teacher in Lyon, Rindala Younes, said the pupils' demands were "legitimate".

The largest day so far was Tuesday 6 October. The interior ministry counted 256,000 marchers nationwide; union organisers said 450,000. Police arrested at least 488 people that day. The government says more than 6,500 people, mostly minors, have been arrested since the movement began.

Violence, and the question of force

The human cost is disputed in its detail but not in its scale. The government says 215 students and 715 police officers have been injured since the start, along with 85 teaching staff. The education ministry says 24 schools have been burned or ransacked.

Rights groups, unions and students accuse the police of disproportionate force. A teenager lost a hand to a police stun grenade on Monday, and a 14-year-old lost several teeth to a tear-gas grenade; an officer has been charged over the second case. Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez has said police opened 10 internal investigations into alleged misconduct.

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu condemned the violence and rejected claims that the government had ordered repression. He told parliament: "Burning down a high school does not improve the state of high school buildings." Students answer that violence is what it takes to be heard. "Gen Z has risen up and wants to be heard," said Lilia, 19.

What it means for Paris

The budget link is real but indirect. Repairing ageing school buildings and filling teaching posts costs money, and no French government has spare money. Lecornu has told ministers to address the students' main demands and to bring first proposals by the end of October. These include replacing absent teachers, reviewing the length of the school day and repairs to buildings. Nothing seen so far ties the unrest to a specific finance-bill measure or a scheduled vote, and this account does not claim one.

The political stakes are clearer. The unrest comes with the 2027 presidential election approaching, when Emmanuel Macron must leave office after two terms, and with the far right strong in the polls. A government that appears either to have lost control of the streets or to have answered with force has little to gain. It will also struggle to promise spending that markets, already uneasy about long-dated European debt, may not wish to fund.

Macron was due to meet senior ministers, including Nuñez and Education Minister Édouard Geffray, after Tuesday's marches. High schools will close for half a day between Wednesday and Friday for student assemblies. The end-of-October deadline for ministerial proposals is the next test: whether it brings funded commitments on teachers and buildings, or only a review.
