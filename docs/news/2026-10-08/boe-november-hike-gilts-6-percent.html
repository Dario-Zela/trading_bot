Bank of England faces November hike pressure as the 30-year gilt yield holds above 6% ahead of the Budget

In one sentence
Three of nine MPC members voted for a hike in September, markets reportedly price about 80% odds for November, and the 30-year gilt yield has topped 6% before the 28 October Budget.

The Bank of England's rate-setters kept Bank Rate at 3.75% in September, but the vote was 6-3, and the November meeting now looks like a live decision rather than a formality. The shift came as the yield on 30-year gilts rose above 6% on 1 October for the first time since 1998. That puts the Bank's next move and the Chancellor's Budget on 28 October in the same frame: both will be judged partly by the price of long-dated government debt.

A split committee

The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 on 17 September to hold. Three members — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill — voted for a quarter-point rise to 4%. The majority was Andrew Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Clare Lombardelli, Dave Ramsden and Alan Taylor.

The arithmetic is simple. One more defection would make the vote 5-4, and a second would carry a hike. Analysts made the same point before the meeting. The Committee's own language narrows the gap further. The statement said that if the conflict in the Middle East persists for an extended period, "as appears to be the case", and the risk of second-round effects increases, "it is likely that policy may have to tighten". It also judged the risks to inflation to be tilted to the upside, more so than in July.

The data fit that caution. CPI inflation rose to 3.1% in August, with higher energy prices cited as the main source of upside risk. The next inflation reading arrives on 21 October, two weeks before the decision.

Some accounts have gone further and named senior officials as open to tightening. That needs care. Ramsden and Bailey both voted to hold in September, and the public record shows only the conditional language above, not a commitment from either. The reading that they would back a hike if pressures persist is an inference from that language, and one that rests on a thin set of secondary sources.

The same caution applies to market pricing. Reports put the chance of a November rise at roughly 80%, a figure that appears in secondary coverage and that could not be checked against a primary source. It is best read as an indication of direction rather than a precise odds estimate.

The long end breaks 6%

Gilts moved first. On 1 October the 30-year yield touched about 6%, with intraday highs reported between 6.02% and 6.07% depending on the data source, before easing back. It was the first time a G7 economy had seen a 30-year yield at that level since the eurozone crisis. The 10-year gilt reached about 5.5%, its highest since 2007, and the FTSE 100 closed 1.08% lower that day.

The cited drivers are energy-led inflation risk, expectations of a tighter Bank of England, heavy gilt issuance and nerves about the public finances. The Bank has itself flagged that the prolonged Middle East conflict has pushed up crude oil and UK wholesale gas prices.

These causes overlap, and it is hard to say which carries the most weight. A hike would raise short-term rates, but it could also help long yields if it persuaded investors that inflation would be contained. In the other direction, a central bank that tightens into weak growth can leave investors more worried about the fiscal arithmetic.

The Budget test

Chancellor John Healey presents the Budget on 28 October, a week before the Bank decides. Higher yields since March will probably lead the Office for Budget Responsibility to raise its forecast for debt interest costs, which would shrink whatever headroom the Treasury has against its fiscal rules before any new measure is announced. The Chancellor therefore faces a bond market that is already pricing risk, with no policy yet on the table to react to.

For households and firms the transmission is direct. A move to 4% would reverse part of the easing cycle and feed through to tracker and new fixed-rate mortgages and to business lending. Longer gilt yields set the benchmark for many fixed-rate borrowing costs as well.

What to watch

Three dates matter. UK inflation on 21 October will show whether energy costs are spreading into broader prices. The Budget on 28 October will show how much the Treasury plans to borrow and how the OBR scores the higher debt interest. The MPC decision on 5 November, which comes with a new forecast round, will show whether any of the six who voted to hold have moved. A 5-4 vote would leave the decision on a knife-edge. A 6-3 vote the other way would settle it.
