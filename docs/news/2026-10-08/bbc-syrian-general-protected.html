BBC finds US and Lebanon are protecting a wanted Syrian general

In one sentence
A BBC and NPR investigation reports that Bassam al-Hassan, a sanctioned ex-Syrian general wanted for crimes against humanity, is sheltered in Lebanon with US backing in return for Austin Tice information.

A BBC and NPR investigation reports that a former Syrian general accused of crimes against humanity is living in Lebanon under an arrangement backed by US agencies. The man is Bassam al-Hassan. He is reportedly being sheltered in return for information about Austin Tice, the American journalist abducted in Syria in 2012. The account rests on the BBC's reporting, which the US and Lebanese authorities have not confirmed. The FBI declined to comment.

Who is Hassan

Hassan ran Syria's chemical weapons programme and was a close adviser to Bashar al-Assad. He is accused of crimes against humanity and is alleged to share responsibility for the 2013 chemical attacks on Ghouta. The US government sanctions him.

After Assad fell in December 2024, Hassan reportedly fled to Iran. An anonymous Syrian businessman told the BBC that talks involving the US, Lebanon and Iran then brought him to Lebanon. The arrangement is believed to date from spring 2025.

The reported arrangement

The BBC says it reviewed a record of a meeting between Hassan, FBI and CIA representatives, and Lebanese security officials. The record is undated in the published account. In it, Hassan is offered residency, protection and help with his living needs in return for information, mainly about Tice. Sources say he receives US money through an intermediary. Another former regime member told the BBC he had received FBI payments several times for his cooperation on the case.

The case for the deal rests on Tice. Hassan told US investigators last year that Assad ordered the journalist executed. A site he identified was searched and nothing was found, though sources question whether the search was complete. Separately, the BBC reported last month that Tice's abduction was a planned operation with Hassan in the chain of command. People connected to the investigation say it has advanced little since Hassan began cooperating.

Reaction and what is contested

The FBI pointed to an earlier statement that its focus remains establishing the facts and securing Tice's return. The CIA and Lebanon's General Directorate of General Security did not respond. Hassan referred the BBC to the FBI and CIA for permission to speak.

The Tice family's spokesperson, Phil Elwood, said: "This is nothing less than aiding and abetting the escape of a wanted war criminal."

Officials from two branches of Syria's new government want Hassan returned for trial. US authorities reportedly want him kept in Beirut, and Damascus has little leverage to change that. A former Lebanese intelligence official told the BBC that Lebanon has similar payment arrangements with the US, but said he knew of none involving Tice.

Why it matters

Governments have long traded leniency for information from people implicated in atrocities. What makes this case sharper is that Washington sanctions Hassan while, on the BBC's account, sheltering him. It also backs accountability for Assad-era crimes, and the new Syrian authorities are asking for the same man. The report says Hassan is not alone: Mohammad Makhlouf is believed to be in Lebanon, Sakr Rustom, Hassan's nephew, is believed to move between the UAE and Lebanon, and Ghassan Nassour is reportedly in the UAE.

The question now is whether Washington or Beirut will respond on the record. The CIA and Lebanese security officials have yet to answer, and so has the question of whether any Tice lead from Hassan has been verified. Damascus's demand for his return will test how far the arrangement can hold.
