Parents rush to secure support plans ahead of Labour's SEND changes

In one sentence
Fear that Labour's SEND reforms will limit support plans is driving a surge in requests, councils say, adding pressure to budgets before the changes arrive.

Parents of children with special educational needs are asking councils for formal support plans in greater numbers, and the reason is the reform programme itself. Labour's planned overhaul of provision for special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) has made many families fear that the plans they would qualify for today will be harder to get tomorrow. Experts warn that the resulting scramble is landing on council budgets that are already stretched.

The plan at the centre of this is the education, health and care plan, or EHCP. It is a legal document that sets out the support a child must receive and can oblige a council to pay for it. Whether such plans will remain available on the same terms is the question the reforms have left open.

What the councils are reporting

The clearest evidence comes from local government. A report from the Local Government Association and the County Councils Network describes a "very significant surge" in requests for assessments, with parents seeking to secure plans before the proposed changes take effect. In some areas, requests were reported to be around one-third higher than expected. West Berkshire council minutes attribute a similar jump to a rush of applications ahead of the new system. Hull, cited by PoliticsHome, saw monthly assessment requests rise from an average of 69 last year to 99.

These are local and council-reported figures, not national statistics, and they should be read that way. They are consistent with one another, though.

How the rush began

PoliticsHome reports that the trigger was comments by Dame Christine Lenehan, the government's strategic SEND adviser. She said the forthcoming schools white paper would "probably" mean fewer EHCPs, and possibly none for mainstream pupils. Parents, campaigners and then MPs reacted angrily. Council reports suggest the speculation that followed has had the opposite effect to what councils had been trying to achieve, which is to steady demand.

The County Councils Network says the uptick is likely to reflect short-term parental uncertainty. Bill Revans, speaking for the network, has said its earlier projection of 840,000 EHCPs by 2028-29 is now likely to be exceeded.

The budget problem

The reforms are meant, in part, to ease council finances. Schools Week reports that the government has pledged to wipe 90 per cent of high-needs deficits and to take on new cost pressures from 2028. It has also effectively shifted responsibility for children with all but the highest needs from councils to schools.

The Institute for Fiscal Studies' Luke Sibieta has warned that the design creates incentives that could make councils' high-needs deficits grow faster in the run-up to implementation. That is the window in which the rush is happening. Every plan issued now is a statutory commitment the council must fund, whatever happens to the rules later.

Ministers take the opposite view over the long term. The government says it expects EHCP numbers to fall once the reforms have bedded in. Under the proposals, from 2029, children with an EHCP can keep it until at least the end of their educational phase. That protection is one reason families have for acting now.

What to watch

The test is whether the surge shows up in national figures. The next annual statistics on EHCPs, and the details in the schools white paper on who keeps what protection, will show whether the rush is a short spike or a lasting rise in demand. Councils will be watching closely, because the 2028 funding settlement is meant to take on their deficits, and the plans issued in the meantime will decide how large those deficits are.
