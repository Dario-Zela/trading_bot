Inside the £1-an-hour factories making official football shirts

In one sentence
A BBC Sport investigation traced Arsenal, Celtic and Liverpool replica shirts to Cambodian factories paying base wages under £1 an hour, with Adidas and Trax defending their records.

Official replica football shirts for some of Britain's best-known clubs are being sewn in Cambodian factories where base pay works out at less than £1 an hour, according to a BBC Sport investigation published on 8 October. The report ties a mass-market fan purchase to a wage that is legal locally but hard to live on, and it puts the question to the kit makers and clubs that sell the badge.

What the BBC found

BBC Sport traced authentic replica shirts for Arsenal, Celtic, Liverpool, Real Madrid and others to two factories in Cambodia. One is the Bowker Garment Factory in Kandal Province, near Phnom Penh, which employs about 3,400 people. The other is Trax Apparel, with about 3,200. Most of the workforce at both is female, and at Trax it is more than 80%.

Five payslips seen by the BBC showed base monthly salaries of $210–$220 (£158–£166). Three others showed about $300 (£226) once overtime and allowances were added. Staff typically work six eight-hour shifts a week. Workers described group targets of 300 to 700 items a day. One said it was "hard to live with this amount".

They also described conditions on the lines. A thermometer at Bowker read 35.2C with 57.6% humidity. Workers reported verbal abuse when targets were missed, and one said they had received two electric shocks from an exposed wire. A 2024 International Labour Organization report found heat problems in 69% of Cambodian garment factories and worker-protection problems in 84%, so the account fits a wider pattern.

The price of a badge

The contrast is with what fans pay. Arsenal's home shirt sold at £85, and one Aston Villa fan quoted in the report paid £95 for his, with a Champions League badge. The average adult replica Premier League shirt has risen 50.7% in price over ten years.

Earlier research on a typical £85 shirt, cited by the BBC, splits it roughly like this:

  • £8.50 for fabric, sewing and shipping
  • £37.45 to the retailer
  • £16.25 to the manufacturer
  • £9.50 for marketing
  • £13.60 in VAT

On that split, the combined cost of fabric, sewing and shipping is a tenth of the shelf price. The wage is a fraction of that tenth.

The responses

Adidas does not own either factory but counts both among its suppliers, and it supplies eight of the 20 Premier League clubs plus Northern Ireland, Scotland and Wales. It said it carries out about 1,200 factory audits a year and that both sites have heat-management measures. It also said its audit records for Bowker showed no electrical accidents. Citing a sample of its Cambodian suppliers, it said average net wages sit 34% above the Anker Research Institute's living-wage benchmark.

Trax acknowledged "periods of elevated temperature" and said average monthly earnings this year are $305–$342 (£230–£258). It said its approach is not based on enforcing output targets. Bowker did not respond. Hummel did not respond either. None of the clubs named replied.

Nike, which supplies three Premier League clubs and England from factories in Thailand, Vietnam and Georgia, was not tied to the Cambodian sites. It said 71% of its key suppliers pay at or above living-wage benchmarks for their locations.

Campaigners called the findings a "very grim picture" (Clean Clothes Campaign). Anna Bryher of Labour Behind the Label said: "Respect has got to be a baseline in sport."

What to watch

The disagreement is over which yardstick applies. The wages are reported as compliant with local minimum-wage rules, while the campaigners and the workers point to living costs. Adidas's figure comes from a sample of suppliers rather than these two sites. The clubs have so far said nothing. Whether any of them, or the Premier League, gives a public answer on supplier wages and disclosure will show if the story goes beyond the one report. The pay figures are the BBC's, from payslips it has seen, and have not been independently audited.
