ChatGPT teen use is limited but an 'unacceptable risk', research finds

In one sentence
OpenAI says teens use ChatGPT for under 15 minutes a day, but Common Sense Media rates its teen product an 'unacceptable risk' after testing 4,000+ prompts; OpenAI disputes the method.

OpenAI says teenagers do not spend much time on ChatGPT. A child-safety group says that is beside the point. The two claims reached the public within days of each other, and they measure different things. Together they show how far the company and its critics still are from agreeing on what counts as evidence of harm.

Two sets of numbers

OpenAI has published its first report on teen use of the chatbot. It says teenagers average under 15 minutes a day on ChatGPT, and that fewer than 2% use it for three hours straight. The company also says that in almost half of teen conversations where a break reminder appeared, the teen took a break or ended the chat within five minutes.

The counter-claim comes from the Youth AI Safety Institute at Common Sense Media. According to Axios and ABC-affiliate coverage, the group tested more than 4,000 prompts, before and after the launch of ChatGPT for Teens. It rated the product an "unacceptable risk", its worst category, for users under 18. It urged OpenAI to keep teens off the service.

The usage figures and the safety rating answer different questions. A short average session says little about what happens in the sessions that matter. An average of under 15 minutes is compatible with a small number of long, vulnerable conversations. It is also compatible with no such conversations. The usage report, as summarised so far, does not settle that.

What the testers say failed

The most serious finding concerns parental alerts. Common Sense says conversations about suicide and self-harm on its test accounts never triggered an alert to a parent. It also says the product fell short of its promises on crisis referrals and age assurance, according to The Decoder.

OpenAI disputes the testing. A spokesperson said: "We welcome rigorous independent evaluation, but we do not believe Common Sense Media's testing accurately reflects how ChatGPT's teen safeguards work in practice." The company argues that much of the parental-alert testing took place before the parent and teen accounts had finished linking, a process it says can take several hours. If that is right, the test accounts may not have been set up as a real family's would be. Common Sense's head, Tom Siegel, called the company's usage report "much more of a marketing announcement that really was not backed up with the engineering work."

The dispute is partly one of method. Test accounts built by a safety group are not a random sample of teenagers. They are designed to probe failure points. A rating based on them says what the product can do under pressure, not how often it happens in the wild. That cuts both ways. It weakens any claim that the rating shows typical teen experience. It also weakens OpenAI's reliance on average time spent, which cannot show whether the safeguards work when needed. Details of the full Common Sense protocol have not been checked here against the report itself.

Not the first warning

The rating is harsher than the one Common Sense has given before. The group has previously called ChatGPT a "moderate risk" for teens, and judged it safer than companion-style bots built around romantic characters. In an earlier study, the Center for Countering Digital Hate found that a determined teenager could get past the chatbot's safeguards.

The new rating is also aimed at a product built for teens. ChatGPT for Teens is the company's own answer to earlier criticism, and its parental controls are the feature Common Sense says did not work in testing.

What to watch

The next test is whether OpenAI publishes the evidence behind its rebuttal, in particular how long account linking takes and how often parental alerts fire in real use. Regulators weighing teen-specific rules for AI products now have a contested rating and a contested usage report to work from, and neither side's figures have been independently audited.
