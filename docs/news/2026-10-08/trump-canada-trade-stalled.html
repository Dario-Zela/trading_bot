Trump says Canada is 'very difficult to deal with' as trade talks stall

In one sentence
Trump again calls Canada 'very difficult to deal with' as trade talks stay stuck, while Ottawa pushes pipelines and a BBC piece weighs Alberta independence.

Talks between Washington and Ottawa on a trade settlement remain stuck, and Donald Trump has again said so in public. Reports on the latest remarks quote him calling Canada "very difficult to deal with" and saying a deal is "not yet satisfactory". The wording echoes remarks he made about Canada during the 2018 NAFTA renegotiation, but the current dispute is a different one, between Trump and Prime Minister Mark Carney.

Where the talks stand

The evidence for a stall is mostly about tone and inaction. The Rio Times reported that Trump said on 5 October he was in no hurry to resume talks and accused Ottawa of treating the United States badly. US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer has also described the outstanding issues as "quite difficult to resolve", according to CBC, while saying Washington remains open to a deal.

The Canadian side has described the sticking points in sharper terms. Carney has said the American position would leave core Canadian industries either as effective subsidiaries of US firms or gradually wound down. He has also said he could not accept language that would limit Canada's freedom to strike trade deals with other countries. Earlier coverage, including NBC's, reported that a round of talks collapsed ahead of a deadline for 50% US duties on a wide range of Canadian goods, and that Carney promised to match them dollar for dollar. This article could not confirm the current tariff schedule or any fresh deadline, and none was cited in the latest remarks.

The numbers, so far, have been less alarming than the rhetoric. Statistics Canada data released on 6 October showed exports to the US up 8.1% in August and imports from the US down 2.5%, widening Canada's monthly trade surplus to C$11.2 billion, according to a Rio Times summary. That suggests trade has been rerouting and holding up, not collapsing, though one month's data settles little.

Why the loonie and energy are in play

Canada and the US trade across one of the world's largest bilateral relationships, with integrated auto and manufacturing supply chains and a large flow of Canadian crude south. A prolonged impasse leaves businesses planning around tariffs they cannot price with confidence, and the Canadian dollar tends to take the first hit when talk turns hostile. Ottawa's response so far has been to look inward. Bloomberg reported on 7 October that, with no end to US tariffs in sight, Carney is using new powers to speed up infrastructure and energy projects.

The Alberta question

That domestic push runs through Alberta. The Rio Times reported that Carney designated a pipeline from Alberta to the West Coast, renamed Pacific Link, a project of national interest on 1 October. Separately, BBC Business has examined what independence for Alberta could mean for its oil economy. The province accounts for roughly 84% of Canadian crude output, by one advocacy-leaning estimate, and critics of separation point to pipeline access and the loss of federal market leverage as its main exposures.

The link to the trade dispute is indirect. A government that cannot get a deal with its largest customer has more reason to build routes to other markets, and that is also the argument Alberta's autonomists use against Ottawa. Neither the BBC piece nor the trade reports establish that one feeds the other.

What to watch

The signals to track are whether Washington sets a new deadline or tariff date, whether Greer's office reopens formal talks, and how Ottawa sequences the pipeline approvals. Until one of those moves, the stall rests on a quote and some contested figures.
