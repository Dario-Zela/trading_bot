Holborn and St Pancras votes in a Labour-Green by-election as the Greens face internal rows

In one sentence
Voters in Starmer's former seat of Holborn and St Pancras choose between Labour's Sagal Abdi-Wali and Green leader Zack Polanski, in a test of Labour's urban base.

Voters in a central London constituency go to the polls today to choose a successor to Sir Keir Starmer. Starmer gave up the seat of Holborn and St Pancras to pursue a new role in international affairs. Labour has held the seat since it was created in 1983, but the Greens have put their own leader on the ballot. The result will be read well beyond Camden as a measure of how far the Green challenge reaches into Labour's urban base.

A safe seat, on paper

In 2024 Starmer took 48.9% of the vote and a majority of 11,572. His nearest rival was a left-wing independent on 18.9%. The Greens were third on 10.4%. By the old arithmetic this is as safe as Labour seats get.

More recent evidence is less comfortable for the party. In May's Camden council elections, across the wards that make up the seat, Labour took 38.8% to the Greens' 28.8%. That is a 10-point gap, close to the 2024 margin between Labour and the independent but with the Greens now clearly the second force. No constituency poll appears to have been published, so the local results are the best guide to where the contest stands.

Labour has fielded Sagal Abdi-Wali, who has led Camden Council since May and has sat as a councillor for Camden Square since 2022. The Greens chose Zack Polanski, their national leader, who saw off a challenge for the nomination from the local councillor Hamza Chowdhury. Fifteen candidates are standing. They include Ewan Cameron for the Conservatives, Peter Newman for Reform UK, Patrick Stillman for the Liberal Democrats and George Galloway for the Workers Party. Polls close at 10pm and a result is expected overnight.

The Greens' conference hangover

Polanski's campaign has been shadowed by a row that broke out at his own party's conference in Brighton. Members voted 1,022 to 813 to adopt a motion declaring that "Zionism is racism", and the policy also calls for Israel to be replaced by a single Palestinian state. Polanski left the conference after his speech on Friday to campaign in the by-election and did not vote. Some senior figures reportedly felt he should have taken a firmer stand against the motion.

Four of the party's five MPs opposed it, as did the former leader Caroline Lucas. A Green spokesperson said the motion has no "material impact on governance or disciplinary procedures", and that the party does not require its politicians to back it. Asked by the BBC whether Zionism is racist, Polanski said he thought it clearly was racism. In a joint statement with the party's MPs and deputy leaders he said there was no conflict between supporting Palestinian rights and supporting the rights of Jewish people. Israel's foreign minister responded by declaring Polanski barred from entering the country.

Triage notes for this edition also point to an internal investigation into a member connected with the motion and to the resignation of a former Welsh deputy leader. This reporting could not confirm either: neither the investigation nor the resignation appeared in the coverage of the conference reviewed here. If they are borne out, they would fit a pattern of a party stretching to hold together a wider coalition than it had even two years ago.

What the result will be taken to mean

Holborn and St Pancras is an unusual electorate: students, professionals, renters and long-established social housing communities, many of them in the kind of urban constituency where Green and left-independent challenges have recently done best. A Green win would be an enormous symbolic blow to Labour, because the seat was its former leader's. A narrow Labour hold would still be read as an erosion of the base, and a comfortable one would let the party argue that the Green surge peaked at the council elections.

The Greens face their own test. Their activists are enthusiastic, and the leader's personal stake raises the cost of losing. Their internal arguments over Israel and Palestine, which have dominated the past week, could cost votes among Jewish residents and moderate Labour switchers while energising the left. How the two effects balance is not something the available data can settle.

The count will show the answer. The thing to watch is the Green share against the 28.8% they took in May, and the size of Labour's lead over it. Behind them come the question of how far the Reform and Conservative votes fall, and whether Galloway's Workers Party draws on the same left-leaning pool as Polanski.
