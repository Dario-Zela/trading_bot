Fed minutes: all backed September's hike, most see another by year-end, with AI buildout flagged as an inflation risk

In one sentence
Fed minutes show unanimous support for September's 25bp hike, a majority expecting another by year-end, and some officials flagging the AI buildout as an inflation risk.

The Federal Reserve's policymakers were unanimous in September. Minutes of that meeting, released this week, show every participant backed a 25-basis-point rate increase. Most of them also expect to raise rates again before the end of the year. The more unusual passage is about where the next inflation pressure might come from. Some officials said the artificial-intelligence buildout could push demand above the economy's capacity to supply it, and that this would add to inflation.

That is a different register from the usual treatment of AI spending, which tends to appear in central-bank commentary as a source of productivity and growth. The minutes put it on the risk side of the ledger, as demand that the economy may not be able to absorb without price pressure.

What the minutes say

Three findings stand out, and the first two are the conventional ones.

  • The hike was not contested. All participants supported the 25bp increase. Minutes often record dissent or reservations, and this record shows none on the decision itself.
  • Another hike is the base case. Most participants see a further increase by year-end. That is a majority view, not a consensus, so the minutes leave room for officials who would stop or pause.
  • The risks lean one way. Almost all officials judged the risks to inflation to be tilted to the upside. When nearly the whole committee describes risk in one direction, the debate over the next move becomes a debate about how much and how soon, not about whether.

The minutes do not suggest an imminent move. Traders do not expect a change at the October meeting, which fits a committee that has just acted and says its next step is likely to come later in the year. The year-end hike the minutes point to would fall at a subsequent meeting, not this one.

The AI argument

The reasoning behind the AI remark is a supply-and-demand one. Data-centre construction, chip purchases, power infrastructure and the labour and materials that go with them are all forms of spending. If that spending grows faster than the capacity to build and supply the underlying goods and services, the gap shows up in prices: of equipment, of electricity, of construction, and of skilled workers. Officials described this as a way demand could outrun supply, and the minutes present it as a reason further rate increases could be justified.

The minutes, as summarised, do not put a number on the effect, and it would be a mistake to read more precision into the passage than it has. The wording is that "some" officials raised it, which is a minority of the committee, not its settled view. But the observation matters because it changes how a boom in capital spending is read. A central bank that treats investment as a supply-side story can look through it. One that treats it as a demand impulse in an economy already facing upside inflation risk has a reason to lean against it.

There is corroborating evidence elsewhere in the day's news that the demand is real. Samsung's third-quarter profit guidance beat estimates as AI demand outstripped memory supply, which is the same mismatch the Fed officials described, seen from the supplier's side.

Households are watching prices too

The minutes arrived alongside fresh survey data that complicates any hope that inflation worries are fading. The New York Fed's latest survey of consumer expectations found households' one-year inflation expectations at their highest since May 2023. The exact level was not in the material available at the time of writing.

Longer horizons moved less, but in the same direction or not at all:

Horizon Latest reading Change
One year Highest since May 2023 Up
Three years 3.3% From 3.2%
Five years 3.0% Unchanged

The five-year figure is the one central bankers watch most closely, because it speaks to whether people believe inflation will return to target. It held at 3%, which is not alarming but is also not close to the 2% target. The three-year reading edged up by a tenth of a point. Respondents also grew more pessimistic about their own finances, which is the combination officials fear: people who expect higher prices and feel less able to cope with them. That tends to feed into wage demands and pricing decisions.

The survey measures households, not markets or economists, and one-month changes of a tenth of a point are within the range of ordinary noise. The one-year jump is the larger signal, and it is the one that fits the committee's judgement that risks lean up.

What it means for markets

If the Fed reads the AI boom as inflationary, the consequence is not a single hike but a longer period of higher rates. That tightens financial conditions for the companies doing the spending. Capital-intensive technology firms borrow against future cash flows, and rate-sensitive assets are repriced when the discount rate rises. There is some tension in this: the same demand that supports the earnings of chipmakers and memory suppliers is what the Fed is worried about.

The backdrop is already uncomfortable for borrowers. A weak Treasury auction this week pushed the 30-year yield to 5.7% and dragged Wall Street lower, and the Treasury has said it plans a buyback. In London, the 30-year gilt yield is holding above 6% as the Bank of England faces pressure to raise rates in November. The Fed minutes add a reason for long-dated yields to stay high: a central bank that is openly weighing further tightening is not one that bond investors will expect to cut soon.

The discovery process rated this story highly on the strength of the AI angle, but a fair reading is that the minutes are a routine release whose main facts, a unanimous vote and a majority leaning towards another hike, were largely expected. The AI-inflation link is the part with implications beyond a single meeting, because it ties monetary policy to the largest investment theme in the market.

What to watch

The October meeting is the first test, and traders expect no move there. The more informative signals are the data that arrive before the next decision after it: whether inflation readings confirm the upside tilt, whether the NY Fed's one-year expectations keep climbing, and whether the AI-related officials' concern is repeated in speeches and in the next set of minutes. If more than "some" participants start to describe the buildout as a demand problem, the case for the year-end hike, and for a longer stay at higher rates, strengthens.
