Bluefin tuna return to UK waters, showing nature can recover, says WWF

In one sentence
WWF cites the return of Atlantic bluefin tuna to UK waters as proof that nature can recover, though warming seas and larger ICCAT quotas complicate the success story.

Atlantic bluefin tuna, once fished out of British waters, are back in them. WWF has pointed to the fish as evidence that nature can recover. The conservation charity cites the return in its Living Planet Report 2026 as one of several examples of wildlife that has rebounded, even as its headline figure shows monitored wildlife populations down an average of 73% since 1970.

The bluefin is the more instructive case than the feel-good framing suggests. It is a long-lived, high-value fish that crosses national boundaries, so a recovery depends on catch limits agreed internationally. That makes it a test of whether quota management works.

A fish that left, and came back

Bluefin were once a fixture off Cornwall and the wider south-west. Accounts differ on when they vanished from British waters. Some place it in the 1960s, others in the 1990s. All attribute the loss, at least in part, to commercial overfishing of the stock. Those dates have not been independently verified here.

The return has been building for several years. UK government marine scientists have reported bluefin in British waters in numbers for successive years, mainly in late summer and autumn. A study led by Cefas, the government's marine science agency, and the University of Exeter drew on 16 years of data. It found the fish feeding around the Celtic Seas, south-west England, the Isles of Scilly and north-west Ireland.

WWF's chief scientific adviser, Mike Barrett, said there are "grounds for hope" even though nature is "in freefall" globally. He added that "it's not too late".

What the management record shows

Because bluefin migrate widely, sightings off Cornwall say more about the wider eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean stock than about any local population. That stock has been managed through catch limits set by the International Commission for the Conservation of Atlantic Tunas (ICCAT) since the 2000s. The EU has credited its recent quota increases to successful efforts to recover the stock.

At its November 2025 meeting in Seville, ICCAT set a total allowable catch of 48,403 tonnes a year for Atlantic bluefin for 2026–2028. The EU's share of the eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean quota rose by 3,661 tonnes, to 25,164 tonnes a year. Iceland's quota rose about 13% and Norway's 25%.

Two caveats apply. First, scientists have noted that warming seas may be drawing warm-water species such as bluefin further north, so the sightings do not prove recovery on their own. They are better read alongside stock assessments than in place of them. Second, the 2025 increases rested on an existing management procedure rather than a new full bluefin assessment. Conservation researchers have warned that the push for higher catches could threaten the Mediterranean recovery.

What to watch

The UK's own rules have been shifting. Targeting bluefin was prohibited for years, and campaigners have criticised the government's move towards limited fishing. The detail of current UK arrangements should be checked against official guidance. The question for ministers and for ICCAT is whether the rebound can survive larger quotas. The stock's next assessment, and the 2026–2028 catch figures set in Seville, will show whether the fish keep coming back to British waters.
