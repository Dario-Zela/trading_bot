Drone cloud-seeding 'rainmakers' draw interest as water stress grows

In one sentence
Drone cloud seeding is pitched as a cheaper, more precise way to make rain, but the evidence so far comes from company claims that have not been peer reviewed.

Cloud seeding, the practice of releasing particles into clouds to coax more rain or snow out of them, is getting a drone-shaped update. Start-ups and research teams argue that small uncrewed aircraft can do the job more cheaply and more precisely than the piloted planes that have dominated the field for decades. As water stress grows in dry regions, governments are paying attention. What the technology can actually deliver is much less settled.

Two approaches

The projects now drawing interest take different routes. In the United Arab Emirates, researchers at the universities of Reading and Bath have worked on drones that give cloud droplets an electric charge, intended to help them merge and fall as rain. The UAE funded the work, and the stated aim is to supplement or eventually replace crewed aircraft that disperse chemicals.

In the American West, the company Rainmaker flies drones that release silver iodide, a conventional seeding agent, and uses radar to track what follows. The company says it has generated about 143 million gallons of water in Utah and Oregon and logged 82 "unambiguous seeding signatures" as of April. Utah and Idaho are reported to be paying the company millions of dollars a year.

These figures are the company's own. They have not been independently verified.

The evidence problem

The difficulty is old rather than new. Weather varies enormously, and there is no control cloud: nobody can observe what would have fallen had the seeding not happened. Rainmaker's workaround is to fly drones in distinctive patterns and then look in radar and satellite data for matching features, such as holes or depressed tops in the cloud. The approach borrows from NCAR's 2017 SNOWIE study, which documented three such signatures.

Outside scientists want the work tested before they rely on it. Katja Friedrich, a cloud microphysics professor at the University of Colorado Boulder who is not involved with the company, has said that without peer review the results are "just numbers on a piece of paper." The company says it will submit its findings for review, a process that could take more than a year. The US Government Accountability Office has previously described the benefits of seeding as unproven.

Scale is a second limit. Even if the signatures are confirmed, some scientists note the water involved is small against the size of a regional drought. Seeding also needs clouds with the right moisture and temperature to begin with, so it cannot create rain in a clear sky. Earlier UAE claims of rainfall gains of around 30 per cent with crewed aircraft rest on the programme's own estimates, and the BBC has previously questioned the cost-effectiveness of that effort.

Regulation and what to watch

Rules are also unsettled. Rainmaker's July 2025 petition to the US Federal Aviation Administration to carry seeding flares on drones remains unresolved, and the Air Line Pilots Association has raised safety concerns. Florida treats cloud seeding as a felony and Tennessee has banned it.

The next real test is publication. Peer-reviewed results from the drone programmes, and a ruling on the FAA petition, would show whether this is a useful tool at the margins of water management or a promising pitch that is still waiting for proof.
