Margaret Hamilton, whose software helped land Apollo 11 on the Moon, dies at 90

In one sentence
Margaret Hamilton, who led the team that wrote Apollo's guidance software and whose priority-based design survived the 1202 alarms during the Apollo 11 landing, has died at 90.

Margaret Hamilton, the computer scientist who led the team that wrote the guidance software for the Apollo Moon landings, has died at 90. Reports say MIT, her former employer, confirmed her death and give the date as 30 September. This account rests on secondary reports that cite the BBC and MIT, and the exact circumstances have not been independently confirmed here.

Hamilton is most often remembered for one night in July 1969, and the reasons say a good deal about how software for critical systems should be built.

Four minutes of alarms

Hamilton led the software engineering division at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory, which held the contract for the Apollo flight software. On 20 July 1969, as Apollo 11's lunar module descended towards the Sea of Tranquility, the onboard computer began raising program alarms coded 1202 and 1201. Neither code had much meaning to the crew in the moment. They meant the computer was being asked to do more than it could.

The cause was a hardware configuration problem, widely reported as the rendezvous radar feeding the computer spurious work while it was also running the landing. The machine was short of processing time, and a less carefully built system might have stalled or crashed during the most dangerous phase of the flight.

The guidance software did not crash. Hamilton's team had designed it around priorities. Each task carried a rank. When the computer ran out of capacity it dropped the low-priority jobs, restarted what it needed, and kept the essential landing work going. The alarms were the software reporting that it was shedding load, and in doing so it kept the descent on course.

A discipline with a name

Hamilton is widely credited with popularising the term "software engineering". She has said she wanted the work taken as seriously as hardware engineering, at a time when programming was often treated as a lesser craft. The Apollo programme made the case better than any argument could. Code was now a component that astronauts' lives depended on, and it had to be designed, tested and reasoned about as one.

In 2016 President Barack Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

The lesson that travelled

The 1202 episode is a textbook case of graceful degradation: when a system is overloaded, protect the core functions and let the rest wait. The idea runs through modern aviation, medical devices and industrial control. It also applies to automated trading and payments systems, where a surge in message traffic or a failing component can't be allowed to take down order handling or risk checks. The design question Hamilton's team answered in the 1960s is the one operators still face. What must keep running when everything else is struggling, and does the software know the difference?

Her approach also treated error as a normal condition. The system was built to detect trouble, report it and recover, instead of assuming that nothing would go wrong.

What next

Expect formal tributes from MIT and NASA in the coming days, and a fresh round of attention for the Apollo flight code, which has been public for years and is still studied by engineers. The details of her final months and any memorial arrangements are matters for her family and the institutions that confirmed her death.
