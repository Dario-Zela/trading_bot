FCC to vote on banning Chinese labs from testing US electronics

In one sentence
The FCC votes October 29 on barring Chinese labs from testing electronics for the US market, using device certification as a lever in US-China decoupling.

The Federal Communications Commission will vote on October 29 on a rule that would bar test laboratories in China from certifying electronics for the US market, according to Reuters. The rule would cover smartphones, cameras and computers. It would widen an earlier crackdown that reached only labs tied to the Chinese state. Most of the policy fight over US-China technology has been about chips and the machines that make them. This vote is about a more mundane gatekeeper: the lab that checks whether a gadget's radio behaves.

Why a test lab matters

Almost any device that emits radio energy needs FCC equipment authorization before it can be marketed in the US. That covers phones, laptops, routers, earbuds and smart plugs. The manufacturer has the product measured, usually at an FCC-recognized lab, to confirm it stays within emissions limits. The lab's report then supports a certification. Without it the product cannot legally be sold.

The commission therefore controls market access by deciding which labs it recognizes. Until recently that recognition rested mainly on technical competence. The FCC's 2025 rules added an ownership test: labs owned, controlled or directed by entities the government considers national-security risks lose eligibility. The FCC began withdrawing recognition from labs linked to the Chinese government in September 2025. By its own account, the FCC later found that a large majority of China-based labs were still testing US-bound products.

That is the gap the new item targets. According to Reuters, it would prohibit test labs and certification bodies in countries that deny reciprocal treatment to US-based labs, a category that includes China. The commission's reasoning is a trade argument as much as a security one. Chair Brendan Carr has said that the great majority of electronics sold in the US are tested in China, which has no recognition agreement with Washington. He has also said fewer than 4% of devices are tested in US labs. In his words, the FCC will "insist on fair and reciprocal treatment in international commerce."

What changes, and when

Two cautions apply. First, reports differ on what the October 29 vote is. Reuters describes a vote to ban, while some outlets, such as Business Today, call it a proposal. The FCC advanced a broader proposal on all China and Hong Kong labs in April, so the October item may be either the final step or another one. The reporting does not settle this.

Second, the timeline is long. Reuters reports the prohibitions are slated to take effect in December 2028, which gives manufacturers more than two years.

The FCC is pairing the ban with a streamlined approval process for devices tested in US labs, or in labs from countries that pose no national-security concern. The aim is to give manufacturers somewhere to go. Western testing firms already run labs in the US, Europe and Taiwan, but capacity is the open question. Reshoring the work means building it.

The cost to device makers

The price gap is the obvious friction. Industry accounts put basic certification testing at roughly $400 to $1,300 at Chinese labs, against $3,000 to $4,000 at US equivalents. Those figures come from trade reporting and are not FCC numbers. For a company shipping one product line, the difference is a rounding error. For one with dozens of SKUs and tight launch windows, it adds up. Queues at non-Chinese labs add delay on top, and the cost is likely to reach consumers in some form.

There is an open question beneath the cost. The current rules did not address the status of authorizations already obtained through labs later barred. Whether existing products would need re-testing under the new rule is not clear from the reporting reviewed.

The Chinese Embassy in Washington has objected to what it calls US over-stretching of the concept of national security. Beijing has several ways to respond, including recognizing US labs less readily or imposing its own certification hurdles, though nothing of that kind has been announced.

What to watch

The October 29 vote is the first marker. The text of the order will show whether it is a final rule or a further proposal, how "reciprocal treatment" is defined, and how the December 2028 date is handled. After that, the questions are practical ones. Does non-Chinese lab capacity grow in time, and does the FCC treat existing certifications as grandfathered? Those answers will decide whether this is a manageable transition for electronics makers or a bottleneck in their supply chains.
