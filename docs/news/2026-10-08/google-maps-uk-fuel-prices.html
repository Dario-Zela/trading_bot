Google Maps adds live petrol and diesel prices in the UK

In one sentence
Google Maps is adding live UK petrol and diesel prices inside its navigation app, putting price comparison at the point of driving just as pump prices rise with crude.

Google Maps is adding live petrol and diesel prices for UK drivers, according to reports from the BBC and the Guardian. Pump prices will appear inside the navigation app, so a driver can compare nearby stations without leaving it for a separate fuel-price website or app.

The timing matters. Pump prices have been rising in step with crude oil, and the Gulf is again a source of supply worries. Price-checking tends to become a habit when prices are climbing, and the feature puts that check in the app many drivers already use to find the nearest station.

What changes

UK drivers could already compare fuel prices, but only by going somewhere else to do it. Dedicated websites and phone apps list the cheapest stations nearby. Google Maps has also shown an estimated fuel cost for a journey, which is a different thing: a trip estimate, not a station-by-station price list. The new feature puts the actual price of petrol or diesel at a forecourt alongside the route.

The reports do not say, and this article could not confirm, how Google gets its prices or how fresh they are. Several existing UK price tools draw on the government's Fuel Finder scheme. It is meant to make major retailers publish forecourt prices within about half an hour of a change. Whether Google uses that data, or something else, determines how far a price on screen can be trusted. A figure that is an hour stale is much less useful to a driver deciding whether to detour.

Why it matters

The scale is modest. A few pence a litre on a 50-litre tank is a few pounds, and a detour to save it can cost most of that back in fuel. But the effect is across a very large number of drivers. If many of them choose a station by price at the moment they navigate to it, spending shifts towards cheaper forecourts. Retailers that have relied on convenience or location then have a reason to cut prices.

The reason that is plausible is that the comparison happens at the point of decision. Someone who has to open a separate app to compare prices will often not bother. Someone who sees the prices on the map they are already using will compare them without trying.

It also fits a wider pattern. Mapping apps began as tools for getting from A to B, and have picked up opening hours, reviews, parking and charging-point availability. Each addition makes the map a place to compare options before spending money, a role once held by dedicated comparison sites.

What to watch

Three things will show how much this matters. The first is how fresh and complete the price data is, especially at independent stations. The second is whether retailers or the competition regulator comment on how prices are being compared. The third is how pump prices move over the coming weeks: if crude keeps pushing them up, more drivers will use a feature like this, and the effect on retailers' margins will be easier to see.
