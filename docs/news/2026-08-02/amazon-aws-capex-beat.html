Amazon's AWS growth hits 37% as Jassy lifts AI capex to $220bn

AWS's us-west-2 availability zone in Oregon — part of the data center footprint behind Amazon's raised capex guidance — Tedder / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AWS growth hit 37% and Amazon raised 2026 AI capex to $220bn, with Jassy predicting AWS could become a trillion-dollar business.

Amazon's cloud business is growing faster than it has in five years, and the company is about to spend more money proving it can keep up with demand than most countries spend on their militaries.

Amazon Web Services posted revenue of $42.2 billion in the second quarter, up 37% from a year earlier — comfortably ahead of the 31% analysts had pencilled in, and the fastest pace since the final quarter of 2021. It was the fifth straight quarter of AWS acceleration. Chief executive Andy Jassy used the moment to raise the company's already enormous 2026 capital spending plan to $220 billion, up from roughly $200 billion guided previously, and to float a number that would have sounded like hyperbole two years ago: AWS, he said, could "very possibly be a trillion-dollar annual revenue business for us in time."

Amazon shares jumped as much as 7% in after-hours trading on the news. The company's total net sales rose 20% year-over-year to $200.6 billion, the first time Amazon has cleared $200 billion in a single quarter, with operating income up 43% to $27.5 billion.

Cloud's fastest lap since 2021

The headline number is the acceleration itself. AWS has now sped up for five consecutive quarters, an unusual trajectory for a business already generating $169 billion on an annualized run-rate basis — large enough that, as a standalone company, it would rank around 24th on the Fortune 500. Growing an operation that size by 37% means adding tens of billions of dollars in absolute revenue that simply didn't exist a year prior, which is why Amazon executives kept returning on the earnings call to the idea that percentage growth understates what's happening.

The demand signal behind that growth is the AWS backlog — contracted revenue not yet recognized — which reached $496 billion in the quarter, roughly two and a half times its level a year earlier and growing at a triple-digit annual rate. Jassy told analysts that most of the capacity Amazon expects to bring online in 2027 is already spoken for, and that early demand signals for 2028 were, in his word, "striking."

The capex bet

The corollary to that demand is spending Amazon itself has struggled to keep pace with. The revised $220 billion capex figure for 2026 — up $20 billion from guidance given only a quarter earlier — is directed overwhelmingly at AI infrastructure: data centers, custom Trainium chips, and the servers and networking gear that go inside them. Jassy attributed part of the increase to rising memory-chip prices, a supply constraint rippling through the entire AI hardware stack rather than an Amazon-specific problem.

"Even at that amount, we will still not have enough capacity to meet all the demand we have in 2026, and I believe this dynamic will also be true in 2027 too. In fact, the demand we already have for 2028 is striking."

That is Amazon's central wager, stated plainly: that spending more than $200 billion a year building capacity is still the conservative choice relative to the risk of turning away customers. It is also the exact dynamic that has made investors nervous elsewhere in the sector — HSBC has pointed to fears of AI overspend as a reason capital keeps rotating out of chipmakers even as hyperscaler capex guidance keeps rising.

A profit number with an asterisk

Amazon's headline net income was $62.6 billion, or $5.75 a share, against $18.2 billion, or $1.68 a share, a year ago — more than a tripling. That figure is worth reading carefully rather than repeating uncritically: it includes a $53.4 billion pre-tax non-operating gain, driven substantially by the mark-to-market value of Amazon's investment in Anthropic, the AI lab it has backed with billions of dollars in funding. Strip that gain out and the more telling number is operating income, which rose 43% to $27.5 billion on genuine sales and margin improvement rather than a paper valuation gain on a startup stake.

The rest of the business supported the quarter without needing an asterisk. Advertising revenue climbed 26% to $19.8 billion. North America segment sales rose 16% to $116.2 billion with a 7.9% operating margin; the International segment grew 15% to $42.2 billion at a thinner 4.1% margin. Together they show a retail operation still expanding at a healthy clip even as AWS dominates the narrative.

What's next

The test for Amazon's trillion-dollar framing isn't the current quarter — it's whether AI workloads keep converting from signed contracts into billed revenue at a pace that justifies capacity built years in advance. Jassy's own comments concede the risk runs in Amazon's favor for now: demand, not spending discipline, is the binding constraint. The next checkpoints are Amazon's Q3 report and whether the AWS backlog keeps compounding at triple-digit rates, alongside broader signals — like the capital rotation HSBC has flagged away from chip stocks — on whether markets keep believing the AI infrastructure buildout pays for itself before the bill comes due.
