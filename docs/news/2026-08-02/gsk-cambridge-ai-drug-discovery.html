GSK opens £400m Cambridge hub, strikes $110m AI drug-discovery deal

In one sentence
GSK is spending £400m on a Cambridge R&D hub and $110m on AI biotech Relation Therapeutics, pricing the cost of competing in AI drug discovery at £500m-plus.

GSK is spending £400m to build a new research campus in Cambridge, and it is spending a further $110m to make sure that campus has something to feed its algorithms. The two announcements, made days apart, are the same story told twice — once in bricks and once in data.

The headline number is the 300,000 sq ft R&D centre GSK is building on the Cambridge Biomedical Campus, confirmed this week, which will house more than 1,000 scientists and become the company's main UK research base. The smaller number, easy to miss beside it, is the $110m GSK has committed to Relation Therapeutics, a London AI biotech, to expand a data-generation partnership feeding Relation's new cell-biology model, MORGAN. Together they describe what it now costs a major drugmaker to compete on AI-driven discovery: not a licensing fee for someone else's software, but a building and a data supply chain of its own.

Leaving Stevenage after fifty years

The Cambridge centre replaces GSK's Stevenage site, which the company has run for more than half a century and will fully vacate by 2029. Some research will shift to upgraded laboratories in nearby Ware, Hertfordshire, but the flagship move is to Cambridge, where the new site will cover oncology, respiratory disease, hepatology, vaccines and HIV.

"This investment will accelerate our R&D and help us deliver new, competitive products," GSK chief executive Luke Miels said, adding that the move reflects "the attractiveness of the UK's life sciences ecosystem."

Chief scientific officer Tony Wood put it more simply: Cambridge, he said, "has built one of the world's best life sciences ecosystems, with leading universities, hospitals and biotech companies" — the same cluster logic that has already pulled AstraZeneca's global headquarters, the Wellcome Sanger Institute and Cambridge University's hospitals onto the same few hundred acres. New prime minister Andy Burnham, less than a fortnight into the job, called the investment "a vote of confidence in British business." GSK's total R&D budget runs to roughly £6bn a year worldwide, with more than £1.5bn of that already spent in the UK.

Paying for data, not software

The Relation Therapeutics deal is easy to misread as GSK licensing an AI model. It is not. MORGAN — Multi-Omic Regulatory Genomics using Artificial Neural Networks — is Relation's own foundation model, designed to predict how a human cell will respond to a genetic tweak or an experimental drug. GSK isn't paying to use it. GSK is paying Relation up to $110m, including cash already advanced plus milestone payments, to manufacture the biological data — large-scale human cellular perturbation experiments across immunology, inflammation and bone and metabolic disease — that MORGAN needs to be trained on in the first place.

"Deepening our understanding of the underlying biology of disease starts with richer data, to be used in models that can give us greater confidence in the discovery of therapeutic targets that can ultimately yield medicines," said David Roblin, Relation's chief executive.

It is a distinction with real consequences. Good models are now relatively cheap to build; the bottleneck is clean, large, purpose-generated experimental data, and producing it at scale requires wet labs, robotics and trained staff — infrastructure, in other words, not code. The arrangement builds on a smaller 2024 collaboration between the two companies and sits alongside Relation's separate pact with Novartis, struck in December, worth $55m upfront against as much as $1.7bn in potential milestones. Two large pharma companies effectively renting the same AI biotech's data-generation capacity in the space of eight months is a reasonable proxy for how quickly this kind of infrastructure spend is accelerating.

The price of entry is rising

GSK is not alone on the campus, and that is rather the point. AstraZeneca reversed a decision to pause its own £200m Cambridge expansion earlier this year, restoring funding for what it calls a "digital lab" intended to run AI over its own drug-discovery data, and separately committed £100m to a "lab of the future" in Macclesfield. AstraZeneca, GSK and the University of Cambridge already co-fund a joint AI-in-medicine research centre on the same turf. What's forming in Cambridge looks less like a shared research district and more like a set of competing, expensively walled compounds that happen to share a postcode.

The pattern across both companies is the same: buy or build the physical capacity to generate proprietary biological data, then buy or build the models trained on it, and keep both in-house rather than renting either from a startup. That is a materially more expensive way to compete than licensing an AI drug-discovery platform off the shelf, and it is one that only a handful of companies GSK's size can fund at all. A smaller biotech can still partner with a Relation or an Exscientia for a slice of a specific programme. Building the equivalent of a 300,000 sq ft campus and a bespoke data-generation contract is a different order of commitment — one that pushes the effective cost of competing in algorithmic drug discovery toward £500m and up, concentrating the field around the pharma majors with balance sheets to match.

What to watch

Construction on the Cambridge site is expected to run over the next three years, with Stevenage's closure completing by 2029 — a long runway during which GSK's R&D productivity, and whether MORGAN actually shortens the path from target to approved drug, will be the real test of whether this spending was infrastructure or overhead. GSK's next quarterly results will be the first chance to see whether investors are pricing in the cost of that bet; the stock has traded roughly 10% below analyst consensus targets since the announcements landed. The more immediate marker is Relation itself — a third major pharma partnership on top of GSK and Novartis within a year would confirm this is now the going rate for admission to the field, not a one-off.
