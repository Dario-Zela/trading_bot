China's H1 growth holds at 4.7% as Politburo doubles down on 'AI Plus'

The Great Hall of the People in Beijing, where China's top political bodies convene. — Xiquinho Silva / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
China's H1 growth held at 4.7%, but a Politburo debt-constrained by 300%+ leverage is now leaning on AI and infrastructure spending as its only viable growth lever.

China's economy grew 4.7% in the first half of 2026, the National Bureau of Statistics said on July 15 — a number that lands squarely inside Beijing's 4.5-5% target band and, on the surface, looks like more of the same grinding, managed growth China has produced for several years running. Look one layer down and the composition tells a sharper story: the government is no longer treating artificial intelligence and infrastructure spending as a lever to pull when growth wobbles. It has made them the plan.

At the Politburo's July 30 meeting — the leadership's regular mid-year economic stock-take — officials flagged what they called new growth drivers and an improving economic structure, and called for deeper implementation of the "AI Plus" initiative alongside stronger basic research and tighter AI governance. Coming from a body that rarely commits to specifics, the language was pointed: this is now core industrial policy, not a cyclical prop.

The headline number, unpacked

H1 GDP reached roughly 69.57 trillion yuan ($10.3 trillion), but the two quarters that make up that figure diverged sharply. First-quarter growth ran at 5%; the second quarter slowed to 4.3%, with the sequential quarter-on-quarter rate falling to just 0.9%. The gap between industrial output and retail sales — a persistent theme this cycle — widened further: manufacturing value-added grew 5.6%, while retail sales limped along at 1.3% for the half.

The supply side told a more interesting story than the headline. High-tech manufacturing value-added rose 13.3% year-on-year, with standout gains in 3D printers (up 48.5%), lithium-ion batteries (up 39.3%) and industrial robots (up 28%). Semiconductor output grew 18.8% in June alone. New growth drivers — the Politburo's own term for high-end manufacturing, the digital economy and modern services — contributed more than 40% of industrial expansion in the first half, according to state data, the highest share on record. Meanwhile construction kept dragging: cement production fell 8%, property sales 13.6%.

That divergence is the story in miniature. China's old growth engine — real estate and heavy construction — is still deflating. Its replacement, an economy built around chips, robots, batteries and AI-driven "new quality productive forces," is scaling fast enough to hold the headline number up, but not yet fast enough to fix the demand-side imbalance underneath it.

Why the Politburo can't just spend its way out

The obvious response to weak consumption would be direct fiscal transfers to households. Beijing has consistently avoided that path, and the debt math explains why. China's macro leverage ratio — total government, corporate and household debt relative to GDP — rose to roughly 302% in 2025, according to a report from the National Institution for Finance and Development, a research body affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences. That is a level exceeding both the US and the eurozone, driven in large part by state and local-government borrowing that has backfilled the hole left by the property collapse.

Goldman Sachs analysts have described a set of structural constraints — deteriorating demographics, a multi-year debt deleveraging trend, and global supply-chain de-risking — that leave less room for the kind of broad-based stimulus China deployed after 2008 or 2015. Economists tracking the July meeting, including Tsinghua's David Daokui Li, have pointed to local governments in particular as fiscally boxed in: falling land-sale revenue and existing debt service leave little room to expand spending on their own account, pushing more of the burden onto the central government's balance sheet and onto targeted, centrally-directed programs rather than direct-to-household relief.

That's the logic that makes AI and infrastructure spending look less like a choice and more like the only lever left. Building out data centres, computing capacity and grid infrastructure is spending that shows up as investment and can be financed through central-government and policy-bank bonds — a channel Beijing still has room to use — rather than through the recurring transfers that a debt-constrained fiscal position can't sustain.

The mechanics of 'AI Plus'

The framework itself isn't new — Beijing's State Council issued guidance on "AI Plus" in 2024, and it has since expanded to six designated action areas spanning science, production, consumption, public services, governance and external relations, with a target of deep AI integration across those fields by 2027. What changed at the July meeting was the emphasis and the company it kept: AI Plus was named alongside the "Six Networks" initiative — water, power grids, computing power, communications, urban pipelines and logistics — as the primary channel for expanding domestic investment demand in the second half of the year. Investment tied to the Six Networks programme is projected to exceed 7 trillion yuan in 2026.

Officials also called for "stable long-term support for basic research" and frontier-technology breakthroughs — language that, per analysts tracking the meeting's wording against April's readout, was notably more forceful than the prior instruction to simply "make good and full use" of existing measures. The shift from cross-cyclical to countercyclical framing, and from managing existing tools to deploying incremental ones "with force," suggests Beijing sees the underlying weakness — that supply-demand gap — as more urgent than it was letting on three months ago.

What it means for positioning

For traders, the read-through is less about whether China hits 4.7% or 4.5% this year and more about durability. A policy built on debt-financed household transfers would be vulnerable to the next downturn in tax revenue or bond issuance capacity. A policy built on AI infrastructure, semiconductors and six categories of physical network investment is harder to reverse quickly — it involves multi-year capital programmes, state-owned enterprise mandates and now, explicitly, Politburo-level backing. That argues for treating China's tilt toward AI and hard infrastructure as a structural feature of the next several years rather than a stimulus package that fades once growth stabilises.

It also narrows Beijing's options if a shock — a sharper property downturn, a fresh round of US tariffs, a credit event among heavily-indebted local government financing vehicles — arrives before the new growth drivers are large enough to carry the economy alone. With leverage above 300% of GDP, the room to respond with the kind of blunt, broad stimulus deployed in past cycles looks thinner than at any point this decade. The property sector's 13.6% sales decline is the pressure point most likely to test that constraint first.

The next formal marker is October's Fifth Plenum, which will set economic direction heading into 2027 and the drafting of the 15th Five-Year Plan's implementation details — though officials have signalled that session will focus as much on Party discipline as on economic policy. Before that, August and September trade and industrial-output data will show whether the high-tech manufacturing surge that propped up H1 growth is broadening or narrowing as the property drag persists.
