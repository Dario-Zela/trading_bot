Thames Water creditors offer government a 'golden share' as MPs push mutual ownership

Prime Minister Andy Burnham arrives at 10 Downing Street on 20 July 2026, days before Thames Water's creditors made their golden-share offer. — Simon Dawson / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Thames Water's creditors offered ministers a Royal Mail-style veto right on a £3.35bn rescue, as Labour MPs push a rival plan to mutualise the debt-laden utility instead.

Thames Water's creditors have decided the surest way to keep the government out of the company is to let it in — a little.

On 21 July, London & Valley Water, the consortium of investment funds now running Britain's biggest water utility, put a revised rescue plan to ministers. It centres on a familiar-sounding number: £3.35bn of fresh equity, plus £6.25bn of new borrowing, against £9.6bn of existing debt written off. What's new is the sweetener attached — a "golden share" that would hand the government a permanent veto over the company's most consequential decisions, modelled directly on the arrangement struck when Royal Mail was sold to Czech billionaire Daniel Křetínský's EP Group last year.

What's on the table

A golden share doesn't buy the government a stake in the company. It buys a lock on the door. Under the Royal Mail precedent, ministers can block a sale, a foreign takeover, or a move of the headquarters and tax residency out of the UK — without owning a single ordinary share themselves. Thames Water's creditors, who between them hold roughly £17bn of the company's £21bn debt pile, are offering the same mechanism, plus a role for local authorities in oversight. Mike McTighe, the turnaround specialist steering the consortium, framed it as recognition of reality rather than generosity: "We fully recognise that Thames Water is a crucial part of our critical national infrastructure. We will work with Andy Burnham, his Government and local authority leaders."

The consortium — Apollo Global Management, Elliott Management, Farallon Capital and Silver Point Capital among them — has reason to be accommodating. An earlier version of this plan was rejected in the spring by then-environment secretary Emma Reynolds on the grounds it would leave customers footing the bill. Since then, the politics have hardened. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July, days before the offer landed, having said on the campaign trail that there was "a very strong case for public ownership" of the company. His team had not engaged with the creditors' proposal as of publication.

The arithmetic ministers are doing

The reason Burnham hasn't simply nationalised Thames Water outright is that doing so is not free. The company is expected to run out of cash by October, and putting it into a special administration regime — a temporary form of state control used when a regulated utility can't pay its way — carries an estimated cost to the taxpayer of at least £4bn, according to advisory firm Teneo. Ofwat, the regulator, still has to sign off on the wider restructuring, including a plan to swap financial penalties for binding "undertakings" to fix leaks and sewage spills, before the deal can go to a three-month public consultation and the High Court for creditor write-down approval. That is a great deal of process to complete before an October deadline that is now less than three months away.

A Burnham ally put the calculation to the Sunday Times in blunter terms: "If it is going to cost the taxpayer £2bn to keep the company afloat then the taxpayer needs to receive something in return; that means control." The golden share is the creditors' answer to that demand — control without the government having to write a cheque, or take Thames Water's £21bn of debt onto the public balance sheet. Deputy Labour leader Lucy Powell has said the prime minister is genuinely weighing alternatives to a straightforward state rescue, telling reporters Burnham "wants to look at and take on" models that put ordinary consumers first.

The mutual option waiting in the wings

The golden share is not the only structural alternative on the table. A group of Labour MPs and metro mayors, led by Hastings and Rye MP Helena Dollimore, has proposed something more radical: that water companies exiting special administration be converted into not-for-profit cooperatives rather than resold to private investors at all. Dollimore, who sits with the Co-operative Party as well as Labour, wants to "end the era of extraction" in the water industry, and has named Thames Water as the obvious test case given its financial state.

The idea has regional political weight behind it. West Yorkshire mayor Tracy Braib argues "greater public control of water companies is the only way to protect our communities," while South Yorkshire's Oliver Coppard frames it as a bill-management tool: a mutual structure would "put control back in the hands of people, help keep bills as low as possible." Praful Nargund of the Good Growth Foundation makes the fiscal case explicit, calling mutualisation a way to secure "the public control and long-term investment we need without taxpayer costs" — the same promise the creditors are making for their golden-share plan, just with the ownership structure inverted.

There is an irony sitting underneath both proposals that is easy to miss: Burnham himself is the first British prime minister to hold Co-operative Party membership, the same party whose general secretary, Joe Fortune, backs Dollimore's mutualisation push. Downing Street has given no sign it plans to nationalise Thames Water outright, but has not ruled out either the golden share or the cooperative model — and the prime minister's own political home sits closer to one of them than his officials have yet let on.

What it says about the model

What both proposals share is an admission. Four decades after water was privatised in England and Wales, the industry's owners are the ones now proposing to dilute the very control rights that made privatisation worth doing — because the alternative, for a company as distressed as Thames Water, is losing the asset altogether. A golden share and a cooperative structure sit at opposite ends of the ownership spectrum, but both concede that unfettered private control of a monopoly utility with £21bn of debt and a crumbling asset base is no longer politically or financially tenable. Whichever way Thames Water goes, the settlement will function as a template for the rest of a sector carrying more than £60bn of combined debt.

The immediate clock is Ofwat's, not Westminster's. Its decision on the regulatory undertakings that unlock the creditors' financing is expected in the coming weeks, with a three-month consultation and High Court approval still to follow before Thames Water's October cash-out date. Whether Burnham's government engages with the golden share, waves through Dollimore's cooperative model, or lets the clock run down into special administration will be the first real test of how his premiership treats Britain's privatised utilities.
