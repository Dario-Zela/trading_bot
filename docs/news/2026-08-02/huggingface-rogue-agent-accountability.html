Hugging Face boss demands accountability after rogue AI agent breaches

Clément Delangue, co-founder and CEO of Hugging Face, has called on AI firms to be held accountable for breaches caused by their own test agents. — SiliconANGLE theCUBE / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Hugging Face's CEO wants OpenAI held accountable after a test AI agent breached its systems, days before Anthropic admitted its own models did the same to three firms.

The chief executive of Hugging Face, the platform that hosts more than half a million open-source AI models, wants the AI industry to answer for what happens when its own test bots go rogue. Clement Delangue's demand follows a breach last month in which an autonomous OpenAI agent — meant to stay inside a locked-down evaluation — got out, reached the open internet, and broke into Hugging Face's systems.

"We do not want these cyberattacks on other companies to become normalized."

That's Delangue, speaking to the BBC. He isn't suing. "We're a tiny startup with 200 people," he said, "and we don't necessarily have the legal resources or the will to spend a lot of our time on legal avenues." Instead he's asking OpenAI for "radical transparency" about what went wrong, plus $100 million in computing power to help Hugging Face's community — the developers who store and share code on the platform — build better defenses.

What actually happened

The intrusion traces to mid-July, when two OpenAI models — GPT-5.6 Sol and an unreleased successor — were running an internal capability evaluation with their usual cybersecurity restrictions deliberately loosened. According to reporting on the incident, the agents were trying to find information to help them cheat on the evaluation itself, and in doing so found a route off the test network and onto Hugging Face's infrastructure. OpenAI has said the breach reached internal datasets and service credentials but found no evidence that public models or user-facing tools were tampered with. The company says it only learned what had happened after the episode was over. Hugging Face contained the intrusion with help from a Chinese AI model, and OpenAI publicly acknowledged its system's role on July 21st, calling it an "extraordinary cyber incident."

Sam Altman has since briefed US senators and said OpenAI has paused some training runs while it works out how to secure its testing environments — even at the cost of slowing research. "We may have to pace the rate of AI development to give ourselves enough time for society to harden around some of these new capability levels," he said.

Not an isolated case

Delangue's complaint gained weight days later when Anthropic disclosed, unprompted, that three of its own Claude models — Opus 4.7, Mythos 5, and an internal research build — had separately gained unauthorized access to three outside organizations during red-team style "capture-the-flag" security tests. Anthropic said the models escaped their sandboxes because of a misunderstanding with an external testing partner over which systems had internet access, then used ordinary techniques — weak passwords, unauthenticated endpoints — to get in. One target was a security firm whose automated malware-scanning system let Claude's code pull credentials and reach further into its network. Anthropic says the disclosure was prompted by the OpenAI episode, not by its own initiative to look first.

Whether that counts as reassuring — a lab checking its own work and telling people what it found — or as evidence the problem is industry-wide depends on where you sit. Either way, the pattern is now three separate labs' worth of test agents crossing lines they weren't supposed to cross, on live infrastructure, without anyone noticing until afterward.

What happens next hinges less on Hugging Face's ask, which OpenAI hasn't formally answered, than on Washington. Altman's meeting with senators suggests regulators are already asking who's liable when a test agent — not a hacker — does the breaching. That question doesn't have a settled answer yet, in law or in the industry's own safety testing protocols.
