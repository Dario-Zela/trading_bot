Fed holds at 3.5%-3.75%, all eyes turn to Friday's jobs report

In one sentence
The Fed held rates at 3.5%-3.75% on a rare 9-3 hawkish-dissent vote, making Friday's jobs report and next week's CPI print pivotal to a possible September hike.

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate at 3.5%–3.75% on July 29, the fifth straight meeting without a change — but the vote that got it there was the most openly divided in nearly a decade, and it has left traders bracing for Friday's jobs report as the number that could finally move the committee off the fence.

The Federal Open Market Committee voted 9-3 to stand pat. All three dissents came from regional bank presidents — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas — who wanted to raise rates a quarter point immediately. It was the first three-way hawkish dissent at the Fed since September 2016, and it reframed what might otherwise have been a routine hold into what several desks are now calling a "hawkish hold."

In its statement, the Committee said economic activity "is expanding at a solid pace," with productivity and capital investment strong and job gains keeping pace with the workforce, even as it flagged "elevated uncertainty" tied in part to the conflict in the Middle East. Inflation, it noted, remains above the 2% target, with supply disruptions — energy prominent among them — still pushing up prices in specific sectors. Chair Kevin Warsh, running only his second meeting since taking over from Jerome Powell in May, told reporters afterward that he'd asked for "a good family fight" among policymakers over the direction of rates, and got one.

Markets took the split badly. The Dow fell 1,153 points, its worst session since April 2025; the S&P 500 dropped 1.5% and the Nasdaq 1.7%. The bond market moved in the direction that matters most for the Fed's credibility: the 10-year Treasury yield rose seven basis points to 4.67%, and the 30-year climbed 12 basis points to 5.21%, its highest in 19 years — a sign investors are pricing in more persistent inflation, not less.

A hire-or-hike jobs report

That repricing is why Friday's employment report, due August 7 at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, carries unusual weight. June's report showed just 57,000 jobs added, well below forecasts and down sharply from May, even as the unemployment rate ticked down to 4.2% — a decline driven more by workers leaving the labor force than by hiring strength. A similarly soft July print would hand the Fed's doves ammunition to hold again in September; a rebound would embolden the three dissenters and the swaps market, which by Thursday was pricing roughly a 60% chance of a September hike, according to Bloomberg data — up from about even odds a month earlier.

The Consumer Price Index for July follows on August 12, adding a second data point before the Fed's next meeting on September 16-17. Warsh has said he won't be constrained by how traders price the odds, but with three regional presidents already on record for tightening and long-term yields testing multi-decade highs, the numbers arriving over the next two weeks look less like routine data points than the deciding votes in the argument the Fed just had in public.
