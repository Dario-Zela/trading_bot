War keeps fuel prices elevated as Big Oil's wartime profits surge

A Chevron oil refinery — the world's refiners are running near-full tilt as war knocks out roughly a tenth of global capacity. — Discott / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Exxon and Chevron say war has taken ~9% of global refining capacity offline, keeping fuel prices high for months — even as the same shock drove Big Oil's Q2 profits to $14.5bn, $12.1bn and $9.8bn.

Gasoline at the pump is averaging just over $4.10 a gallon in the United States this week, jet fuel is running near $7.70, and neither is coming down soon. That is the blunt message from the two biggest American oil companies, delivered not as a warning to markets but as an explanation for why their own second-quarter profits just went vertical.

In one sentence: War has knocked out roughly a tenth of the world's refining capacity, and Exxon and Chevron say the resulting fuel-price squeeze — which just helped deliver blowout quarterly profits — will outlast the summer.

ExxonMobil booked $14.5 billion in net income for the quarter, more than double the year-earlier figure and its best showing since 2022, when the last major shock — Russia's invasion of Ukraine — sent crude prices spiking. Chevron did even better in relative terms: profit of roughly $12 billion against $2.5 billion a year ago, a near-400% jump and the highest quarterly result in the company's history. Shell's adjusted earnings came in at $9.8 billion, up 128% and its second-best quarter in four years. Combined, the three companies earned something like $400 million a day for three months running.

A refining system with nowhere to hide

What makes this earnings season different from the usual crude-price story is where executives say the real bottleneck sits — not in the ground, but in the refineries that turn oil into the gasoline, diesel and jet fuel people actually buy. Iran's effective closure of the Strait of Hormuz, sustained Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining infrastructure, and a Chinese export ban have together taken close to 10% of global refining capacity offline, according to figures cited by both companies. Roughly 5 million barrels a day of refined-product capacity, by their account, simply cannot reach world markets right now.

The plants still running are running hard. ExxonMobil's Gulf Coast refineries operated at 95% utilization in the second quarter; Chevron's U.S. facilities hit 97%; Shell's ran above 100% before scheduled maintenance pulls that rate back down. There is, in other words, very little slack left to absorb a further shock. "I've never seen the available capacity relative to demand as low as it is today," ExxonMobil chief executive Darren Woods said. "It's going to take a while for the industry to climb its way out of that hole."

Exxon's finance chief, Neil Hansen, put the point more sharply: refining, not crude supply, is "the constraint pain point in the energy system" — and one he suggested markets have been slow to price in properly, focused as they typically are on oil production and OPEC+ decisions rather than downstream capacity.

Why crude falling hasn't brought relief

The disconnect shows up starkly in the numbers. West Texas Intermediate crude has fallen roughly 26% from its May peak, yet retail gasoline is down only about 10% over the same stretch; diesel has fallen just a sixth as much as crude in percentage terms. Normally, cheaper crude flows fairly quickly into cheaper fuel at the pump. This time it hasn't, because the constraint isn't the price of the raw input — it's the physical capacity to process it. A refinery running at 97% has essentially nothing left to give even if crude gets cheaper by the barrel.

Chevron chief executive Mike Wirth was direct about where that leaves prices through the autumn: "I think we're going to see some upward pressure on product pricing here into the third quarter and perhaps beyond that." His finance chief, Eimear Bonner, framed it as the erosion of a buffer that had until now kept things from getting worse: "The shock absorbers that have mitigated the volatility up until now, those continue to be drawn down."

The structural argument — and who pays for it

The distinction the companies are drawing — structural versus cyclical — is not academic. A cyclical shortage resolves itself as prices rise, demand softens and supply catches up over a few months. A structural one, rooted in refineries destroyed, sanctioned or rerouted by an active war, does not resolve until the underlying conflict does, or until new capacity is built — a process that takes years, not weeks, in an industry that has been reluctant to invest in new refining for over a decade.

That asymmetry is precisely what makes this earnings season notable beyond the headline totals. Crude oil is a globally traded, heavily hedged commodity; airlines, shippers and fleet operators have decades of tools — futures, swaps, forward purchase agreements — for managing its swings. Refined products are a thinner, more regional market with far less liquid hedging infrastructure. A trucking company or airline can lock in a crude price reasonably well; locking in a diesel or jet-fuel price is harder and more expensive, precisely when the gap between the two has never mattered more. The result is that the cost of the war is landing disproportionately on the businesses least equipped to absorb it, while the companies that own the refineries and produce the crude post record quarters.

What comes next

The near-term catalyst is the pace of the Iran conflict itself: a ceasefire or a reopened Hormuz corridor would ease the pressure faster than any refinery could be rebuilt. Barring that, the next data points worth watching are the EIA's weekly gasoline and diesel figures — the next release lands August 4 — and third-quarter refining-margin guidance from the majors, which will show whether utilization rates can climb any further or whether 95–97% is effectively the ceiling. Until either the war cools or new capacity comes online, Exxon and Chevron's message amounts to the same thing said two different ways: elevated fuel costs are not a spike to be waited out, but the new baseline.
