Trump pauses Iran strikes 'subject to' a deal as Kuwait downs Iranian drones

Trump's February 28, 2026 social media announcement of the strikes that opened the war on Iran, now in its sixth month. — Donald J. Trump / Executive Office of the President (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump paused planned Iran strikes 'subject to' a deal Tehran's own negotiator denies exists, while Kuwait downed Iranian drones hours later.

Five months into a war that has killed Iran's supreme leader, closed and reopened the Strait of Hormuz half a dozen times and dragged the Gulf's smaller states into a fight they never chose, Donald Trump said Saturday he was calling off a fresh round of planned strikes on Iran — for now. The condition, in his own words on Truth Social, was that Washington and Tehran could "rapidly make a DEAL." Within hours, Kuwait's air defences were shooting down Iranian drones aimed at a government facility and civilian vehicles on Bubiyan Island, and Iran's own parliamentary speaker was publicly denying any deal existed at all.

That contradiction — a president announcing a pause built on negotiations the other side says aren't happening — is the story now. Markets that have spent five months pricing war risk into every barrel of oil are being asked to decide whether this is the beginning of an off-ramp or just the latest pause in a conflict that has repeatedly found reasons to continue.

A pause with conditions attached

Trump's language was carefully hedged. The U.S., he said, remained "locked and loaded and ready to go" but had agreed to cancel the next wave of attacks "subject to being able to rapidly make a DEAL" — one that would require, in his telling, the "Immediate, Complete, and Total OPENING" of the Strait of Hormuz and an end to Iran's nuclear ambitions. It was the kind of statement built to be claimed as a win however events unfold: proof of diplomatic progress if talks hold, justification for resumed strikes if they don't.

Iran's response undercut the framing almost immediately. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, the parliament speaker who has emerged as Tehran's de facto interlocutor with Washington — reportedly in indirect contact with envoy Steve Witkoff and Trump son-in-law Jared Kushner — said flatly that "no negotiations have been held with the US," and accused the administration of using "fake news" to manipulate oil and financial markets. It is not the first time the two sides have offered dueling accounts of the same conversation; Trump has repeatedly described talks as "constructive" or "productive" only for Iranian officials to deny they occurred as described, or at all.

The war itself began on February 28, when the U.S. and Israel launched Operation Epic Fury after Tehran refused to halt uranium enrichment. The opening strikes killed Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, a scale of decapitation that made a short conflict — the kind Trump initially suggested was possible — much harder to achieve, since a fractured Iranian state has fewer channels through which any single deal can bind the whole apparatus, including its regional proxies and drone units.

Kuwait in the crossfire

That command fragmentation is visible in Kuwait, which has become one of the war's most exposed bystanders by virtue of hosting Camp Arifjan, Camp Buehring and Ali Al Salem Air Base — American installations Iranian officials have named directly as targets should Washington strike Iranian soil. On Saturday, Kuwait's Defence Ministry said its forces had detected "hostile drones" in national airspace since early morning and destroyed them before they reached their targets, though falling debris caused damage to a government facility and civilian company vehicles on Bubiyan Island in the country's north. No injuries were reported.

It was not an isolated incident. Iran has struck US-linked sites in Bahrain, Jordan, Qatar and the UAE at various points since February, and officials in Tehran have warned that any state "cooperating" with American forces risks being drawn further into the war — with some rhetoric going as far as floating cross-border operations against Kuwaiti bases, a threat most regional analysts still regard as far beyond Iran's conventional capacity to execute. The drone strike lands at an awkward moment for that argument: it came after Trump's pause announcement, not before it, suggesting either that Iranian units are not fully synchronized with whatever diplomatic channel Tehran is or isn't using, or that the strikes were meant precisely to demonstrate that a presidential statement in Washington changes nothing on the ground.

The State Department's response was to reiterate, not soften, its posture: Americans in the Middle East should consider departing now, and those elsewhere should reconsider travel to the region altogether. It is advisory language the department has used repeatedly since March, when a more urgent "DEPART NOW" warning went out for Iran, Iraq, Jordan, Lebanon and Israel. Its persistence through a supposed de-escalation signal is itself a data point: the U.S. government's own risk assessment has not moved.

What markets are actually pricing

Oil has moved on almost every twist of this war, and Hormuz has been the fulcrum. Roughly 27% of the world's seaborne crude and products trade passes through the strait, and Iranian forces have periodically declared it closed since early March, attacking tankers attempting transit. Brent crude has risen close to a fifth since the war's opening in February, and traffic through the strait has fallen by more than half in some recent weeks as shippers reroute or wait out the risk. A credible reopening — the centrepiece of the deal Trump described — would be the single biggest bullish catalyst oil markets have had all year for lower prices.

That is exactly why the conditional wording matters more than the headline. A pause "subject to" a deal is not a ceasefire; it is a suspended sentence. Traders who spent five months learning that every de-escalation signal from this war has come with an expiration date — and that Iranian drone units keep operating on their own schedule regardless of what Washington announces — have little reason to fully unwind the risk premium on a single Truth Social post, particularly one the other side denies is grounded in any actual negotiation.

What comes next

The test is not what Trump says but whether the pause survives contact with the next Iranian strike, or the next Israeli one. Ghalibaf's denial suggests Tehran either isn't ready to confirm a framework publicly or genuinely hasn't agreed to one — a distinction traders will struggle to price cleanly either way. Watch the Strait of Hormuz transit data over the coming days as the most concrete signal of whether "opening" is more than a talking point, and watch whether CENTCOM, which has continued strikes on Iranian energy targets even after Trump's announcement, treats the pause as binding or as one more pause among several. Until Kuwait, Bahrain and Qatar stop reporting intercepted drones, the market's risk premium has little reason to move.
