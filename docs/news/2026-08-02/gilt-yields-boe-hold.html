Gilt yields stay above 5% after BoE's split 6-3 hold

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose Monetary Policy Committee split 6-3 to hold rates. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
BoE held Bank Rate at 3.75% on a 6-3 vote — but the three dissenters wanted a hike, not a cut, and gilt yields are stuck above 5% as a result.

UK borrowing costs are stuck at levels not seen since the spring, and Thursday's Bank of England decision did nothing to shift them. The Monetary Policy Committee voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, but the margin was narrower than the 7-2 split traders had priced going in — and the three dissenters were not pushing for an earlier cut. They wanted a hike.

Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill all voted to raise Bank Rate a quarter-point to 4%, arguing that energy-driven inflation risk had grown too large to sit out. The Bank's own minutes conceded the point even as the majority held firm, warning that higher energy prices are "likely to push inflation higher later this year, with risks now tilted to the upside."

Higher energy prices are likely to push inflation higher later this year, with risks now tilted to the upside.

That matters because it inverts the debate markets had been having. Through the spring, the question was how soon the MPC would start cutting. Thursday's vote reframed it: whether the committee can hold the line at all, or whether the next move is up rather than down.

Oil does the talking

Brent crude has risen roughly 23% over the past month, touching above $90 a barrel after the deaths of US service members and a sharp escalation in Middle East hostilities, before settling near $87.84 on 31 July. That has fed straight into the UK inflation print: headline CPI rose to 2.2% in July from 2.0% in June, even as core inflation — which strips out food and energy — eased slightly to 3.3% from 3.5%. The divergence is the whole story in miniature: underlying price pressure is cooling, but the energy shock is arriving fast enough to overwhelm it in the headline number the MPC has to answer for.

Gilt markets read the vote as confirmation that the hold cycle will run longer than assumed. The 10-year yield touched 5.06% last week, its highest since May, and has held above the 5% threshold since the decision — a level that raises the government's refinancing costs just as the Debt Management Office works through a heavy autumn issuance calendar. Every extra basis point on the curve is money out of the fiscal envelope Rachel Reeves is already managing tightly.

What the split signals

A 6-3 vote with hawks on the losing side is a different animal from a 6-3 vote with doves losing. It tells markets the committee's center of gravity has not shifted toward easing — if anything, three members think the majority is already behind the curve on inflation. That closes off, for now, the near-term cut scenarios sterling and rates desks had begun to build into curve positioning.

The next test comes at the MPC's 17 September meeting, by which point at least one more CPI print and clearer signal on where Brent settles should be in hand. Until then, gilts above 5% look less like an anomaly and more like the market's honest read of a central bank that just told everyone its hawks are gaining ground, not losing it.
