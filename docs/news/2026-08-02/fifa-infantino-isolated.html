Infantino's $20bn FIFA overhaul crumbles as adviser quits, Asia joins opposition

FIFA president Gianni Infantino, whose plan to sell a stake in the World Cup to private investors collapsed within days of its unveiling. — Ricardo Stuckert / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Infantino's $20bn Kushner-backed World Cup investment plan collapsed within a week as UEFA, Cordeiro, CONCACAF and Asia's confederation revolted — and UEFA now says it has lost confidence in him entirely.

Six days. That is roughly how long it took Gianni Infantino's plan to sell a stake in the World Cup to go from unveiling to wreckage, taking a chunk of his own authority down with it. On July 28, FIFA announced it wanted to fold its commercial rights and tournament operations — including the men's and women's World Cups — into a new $20 billion subsidiary, selling about a fifth of it to outside investors. By the following Friday the plan was dead, FIFA's own chief operating officer was accusing the president of deceiving his staff, and by Saturday UEFA had gone further still, declaring it no longer had confidence in Infantino's leadership at all.

The episode is a case study in what happens when a sports body tries to run a private-equity-style transaction the way it runs everything else — from the top, fast, and without lining up the stakeholders first.

The pitch

The vehicle was to be called FIFA Forward Enterprise, and the mechanics were straightforward enough on paper. FIFA would carry its broadcasting, sponsorship, ticketing and licensing rights, plus the operational running of its events, into the new entity, then sell roughly 20 percent of it to external investors to raise up to $4.2 billion — implying a $20 billion valuation for the whole. FIFA had lined up JPMorgan to run the process, and named its anchor investor: Thrive Eternal, a fund tied to Joshua Kushner, the venture capitalist who founded Thrive Capital and is the younger brother of Jared Kushner, Donald Trump's son-in-law and a co-chair of the White House task force for next year's World Cup.

The optics — a Trump-adjacent financier taking a stake in football's showcase tournament, decided without a vote of FIFA's member federations — did most of the damage on their own.

The revolt

UEFA moved first and hardest. At an emergency meeting on July 30, all 55 of its member associations voted to boycott every FIFA competition, the World Cup included, unless the plan was dropped. "The World Cup is not for sale," the confederation said, calling the process a secret scheme cooked up on a fast-track timescale.

The next day, Carlos Cordeiro resigned. A former Goldman Sachs banker and past president of the U.S. Soccer Federation, Cordeiro had been Infantino's own representative on the White House task force for the 2026 tournament — hardly an outsider. "I cannot stand by while FIFA considers selling a stake in the World Cup," he wrote, calling the subsidiary "a bad deal for football" and noting that FIFA already sits on billions in reserves and carries no debt, making the case for the sale hard to square. He urged other senior staff to speak out too.

Some did. FIFA's chief operating officer, Kevin Lamour, told colleagues the plan was "a project of one person" and that staff had been "deceived." On the same Friday, the Asian Football Confederation — 47 member associations — added its voice, saying the episode had "exposed fundamental weaknesses in FIFA's consultation and decision-making processes" and calling for an urgent governance review, though it stopped short of threatening a boycott. CONCACAF, representing 41 members across North and Central America and the Caribbean, rejected the plan outright. FIFA had originally set September 19 as the date by which it wanted federations to formally back the proposal; the opposition made that deadline irrelevant six weeks early.

Retreat, then reckoning

Facing three of FIFA's six confederations in open revolt and his own inner circle abandoning him, Infantino folded late on July 31: "This proposal will not proceed," he said in a statement.

That did not end it. On August 1, UEFA escalated from opposing the deal to opposing Infantino himself, saying his leadership "has not only lost UEFA's confidence but also that of many other members of the football family" and demanding a full accounting of who devised the plan and how. "We must identify those responsible and hold them to account," the statement read. CONCACAF followed with its own rebuke, describing "a pattern of missteps" and "leadership that has stopped putting football first," and insisting that "accountability cannot be optional."

The political undertow is now the story. Victor Montagliani, CONCACAF's president and a FIFA vice-president, is reported to be weighing a challenge to Infantino at the next FIFA presidential election, scheduled for March 18, 2027, in Morocco — the clearest sign yet that this was not simply a failed transaction but a crisis of trust in how Infantino runs the organization.

What to watch

FIFA has not said who will lead the promised governance review, or whether it will publish findings before the 2026 World Cup kicks off across the U.S., Mexico and Canada next summer. For anyone watching the broader trend of private capital moving into major sports properties — stakes in leagues, federations, even international tournaments — the lesson here is not that investors were wrong to be interested, but that the deal died on governance grounds before it ever reached a term sheet fight. Infantino tried to sign the transaction before signing up the votes. The candidacy deadline for FIFA's presidency falls in November; whether Montagliani or another challenger actually enters the race will be the next marker of how much damage this episode has done.
