Cambridge professor defends work amid plagiarism row, calls criticism 'racially motivated'

Cambridge University's Senate House, where the plagiarism row over Professor Jason Arday's doctoral work has become a proxy fight over race and academic scrutiny. — Cmglee / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Arday admits errors but denies lying, casting the plagiarism row as racially motivated as 12,000+ people, including MPs, back him.

Jason Arday spent the first days of August doing something he had spent his career avoiding: publicly defending his own work, sentence by sentence.

The Cambridge sociologist — who in 2023 became, at 37, the youngest Black professor in the university's history — has spent the past fortnight fending off accusations that large sections of his 2015 PhD thesis and at least one journal article lifted passages, in some cases nearly word for word, from other scholars' work without proper credit. Arday does not deny the errors. He denies the word "liar."

"What I am not is a liar," he told The Times. He has described the affair as "a very well orchestrated and co-ordinated campaign to unseat me," and said its motive was "aligned to ableism and racism."

The allegations

The row began last month when Nathan Cofnas, a philosopher and former Cambridge academic who has clashed publicly with the university over its diversity policies and is now suing it, published an analysis identifying dozens of passages in Arday's dissertation that closely mirrored a 2009 PhD thesis by Paula Zwozdiak-Myers, a researcher now at Brunel University London. The Telegraph followed with its own count, putting the number of identical or near-identical passages above 100. Retraction Watch has separately reported that Times Higher Education prepared its own investigation last September but shelved it after Arday retained the defamation specialists Carter-Ruck to challenge the story.

A 2018 article in the journal Social Sciences, in which Arday described interviewing 32 students, drew particular scrutiny after some quotations appeared to trace back to an epidemiologist's earlier research rather than Arday's own fieldwork. That paper was quietly corrected in December 2023, with twelve quotations altered and a claim about independent coding validation removed.

Both Cambridge and Liverpool John Moores University, which awarded Arday's doctorate, say they investigated and found no misconduct. Cambridge has said only that Arday was "cleared of allegations of plagiarism regarding his thesis and journal publications," while declining to examine matters that predate his 2023 appointment, citing lack of jurisdiction.

Arday's defence

Arday's own explanation leans heavily on his biography. Diagnosed with autism as a child, he did not speak until he was 11 and could not read or write until 18; he has said he relied on mimicking other people's language patterns to make sense of academic writing, and that he received inadequate PhD supervision and could not afford a proofreader when he was starting out. "I didn't murder somebody," he said of the backlash. "I can't make people believe me. Nobody lives their life having a receipt for every" action, he added, an argument aimed less at the specific citations than at the demand for documentary proof of a life story that has also come under separate scrutiny — including revised details about a £5 million charity fundraising effort he said took place over 20 years with help he could not name due to non-disclosure agreements, and a treadmill endurance challenge clarified to have run 12 days rather than six.

The petition

What distinguishes this case from the usual slow grind of academic misconduct proceedings is the scale of the public mobilisation around it. A letter organised by the Good Law Project, titled "Stand in solidarity with Jason Arday," has drawn more than 12,000 signatures, including Green Party leader Zack Polanski, Labour MPs John McDonnell, Bell Ribeiro-Addy and Clive Lewis, Lord Simon Woolley, Lord Paul Boateng and several Cambridge college heads. The letter states that "entirely false allegations of plagiarism were made against Professor Arday" and accuses "right-wing media" of an orchestrated smear.

That framing has infuriated critics, who note that the underlying textual overlaps are not seriously in dispute — Arday himself has acknowledged errors — and argue that treating documented citation problems as a racially motivated hoax forecloses the kind of scrutiny any other academic's work would face. Supporters counter that the volume and speed of the pile-on, much of it from commentators already hostile to Cambridge's diversity initiatives, looks less like disinterested fact-checking than a campaign with a target picked in advance.

What's next

Neither university has announced a fresh, independent review, and Cambridge's stance — that anything predating 2023 falls outside its remit — leaves the thesis itself in an odd limbo: acknowledged as flawed, but nobody's formal responsibility to re-examine. The next pressure point is likely to come from the journals Arday has published in, several of which have not yet said whether they will reopen his record beyond the 2023 correction already issued. Until one does, the dispute will keep running on parallel tracks — a citation argument that both sides agree happened, and a fight over what it means that neither side is close to settling.
