Sainsbury's sells Argos for £120m, a fraction of its 2016 price

An Argos storefront — the catalogue retailer Sainsbury's bought for £1.4bn in 2016 is being sold for a fraction of that price. — Henry Spooner / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Sainsbury's is selling Argos for £120m, a 91% loss on the £1.4bn it paid in 2016, to a PE-backed team of ex-Morrisons and On the Beach executives.

Sainsbury's has agreed to sell Argos for at least £120m, a decade after paying £1.4bn for it — a loss of roughly £1.28bn, or 91% of the original price, on paper.

The buyer is Swift Partners, a newly formed vehicle backed by private equity firm True Capital and fronted by two familiar names in British retail: Richard Pennycook, the former Co-operative Group chief executive who now chairs On the Beach and Two Sisters Food Group, and Trevor Strain, who spent years as finance chief and then chief operating officer at Morrisons. True Capital's Matt Truman and Paul Cocker are providing the financial backing.

Under the terms, Sainsbury's will receive £70m in cash when the deal completes, expected in February 2027, with a further £50m in deferred consideration paid out over the following three years — though separation costs are expected to eat into that. The supermarket will also book a one-off, non-cash impairment charge of £350m, while the disposal is expected to cut net debt by around £250m. Shares in Sainsbury's rose more than 5% to 374p on the news.

A £1.28bn round trip

Sainsbury's bought Home Retail Group, Argos's parent, for £1.4bn in 2016, seeing off a rival approach from South African furniture group Steinhoff to complete the deal that September. The logic at the time was scale and cross-selling: Argos concessions inside Sainsbury's stores, shared logistics, a loyalty scheme spanning both brands. Instead, catalogue retail was overtaken by exactly the forces the merger was meant to hedge against — Amazon-style convenience, and more recently the rise of Temu and Shein undercutting general merchandise on price. Argos generated £4.1bn in sales last year against Sainsbury's £25.9bn grocery business, but its most recent quarter showed revenue down 0.5% even as volumes grew 2.2%, a sign of persistent pricing pressure. In Sainsbury's own accounts, Argos was already carried at a value of just £344m — itself a fraction of the purchase price, and still well above what Swift is now paying.

Chief executive Simon Roberts framed the sale as a simplification, saying it "allows us to focus all our resources and investment on the significant opportunities ahead" in food. Pennycook said the new owners see "real opportunities" to sharpen Argos's customer offer and digital capability — the standard private-equity pitch for a business a listed group no longer wants to run. The deal covers Argos's standalone stores, in-store concessions, its online operation, logistics network, Argos Care and Argos Pet Insurance, plus sourcing offices in Shanghai and Hong Kong. Sainsbury's keeps a commercial tie-up covering Nectar loyalty points, Nectar360 retail media and Habitat-branded products.

The episode joins a lengthening list of 2010s-era retail mergers that failed to deliver the promised synergies, as physical-format consolidation ran headlong into e-commerce disruption. What is new is the buyer: a private-equity vehicle willing to take on a deeply discounted, still-large retail operation on the bet that experienced operators can extract value where a supermarket group could not. Completion in February 2027 is the next formal milestone; how Swift Partners actually restructures Argos — store count, headcount, digital investment — will be the real test of that bet.
