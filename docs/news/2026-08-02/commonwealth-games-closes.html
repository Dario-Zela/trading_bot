Commonwealth Games Glasgow closes with Kerr's mile gold and a boxing judging row

The SSE Hydro in Glasgow, which hosted the Commonwealth Games closing ceremony — Bhaskaranaidu / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Glasgow 2026 closed with Josh Kerr's home mile gold and a boxing judging row over England's Dimeji Shittu — with no economic impact figures published either way.

Glasgow's Commonwealth Games ended on Sunday evening the way it began eleven days earlier — with Simple Minds, a packed Hydro arena, and a Scottish crowd trying to work out how much of what just happened they're allowed to keep.

The answer, competitively at least, arrived a day early. On Saturday night at a sold-out Scotstoun Stadium, Josh Kerr produced the kick that home fans had been waiting a fortnight for, surging clear with 250 metres to run and taking the men's mile — a distance restored to the Games programme for the first time in 60 years — in 3:54.12. Cameron Myers of Australia took silver in 3:55.26, with Kenya's Timothy Cheruiyot third. "It doesn't get any better than this," Kerr said afterwards. "In front of a Scottish crowd where I learned how to race. I came here week after week growing up as a kid." It was the world 1500m champion's first Commonwealth medal, won on the track where he trained as a junior.

A gold that stayed on the mat

Kerr's moment was the clean story of the Games. The boxing arena supplied the messy one. England's Dimeji Shittu lost the men's 80kg final to India's Ankush Panghal on a split decision, and the reaction from ringside was immediate. Former world champion Richie Woodhall, commentating for TNT Sports, didn't hedge: "The referee has cost Shittu the contest. He has been robbed. I really feel for him." Shittu had appeared to land the cleaner work through the second round yet lost it on three of the five cards, while a point deduction for repeatedly dipping his head compounded the sense of injustice — though four of the five judges gave Panghal the final round outright, meaning the deduction changed nothing.

It wasn't the only flashpoint. Ugandan heavyweight Aziz "Ringo" Abdul, disqualified in his quarter-final against England's Damar Thomas for head-butting, alleged the judging had favoured a boxer fighting in his home nation and cast the decision in racial terms. Commonwealth Sport's chief, Dr Donald Rukare, rejected that framing — "we believe that every athlete is treated fairly in a non-discriminatory manner" — and noted World Boxing had received no formal complaint from Uganda's delegation. Amateur boxing already scores each bout with five judges and randomly discards two cards to blunt any single outlier's influence; the Shittu decision has revived the recurring argument that AI-assisted scoring could do better. As with every previous version of that argument, no boxing federation has actually moved to adopt one.

The ledger nobody's published

What both stories share is a gap in the record. Glasgow's organisers went into these Games — a scaled-back, single-city rerun of the 2014 edition, built around existing venues to keep costs near £150 million — promising a tourism and business lift echoing the £120 million impact claimed for 2014. Eleven days on, with the athletes' village emptying and the Hydro handing the Games baton to Ahmedabad for 2030, no visitor numbers, hotel occupancy figures or broadcast valuations have been published. The claims made in the bid documents remain claims. It's not unusual — host cities rarely produce hard economic accounting before the crowds have even left — but it means the two things people will actually remember about Glasgow 2026, a Scottish mile winner and a boxing scorecard row, arrive with a full data trail, while the economic case for hosting arrives with none at all.

Ahmedabad now has four years to build its own case, and will presumably be watched for the same gap. Closer to home, expect Shittu's camp to lodge a formal appeal with World Boxing in the coming days — the more concrete test of whether Saturday's outrage amounts to anything beyond a strong TV soundbite.
