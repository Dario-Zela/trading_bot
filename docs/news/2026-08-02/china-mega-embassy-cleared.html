High Court clears way for China's 'mega-embassy' in London

The former Royal Mint building on Tower Hill, opposite the Tower of London, which China bought in 2018 and plans to redevelop into its largest European embassy. — Julie Cookson / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The High Court dismissed a legal challenge to China's planned mega-embassy near the Tower of London, clearing the way for construction to proceed.

A High Court ruling handed down on July 31 has cleared the last legal obstacle to China building what will become its largest embassy in Europe, on a 5.5-acre site directly across the road from the Tower of London.

Two judges in the King's Bench Division — Lord Justice Dingemans and Mrs Justice Lieven — dismissed a challenge brought by the Royal Mint Court Residents' Association, ruling that Housing Secretary Steve Reed had acted lawfully when he approved the project in January. The judgment did not weigh the merits of the planning decision itself, only whether ministers had followed the correct process. On that narrow question, the court found no fault: officials had, in the judges' words, "conscientiously and carefully had regard" to residents' objections before waving the scheme through.

A decade-long fight over a Grade II site

China bought the former Royal Mint site in 2018 for £255 million, and has spent much of the time since trying to convert the 19th-century compound — designed by Robert Smirke, a stone's throw from Tower Bridge — into a 20,000 square-metre diplomatic complex roughly ten times the size of its current London embassy. Tower Hamlets council rejected the plans in 2022 and again in 2024, citing security and safety concerns severe enough that the decision was ultimately called in by central government, which approved it on Reed's signature in a 240-page ruling in January.

The objections were not limited to traffic and noise. Residents, along with Hong Kong pro-democracy activists and some Conservative and Liberal Democrat MPs, argued the compound's design — including basement rooms whose purpose China's government declined to specify — could function as a surveillance and detention facility reaching into a democracy the Chinese Communist Party has an increasingly fraught relationship with. American officials weighed in too: The Telegraph reported a senior US source describing Washington as "deeply concerned about adversaries exploiting the critical infrastructure of our closest allies," a nod to the site's proximity to City of London data cabling.

What the court actually decided

None of that made it into the judges' reasoning, because judicial review in England tests lawfulness of process, not wisdom of outcome. The residents' association had argued the government failed to properly weigh the risk that the embassy could be used to monitor or intimidate Chinese dissidents on British soil, and that fire safety could not be enforced given diplomatic immunity. The court found neither argument sufficient to overturn the minister's decision, and stated plainly there had been "no unfairness" in how the process was run.

Luke Pollard, who heads the residents' campaign, rejected any suggestion the fight is over: "Beijing thinks this judgment is the final word. It isn't. The appeal is already in the works." The group says it has raised more than £220,000 to pursue the case to the Court of Appeal and, if necessary, the Supreme Court.

Reading the signal

For a government that has spent the past year publicly re-engaging with Beijing on trade while still naming China a "systemic challenge" in security terms, the ruling is a useful data point: the planning system was not going to be quietly repurposed as a lever against a project ministers had already chosen to approve. Whatever discomfort lingers in Whitehall over the optics of a Chinese state compound overlooking the Tower of London, it did not translate into the courts finding a legal reason to stop it.

Construction cannot begin immediately — the residents' association has confirmed it will seek permission to appeal, which could delay groundbreaking by months. The next milestone is whether the Court of Appeal agrees to hear the case at all.
