Snapchat and LinkedIn join platform crackdown on 'AI slop'

LinkedIn's Sunnyvale campus. The company rolled out a user-facing AI-content reporting tool on July 30, 2026. — LPS.1 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Snapchat will stop recommending fully AI-made Spotlight videos and LinkedIn added an AI-slop reporting button, joining YouTube and Substack in policing synthetic content.

The pitch to advertisers and creators alike has always been the same: platforms grow by showing people more of what keeps them scrolling. This week, two of the biggest names in that business said the quiet part differently — sometimes growth is the problem.

On July 30, LinkedIn added a button letting users flag posts that look like "AI slop" — mass-produced, synthetic content dressed up as a personal essay or hot take. A day later, Snap announced that Spotlight, its short-video feed, will stop recommending videos that are wholly AI-generated. Neither company is banning artificial intelligence outright. Both are drawing a line between AI as a tool and AI as a substitute for a person actually saying something.

What actually changed

Snap's version is an algorithm change, not a ban. Fully AI-generated Spotlight videos are still allowed on the app — they simply won't be pushed into the recommendation feed that drives views. Content edited or enhanced with Snap's own AI tools remains eligible, and will carry a transparency label showing AI was involved. In a newsroom post, the company put the rationale plainly: "As low-quality, repetitive, AI-generated content becomes increasingly common across the internet, we want Spotlight to remain a place where people can discover authentic creativity from real people." Snap said unique Spotlight contributors are up more than 120% year-on-year, framing the move as building on momentum rather than reacting to a crisis. It follows a smaller signal the company sent in April.

LinkedIn's tool is more direct: a "Seems like AI slop" option tucked into the three-dot menu on any post. Click it, and the post disappears from your feed; the person who wrote it gets a private note that their content "may have come across as inauthentic," and flagged posts see their algorithmic reach cut. Chief product officer Hari Srinivasan called it "a top priority for all of us." The company also pulled its "enhance your post" AI writing tool from the compose box, swapping it for a proofreading feature that corrects grammar without rewriting anyone's voice. The trigger is not subtle: one recent study found 41% of long-form LinkedIn posts are likely AI-written, and LinkedIn has said it has blocked billions of AI-generated posts in recent months.

Not the first, and not likely the last

What makes this week notable isn't novelty — it's synchrony. YouTube got there first. A July 2025 policy update redefined "repetitious content" as "inauthentic content" for monetization purposes, and by mid-July 2026 the platform had spelled out three specific categories barred from earning money: repetitive AI filler, emotionally manipulative videos, and AI-generated "experts" dispensing finance or health advice. The enforcement has had teeth — 16 channels with a combined 4.7 billion views and 35 million subscribers were terminated, erasing an estimated $10 million in annual creator revenue. Substack, meanwhile, has built AI-detection scoring (via a tool called Pangram) directly into how posts are displayed, though writers can opt out. Meta moved to match YouTube's original policy within days last year, and Google's March 2026 core search update made what it calls "scaled content abuse" a primary enforcement target.

The common thread is that none of these platforms are trying to eliminate AI-assisted content — creators using AI tools to write, edit or animate remain welcome, often with a disclosure label rather than a penalty. What's being pushed out is the fully automated, low-effort variety: template videos with swapped captions, slideshow explainers with synthetic narration, posts optimized purely to trip the recommendation algorithm rather than say anything.

Why this is a business decision, not a values statement

It's tempting to read these moves as corporate conscience catching up with AI's downsides. The more useful reading is economic. Recommendation feeds are how these platforms compete for attention, and attention is what they sell to advertisers. A Fortune analysis in June noted that the backlash against AI slop is already showing up in boardrooms — Publicis Groupe and the Hachette-led Publishers Association have both flagged synthetic content as a brand-safety and trust risk. If a feed fills up with content nobody wanted to see and nobody made, engagement erodes, and so does the case for ad spend. Trust in AI-generated content specifically is thin: one widely cited figure has 49% of US adults now using chatbots regularly, while 69% say they trust AI companies only minimally. Platforms that let synthetic volume degrade their feeds are betting against that gap closing anytime soon.

That reframes curation as a competitive feature rather than a compliance chore. Spotify, YouTube and now Snap and LinkedIn are each, in effect, telling users and advertisers: our algorithm still rewards people, not content mills. Whether that promise holds under enforcement — flagged posts still need reviewing, AI-generated video still needs detecting reliably — is the harder question, and one none of these companies has fully answered.

What to watch

The test will be enforcement, not policy language. YouTube's 16-channel purge shows platforms are willing to remove real revenue to make the point; whether Snap and LinkedIn follow through with comparable numbers — rather than quietly softening thresholds once creators complain — will say more than either announcement did. Also worth watching: whether Instagram, TikTok or X issue their own versions in the coming months, which would turn this from a two-platform story into an industry standard.
