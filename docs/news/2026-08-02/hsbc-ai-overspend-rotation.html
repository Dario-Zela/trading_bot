AI-overspend fears keep rotating capital out of chips, HSBC says

A 12-inch silicon wafer of the kind produced by the foundries now caught in the AI capex repricing. — DrHughManning / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
HSBC's new model says the highest-odds AI scenario has chip and data-centre suppliers gaining while hyperscalers footing the capex bill lag behind.

Chip stocks just had their worst week of the year, and the reason has less to do with silicon than with balance sheets. On July 29, more than $1 trillion was wiped from semiconductor names in a single session — Nvidia alone shed roughly $238bn in market value, SK Hynix closed down 9.6% after posting a record quarterly profit that still missed forecasts, Samsung Electronics fell over 5%, and SoftBank, a proxy for the trade via its stake in Arm, dropped nearly 7%. The proximate worry: that AI infrastructure spending is "peaking faster than expected," as one Forrester analyst put it, and that near-term revenue still doesn't justify the outlay.

HSBC has now put a number on that anxiety. In a note published Thursday, analyst Alastair Pinder unveiled a clustering model that tracks hyperscaler, semiconductor, software and Chinese internet equity returns to work out which of five competing AI narratives markets are actually pricing. The bank assigns its highest probability — 37% — to what it calls "hyperscaler overspend": a regime in which data-centre and chip suppliers outperform "at the expense of the capex spenders" themselves. Under that scenario, HSBC projects semiconductors returning 11.8% annualised and tech hardware 12.3%, with the rotation flowing specifically into Korea and Taiwan — SK Hynix, Samsung, TSMC — rather than away from them.

Where the balance-sheet risk actually sits

That is a sharper claim than the week's headlines suggest, and it points the finger at the hyperscalers rather than their suppliers. The evidence is showing up in credit markets before equities. Five-year credit-default-swap spreads on Oracle, Meta, Amazon, Alphabet and Nvidia have all hit records in recent days as the "Big Five" hyperscalers lean on debt to fund a capex run-rate now expected to exceed $690bn in fiscal 2026 — enough to push free cash flow at several of them toward zero or negative as the spending front-loads. Oracle's CDS has climbed to roughly 215 basis points, from 144bp at the start of the year, its highest level since the 2008 crisis. As Société Générale's head of U.S. equity strategy put it: "For hyperscale computing companies, it's CDS, not EPS, that matters now." Chip and data-centre suppliers, by contrast, are largely getting paid against contracted backlog regardless of whether the hyperscalers' own AI bets ever clear a return.

Five scenarios, one dominant

HSBC's other four regimes are lower-probability but not trivial. "AI positioning capitulation" (26%) would see crowded AI trades unwind broadly into pharma and biotech, with semiconductors falling 14.3%. "China competition concerns" (20%) — triggered by Moonshot's Kimi K3 model launch and reports of progress on Chinese lithography tools — would send capital into mainland media and consumer names instead. "AI disruption fears" sit at 9%, and outright "AI euphoria," in which the whole supply chain re-rates higher and semiconductors gain 19.1%, is judged least likely at 8%.

The common thread is that institutional money is no longer treating "AI exposure" as a single trade. Two years into the infrastructure build-out, HSBC's framework says the market has moved from asking whether AI capex will keep growing to asking who eats the loss if the returns disappoint — and its base case says that won't be the people selling the chips.

The next test comes fast: hyperscaler capex guidance in upcoming earnings will show whether the spending pace itself is slowing, and Friday's U.S. payrolls report will shape the rate path that high-multiple chip and software names remain unusually sensitive to.
