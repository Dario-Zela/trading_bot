BEYOND THE TAPE · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-02 · 3 min read

Mountaineer Nirmal Purja killed in Broad Peak avalanche

Nirmal Purja at Everest base camp, April 2021. — Iamthanes / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nirmal Purja, who redefined extreme mountaineering, died with nine others when two avalanches swept his expedition off Broad Peak on July 30.

Nirmal Purja spent his career making the impossible look almost routine. In 2019 he climbed all fourteen of the world's 8,000-metre peaks in six months and six days, a feat that had previously taken the fastest climbers seven years. On July 30, that same appetite for the improbable — a new route up Pakistan's Broad Peak, established with fixed ropes as he went — put him in the path of an avalanche that killed him and nine members of his expedition.

Elite Exped, the company Purja founded, confirmed the deaths on August 1 after a two-day search in the Karakoram. Two avalanches struck the group below Camp III, at roughly 6,600 metres, sweeping the climbers down the mountain's slopes. Rescue teams recovered the first bodies in poor weather before confirming there were no survivors. "It is with profound sadness and immense heartbreak that we confirm Nirmal 'Nimsdai' Purja tragically lost his life," the company said.

A team, not a solo record

Purja, 43, was born in Myagdi district and followed three older brothers into Nepal's Gurkha regiments before becoming the first Nepali accepted into the British Special Boat Service. He left the military in 2018 to attempt what he called Project Possible — summiting all fourteen of the planet's highest mountains in under seven months, a mark that had stood since the 1980s. Netflix's 2021 documentary 14 Peaks: Nothing Is Impossible turned that achievement into a global story and, more consequentially, redirected attention toward the Nepali and Sherpa climbers who had long done the hardest work on Himalayan expeditions with little of the credit.

He did not die alone, and the names around his matter. Pur Bahadur "Yukta" Gurung and Nima Sherpa, his longtime climbing partners at Elite Exped, were killed alongside him. Nadhira Al Harthy, the first Omani woman to summit Everest, and American Mallory Geis, on her first 8,000-metre attempt after closing her Pilates studio in San Antonio to chase mountaineering, were also lost, along with Gyalu Sherpa, Nawang Thendu Sherpa, Kili Pemba Sherpa, Chinese climber Wang Zhong and Pakistani guide Sohail Sakhi.

Fame did not change the arithmetic

Broad Peak's 8,051-metre summit ranks it the world's twelfth-highest, and its slopes above Camp II have long carried a reputation among Karakoram climbers for unstable snowpack after storm cycles — conditions that had been building through a windy, humid stretch in the days before the group set out. That Purja, arguably the most technically accomplished high-altitude climber of his generation, died in exactly the kind of slide that ends careers less storied than his is the detail worth sitting with. Records and sponsorship deals do not change what a mountain does when a slope fails underneath a rope team.

Nepal's prime minister, Balendra Shah, called mountaineering "the supreme expression of courage, discipline, sacrifice and indomitable confidence that challenges human limits" in a tribute released Saturday, framing Purja's death as continuous with the achievements rather than a contradiction of them. That is the more comfortable reading. The harder one is that the margins on an 8,000-metre peak stay thin regardless of who is climbing it, and that the sport Purja did more than anyone to popularise will keep extracting the same price from the climbers who follow him onto it.

Pakistani authorities have not said when — or whether — recovery efforts for the remaining bodies will resume given the terrain and weather.
