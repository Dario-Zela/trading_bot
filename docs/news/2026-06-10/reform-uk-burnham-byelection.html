Labour holds Burnham and Makerfield on a slashed majority as Reform surges in the heartlands

Andy Burnham, Greater Manchester Mayor and Labour's candidate for the Burnham and Makerfield by-election — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Labour retained Burnham and Makerfield but on a sharply reduced majority, as Reform UK's strongest-ever heartland by-election performance signals a durable realignment in the post-industrial North.

Labour has held the Burnham and Makerfield constituency, but the majority that came back from the count was a shadow of what the party once considered safe ground. Andy Burnham — the Greater Manchester Mayor who vacated his regional post to stand — held the seat, yet Reform UK's Robert Kenyon, a local plumber born in the constituency, ran him closer than most incumbents with a national profile would expect. The result marks Reform's strongest by-election performance in traditional Labour heartland, and the numbers behind it are harder for Westminster to dismiss than the headline outcome.

The seat opened up after Josh Simons resigned in May, clearing a path for Burnham to enter Parliament in what was widely understood as positioning for a future Labour leadership contest. The personal premium that Burnham carries — high visibility, genuine regional credibility, a brand Labour's central operation cannot manufacture — put a ceiling on how far the swing could run. Without it, the result might not have been a result at all.

The ground beneath the seat

At the 2024 general election, Labour won Makerfield with 45.2% against Reform UK's 31.8%, a margin of 13.4 percentage points and just over 5,400 votes. The June by-election slashed that to a fraction. The pre-election polling from Survation — Labour at 49%, Reform at 39% — anticipated a closer race than 2024 delivered, but even that survey was conducted before Reform's strongest canvassing push landed.

The cleaner signal came from the May 2026 Wigan council elections, where Reform UK won all eight council wards that sit inside the Makerfield constituency with roughly half the vote. By-elections compress dynamics: national media attention, targeted ground campaigns, and a candidate of Burnham's stature all pull the result away from the underlying grain of the place. But those council numbers are closer to what the constituency looks like on a quiet Thursday, and they are striking.

Nationally, PollCheck's seven-poll rolling average as of early June places Reform UK at 27.7%, ahead of Labour on 18.7% and the Conservatives on 18.1%. Electoral Calculus modelling of that polling puts more than 100 Labour-held seats in the Midlands and North in jeopardy at the next general election. Makerfield — held, just — sits in the middle of that map.

What the swing implies

Reform's trajectory is not simply a protest vote looking for a home. Nigel Farage's party has now demonstrated, across the May local elections and this by-election, that it can consolidate working-class voters in post-industrial seats who previously drifted between Labour, the Conservatives, and abstention. In Hartlepool, Reform swept all 12 available council seats. In Tameside, it ended 47 years of Labour dominance. These are not random data points; they describe a party acquiring territorial depth.

For the Starmer government, the implications are layered. A slimmer parliamentary cushion makes every Commons vote harder to manage — the welfare revolt in June, with more than 120 Labour MPs threatening to vote down cuts to disability benefits, offered an early preview of what governing on thin margins looks like. A majority built partly on seats like Makerfield is a majority that has to be defended at every by-election, every vote, every budget line that plays badly in the North.

The next hard test arrives in the autumn, when the spending review resolves what March's Budget left open and Reform's local election foothold either consolidates or fades under the pressure of governing expectations the party has never yet had to meet. Whether Burnham's personal vote outlasts the by-election moment — and whether it transfers to any successor Labour candidate — is the question the party should be asking before 2029.
