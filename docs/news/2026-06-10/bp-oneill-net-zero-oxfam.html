BP scraps net-zero unit under CEO Meg O'Neill as Oxfam demands dividend and pay rethink

BP's global headquarters at 1 St James's Square, Westminster, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP's new CEO Meg O'Neill has dissolved the company's low-carbon unit and rebuilt around oil and gas, as Oxfam challenges dividend payouts that outpace earnings.

Meg O'Neill took the helm at BP on 1 April 2026. By 9 June, barely ten weeks later, she had dismantled the company's dedicated "low carbon energy" business unit — the structural embodiment of the green ambitions her predecessors had staked the company's reputation on. Renewable energy now falls under a catch-all category labelled "other businesses and corporate." What replaced the old order are two stripped-back segments: upstream, covering oil and gas production and trading, and downstream, covering refining, pipelines, and retail. BP has returned, in form and intent, to its pre-2020 shape.

"Focusing BP around two distinct segments is an important step in accelerating delivery. It will reduce complexity and strengthen execution," O'Neill said in announcing the restructuring. The language is operational, not ideological — but the message to the market is unmistakable.

Ripping up the green playbook

The path to this moment runs directly through BP's balance sheet and its shareholder register. In 2020, then-CEO Bernard Looney announced what was at the time one of the most ambitious decarbonisation strategies in the industry: a 40% cut in oil and gas production by 2030, repositioning BP as a solar and wind-led energy transition company. That target was quietly trimmed to 25% in 2023. Under Looney's successor Murray Auchincloss, who took over in 2023, the target was abandoned altogether, and the company began cutting its clean energy budget — eventually by roughly £3.95 billion a year, while increasing oil and gas investment by around 20% to approximately £7.9 billion annually.

O'Neill, a 55-year-old American from Boulder, Colorado who previously ran Australia's Woodside Energy and spent 23 years at Exxon Mobil, is the first woman to lead a top-five global oil major. She was chosen, in part, to complete what Auchincloss started — to bring the kind of operational rigour to BP that hedge fund Elliott Investment Management, which disclosed a 5% stake in April 2025, had demanded. Elliott pushed for the separation of upstream and downstream units, the removal of the strategy chief who oversaw BP's green pivot, and a free cash flow target of $20 billion annually by 2027, some 40% above what BP was then tracking.

The June 9 restructuring delivered almost everything on that list. BP's underlying profit in 2025 came in at $7.5 billion on operating cash flow of $24.5 billion — respectable numbers, but ones the company partly achieved by cutting £22 billion in costs, the bulk of which fell on clean energy businesses. BP is not in crisis; it is in managed retreat from a strategic position that, by the company's own admission, generated insufficient returns.

Shareholder revolt, from both flanks

The difficulty is that BP is retreating simultaneously from commitments made to two different groups of investors — those who wanted more fossil fuel discipline, and those who wanted credible climate alignment.

At BP's April 2026 annual general meeting, both groups made themselves heard. The board-backed resolution to scale back two company-specific climate disclosure obligations failed to secure the 75% supermajority required; each received around 47% support. A separate proposal, tabled by the Australian Centre for Corporate Responsibility, called on BP to justify its capital discipline on oil and gas investments — nearly 26% of shareholders voted in favour, a figure that obliges the company to consult investors and report back.

Climate shareholder group Follow This had attempted to put a further resolution to the AGM asking BP to disclose how it would protect shareholder value in a scenario of declining fossil fuel demand. BP declined to include the resolution in the meeting notice. Follow This indicated it may take legal action.

The AGM unfolded six weeks after the controversial ouster of chairman Albert Manifold for what BP described as "unacceptable" conduct — adding a governance crisis to the strategic turmoil. BP has now cycled through three chief executives and three chairmen in three years.

Oxfam's dividend challenge

Layered over the climate-versus-returns conflict is a third pressure: the question of who benefits from the fossil fuel economics that make this pivot attractive in the first place.

Oxfam released a report earlier this year projecting that the six largest fossil fuel companies — BP, Shell, Chevron, ConocoPhillips, Exxon, and TotalEnergies — would collectively earn close to $3,000 a second in profits in 2026, a total of approximately $94 billion. The figure represents an increase of roughly $37 million a day compared with 2025. Oxfam's analysis found that rather than directing these profits toward the clean energy transition, the companies collectively returned $403 billion to shareholders in 2024 alone.

The organisation singled out BP's dividend policy for specific criticism, noting that the company's 4.71% dividend yield is not well covered by earnings. The report called for temporary caps on dividend payouts, greater prioritisation of worker compensation, and enhanced investment in climate action — framing it as a structural incompatibility: a company cannot simultaneously sustain high shareholder distributions and fund the kind of low-carbon transition its earlier commitments implied.

BP has stated its first capital allocation priority is a "resilient dividend," targeted to grow by at least 4% per ordinary share annually. The commitment sits awkwardly alongside Oxfam's arithmetic.

The repricing question

For investors positioned on the energy sector's ESG transition, the sequence of events matters. BP's reversal is not unique — Shell cut its low-carbon investment targets by 37%, Equinor by 41%, according to 2025 figures — but BP's was the deepest and most institutionally complete. The formal elimination of the low carbon business unit removes even the organisational infrastructure for a future pivot, should economics or regulation eventually require one.

The risk, from a portfolio perspective, is not just at the company level. ESG-screened energy funds that weighted heavily on BP's transition credentials may face reallocation decisions. Broader index flows into green energy instruments could face headwinds if BP's strategic choice is validated by the market. Conversely, pure-play fossil fuel positions in the sector may benefit from what amounts to a rerating of the industry's decarbonisation timeline.

The next concrete waypoints are BP's second-quarter earnings, due in late July, which will offer the first financial data under the restructured segments; and any further legal developments from Follow This, whose potential action against BP could set a precedent for how companies handle inconvenient shareholder proposals. Oxfam's calls for policy-level dividend caps have not yet attracted legislative support in Westminster, but the political arithmetic around energy company profits — against a backdrop of persistent consumer energy costs — has rarely been more visible.
