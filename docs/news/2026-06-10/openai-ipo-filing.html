OpenAI files for IPO as Wall Street weighs AI appetite against inflation risk

Sam Altman, OpenAI chief executive, confirmed the company's confidential IPO filing on June 8 — Wikimedia Commons / The White House
In one sentence
OpenAI filed for IPO targeting an $850B+ valuation, but rising inflation and a 3.5% Fed rate make pricing a pre-profitable AI giant harder than the hype suggests.

On June 8, OpenAI's chief executive Sam Altman confirmed that the company had submitted a confidential registration statement to the Securities and Exchange Commission, setting up what could be one of the largest technology debuts in US market history. Two days later — on the morning this edition went to press — the Bureau of Labor Statistics released May consumer price index data, a print that carries outsized significance for any company whose valuation rests on future earnings rather than current profit.

The numbers inside the filing

OpenAI last raised private capital at an $852 billion post-money valuation, completing a $122 billion round backed by Amazon, Nvidia, and SoftBank in April. The IPO is targeting a public debut as early as September, with Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, and JPMorgan Chase leading the offering. Early market soundings suggest a target range of $730 billion to north of $1 trillion; the company will trade on the Nasdaq under the ticker OPENAI.

The revenue trajectory is genuinely striking. From $3.7 billion in 2024, OpenAI reached an annualised run rate of roughly $25 billion by early 2026, propelled by ChatGPT subscriptions and an expanding enterprise API business that has scaled faster than most technology companies in living memory. But the cost side tells a different story. The company burned cash at a rate that produced a negative 122% operating margin in the first quarter of this year — spending approximately $2.22 for every dollar it received. Internal projections, reported ahead of the filing, show a $14 billion operating loss for 2026 and cumulative losses approaching $44 billion before the business reaches breakeven, which the company's own models place around 2029 to 2030.

These are not anomalous numbers for a capital-intensive infrastructure build. They are, however, unusual numbers to present to public markets when the risk-free rate sits at 3.50–3.75% and shows little sign of moving down.

The inflation problem

The Federal Reserve has held its benchmark rate steady through its April meeting, and the path from here depends heavily on whether inflation is actually cooling. April's headline CPI reading — 3.8%, above the 3.7% consensus — offered little encouragement. Energy costs jumped 17.9% year-on-year, the sharpest move since September 2022, driven in large part by crude prices responding to US strikes on Iranian targets and the resulting risk premium on Middle East supply. Core inflation edged to 2.8%, also above expectations.

For rate-sensitive assets, and few are more rate-sensitive than pre-profitable companies trading on long-duration growth expectations, the direction of CPI is not an abstract variable. Sustained inflation reacceleration pushes the Fed's easing calendar further out, lifting the discount rate against which future cash flows must compete. For a company like OpenAI — where the bulk of projected profit lands in 2028–2030 — even a modest shift in rate expectations can move the fundamental valuation by tens of billions of dollars.

Goldman Sachs, one of OpenAI's lead underwriters, has separately shifted its official forecast to no rate cuts in 2026. That creates an awkward backdrop for the roadshow those same bankers will conduct later this year.

A crowded queue

OpenAI is not approaching public markets alone. Anthropic filed its own confidential S-1 on June 1 at a reported valuation of $965 billion — a week before OpenAI made its move. SpaceX, a different kind of technology company but one that competes for the same pool of large-cap growth capital, is expected to list at roughly $1.75 trillion. If all three offerings proceed on schedule, the second half of 2026 will represent the most concentrated wave of mega-cap, pre-profitable tech issuance in a generation.

That concentration matters beyond the headlines. IPOs compete for institutional capital, and three simultaneous offerings from companies without net income will test investor capacity in ways that only become visible when order books open. For OpenAI specifically, the challenge is positioning against Anthropic — a structural competitor in AI infrastructure — while arguing that two near-simultaneous AI listings reflects genuine market depth rather than supply overwhelming demand.

The Nasdaq fell more than 3% earlier this week as Iran-related geopolitical risk spread through global equity markets, hitting technology names particularly hard. OpenAI's principals chose to file into that environment — which either signals confidence in durable demand for AI exposure, or reflects a calculation that waiting for calmer conditions would cost more than the timing risk itself.

What to watch

The May CPI print landing this morning is the immediate variable. A reading materially above consensus would reinforce the Goldman forecast of no Fed cuts this year and harden the cost-of-capital environment against which the OpenAI IPO will eventually price. A softer number gives equities — and AI names in particular — some room to recover ahead of the roadshow.

Beyond that, the S-1 must become public at least 15 days before any roadshow begins. That filing will reveal OpenAI's full audited financials, risk disclosures, and the legal mechanics of its corporate restructuring from a nonprofit-controlled entity to a standard for-profit structure — a transition that has already generated regulatory scrutiny and litigation from co-founder Elon Musk, who is separately seeking damages of up to $134 billion. The roadshow itself, expected in late summer or early autumn, will be when the market delivers its actual verdict: whether an $850 billion AI company, still losing billions of dollars a year, can hold its valuation when real money is on the table.
