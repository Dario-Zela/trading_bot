Rolls-Royce defence and power systems tailwinds build quietly as consensus stands still

A Eurojet EJ200 engine on display at Paris Air Show 2013 — the powerplant of the Eurofighter Typhoon, built partly by Rolls-Royce, with new orders from Italy, Germany, Spain and Turkey extending production to the 2030s — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rolls-Royce's defence backlog and power-systems orders keep building, but analyst price targets have not moved in two months — July 30 half-year results are the next forcing function.

Rolls-Royce Holdings is not a company that makes subtle moves. The engineering group turned a £1 billion net loss into a £3.5 billion underlying operating profit in under three years, and its chief executive, Tufan Erginbilgic, has raised mid-term targets twice. Yet the stock sits at around 1,225p — roughly 14 percent below its 2026 high — and analyst price targets have barely stirred in almost two months. The business keeps accelerating. The consensus has not caught up.

The gap is visible in the numbers. Full-year 2025 results, published on 26 February, showed underlying revenue of £20.1 billion — up 14 percent — and free cash flow of £3.3 billion. Defence revenue rose 8 percent to £4.8 billion, with transport up 20 percent and combat up 7 percent. Power Systems — the MTU-branded division covering data-centre generators, naval platforms, and land-defence vehicles — grew 19 percent to £4.9 billion (€5.72 billion), with power-generation sales to data centres rising 35 percent. Order intake at Power Systems surged 21 percent to €7.14 billion. Defence carried a £17.4 billion backlog into 2026, equivalent to more than three years of revenue, with around 90 percent order coverage for the current financial year.

The lag

Most analyst targets were last set in April or early May. JPMorgan moved its target to 1,500p on 7 April; Jefferies reiterated a Buy at 1,530p on 30 April; Berenberg nudged its number up to 1,270p on 11 May. The consensus across seven tracked analysts stands at roughly 1,390p, implying 13 percent upside from current levels. No major revision has followed.

What has followed: US strikes on Iranian targets this week, pushing defence-sector risk premia sharply higher across European markets. The UK government's commitment to raise defence spending to 3.5 percent of GDP by 2035 — up from 2.5 percent by 2027 — had already expanded the order-book horizon for Rolls-Royce's combat and transport engines. Eurofighter commitments from Italy, Germany, Spain, and Turkey have extended EJ200 original-equipment production visibility into the 2030s. The Iran escalation adds another layer: allied governments that have been debating procurement timelines now face renewed urgency.

The power-systems angle is less geopolitically driven but equally durable. AI infrastructure spending has not decelerated. Rolls-Royce's mtu generators are a dominant choice for data-centre backup and primary power, and Power Systems is expanding its US manufacturing capacity — output from its Minnesota facility is scheduled to increase by more than 120 percent in 2026. The division's margin jumped to 17.4 percent from 13.1 percent in 2024.

Rolls-Royce guides £4.0–4.2 billion in underlying operating profit for the full year 2026, rising to £4.9–5.2 billion by 2028. First-half 2026 results are due on 30 July — the next formal point at which the analyst pack will need to mark its numbers to the current environment. If the defence order flow and power-systems trajectory hold through the half, target revisions are likely to follow. The arithmetic on where those targets might land is not complicated.
