Nestlé volumes recover under new CEO after years of margin-chasing missteps

Nestlé's headquarters in Vevey, Switzerland — the company is betting on volume recovery after years of margin-first strategy ceded market share to rivals. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nestlé's new CEO Philipp Navratil is pushing volume-led growth after years of margin-chasing eroded market share — early data suggest the pivot is working.

For most of the past decade, Nestlé's strategy rested on a simple premise: better margins beat bigger volumes. Under Mark Schneider, who ran the company from 2017 until his departure in 2024, the Swiss food giant raised prices aggressively, culled lower-margin SKUs and targeted an underlying trading operating profit margin of 17.5%. The logic was defensible at the time. The execution left the market-share ledger looking ugly.

Volumes fell in six of the seven quarters before Schneider left. Consumers, squeezed by the very price increases that were padding Nestlé's income statement, quietly switched to cheaper alternatives. Competitors — Unilever, private-label producers, regional brands — were more than happy to take the business. By mid-2024 Schneider himself acknowledged his company was "still in repairing mode."

A pivot with a turbulent handover

Laurent Freixe replaced Schneider in September 2024 and moved quickly to reverse the emphasis: marketing spend was lifted toward 9% of sales (up from 7.5% in 2023), accessible-price tiers were expanded alongside the premium lines, and the internal drumbeat shifted toward Real Internal Growth — the volume-and-mix metric, known as RIG, that Schneider had long subordinated to pricing.

Freixe never completed the journey. He was dismissed in September 2025 following an investigation into an undisclosed relationship with a direct subordinate that breached Nestlé's code of conduct. The board moved rapidly, appointing Philipp Navratil — a 24-year Nestlé veteran who had built his career running the company's coffee businesses, from Mexico to Nespresso — as chief executive with immediate effect.

Navratil inherited a strategy rather than originating one, but he has doubled down on it. His first major move was restructuring executive bonuses around a RIG "gatekeeper": price increases alone can no longer count as performance success. It is an unusually explicit institutional commitment to the idea that volumes matter.

The data are moving, cautiously

The numbers from 2025 show early but fragile progress. Full-year organic growth came in at 3.5%, with RIG of 0.8% — still modest, but the trajectory matters: the first half logged RIG of just 0.2%; the second half came in at 1.4%. In the first quarter of 2026, RIG reached 1.2%, well ahead of analyst forecasts of 0.1%, as coffee delivered organic growth of 9.3% and confectionery volumes strengthened.

"Improving organic growth, RIG and market share trends in the second half show that our actions are working," Navratil said at the February results presentation.

The margin picture is less comfortable. The 2025 underlying trading operating profit margin landed at 16.1%, down 110 basis points year-on-year, hit by higher coffee and cocoa input costs. That sits below the 17%-plus target the company has long communicated. Navratil's guidance calls for margin recovery through 2026, weighted toward the second half — a pattern that leaves the full-year outcome as the real test.

Premium and accessible: the portfolio bet

The strategic wager at the centre of Navratil's plan is that Nestlé can hold pricing power in premium categories while expanding volume in accessible ones. Coffee is the clearest expression of this: Nescafé anchors the mass market while Nespresso and the capsule ecosystem drive trade-up. The company calls coffee its "single biggest growth engine" and is investing an additional CHF 0.6 billion across the group in 2026 to fund brand-building behind its highest-potential platforms.

Around that core, the portfolio is being simplified. Navratil has put the ice cream business (outside the United States) and the water division up for sale or partnership, concentrating the group on four categories — Coffee, Pet Care, Nutrition and Food & Snacks — where structural growth and pricing power overlap. Those four now generate around 30% of sales through what the company labels "high-potential growth platforms," delivering high-single-digit growth rates.

The question the sector is watching is whether a food company of Nestlé's scale — CHF 91 billion in revenues in 2024, down to CHF 89 billion in 2025 partly from divestitures and currency — can sustain both volume and margin recovery simultaneously, or whether one must be sacrificed for the other. If Navratil can demonstrate that rising RIG and recovering margins coexist into the back half of 2026, it reshapes the valuation debate for the entire packaged-food sector, where most large peers face the same structural tension.

The next data point is the half-year results, expected in late July. The H2 2025 exit rate of 1.4% RIG and the Q1 2026 print of 1.2% are encouraging; sustaining that cadence while costs moderate and margin rebounds is the sequence Navratil needs to prove out.
