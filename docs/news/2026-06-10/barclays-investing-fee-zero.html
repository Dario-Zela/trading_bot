Barclays cuts direct investing fee to zero, escalating UK broker price war

In one sentence
Barclays has zeroed its Direct Investing platform fee, tying Freetrade as the UK's cheapest fund-holding platform and forcing the hand of rivals before the next ISA season.

Barclays has eliminated the monthly account fee on its Direct Investing platform, removing a charge that previously cost customers up to £550 a year and placing the bank alongside the cheapest retail investment platforms in the UK.

The change, effective from the end of May, means customers holding funds in an ISA or general investment account — through the platform recently rebranded from Smart Investor — now pay nothing for custody. A dealing charge of £6 per transaction remains for shares, ETFs and bonds, but fund trades were already commission-free. For a customer contributing the maximum £20,000 ISA allowance into funds and making two trades a year, the total annual cost is now zero.

At £50,000, the annual saving is £125. At £300,000 — near the top of the previous fee schedule, which ran at 0.25% on balances up to £200,000 and 0.05% above that — the saving reaches £550. Existing customers require no action.

The price war goes vertical

The move lands in the middle of the most aggressive pricing cycle in UK retail broking in recent memory. Hargreaves Lansdown, the market's largest direct-to-consumer platform, cut its headline fee from 0.45% to 0.35% in March — its first major pricing overhaul in over a decade — while slashing share dealing charges from £11.95 to £6.95. Lightyear has removed personal account commissions entirely and cut its foreign exchange charge to 0.1%. JPMorgan Chase entered the UK DIY investing space this year, and Webull has extended zero-commission pricing to Hong Kong equities within its UK ISA wrapper.

None of those rivals, however, had taken the platform fee all the way to zero. The move leaves Barclays and Freetrade as the two cheapest options for fund investors making a handful of trades per year.

"This is a very big move which will shake up the direct investing market," said Holly Mackay, chief executive of research firm Boring Money, adding that Barclays banking customers with the app "will struggle to make a case to buy funds anywhere else."

Sasha Wiggins, chief executive of Barclays Private Bank and Wealth Management, framed the decision in terms of broadening the investor base rather than targeting rivals directly. "By removing our Direct Investing customer fee, we are helping to make it more straightforward for people to take the next step and invest with confidence."

That framing fits a wider political context. Barclays cited government figures suggesting around seven million UK adults hold more than £10,000 in cash savings while remaining outside the investment market — the same pool that the Chancellor's Leeds Reforms are designed to nudge toward higher-return products. With roughly 20 million UK retail banking customers, Barclays has distribution advantages that standalone fintechs cannot easily replicate: activating dormant savers from within an existing app requires far less friction than acquiring new customers from scratch.

The structural implication is one the incumbent platforms have been resisting for years. As headline charges approach zero, the business model shifts from margin-per-account to volume and cross-sell — savings interest, mortgage referrals, premium tiers, advisory upsells. For Barclays, that transition is less disruptive than it would be for a pure-play like Hargreaves Lansdown, which has historically derived a larger share of revenue from platform fees.

Industry observers expect competitive responses before the next ISA season opens in April 2027 — with Hargreaves Lansdown, which retains the sector's largest customer base, facing the sharpest pressure to act.
