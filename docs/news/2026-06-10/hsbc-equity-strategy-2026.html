HSBC reaffirms bullish equity stance even as its own shares lag peers

HSBC's Hong Kong headquarters, designed by Norman Foster — the institutional heartland of the bank reaffirming its most bullish equity call of the year — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC's Max Kettner holds maximum overweight on global equities, yet the bank's own shares lag UK peers — raising pointed questions about institutional credibility.

At a moment when much of the market is hedging and hand-wringing, HSBC is doubling down. In a note published on June 8, Max Kettner, the bank's chief multi-asset strategist, maintained his maximum overweight allocation to global equities — the firm's strongest possible positioning — arguing that the familiar catalogue of risks investors are citing has already been absorbed by prices.

The core of Kettner's argument has a certain bluntness to it. Middle East tensions, tariff uncertainty, sticky inflation, doubts around AI valuations — all of it has been visible for months. "'Less bad' news flow is good enough," the note states, making the case that markets do not need full resolution of geopolitical risk to stay on course. Systematic investors, Kettner argues, still carry light positioning, which means capacity for further rally if sentiment improves even at the margin.

The numbers behind the call are not without heft. Excluding technology, first-quarter S&P 500 net income rose 11% quarter-on-quarter, and the earnings beat rate reached its highest level since the post-pandemic rebound of 2021. No major U.S. tech company missed consensus EPS in Q1. HSBC's house S&P 500 target of 7,500 by year-end implies meaningful further upside from current levels. Within equities, Kettner favours emerging market Asia, Japan, and European banks, while flagging that a rotation back into U.S. technology is plausible — recent AI pessimism has nearly erased the sector's valuation premium, setting the stage for a potential V-shaped rebound.

One quantified trigger sits in plain sight: U.S. Treasury yields above 4.3% mark what HSBC calls a "Danger Zone" that could broadly pressure asset classes. Today's May CPI print — the inflation reading the Fed's rate-cut path now pivots on — is precisely the catalyst that could nudge yields toward or away from that threshold.

When the cobbler's shoes need mending

The bank's conviction reads differently, however, when set against its own share price. HSBC's stock hit a record in late February after a first-quarter earnings beat and a restructuring update under CEO Georges Elhedery that ran six months ahead of schedule. Since then, the shares have faded. Year-to-date HSBC is up around 14% — a healthy return in isolation, but one that trails the gains posted by Barclays, Lloyds, and NatWest, all of which have surged on domestic UK rate-cut optimism that HSBC's Asia-heavy model does not capture in the same way.

Analysts have added a valuation wrinkle. HSBC's shares trade at roughly 14.7 times earnings against a European banking peer average of 11.3 times, yet the bank's earnings growth forecast — around 11% annually — trails the broader market's expectation of 17%. A premium multiple can be justified; a premium multiple with below-market earnings growth requires a more persuasive narrative to sustain.

The bullish case for HSBC-the-stock rests on Asia wealth management, Gulf expansion, and sustained cross-border trade finance flows — credible long-cycle tailwinds. But each carries execution risk, particularly given exposure to Hong Kong commercial real estate and China's softening export momentum. The market is not saying the bank is wrong. It is, more quietly, asking whether HSBC is as cheap as the bank itself believes.

That question will not resolve on a single data point. With inflation figures landing today and Treasury yields close to HSBC's own risk threshold, the near term offers a prompt stress-test of the conviction Kettner has spent months defending.
