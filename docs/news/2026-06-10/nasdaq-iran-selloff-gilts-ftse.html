Nasdaq 100 falls more than 3% as Iran shock and tech rout spread across global markets

The Nasdaq MarketSite in Times Square, New York — home index to the technology stocks hit hardest on Wednesday — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BTIG's June 9 technical warning proved accurate as the Nasdaq 100 fell 3%+, with Marvell Technology's 12% drop leading a semiconductor rout compounded by Iran-driven risk-off.

A bruising Wednesday session handed the Nasdaq 100 its sharpest single-day decline in weeks, as a wave of semiconductor selling — telegraphed almost to the hour by BTIG the previous day — converged with fresh geopolitical shock from US military strikes on Iran. The result: a drawdown exceeding 3% on the index, and the deepest single-day loss for any major Nasdaq 100 component landing on Marvell Technology.

The fall was not without its roadmap. On Tuesday, BTIG chief market technician Jonathan Krinsky flagged that in 17 of 21 historical instances where the Nasdaq dropped 4% or more on a Friday, the index had broken that session's intraday low within five trading days — a 90% strike rate. The relevant Friday was June 5, when the index shed 4.18% in its worst session since April 2025. Krinsky's note on June 9 called the probable trajectory; Wednesday's session confirmed it, with the Nasdaq briefly touching 24,980 — clearly below the June 5 intraday floor — before recovering slightly into the close. BTIG extended its bearish view the same afternoon, flagging that conditions favour further downside until a clean technical base is established.

Semiconductors absorb the worst

Marvell Technology led the Nasdaq 100 lower, declining roughly 12%. The move stands out partly for its scale and partly for its speed of reversal: Marvell had surged nearly 90% in under three weeks in May and early June, driven by record quarterly earnings and a high-profile endorsement from Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, who called it a potential "next trillion-dollar company" at Computex. Wednesday unwound a significant portion of that premium.

The selling was broad across the sector. Advanced Micro Devices fell 6.2%, Intel dropped 5.0%, Micron lost 4.8%, and Broadcom gave back 3.5%. The QQQ — the Nasdaq 100 ETF — fell 2.3% on the day. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index had already retreated more than 10% from its late-May peak; Wednesday deepened that wound.

No single earnings data point explains the rout: Marvell's most recent quarter was record-setting, with revenue up 28% year-on-year to $2.4 billion and forward guidance implying 35% growth. The dynamic is closer to valuation fatigue — a sector that priced in explosive AI demand, then encountered a combination of profit-taking, memory market deterioration, and softening smartphone volumes the spring rally had largely ignored. The open question for investors is whether this represents a temporary recalibration or the beginning of a more durable sector rotation. BTIG's bearish extension suggests it is the latter; the structural demand case for AI accelerators argues it is the former.

The Atlantic dimension

The selloff did not stop at Manhattan. London's FTSE 100 opened Wednesday in negative territory, declining 0.68% to 10,338 as overnight Nasdaq futures signalled the direction before the 8am bell. The dual trigger — Iran geopolitics and US tech weakness — made for an unusually difficult session to hedge.

Iranian tensions were already written into UK bond markets. The 10-year gilt yield edged back above 4.9%, near its highest since late May, as oil prices elevated by the conflict reinforced inflation expectations. Brent crude remains roughly 36% above its pre-conflict floor — a persistent headwind for consumer-facing sectors. Sterling softened against the dollar to around 1.34, with cross-asset deleveraging appearing to outweigh any conventional safe-haven benefit to the pound.

The traditional defensive buffers showed uneven utility. UK defence stocks partially bucked the index trend — one of the more consistent patterns in this conflict cycle — but energy shares posted no meaningful gains despite the elevated oil risk premium. The FTSE was left without the sector offsets that might ordinarily cushion a global equity selloff, producing a loss that was smaller in percentage terms than Wall Street's but broader than the headline suggests.

What comes next

The immediate test for rate-sensitive technology stocks and the UK rate picture alike was May CPI data from the United States, released Wednesday morning. A soft reading would give tech bulls room to recover, softening Federal Reserve rate expectations and relieving pressure on long-duration growth names; a firmer number would extend the valuation squeeze. On the geopolitical axis, the Iran situation remains active: ceasefire negotiations are under way, but the Strait of Hormuz risk premium is still embedded in oil and feeding through to inflation forecasts in both the UK and eurozone.

For the semiconductor sector specifically, BTIG's technical framework sets a clear marker: a sustained closing base above the June 5 intraday low would undercut the bearish case; further selling into the weekend would invite a fresh reading of the same three-decade pattern Krinsky invoked on Tuesday. The multiples at which investors are prepared to hold AI hardware exposure appear to have been reset. Whether the reset is a floor or a ceiling remains the sector-defining question for the coming fortnight.
