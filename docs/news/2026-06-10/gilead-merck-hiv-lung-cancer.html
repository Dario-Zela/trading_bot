Gilead and Merck report promising HIV phase III data while winding down lung cancer tie-up

Gilead Sciences headquarters campus in Foster City, California, where the company is betting its antiviral franchise against diversification into oncology. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Gilead and Merck's once-weekly HIV pill matched daily standards in Phase 3, even as their lung cancer combination trial was stopped for missing its efficacy bar.

Two pieces of clinical news from Gilead Sciences and Merck landed simultaneously on June 8, capturing the tension inside large pharmaceutical portfolios: a trial that exceeded expectations in one therapeutic area and a costly failure in another. Together, they sharpen a question that traders and analysts have been probing since Gilead began its oncology push — whether the company belongs in cancer at all.

A once-weekly pill to reshape HIV care

The headline result was unambiguously positive. Two pivotal Phase 3 studies — ISLEND-1 and ISLEND-2 — met their primary endpoint at Week 48, demonstrating that a once-weekly oral single-tablet combination of islatravir (Merck) and lenacapavir (Gilead) was statistically non-inferior to established daily regimens in virologically suppressed people living with HIV-1.

The primary endpoint was the proportion of participants with HIV-1 RNA at or above 50 copies per millilitre, measured using the FDA snapshot algorithm — the standard yardstick for antiretroviral non-inferiority trials. In ISLEND-1, a double-blind study, the combination held its own against Biktarvy (bictegravir, emtricitabine, and tenofovir alafenamide), the current daily-pill standard. In the open-label ISLEND-2, it matched a broader basket of standard-of-care regimens including integrase inhibitors, boosted protease inhibitors, and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors. Safety profiles were broadly comparable across both studies, with no new signals identified.

"Long-acting oral therapies represent a new wave of transformational innovation in HIV drug development, with the potential to reshape the landscape of care," said Jared Baeten, Gilead's head of virology. Dr. Eliav Barr of Merck offered the counterpart: "These results underscore our shared focus on continuing research to help people living with HIV."

The commercial significance is hard to overstate. If approved, islatravir/lenacapavir would become the first oral long-acting HIV treatment taken just once per week — a meaningful step beyond the daily-pill regimens that have defined HIV care for two decades. The most immediate rival in the long-acting space is ViiV Healthcare's cabotegravir (Apretude), which requires bimonthly injections for prevention and has been moving toward every-four-month dosing for 2026. An oral weekly pill would sidestep the injection infrastructure problem that has limited uptake of injectable regimens in lower-resource settings.

The path to market still runs through regulators. Gilead and Merck said they plan to file Phase 3 data with regulatory authorities globally and to present detailed findings at a future scientific congress, without providing specific filing dates. Gilead's existing portfolio already includes the bictegravir/lenacapavir combination for virally suppressed patients, which was filed with the FDA with a PDUFA date of August 27, 2026 — a near-term read on the agency's appetite for lenacapavir-based regimens.

HIV has been the reliable foundation of Gilead's business for the past decade. The company raised its 2026 HIV sales growth forecast to 8% year-over-year in its first-quarter update and lifted full-year product sales guidance to between $30.0 billion and $30.4 billion.

Cancer stumble closes a chapter

The same day brought the less welcome announcement. Merck and Gilead said the external Data Monitoring Committee reviewing KEYNOTE-D46/EVOKE-03 had recommended stopping the trial. The study was testing Gilead's Trodelvy (sacituzumab govitecan, a Trop-2-directed antibody-drug conjugate) in combination with Merck's Keytruda (pembrolizumab) against Keytruda monotherapy in approximately 620 patients with previously untreated metastatic non-small cell lung cancer whose tumours expressed PD-L1 with a tumour proportion score of 50% or higher — the population where checkpoint inhibitor monotherapy already performs best.

The DMC's reasoning was clinical rather than precautionary. A numerical improvement in progression-free survival was observed, but it did not reach statistical significance. More decisively, the committee assessed that the probability of achieving a statistically significant overall survival benefit at the planned final analysis was low enough to warrant stopping. No new safety signals were identified, making this unambiguously an efficacy failure.

For Trodelvy, the miss matters. The drug is approved in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer and HR-positive/HER2-negative breast cancer, but Gilead has been working to expand the label into lung — one of the largest oncology markets by volume. This combination strategy, pairing a Trop-2 ADC with a PD-1 inhibitor in a PD-L1-selected population, appeared logical: each agent has meaningful single-agent activity in NSCLC, and the rationale for complementary mechanisms was defensible. The trial enrolled the highest-expressing PD-L1 patients precisely because that population provides the cleanest signal.

The fact that even a numerical PFS advantage could not clear the statistical bar — in a group where Keytruda alone is already known to generate durable responses — is the key data point. Adding the ADC burden, including dose interruptions and tolerability trade-offs, without a demonstrable survival edge represents the kind of outcome that closes off a development pathway rather than merely delaying it.

The reallocation signal

Read together, Monday's announcements encode something more strategic than two independent trial outcomes. Gilead's partnership with Merck was forged in 2021 out of complementary assets: Merck needed a treatment-setting partner beyond Keytruda combinations; Gilead wanted to demonstrate Trodelvy's breadth. In HIV, the same pairing is now generating positive Phase 3 data from a mechanism — once-weekly oral dosing — that plays directly into Gilead's core franchise strengths.

The contrast is worth examining carefully. HIV drug development carries known pharmacological parameters, a validated non-inferiority framework, and a patient population with decades of carefully phenotyped trial data. Lung cancer — particularly metastatic first-line NSCLC — is a field where numerous well-reasoned combination hypotheses have failed to beat well-established monotherapy, and where the competitive moat shifts with every label expansion from Roche, AstraZeneca, and Bristol Myers Squibb.

Gilead's decision not to redouble its oncology effort is not a surprise. Its portfolio strategy has been explicit about virology and HIV as the core. The more telling signal is timing: shedding a marginal cancer collaboration on the same day an antiviral franchise win lands reinforces the message to investors that capital allocation is moving toward defensible territory. GSK's $10.6 billion acquisition of Nuvalent, announced earlier this week, illustrates the price of serious oncology ambition — a benchmark against which Gilead's retreat looks, at minimum, financially prudent.

For HIV specifically, the next catalysts are sequenced. The bictegravir/lenacapavir FDA PDUFA date in late August will be the first regulatory test for a lenacapavir-based oral combination in the treatment setting. A positive outcome would validate the mechanism and set a commercial runway into which ISLEND data can eventually feed. Gilead and Merck have not yet announced a regulatory filing timeline for the once-weekly ISL/LEN regimen, but with Phase 3 data now in hand, a submission before the end of 2026 seems plausible.

What to watch: whether the FDA grants priority review to bictegravir/lenacapavir in August, and how Gilead characterises the ISL/LEN filing timeline on its next quarterly call. Those two data points will determine whether the HIV bet plays as fast as the market is currently pricing it.
