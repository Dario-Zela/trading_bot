BoE MPC members signal slower rate-cut pace as services inflation stays sticky

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, City of London — the MPC meets next on 18 June — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The BoE holds at 3.75% as services inflation at 3.2% and Middle East energy risk leave MPC members signalling a slower easing path than global peers.

Bank of England policymakers have spent the past week reinforcing a message the market has heard before but needed to recalibrate around: sticky domestic services prices mean any easing of UK borrowing costs will remain gradual, even as other major central banks look to build momentum in their cutting cycles.

The Bank Rate has sat at 3.75% since December 2025, and the MPC's public remarks this week offered no encouragement to those hoping the June 18 decision might end the pause. The April vote was 8–1 to hold, with one member pushing for an outright hike — a distribution that reflects how unsettled the committee remains about domestic price pressures running above what the 2% headline target requires.

Services inflation fell to 3.2% in April from 4.5% in March, its lowest reading in more than four years. But the Bank has been deliberate about resisting that headline. Much of the drop reflected base effects — unusually sharp rises in water charges and vehicle excise duty a year earlier rolling out of the annual comparison — rather than genuine underlying disinflation. Wage settlements are running at a median of 3.5% for 2026, a fraction above the 3.25% the Bank estimates as consistent with hitting the 2% CPI target over the medium term.

The speakers' circuit

Megan Greene delivered the most pointed recent assessment. Her June 2 speech at the University of Derby Business School — titled Here we go again? — drew a direct parallel between the current energy shock, driven by the Middle East conflict, and the inflationary episode of 2021–22. She put the balance of price risks squarely on the upside, and had previously said that "the case for hiking rates grows as the conflict wears on," although she added she wanted to wait for more data before endorsing an actual increase. At the same weekend of MPC appearances, Catherine Mann — the committee's most consistent hawk — warned in Dubrovnik that "the good luck of the low-inflation era has run out," reinforcing the theme.

Chief economist Huw Pill has repeatedly called for "a more cautious pace in withdrawing monetary policy restriction." His preferred characterisation of the current stance is a "skip rather than a halt" in the normalisation cycle — a formulation designed to resist any inference that the Bank has finished cutting. What he wants to avoid are reductions that are "either too deep or too rapid."

Governor Andrew Bailey took a more accommodating line at the Reykjavik economic conference in late May, arguing the Bank can "temporarily tolerate above-target inflation to provide some support for the real economy" — but only as long as longer-term expectations do not become unmoored. He has made clear that rate hikes are not his base case.

June 18 and beyond

The BoE's posture is leaving sterling relatively well-supported against a backdrop of global easing. The ECB held rates in June as eurozone growth dimmed. The Federal Reserve is waiting on this morning's US CPI print before signalling its next step. Fewer cuts priced into UK rates means higher carry, and the pound has benefited from that arithmetic.

Markets have swung substantially since January, when two 25 basis-point cuts by December 2026 were the consensus. Swaps now price a possible 25bp hike — a 50-point shift in expectations driven by Middle East energy risk and domestic services stickiness in combination.

The June 18 decision will be accompanied by the Bank's quarterly Monetary Policy Report, which will update the inflation forecasts under new energy-price assumptions. A cut to 3.50% is not impossible if the May CPI release — published the morning of June 17 — shows services prices falling on an underlying basis rather than by base effect. On current form, that is a high bar. The committee has spent a week telling anyone who will listen exactly that.
