Ukraine targets Russian oil infrastructure with long-range drones as frontline holds

The Port of Novorossiysk on Russia's Black Sea coast, home to the Sheskharis oil transshipment complex struck by Ukrainian drones on 8 June. — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY 3.0
In one sentence
Ukraine struck Sheskharis, Russia's largest Black Sea oil terminal, as part of a drone campaign targeting Moscow's energy revenues while the eastern front held static.

On the night of 7–8 June, Ukrainian drones reached one of Russia's most strategically important oil export nodes: the Sheskharis transshipment complex on the Black Sea coast at Novorossiysk. Fires broke out at the Grushovaya oil depot — part of the Chernomortransneft network under the Transneft umbrella — with local residents reporting more than 50 explosions. NASA's FIRMS satellite monitoring service detected thermal anomalies at 02:48 local time, confirming the attack's scale.

The strike is not isolated. Days earlier, drones reached more than 1,000 kilometres into Russian territory, hitting targets in the Krasnodar and Volgograd regions on the night of 5–6 June. On 3 June, an oil terminal near St Petersburg was set ablaze — demonstrating again that no part of Russia's energy export chain lies comfortably beyond Kyiv's reach.

Energy infrastructure as a second front

Ukraine's targeting logic rests on a clear financial argument: Russian hydrocarbon revenues are what pay for the war. In the first two months of 2026, Moscow's oil and gas receipts fell 47 per cent, squeezed by Western sanctions and the drone campaign. Research compiled by Reuters indicated that strikes on port infrastructure had at points reduced Russia's oil export capacity by roughly 40 per cent. At Brent prices above $100 a barrel, that shortfall translates to tens of billions of dollars in annualised revenue foregone — a material constraint on a war economy already running billions over budget.

Sheskharis is a particular prize. The complex serves as the terminal point for major trunk pipelines out of Russia's southern fields, with storage tanks holding roughly 1.2–1.4 million tonnes of crude for maritime export. Degrading or suspending operations there, even temporarily, compounds pressure on the entire export logistics chain.

On the ground, the eastern front remained broadly static across the same period. Russia continued probing assaults on Dobropillia, Vovchansk and Kostiantynivka in Donetsk without the territorial breakthroughs Moscow had sought in earlier offensives. Ukrainian defensive lines held, and small counterattacks disrupted Russian supply routes up to 20 kilometres behind the front. The pattern is familiar: neither side has the mass to force a decisive shift, so attrition defines the tempo.

Ukraine's drone campaign has become the operational substitute for the offensive capacity Kyiv cannot sustain on the ground. Each strike on a refinery, depot or export terminal is an argument that the war can be won not by recapturing territory metre by metre, but by making Russia too financially constrained to sustain it. The strategic gamble is whether that attrition reaches Moscow's treasury fast enough — and whether Russia's alternative export routes and storage reserves absorb enough of the damage to keep revenues flowing.

The next pressure test comes when Russia's finance ministry publishes revised budget projections in the weeks ahead. Oil markets are already pricing a geopolitical risk premium into Brent, amplified by concurrent tensions in the Middle East. Any sustained reduction in Black Sea throughput sharpens that signal further. The Sheskharis strike adds one more deliberate data point to a campaign that is anything but improvised.
