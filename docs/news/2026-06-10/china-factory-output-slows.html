China factory output slows as US tariff pressure bites into export momentum

Freight containers at Yiwu Port, China — a gateway for export goods now facing sustained US tariff pressure. — iamdanw / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
China's factory output slid to 4.1% growth in April — well below forecast — as US tariff pressure erodes external demand and the manufacturing PMI stalls at the expansion threshold.

China's factory machine is losing revs. Industrial output grew just 4.1 percent in April year on year — the most recent monthly figure from the National Bureau of Statistics — landing well below the 5.9 percent economists had pencilled in and down sharply from 5.7 percent in March. The deceleration was broad-based: mining and manufacturing both clocked sub-4 percent growth, crude steel output fell 2.8 percent and cement dropped 10.8 percent.

The April miss matters beyond the headline. China's first quarter had looked resilient — value-added industrial output expanded 6.1 percent year on year, the best run in several quarters. The April reading has since confirmed that momentum has faded faster than Beijing's planning assumptions allowed. The official manufacturing PMI fell to 50.0 in May from 50.3 in April, sitting precisely at the expansion-contraction threshold and marking a three-month low. Small and medium enterprises, which account for the bulk of export-oriented production, reported PMI readings of 48.6 and 48.5 respectively — both in contraction.

The external demand sub-index tells the story clearly. New export orders touched 49.1 in March before recovering modestly to 50.3 in April — not a collapse, but far removed from the surge that powered 2025's record $1 trillion trade surplus. Exports to the United States have contracted by roughly 10 percent on a year-on-year basis; the effective US tariff rate on Chinese goods, combining the "fentanyl" levy and the reciprocal tariff, sits at approximately 30 percent — the highest of any major trading partner. Supply-chain rerouting through Vietnam has blunted some of that impact, but the workaround has limits: US authorities have moved to impose a 40 percent levy on goods transshipped through third countries.

Stimulus versus headwinds

Beijing's response has been calibrated rather than forceful. China set a GDP growth target of 4.5 to 5 percent for 2026 — the lowest since the early 1990s — and fiscal policy has expanded to include an effective envelope of roughly RMB 12 trillion across special treasury bonds, local special bonds and reserve-ratio cuts. The NBS itself has called for "more proactive fiscal measures and moderately accommodative monetary policy." What has not materialised is the blunt demand-side stimulus that might have offset the tariff drag: retail sales grew just 0.2 percent in April, against a forecast of 2 percent, and fixed-asset investment contracted 1.6 percent.

The commodity read-through is direct. China accounts for roughly 70 percent of global seaborne iron ore demand; steel production fell 5 percent in the first quarter. Iron ore prices are forecast to average around $95 to $102 per tonne for the year — well below recent peaks. Copper demand has softened since mid-2025. On currency, the offshore yuan has strengthened about 3 percent against the dollar this year, but Macquarie analysts have flagged that an unwinding of CNY carry positions — possible if stimulus ramps up sharply — could push the exchange rate to six or even five per dollar, compressing the margins of export-sector hedgers.

The next hard data point is the NBS release for May, expected in the third week of June. If industrial output fails to rebound from April's 4.1 percent, the case that tariff headwinds are overwhelming policy support will be difficult to rebut.
