GSK pays $10.6 billion for Nuvalent in landmark oncology pivot under new chief executive

In one sentence
GSK pays $10.6bn at a 40% premium for Nuvalent's two FDA-pending lung cancer drugs, as new CEO Luke Miels bets precision oncology can outrun the looming HIV patent cliff.

GlaxoSmithKline has agreed to acquire Nuvalent, a Cambridge, Massachusetts biotech focused on lung cancer, for $10.6 billion in cash — the largest deal the British pharmaceutical company has struck in more than a decade, and the most emphatic signal yet of where its new chief executive intends to take the group.

The offer of $124 per share, announced on Monday, represents a 40% premium to Nuvalent's most recent closing price and a 26% premium to its 30-day volume-weighted average. Net of cash acquired, GSK's outlay is estimated at $9.4 billion. Nuvalent shares surged roughly 39% in premarket trading. GSK's own stock fell around 3%.

Luke Miels, who took over as chief executive on 1 January after Emma Walmsley stepped down earlier than originally planned, had signalled a preference for bolt-on transactions in the $2 billion to $4 billion range. The Nuvalent bid is more than twice that upper bound. Miels acknowledged the departure directly: "It's larger than the bracket because it was unusual. It's essentially three products in one."

The assets that changed the calculus

Nuvalent was founded in 2017 by a Harvard chemist and built around a specific clinical thesis: that drugs already approved for ROS1 and ALK mutations in non-small cell lung cancer — a therapeutic space dominated by Pfizer and Roche — generate resistance and tolerability problems that better-engineered molecules could sidestep.

Its two lead candidates, zidesamtinib and neladalkib, are both under active FDA review. Zidesamtinib targets ROS1-positive NSCLC, with a regulatory action date of 18 September 2026; neladalkib, an ALK inhibitor, carries a PDUFA date of 27 November. Both hold FDA Breakthrough Therapy and Orphan Drug Designations. UBS has estimated peak annual sales for zidesamtinib alone at close to $2 billion. A third asset, NVL-330, targets HER2-altered NSCLC and is in Phase I trials.

Miels said GSK had tracked Nuvalent — internally code-named "Nashville" — for over a year, and that data presented at the ASCO Annual Meeting in early June convinced the team to move. That timing matters: if regulators approve on schedule, GSK could have two commercial oncology products before the year ends. The distinction from a typical large pharma acquisition is real. Most deals at this scale are bets on distant optionality; this one is a bet on a known molecule that may be on the market within months.

A patent clock that cannot be stopped

The strategic urgency behind the acquisition is not hard to locate. Dolutegravir, the HIV medicine at the centre of GSK's cash generation, produced £5.4 billion in revenue last year — roughly 20% of group sales. Its patents begin expiring in 2028, with the window narrowing substantially by 2030. Generic competition does not arrive overnight, but modelling it away is not a strategy.

To bridge that gap, GSK has set a target of £40 billion in annual revenues by 2031, up from £32.7 billion today. GSK's oncology revenues grew 43% in 2025, reaching approximately £2 billion — but that is still only around 6% of group sales. The arithmetic requires oncology to grow much faster and from a larger base to function as a structural offset to the HIV shortfall.

The Nuvalent deal is the third acquisition GSK has completed in 2026 alone. In March, Miels paid $2.2 billion for RAPT Therapeutics and its food allergy antibody; in April, $950 million for 35Pharma and an activin signalling inhibitor targeting pulmonary hypertension. The cadence makes clear that Miels is not waiting for 2028 to start building the replacement portfolio.

The industry context is equally pressured. A cumulative patent cliff estimated at $300 billion or more in foregone revenues has produced one of the more active pharmaceutical M&A cycles in recent memory. Merck is reported to be in advanced talks to acquire Revolution Medicines for as much as $32 billion; oncology and specialty assets are commanding elevated premiums across the sector.

What the market priced in — and what comes next

The 3% decline in GSK shares on announcement day is familiar territory for large all-cash pharmaceutical acquisitions: investors focus on near-term dilution before they focus on pipeline potential. GSK confirmed low single-digit percentage dilution to core earnings per share through 2028, turning accretive in 2029. Full-year 2026 guidance — 7% to 9% growth in core operating profit and EPS — was held unchanged. The company said it expects to fund the deal through new and existing debt facilities, with no anticipated impact on its credit rating.

Barclays analysts called the strategic logic sound, citing the offset potential against the dolutegravir loss of exclusivity if approvals arrive on time. That conditional does real work. Both drugs are still at the FDA; neither has faced the commercial realities of competing against alectinib, lorlatinib, and crizotinib — all established and entrenched in the NSCLC market.

Miels is making a specific bet: that precision beats incumbency, that physicians will switch for a cleaner tolerability profile, and that the combined revenue from two near-simultaneous launches could make the transition from HIV-dependent economics to oncology-led growth more than a mission statement. The two FDA action dates — 18 September and 27 November — will begin to settle the question.
