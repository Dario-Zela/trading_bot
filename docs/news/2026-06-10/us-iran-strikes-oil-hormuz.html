IRAN STRIKES · By C. Tanaka · 2026-06-10 · 6 min read

US strikes Iranian targets; oil surges as Hormuz risk reprices and allies urge restraint

The Strait of Hormuz, seen from NASA's Terra satellite — the chokepoint through which roughly 20 million barrels of oil and gas passed daily before the 2026 conflict closed it to normal traffic. — NASA / MODIS Land Rapid Response Team, Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US struck Iranian air defence sites near the Strait of Hormuz on 9 June after Iran downed an Army Apache helicopter, sending Brent crude back toward $100 and nuclear talks into deeper uncertainty.

The order came down the chain at 5 p.m. Eastern time on Monday: U.S. Central Command forces began striking targets across Iran's southern coast, hitting air defence systems, ground control stations, and surveillance radar sites near the Strait of Hormuz with precision munitions delivered by Air Force and Navy fighter jets. Shortly after 9 p.m., CENTCOM declared the operation complete.

The trigger had come roughly 24 hours earlier. An Army AH-64 Apache helicopter on patrol above the strait was shot down by Iranian forces. The aircrew — both pilots — were rescued in what the Pentagon described as the first-ever unmanned sea-drone rescue in U.S. military history, a Saronic Corsair operated by CENTCOM's Task Force 59 pulling them from the water within two hours. President Trump confirmed the incident on social media, calling it an act of unjustified Iranian aggression and making clear that a response was coming.

That sequence — provocation, rescue, retaliation — played out against a conflict that has been building since late February 2026, and it is now recalibrating market and diplomatic assumptions heading into summer.

Three waves, four sites

CENTCOM characterised the June 9 action as "a proportional response to recent attacks on U.S. forces and international commercial ships transiting regional waters." The operation unfolded across three waves, with strikes touching Iranian facilities at Sirik, Jask, Qeshm, and Bandar Abbas — sites stretched along the southern Iranian coast that provide overlapping radar and command coverage of the Hormuz chokepoint.

The target selection carries its own signal. By concentrating on surveillance and air-defence infrastructure rather than naval bases or any element of the nuclear programme, Washington indicated it was responding at a level calibrated to the incident, not seeking to widen scope. That is not de-escalation so much as controlled pressure — a message that the U.S. retains both the capability and the willingness to strike again, while stopping short of the kind of target set that would force a qualitatively different Iranian response.

Tehran did not read it as a gesture of restraint. Iran's Army Ground Forces commander issued a statement vowing a "decisive response," and the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps followed within hours with drone strikes targeting the U.S. Fifth Fleet's headquarters in Bahrain and bases in Kuwait and Jordan. Iranian state media, citing Tasnim agency, quoted officials saying Iran "will give a decisive response to the US aggression, which is carried out under the justification of the Apache helicopter." The statements arrived without a timeline, without named targets, without military specifics — the standard rhetorical posture of a government maintaining threat credibility without committing to an action that could invite disproportionate punishment.

The oil market's abrupt recalculation

Energy markets had spent most of May retracing from the conflict's initial shock. After Brent briefly touched $126 a barrel in late April — its highest in four years — a tentative ceasefire framework and quiet U.S.-Iranian back-channel progress had allowed prices to shed roughly 20 percent from that peak, drifting toward the low $90s by early June. The implied message from traders: the worst-case Hormuz scenario was being discounted.

The June 9 exchange interrupted that drift sharply. Brent crude futures jumped more than 3 percent in response to Iran's retaliation warnings, pushing back above $99 a barrel before settling somewhat as the immediate military exchange appeared contained. The reaction in natural gas and refined products was similar, and European and U.S. equity markets sold off in tandem, with the Nasdaq 100 falling more than 3 percent by Tuesday morning.

The structural context is what makes the repricing significant. The Strait of Hormuz, through which some 20 million barrels of crude, refined products, and liquefied natural gas passed daily before the conflict began, remains effectively closed under a dual blockade. The International Energy Agency has described the disruption as the largest in the history of the global oil market. What Monday's exchange reintroduced was the tail risk: not that prices stay elevated, but that any further escalation — Iranian retaliation catching a commercial vessel, a miscalculation near Bandar Abbas — could ratchet the scenario into one the market had begun quietly forgetting. The brief spike back toward $100 is best understood as the market re-widening that risk premium, not fully pricing a new scenario. Whether it holds depends entirely on what Iran does next.

Restraint abroad, fragility at the table

Britain's Foreign Office issued a call for restraint and de-escalation in the hours after the strikes concluded, reaffirming security solidarity with Washington while emphasising the primacy of diplomatic solutions. The calibrated distance in that language — supportive of the alliance, sceptical of the trajectory — reflects a UK government that has navigated a particularly delicate path through the broader conflict, having initially declined to open British bases to offensive operations before relenting in early March for what Downing Street described as a "specific and limited defensive purpose." British aircraft have since operated in a missile-defence role across the region but have not participated in offensive strikes.

Trump, meanwhile, has made no secret of his frustration with NATO allies who have not provided more active military support, telling reporters as recently as June 5 that he was "still disappointed" with the alliance.

The diplomatic backdrop is particularly fragile because U.S. and Iranian negotiators had, until this week, appeared to be making incremental progress on nuclear issues. Trump himself said in late May that a memorandum of understanding was "largely negotiated." But he also acknowledged the difficulty plainly: "The only point that really mattered, nuclear, was not" agreed, with Iran "unyielding." The IAEA has confirmed that Iran holds 440.9 kilograms of uranium enriched to 60 percent purity — a short technical step from weapons-grade levels. The June 9 exchange has stiffened domestic political constraints on both sides, narrowing the space in which negotiators can operate.

What to watch next

The near-term flashpoints are specific. Iran's promised response — whenever and wherever it materialises — will determine whether June 9 is categorised retrospectively as a contained incident or as the resumption of sustained escalation. Any Iranian strike on Gulf commercial shipping or a U.S. partner-state facility would almost certainly push Brent back through $100 and toward prior highs, with direct downstream effects on European energy pricing and central bank calculations.

In the United States, the May CPI print due Wednesday morning will interact with the oil spike to complicate the Federal Reserve's rate-cut path. Ofgem's autumn price cap review, already flagged as sensitive to Middle East wholesale gas prices, will absorb whatever the Hormuz situation delivers through July. And U.S.-Iran negotiators, if the talks survive this week, face a narrowing window: the longer military exchanges continue, the harder it becomes to separate the nuclear file from the military dynamic before each closes off the other entirely.
