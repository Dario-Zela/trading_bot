Airbus needs an unprecedented H2 surge to hit targets; Boeing deliveries jump 33% after Max fix

An Airbus A320neo lifts off from Toulouse Blagnac Airport on its first flight — Wikimedia Commons / Paul Nelhams (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Airbus must deliver ~87 aircraft a month through December to hit its 870-unit target — a pace it has never sustained — while Boeing posts a 33% May delivery jump after clearing its Max wiring fix.

Airbus delivered 262 commercial aircraft in the first five months of 2026. To hit its full-year target of 870 units, it must average roughly 87 jets a month for the remaining seven. That figure would exceed any monthly rate the planemaker has ever sustained; its pre-pandemic record year, 2019, averaged 72 a month across all 12.

Meanwhile, Boeing reported 60 deliveries in May — 33 per cent ahead of the same month a year earlier — after a 737 Max wiring defect that had briefly depressed output was cleared. Its year-to-date total of 250 aircraft trails Airbus by only 12, a gap so narrow it flatters neither manufacturer.

Together the two data points frame the central tension in commercial aerospace right now: the market leader is gambling on an unprecedented second-half surge to validate its production ambitions, while its recovering rival has cleared a self-inflicted bottleneck but remains structurally capped well below the pace that would close the gap.

The arithmetic problem

Airbus CEO Guillaume Faury framed 2026, in February, as "another year of progressive ramp-up." The reality has been choppier. January yielded just 19 deliveries, the planemaker's worst start in years. Q1 as a whole came in at 114 aircraft, down 16 per cent year-on-year. The spring offered some relief — 67 aircraft in April and 81 in May — but the cumulative tally through May still leaves 608 units to be handed over across the final seven months of the year.

Do the maths: Airbus needs an average of roughly 87 aircraft per month through December — rising toward 88 or higher if June tracks closer to April than to May. That would require a back half so heavily loaded that individual months would need to set records. The 2019 benchmark of 863 full-year deliveries averaged 72 a month. Sustaining 87-plus across six consecutive months would be a different order of magnitude, not a marginal extension of past performance.

Airbus's own guidance language reflects the problem. The expected 2026 delivery profile is, in the company's telling, "back-end-loaded" — an acknowledgement that H1 has not built the buffer needed to coast to 870.

The engine room

The structural brake is supply chain — specifically engines. Pratt & Whitney, which powers the A320neo family that accounts for the bulk of Airbus's backlog, is failing to deliver engines at contracted volumes. Faury was unusually direct earlier this year: "We have an unsatisfactory situation with less engines than what we would need for [2026]."

The consequences are already embedded in guidance. Airbus has pushed back its production rate target, now expecting to reach 70–75 aircraft a month by end-2027 rather than 75 per month during this year. That delay is a partial admission that even if the H2 surge materialises for 2026 guidance purposes, it does not solve the underlying constraint: Pratt & Whitney is simultaneously trying to manufacture new geared turbofan engines for freshly assembled A320s and rework existing GTF-powered aircraft with known component defects already in service.

Fuselage panel supply caused its own drag in Q1, though that bottleneck appears to have eased through spring. The engine constraint is less tractable — and, crucially, it sits outside Airbus's direct control. Years of post-COVID supply chain rebuilding have not yet resolved it.

Boeing clears the backlog

In March, Boeing disclosed that small scratches caused by a machining error had been found on wiring in roughly 25 737 Max aircraft still in the production system. The company paused ticketing and delivery on the affected jets; the defect did not affect in-service aircraft and raised no airworthiness concern. The rework took days rather than weeks, but it compressed a month of deliveries enough to register in the data.

May was the release valve. Boeing handed over 60 aircraft — 51 Maxes, six 787 Dreamliners, a 777 freighter, and a 767 freighter — 33 per cent above the same month in 2025. Part of that jump reflects genuine production momentum; part reflects pent-up inventory cleared once the wiring rework was complete. Both matter, but they tell different stories.

The year-to-date tally — Boeing 250, Airbus 262 — is the closest the competitive gap has been in years. Boeing is targeting roughly 500 737 deliveries for the full year and plans to lift its Renton production rate from 42 to 47 aircraft a month this summer. CEO Kelly Ortberg's recent headline was reopening the China narrowbody market, a signal that regulatory clearance is no longer Boeing's primary constraint. Manufacturing capacity is — and the gap between where Boeing builds today and where Airbus needs to be to reach 870 underscores that capacity, not demand, is the binding variable across the entire sector.

What comes next

June delivery reports, due in early July, are the immediate tell. For Airbus, June is the last month of H1 and the clearest preview of whether the May cadence — 81 aircraft — can be sustained. A slip back toward April's 67 makes the second-half arithmetic considerably harder.

Boeing's planned ramp to 47 Maxes a month is the number to track through the summer. A rate increase of that size, sustained without disruption, would be the clearest signal yet that the manufacturer's long recovery from its 2019–2024 production and regulatory difficulties has entered a durable phase.

The broader signal for aerospace investors is not which company wins the delivery race — order books at both manufacturers remain deep. It is what the outcome implies for the supply chain infrastructure underneath them. If Airbus cannot thread the needle to 870, the shortfall is not a demand problem. It is a production problem: Pratt & Whitney, fuselage panel suppliers, and the wider tier-one network have proved more difficult to scale than executives projected, and that constraint is now the defining variable for aerospace earnings into 2027.
