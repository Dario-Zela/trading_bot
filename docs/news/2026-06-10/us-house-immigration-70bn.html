US House passes $70 billion immigration enforcement package

ICE headquarters, Southwest Waterfront, Washington DC — ajay_suresh / Flickr / CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
The House passed a $70B enforcement bill 214–212, locking in ICE and Border Patrol funding through 2029 and tightening the labour supply the Fed is watching.

By a margin thin enough to feel the draught, the House of Representatives voted 214 to 212 on Tuesday to send a $70 billion immigration enforcement package to President Trump's desk — capping a four-month standoff that had effectively paralysed the Department of Homeland Security.

The Secure America Act, already cleared by the Senate 52–47 last Thursday, allocates $38 billion to Immigration and Customs Enforcement, $26 billion to Customs and Border Protection, and retains a further $5 billion in flexible funds for unforeseen operational needs. All of it runs through September 2029 — the end of Trump's first term — giving the administration a locked budget line that Democratic opposition can no longer disrupt.

From Minneapolis to reconciliation

The route to Tuesday's vote was turbulent. After federal agents fatally shot two American citizens during a Minneapolis enforcement operation earlier this year, Democrats refused to fund ICE and CBP without binding procedural reforms. The impasse produced what Speaker Mike Johnson called "the third Democrat government shutdown" — a 76-day partial shutdown of the Department of Homeland Security. Negotiations went nowhere.

Republicans eventually turned to budget reconciliation, the procedural shortcut that lets the Senate majority bypass the usual 60-vote threshold. Every Senate Democrat voted no. In the House the margin was two votes.

Tom Homan, the administration's border czar, said the funding would enable "increased targeting" of enforcement operations and drive arrest numbers higher. No specifics were given on deployment timelines or geographic scope — the $5 billion discretionary reserve offers the administration considerable latitude on both.

The number the Fed will want

The bill's economic significance is not its price tag but its labour-supply implications. The US recorded negative net migration in 2025 — an estimated outflow of up to 295,000 people — for the first time in living memory. Labour-force participation softened in the first quarter of 2026. Real wage growth nearly halved for all workers last year; the compression was sharpest for low-wage earners, precisely the cohort most concentrated in construction, agriculture, hospitality and food processing.

Sustained enforcement at the scale the funding enables would narrow an already tight pool of workers in those sectors. In theory: tighter labour supply, upward wage pressure, pass-through into services inflation — which is the variable keeping the CPI at or above 3 per cent and the Federal Reserve cautious. Whether enforcement actually accelerates fast enough to move the needle is a genuine open question.

The dynamic also cuts both ways. Economists at Brookings and Fwd.us have noted that immigrants add to consumer demand as well as to labour supply, meaning the net inflation impulse is likely smaller than the labour-supply story alone suggests.

May's CPI print lands this morning — the same day the House passed the Secure America Act. Watch services inflation in particular; it is the sub-index that would reveal whether the crackdown is already showing up in prices, and the one that will shape the Fed's next move.
