May CPI due today: Fed rate-cut path hangs on this morning's inflation print

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., headquarters of the Federal Reserve System — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
May CPI consensus sits at 4.2% year-on-year; a hot print ahead of new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's first FOMC meeting could bring September rate hike pricing sharply forward.

The monthly inflation report that serves as the Federal Reserve's last significant data point before its June policy meeting arrives this morning at 8:30 AM Eastern. May's Consumer Price Index is expected to show headline inflation at around 4.2% year-on-year — up from 3.8% in April and the highest reading since May 2023 — with a monthly gain of roughly 0.5%. The data lands at an unusually consequential moment: the Fed has a new chair, the bond market is pricing in hikes rather than cuts, and the institutional consensus is markedly more hawkish than it was a year ago.

What the consensus numbers say

Core CPI — which strips out food and energy and carries more weight in Fed deliberations — is seen rising 0.3% month-on-month and 2.9% year-on-year. Bank estimates diverge more than usual: Goldman Sachs is forecasting a softer monthly core of 0.17%, while JPMorgan sits at 0.27%. TD Securities, which sits toward the lower end of consensus, says it expects "core CPI inflation to take a breather in May" after what it described as a shelter-driven surge in April, when the series hit 0.38% month-on-month.

The range matters. Below 0.25% on the monthly core would suggest that tariff pass-through is front-loaded and beginning to ease — a disinflationary reading that would unwind late-year rate-hike bets and give relief to long-duration equities. A print at 0.30% or above sustains the case for entrenched inflation, pulling forward the expected timing of the first hike and compressing valuations in rate-sensitive sectors.

Warsh's first test

Kevin Warsh was sworn in as the 17th Federal Reserve Chair on May 22, succeeding Jerome Powell. His first FOMC meeting convenes June 16–17. The institution he inherited is leaning harder toward tightening than markets expected when his nomination was announced. Minutes from the April FOMC meeting showed an 8–4 vote to hold rates — the most internal dissent recorded since 1992 — with several officials stating on the record that rate hikes would be appropriate if inflation remained above target.

CME FedWatch puts the probability of unchanged rates at the June meeting above 96%. What has shifted is the longer end: December rate hike probability has climbed to 54.1%. Bond markets have moved with it. The 30-year Treasury yield broke through 5% last week — its highest since 2007 — and the 10-year has settled near 4.6%.

Two structural forces are driving the acceleration. Oil prices have risen more than 50% since late February, with Middle East conflict — now extending to direct US military action against Iranian targets — keeping energy supply risk elevated. Tariff pass-through is adding a second layer, with Deutsche Bank's tracking showing apparel, furniture and IT goods running above historical trend in the monthly data.

If today's print comes in at or above the consensus, the sequence is straightforward: yields push higher within the session, the dollar strengthens against rate-differentials, and algorithmic rebalancing will test equity valuations before the FOMC has said a word. The specific number to watch on the tape is monthly core. Anything at 0.30% or higher puts September's meeting firmly in play as a live hike decision — and that is what the bond market is already beginning to price.
