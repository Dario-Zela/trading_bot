China protests US order on DeepSeek as discriminatory

In one sentence
China's Commerce Ministry says a new US order on DeepSeek is discriminatory and breaches a leaders' consensus; the order's details and the US response are unconfirmed.

China has accused the United States of singling out DeepSeek, the Chinese artificial-intelligence developer, and says a new American order against the company breaks an understanding reached by the two countries' leaders. Beijing wants the order withdrawn.

The complaint comes from China's Commerce Ministry. According to a report from Benzinga, which is the only account available, the ministry called the US measure discriminatory. It also said the measure breaches the consensus reached by the two leaders and demanded that it be revoked.

That is the extent of what can be stated with confidence. The details of the American order have not been confirmed. The issuing agency, the date, the legal basis and the scope are all unclear. It is not known whether the order restricts DeepSeek's app on government devices, limits US firms from supplying the company, or does something else. Nor is it clear which meeting or call produced the leaders' consensus, or what it covered. Any reading of the dispute has to allow for those gaps.

Why a single company matters

DeepSeek is a Chinese AI lab best known for releasing capable language models at what it said were low development costs. That drew attention in Washington, where officials have been wary of Chinese AI systems over data security and the possibility that US technology and chips are reaching them. Restrictions on the company would fit that pattern of concern, though this order's specifics remain unverified.

Beijing's reply is notable for how it is framed. The ministry did not just object to a rule. It argued that Washington had broken something the two presidents agreed. That turns a narrow product restriction into a question about whether the current truce in US-China economic relations still holds. A complaint about an app is easy to dismiss. A complaint that a leaders-level understanding has been breached is harder to ignore, and it gives China a basis to retaliate in kind.

What the claim does and does not show

The word "discriminatory" is a standard line in Chinese trade complaints, and it does not by itself prove the US order is unusual. The ministry's account of what the leaders agreed is also Beijing's own. Washington may read the same understanding differently, or argue that a security measure falls outside it. No US response has been reported in the material available.

The practical stakes depend on the order's content. If it narrowly limits official use of one app, the commercial effect is small. If it restricts American suppliers, cloud providers or users from dealing with DeepSeek, the effect on Chinese AI firms that rely on US access could be larger. That cannot be judged until the text is public.

What to watch

The first marker is a US statement or published order text that confirms the agency and the scope. The second is whether Beijing follows its demand with a concrete countermeasure, or keeps to words. If either side links the dispute to the wider trade truce, the argument will move beyond a single AI app. For now, one outlet's account is all there is, and the order itself still needs to be seen.
