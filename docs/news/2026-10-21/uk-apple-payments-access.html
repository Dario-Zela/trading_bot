UK asks Apple to open its payments technology to rivals

In one sentence
The UK's CMA has proposed forcing Apple to give rival wallets access to the iPhone's tap-to-pay chip on cheaper terms, with consultation open until 17 November.

Every time an iPhone user taps to pay in a shop, the transaction runs through a small radio chip in the phone, and Apple decides who gets to use it. Britain's competition regulator now wants to change that. On 20 October the Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) published proposals that would force Apple to give rival wallets access to the iPhone's contactless hardware on fairer terms. The aim is to let banks and fintech firms compete with Apple Pay on Apple's own devices.

The proposals are a consultation, not a final ruling. Comments close on 17 November, and a final decision will follow.

What the CMA is proposing

The technology at issue is NFC, or near-field communication, the short-range wireless standard behind contactless payments. On an iPhone, Apple Pay and Apple Wallet get privileged use of it. A bank that wants its own app to take a tap-to-pay transaction has to go through Apple's rules.

Under the CMA's draft, Apple would have to provide access to the main international contactless standards. The regulator would set principles for what Apple can charge rather than fix a price. Apple could still charge for access, but the CMA expects those fees to be "substantially lower" than what Apple currently charges developers and payment providers.

The CMA has legal force behind this. It has designated Apple's mobile platform as having "strategic market status" under Britain's digital markets regime. That designation allows it to impose binding conduct requirements, with fines of up to 10% of global turnover for breaches.

Why the existing access has not worked

Apple has not been fully closed. Since 2024, rivals have been able to reach NFC through commercial agreements with Apple. In practice, companies must sign those agreements, comply with Apple's security and privacy rules, and pay associated fees.

The regulator's view, as reported, is that this arrangement has failed. The CMA said that "not a single firm has launched a wallet-type product to compete with Apple's in the UK". The market is large. The CMA says 39% of all UK payments are now made by contactless, and Apple Wallet is the most popular way to do it.

That is the core of the competition argument. If a payment method is so widely used and no serious rival can build one on the same phone, the regulator reads that as a sign the terms of access are the obstacle. Whether the terms really are the main obstacle, or whether customers simply like Apple Pay, is something the consultation will test. A lack of rival products is evidence of a problem, but it does not by itself prove the cause.

Who gains, and who objects

The direct beneficiaries are the banks and fintechs the brief points to. Revolut welcomed the proposals as a step toward competition. Reporting suggests that firms such as PayPal, Revolut and Monzo would be likely to benefit if the changes are finalised. A bank with its own wallet could keep the customer relationship and avoid paying Apple for each step of it.

Apple opposes the plan. It argues that its platform already lets UK developers offer contactless features inside their apps. It also says the CMA's approach would weaken privacy and security protections. One trade report, citing unnamed sources, says Apple is preparing to challenge the plans in court. That is not an Apple statement, and it should be read as unconfirmed.

The security argument is not frivolous. A payment chip that more parties can reach is a larger surface to defend. But the regulator's counter is that access can be conditioned on security standards without Apple setting the commercial terms. How the final rules draw that line will decide how much of Apple's objection survives.

What to watch

The first date is 17 November, when the consultation closes. After that the CMA has to publish a final decision, and the question is whether the fee principles stay vague or become something that can be measured against Apple's current charges. Any legal challenge from Apple would follow the final decision, not the draft. Other regulators will also be watching how a binding access rule performs in a large contactless market. If rival wallets do appear on iPhones in the UK, the template is easy to copy elsewhere. If they do not, the test will have shown that opening the door was not enough.
