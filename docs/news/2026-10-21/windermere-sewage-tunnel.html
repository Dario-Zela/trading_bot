Giant tunnel proposed to end sewage spills into Windermere

In one sentence
A £3.6bn plan for a 12-mile tunnel to end sewage entering Windermere awaits a government decision, with bill-payers or the nation yet to be chosen as the funder.

A £3.6bn plan to take every drop of sewage out of Windermere, England's largest lake, has been put to government. It would do so by building a tunnel of up to 12 miles (19km) to carry waste away from the shore. The scheme is backed by United Utilities, the regional water company, along with regulators, local authorities and campaigners. It is also an early test of who pays when the fix for a protected water body costs billions rather than millions.

The proposal, called "Only Rainwater into Windermere", rests on a simple principle: nothing but rain should enter the lake. Reports of the plan describe a tunnel about 4m wide, together with up to 86 miles (138km) of new sewage pipes. At the end of the tunnel a single new treatment works would handle the waste. Two variants are on the table, differing in where treated effluent would go: into the River Leven, or out to the Irish Sea at Ulverston.

A fragmented system

The problem the plan is meant to solve is structural. Sewage around Windermere is handled by a patchwork of public treatment works, private facilities and septic tanks. In heavy rain, overwhelmed works spill raw sewage into the lake. Even treated effluent carries high levels of nutrients, which have fed algal blooms.

United Utilities had previously proposed something smaller. In 2024 it set out more than £150m of investment in nine treatment sites and the six storm overflows feeding the lake, aiming to cut spills from those overflows to 60 a year. Campaigners and the law firm Leigh Day argued that fell well short of removing sewage infrastructure from the lake altogether. The tunnel plan goes to that goal. About 12,800 people who are not now on the sewer network would be connected to it for the first time.

In March 2025 the then Environment Secretary, Steve Reed, promised to stop all sewage entering Windermere. The tunnel is one answer to how that could be done.

Who pays

The £3.6bn figure is subject to government approval, and two funding routes are offered. In the first, United Utilities customers pay, adding up to £3.17 a month to bills depending on the route chosen. In the second, proposed by Nigel Wilkinson of the campaign group Love Windermere, costs are spread nationally across England, adding up to 35p a month.

The gap between those numbers is the political heart of the plan. A regional charge asks customers in the North West to pay for a lake that draws visitors from across the country. A national charge treats Windermere as a shared asset, and would set a precedent that other polluted sites could cite. Campaigners have also long argued that the regulator, Ofwat, should not let the cost of illegal spills fall on bill-payers.

United Utilities' chief executive, Louise Beardmore, called the tunnel a "100 year solution for this lake". The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs said: "We share the public's determination to protect this iconic lake." It added that more work is needed to test alternatives.

What to watch

The next step is a government decision on whether to develop the plan further. Reports so far give no construction timetable and no contractor. Until ministers choose between the two funding models, and between the two discharge routes, the figure of £3.6bn remains an estimate rather than a commitment. How Defra answers on funding will signal whether comparable schemes elsewhere can expect a national or a local bill.
