UK approves Shell's Jackdaw gasfield, prompting Labour climate revolt

In one sentence
The government has approved Shell and Equinor's Jackdaw gasfield, leaving Rosebank undecided and testing whether energy-security arguments can coexist with Labour's climate pledges.

The government has approved Jackdaw, a Shell-and-Equinor gasfield about 150 miles east of Aberdeen. Ministers can defend the decision as legally orthodox and as sensible for energy security. The harder question is whether it can be reconciled with the climate commitments Labour campaigned on.

Energy Secretary Miatta Fahnbulleh announced the consent on Wednesday, according to reports of the announcement. She has not decided on Rosebank, the larger field off Shetland. She has said that decision will take "a bit longer" and will not be taken alongside Jackdaw's.

A consent that had to be redone

Jackdaw's path to this point was set by the courts. The previous Conservative government approved both Jackdaw and Rosebank. In January 2025 the Court of Session in Edinburgh found those consents flawed, because the environmental assessments had not counted "scope 3" emissions: the greenhouse gases released when the extracted fuel is burned. The reasoning followed the Supreme Court's Finch judgment, which held that the downstream emissions of a fossil-fuel project must be assessed.

That ruling did not ban new North Sea production. It required the assessment to be done properly before a minister decides. The government has described these decisions as quasi-judicial, taken on the law rather than on party policy. That framing matters to the political fight. It lets ministers argue that the consent follows a legally compliant process, not a change of heart on fossil fuels.

The approval does not in itself show how the scope 3 assessment came out, or how heavily the emissions weighed against energy security. Those details will determine whether the decision survives legal challenge. Greenpeace's earlier challenge is what quashed the original consents, and campaigners have not hidden their appetite for another.

The manifesto problem

Labour's 2024 manifesto promised no new licences for oil and gas exploration. Supporters of Jackdaw and Rosebank argue the pledge does not apply, because both fields were licensed and consented before Labour took office and are being re-decided, not newly opened. The government has leaned on that distinction.

The argument has a strong textual case and a weak political one. Reaction on the day shows why:

  • Danny Gross of Friends of the Earth said Jackdaw "won't make a jot of difference to our energy bills" and would add to climate change.
  • Kyle Lischak of ClientEarth argued that more North Sea gas will not lower bills, because it is traded internationally and not reserved for UK customers.
  • Andrew Bowie, the shadow energy secretary, welcomed the approval but said Labour had been forced into it. He called for Rosebank to be approved, the licence ban lifted and the energy profits levy scrapped.

The developer, Adura, the Shell–Equinor joint venture, says Jackdaw could supply up to 6% of UK gas output at its peak. Environmental groups put the figure nearer 2%. The gap between those numbers is the core of the energy-security debate. Adura and its supporters also cite up to 3,500 jobs and £28bn for the economy, figures that come from the industry side.

How far the revolt goes

The Guardian reports a climate backlash within Labour aimed at Andy Burnham, who as prime minister has promised a "pragmatic approach" to the North Sea. The reporting available so far is thin on the scale of that revolt. No named Labour MPs appeared in the coverage reviewed for this article, and the size of any rebellion remains unconfirmed. Not all the pressure comes from inside the party. Green leader Zack Polanski has called approval of Jackdaw and Rosebank "a dereliction of duty", and polling suggests Labour under Burnham has been winning back voters who had drifted to the Greens. A visible retreat on climate could put that recovery at risk.

The backdrop is a fuel-price shock from the Gulf conflict, which is also pushing inflation up. Ministers can argue that this is the wrong moment to leave domestic gas in the ground. Opponents reply that the output is small against national demand and that the lasting costs fall on the climate.

What to watch

Three things will settle whether this is a one-off or a template. The first is Rosebank, a far larger project, where the same scope 3 logic applies and where a decision is pending with no date. The second is any fresh legal challenge to Jackdaw's assessment. The third is whether Labour backbenchers move beyond criticism to votes or formal pressure. If Rosebank follows Jackdaw, the claim that these are legacy decisions, not new policy, will be hard to sustain. If it is refused, the government will need to explain where it draws the line.
