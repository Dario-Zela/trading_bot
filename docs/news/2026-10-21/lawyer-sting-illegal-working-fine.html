BBC sting catches lawyer offering to make £45,000 illegal-worker fine disappear

In one sentence
A BBC undercover reporter filmed a Bradford solicitor, who denies the claim, advising how to dodge a £45,000 illegal-worker fine, prompting a Home Office review of fine collection.

A solicitor in Bradford has been filmed telling an undercover BBC reporter how a shopkeeper might escape a £45,000 fine for employing an illegal worker. The solicitor denies it. The footage, reported on 21 October by the BBC's UK editor, Ed Thomas, has already prompted the Home Secretary to order a review of how such fines are issued and collected.

The pitch

The BBC's reporter posed as the owner of a mini-mart that had been fined £45,000 for employing someone without the right to work. The solicitor was named as Mohammad Alias Yousaf, who also goes by Alias. He was secretly filmed. According to the BBC's account, he said the fine could be got out of if the owner found someone overseas willing to declare themselves the true owner of the shop.

The logic is simple, and that is the worry. Civil penalties for illegal working fall on the employer. If the person named on the notice is not the real proprietor, or can be made to look that way, the Home Office's paperwork lands on someone it cannot reach. The BBC's headline, 'We don't pay', gives the flavour of the advice as the programme presented it.

Alias has denied ever giving that advice, or ever providing false information to the Home Office. The BBC's published material does not name his firm, and no response from the Solicitors Regulation Authority had been published at the time of writing.

A regime with a leak

The Immigration, Asylum and Nationality Act 2006 lets the Home Office fine employers who hire adults without leave to work, unless they carried out the prescribed right-to-work checks. The government has made the penalties a centrepiece of its tougher line on illegal working, and a single fine in the tens of thousands of pounds is the kind that closes a small shop.

Collection has always been the weak point. Free Movement, the immigration law site, calculated in 2019 that about £120m of fines were issued over five financial years while the Home Office collected £55m. Some of the gap was appeals and instalments, but the site's headline put it bluntly: roughly half were appealed or evaded. That is an old figure and not a measure of how many fines are dodged with a lawyer's help.

This is the question the sting raises. A penalty regime aimed at employers and workers can be undercut by the advisers those employers hire. One recording shows what one solicitor is alleged to have said. It does not show a pattern across firms. The BBC has also gone after immigration advisers before: an earlier investigation into firms coaching migrants to fake gay asylum claims led the SRA to say it was urgently following up with the regulated firms named.

What follows

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood's review of how illegal working fines are issued and collected is the first concrete consequence. Two things are worth watching. One is whether the SRA opens an investigation into the solicitor, and what it says about the professional conduct rules that bar lawyers from misleading the authorities. The other is whether the review's findings address how the owner named on a penalty notice gets verified, since that is where the alleged advice would bite.
