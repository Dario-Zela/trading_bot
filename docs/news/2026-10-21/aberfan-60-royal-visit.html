Royal Family to visit Aberfan 60 years after the disaster

In one sentence
On the 60th anniversary of the Aberfan disaster, the King, Queen and Prince of Wales visit a village whose 144 dead led to Britain's first statutory tip-safety regime.

The King, the Queen and the Prince of Wales are due in Aberfan today, sixty years to the day after a mountain of colliery waste slid down onto the Welsh village and killed 144 people, 116 of them children.

The programme, as announced, takes in the Aberfan Memorial Garden and the community centre. William is then due to unveil a memorial tree at Ynysowen Community Primary School. The King confirmed the trip to members of the Senedd at the opening of the new session on 15 October. No account of the visit itself was available at the time of writing, so this piece rests on the announcement and the history.

The morning

At about 9:15am on Friday 21 October 1966, Tip 7, a heap of coal spoil on the hillside above the village, gave way after days of heavy rain. The slurry ran down onto Pantglas Junior School and the houses beside it. The school day had barely begun. Most of the dead were pupils; 28 adults died with them.

The tip was one of several perched above the village, and it was built on a spring. Water had been working through the waste for years.

The reckoning

A tribunal under Lord Justice Edmund Davies found the National Coal Board responsible. Its report described the disaster as a tale of "bungling ineptitude" by men charged with tasks for which they were unfitted. It was not an act of nature.

That is the point worth holding on to sixty years later. The tip belonged to a nationalised industry, and its owner was the state. The Mines and Quarries (Tips) Act 1969 followed, creating a statutory regime for tip safety. It arrived only after the children were dead.

The aftermath left its own sourness. Money from the public disaster fund was used to clear the remaining tips above the village, a cost many in Aberfan believed the Coal Board should have borne. Later governments repaid it, but it took decades.

The royal shadow

The visit also carries an older embarrassment. Queen Elizabeth II came to Aberfan eight days after the disaster, once the mass funeral had taken place. Her decision not to go sooner is widely reported to have been among the great regrets of her reign. She returned several times, and opened the memorial garden on the school site in 1974.

Her son has a personal thread of his own. King Charles has asked to meet Gareth Jones, who was six when Prince Philip visited the village the day after the tragedy and was photographed beside him.

What follows

The village does not need royal attention to remember. The memorial garden, the arched graves above the village and the annual gathering in the cemetery do that work. What the day offers is a public occasion on which a state that once failed Aberfan is seen to turn up on time. The next test is less ceremonial: whether tip safety in Wales, where thousands of disused coal tips remain, is funded and inspected as the 1969 Act was meant to guarantee.
