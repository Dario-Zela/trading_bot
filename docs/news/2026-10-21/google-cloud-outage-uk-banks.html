Google Cloud outage briefly hits UK banks and websites

A Google sign outside its London premises; the outage was traced to a power event in the company's London cloud region. — Reuters / Andrew Kelly
In one sentence
A power fault at one Google Cloud zone in London took down UK banking apps, court video links and other services for hours on 20 October, reviving concentration-risk worries.

A fault at a single Google data centre in London on Tuesday was enough to knock out parts of mobile banking, court video links and a scattering of consumer apps across Britain. The disruption has been largely fixed. The questions it raised about how much of the UK's digital plumbing sits with a handful of American companies have not been.

What happened

The trouble began shortly after 6am UK time on Tuesday 20 October and, by the accounts available, ran well into the afternoon. Google's status page blamed a "power event" in its London cloud region. In its words: "Due to a power event we had suffered an outage within europe-west2-b zone." Google said the incident was confined to that one zone, though other parts of Europe saw "elevated errors and intermittent network issues."

A "zone" is one of several separate facilities within a cloud region. Customers who spread their systems across zones are meant to ride out the loss of one. That plenty of services still failed suggests that many were not set up that way, or that something tied the zones together more tightly than the design intended. Google has not said which. It said it would carry out an internal investigation and publish an analysis once that concluded, so the root cause beyond "power event" is unconfirmed.

One caution on timing: a third-party incident tracker put the total at about seven hours and 52 minutes, but the more established reports describe only a morning start and a disruption "into the afternoon", with no firm end time. The precise duration should wait for Google's own report.

Who was hit

UK banks felt it most. Revolut said it was "experiencing a service disruption affecting multiple services" and stressed that "all our customers' money and data are safe." Reports also cited problems with its card payments and cash withdrawals. Halifax told customers on X that they were "having issues with our app and online." HSBC was reported to have acknowledged that some customers had trouble reaching services, and Lloyds said it was resolving a problem; neither bank, on the reports seen, tied its difficulties directly to Google.

The effects spread beyond finance. Live-streams from courts failed across Britain, including coverage of the Russell Brand trial. The UK Courts and Tribunals Service said it was "aware of an issue affecting Cloud Video Platform (CVP), used for some remote hearings." Outage trackers showed complaints about delivery firm DPD and others, and British media reported e-bike rental and budgeting apps affected. Royal Mail said deliveries were unaffected.

As of late Tuesday, Google said the problem was mitigated for most customers, with engineers still working on the small number remaining.

The concentration question

None of this is new in kind. Regulators on both sides of the Channel have spent years worrying that banks, insurers and payment firms rely on the same few providers, chiefly Amazon, Microsoft and Google, for core systems. The UK has built a regime for designating "critical third parties", under which the largest cloud suppliers to finance can face direct oversight by the Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority; the EU's Digital Operational Resilience Act does something similar. No regulator was named in the coverage of Tuesday's outage, and any regulatory response has yet to be reported.

What the episode shows is how modest the trigger can be. This was not a cyber attack or a global software failure but a power problem in one building cluster in one city. The customers affected also ranged from a neobank to a court service, which points to a common dependency that few of them advertised.

The counter-argument deserves a hearing. Large clouds are generally more reliable than the in-house data centres they replaced, and a morning of disruption without reported loss of customer funds is, in that light, a contained failure. The difficulty is correlation: when one provider falters, many firms fail together, and rivals cannot offer one another a way round.

What to watch

The next concrete marker is Google's post-incident analysis, which should say why a single-zone power fault reached so many customers and how long it lasted. Banks may also be pressed to explain which systems failed and whether their resilience plans assumed a London-region fault. Whether the Bank of England or FCA comments, and whether any of the affected firms is named in the critical-third-party process, will indicate whether this is treated as a routine blip or a case study.
