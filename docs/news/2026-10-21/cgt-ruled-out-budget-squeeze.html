Chancellor rules out Capital Gains Tax rise as Badenoch dares Labour to cut spending

In one sentence
The Chancellor has reportedly rejected a Capital Gains Tax rise before the Budget, narrowing revenue options as Badenoch says Labour lacks the courage to cut spending.

The Chancellor has rejected calls to raise Capital Gains Tax in the coming Budget, according to BBC Politics. The same day, Kemi Badenoch said Labour "will not have the courage" to cut spending. Together the two remarks put the pre-Budget argument in a narrow space: one revenue lever is reported closed, and the opposition is saying the other side of the ledger will stay shut too.

The reporting is thin. The BBC item records a rejection of calls for a rise. It does not publish a Budget measure, a new rate or a direct quote, and it is the only outlet in this cluster. Pre-Budget denials are also not binding. The Budget is the decision, and the Treasury's usual line is that tax matters are set out at fiscal events rather than in comment on speculation. Other pre-Budget reports have described the idea as being rejected internally, but the Treasury has not confirmed that on the record.

What a CGT denial does and does not do

Capital Gains Tax is a small tax by receipts but a large one by attention. It falls on profits from selling shares, second homes and business assets, and it is the lever most often named when a chancellor needs money without touching income tax, National Insurance or VAT. Rates and allowances are set by the Chancellor, and past increases came with warnings about asset sales being brought forward or delayed.

That last point is what matters to investors. A higher rate announced in advance encourages sales before it takes effect, which pulls revenue forward and lowers the later yield. Ruling out a rise removes that incentive. It also removes the cost the Treasury's own forecasters would otherwise attach to behavioural change.

The denial does not close the fiscal gap. If the arithmetic needed a CGT rise, it now needs something else: another tax, lower spending, or higher borrowing. The reporting gives no figures on how large that gap is, and none are asserted here.

Badenoch's challenge

Labour has governed since July 2024, with Keir Starmer as Prime Minister. Badenoch has led the Conservatives since November 2024. Her argument, as reported by BBC Politics, is that Labour lacks the nerve to cut spending. The surrounding detail was not available, so it is unclear whether she named any specific cut.

The jibe cuts both ways. A claim that the government will not cut invites the question of what the opposition would cut, and where. Without named items it works as a position rather than a plan. The Conservatives' own spending-cut position is therefore the thing that comes under test, and the party's own answer will matter more than the line.

The gilt market reading

For the gilt market the sequence is simple. Each revenue lever taken off the table before the Budget raises the odds that the gap is met by borrowing or by a tax that bites more broadly. Gilt investors price issuance, and the Debt Management Office's remit follows the Budget's borrowing figures. A dearer path for borrowing would be reflected in yields on longer maturities, where supply is most sensitive.

That inference rests on a single report of a denial and has not been tested against any forecast. Inflation is a further constraint: the latest reading of 3.5% was above forecasts (see the related inflation story in this edition), which limits how much the Bank of England can offset a heavier gilt supply.

What to watch

The next fixed points are the Budget itself and the Office for Budget Responsibility's forecast published alongside it. The size of the borrowing revision and the DMO's updated issuance plan will show where the squeeze landed. Until then, the CGT denial is a statement of intent, not a closed question.
