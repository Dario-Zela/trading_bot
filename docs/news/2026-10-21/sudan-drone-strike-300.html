Sudan army drones reportedly kill more than 300 at mourners' gathering and fuel market

In one sentence
Army drone strikes on mourners in West Kordofan and a fuel market in Abyei reportedly killed 300-plus people, but the figure combines two unverified tolls and the army disputes who was hit.

Two separate sets of drone strikes in Sudan's Kordofan region and the disputed Abyei area have reportedly killed more than 300 people since Sunday evening. The victims include mourners travelling to a funeral and traders and others at a fuel market. The combined figure is not an independently confirmed count. It adds together two tolls from different places, and both rest on local officials and monitoring groups, with limited independent verification. The army has acknowledged one of the attacks and has not answered for the other.

Two attacks, two tolls

The first strikes came on Sunday evening in West Kordofan. Sources told the Sudan War Observatory, which tracks the conflict, that attacks in two areas killed about 150 people. Most of those deaths, around 145, were on a truck carrying roughly 200 mourners from an area north of Abyei as it arrived in Al-Dabab. A tent where mourners had gathered was also hit, killing five more. About 150 people were reportedly injured across the attacks. Drones also struck a market in Al-Mujlad the same evening, and the toll there was not known. The Observatory believes the drones were launched from Kadugli, an army-held city in South Kordofan.

Al Jazeera reported a lower figure for one of the sites, at least 80 dead at a transport hub in al-Dabba after a market day. The monitoring group Emergency Lawyers and the RSF's self-declared administration attributed the attacks to the army. The army has not responded to that allegation.

The second attack came on Monday at about 11:30 local time. Drones hit Amiet Market, also called Al-Na'am Market, roughly 10km from Abyei town. Abyei's caretaker information minister, Yohanna Akol Ngor, put the toll at 153, up from 73 earlier. He has since told Eye Radio that more than 220 died and that the dead were buried in two mass graves. The figures conflict and have not been reconciled. He described a "horrible and shocking situation" and said fires hampered the recovery of bodies.

Médecins Sans Frontières said its Ameth Bek Hospital in Abyei had received more than 20 patients, several critical and some with burns. The "more than 300" in the BBC's headline therefore appears to combine the West Kordofan toll of about 150 with the Abyei toll of 153. It is a sum of two claims, not a single verified count.

What is known about who was hit

The Abyei attack is the better documented of the two. BBC Verify analysed satellite images showing burnt buildings in the market's north-east corner, an area normally full of trucks. Verified footage shows a large smoke plume, at least one petrol tanker and two separate large fires. A person on the footage says five missiles were fired.

A senior army officer, speaking anonymously, said the army carried out the strike. He said it targeted fuel tankers arriving from South Sudan and bound for the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces (RSF), and that those killed were RSF members and fuel smugglers. He also said the strike fell outside the demilitarised zone. "We do not target civilians," he said. The army has said before that it does not attack civilians, even when it believes RSF fighters are among them.

The accounts of who died differ. Ngor denies any RSF presence in the area. Reuters, citing three diplomatic sources, an aid worker and a conflict researcher, reported that the depot had previously served as an RSF resupply point and that Abyei is a transit route for RSF fuel. Radio Tamazuj, citing its own sources, reported that the RSF bought fuel at the market and that the fuel-selling section was hit, but also that most of the dead were civilian traders. The market serves Ngok Dinka and Misseriya traders and people displaced by the war.

These accounts are not necessarily incompatible. A site can be a military supply point and also be full of civilians. Under the laws of war, that is the question that matters: whether a strike distinguished between the two and whether the expected civilian harm was proportionate. A toll in the low hundreds at a crowded market is hard to square with that standard, whatever the military value of the fuel. For the funeral strike there has so far been no military rationale offered at all.

Why Kordofan, and why drones

Greater Kordofan, rich in gold and oil, links RSF-held Darfur with Khartoum, which the army has retaken. Fighting there has intensified over the past year. Abyei adds a second layer. Sudan and South Sudan both claim the oil-rich area, and it has been jointly administered and contested since South Sudan's independence in 2011. A UN force of more than 2,800 uniformed personnel patrols it. In December 2025 a drone strike on a UN logistics base in nearby Kadugli killed six Bangladeshi peacekeepers.

The war between army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo began in April 2023. ACLED counts at least 59,000 dead, though aid groups say the real toll is higher. More than 13 million people have been displaced by the fighting, by some counts more than 14 million. About 20 million face acute hunger, with a growing risk of famine in parts of Darfur and Greater Kordofan.

Both sides now rely heavily on drones, and foreign suppliers continue to arm them despite a partial arms embargo. The RSF has also used them: on Monday a drone strike near Khartoum airport killed four people, including three children, according to a medical source cited by AFP. A military source said air defences downed several other RSF drones aimed at the airport. Cheap drones let each side strike deep behind the lines without committing ground forces, but they also put civilian gatherings within reach.

Reaction and what to watch

South Sudan's foreign ministry blamed Sudan's army and summoned Sudan's ambassador in Juba to "convey its strong protest". It urged Sudan to avoid actions that endanger civilians. Officials and residents fear the conflict could spill across the border. UN spokesperson Stéphane Dujarric said the organisation was alarmed by continued deadly drone strikes that are killing civilians, including children.

The diplomatic backdrop is poor. On Friday the United States asked the UN Security Council to back a humanitarian truce and expand the arms embargo. China and Russia opposed the request.

Three things will settle how large this story becomes. The first is whether an independent body or a named casualty list can reconcile the Abyei figures, which range from 153 to more than 220. The second is whether the army answers for the Kordofan strikes and the funeral convoy in particular. The third is whether the fuel trade through Abyei, which supplies both combatants and civilians, is disrupted, with consequences for aid and fuel supply in an area already near famine. The next Security Council discussion of the US truce proposal will show whether the deaths change any country's position.
