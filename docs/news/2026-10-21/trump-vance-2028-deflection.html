Trump plays down 2028 Vance endorsement, promises 'more growth'

In one sentence
Trump again declined to commit to endorsing JD Vance for 2028, and his upbeat growth and budget promises came without figures in the single report available.

Asked whether he has talked about endorsing JD Vance for the 2028 Republican nomination, Donald Trump has again declined to anoint anyone. According to a Benzinga report, which is the only source attached to this item, he played down the idea and promised "more growth" and a "strong budget". No primary transcript or wire copy was available, so the exact wording of his remarks, and the setting in which he made them, could not be independently confirmed. The growth and budget claims arrived without figures.

A familiar answer

The deflection fits a pattern that has held for months. After the Washington Post reported in August that Trump had told donors in the Oval Office the party needed to elect Vance, he denied making any such endorsement. He said it was "way too early" to be thinking about it, while calling the vice-president "great". Six Trumpworld advisers told the Post that Trump says different things to different people and could still change his mind.

Earlier in the year, asked in a Fox interview whether Vance was his successor, Trump answered "No", while describing Vance as very capable and saying it was too early. He has also floated a Vance-Rubio or Rubio-Vance ticket and has not cleared the field for either man. Secretary of State Marco Rubio is widely seen as the other front-runner, and has reportedly said he would not run against Vance.

There is a practical logic to the reticence. Trump is a second-term president who cannot run again, and an early endorsement would hasten the lame-duck dynamic he has so far resisted. Neither Vance nor Rubio has announced a bid. People close to Vance have said they expect Trump to decide only after the midterms.

Growth and budget without numbers

The economic half of the remarks is harder to assess. A promise of "more growth" and a "strong budget" is a political slogan rather than a forecast. The version available gave no target for GDP, no deficit figure and no reference to a specific spending or tax measure.

That matters because the budget debate in the UK and elsewhere is currently dominated by concrete numbers. Inflation in Britain has risen to 3.5% on fuel costs linked to the Gulf war, and diesel prices remain high in the US as well. Against that backdrop, a general assurance of growth tells investors little they can price.

What to watch

The substantive test comes after the midterms. If the Republican party does well, Trump's room to shape the succession grows; if it does badly, rival candidates may move sooner. Until then, a non-answer on Vance is better read as a holding position than as news. On the economic side, the figures to look for are the administration's next budget projections and the next quarterly GDP release, which would show whether the promised growth is arriving.
