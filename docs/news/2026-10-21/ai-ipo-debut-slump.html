Oversubscribed AI IPO falls 32% on debut as midterm polling hits data-centre stocks

In one sentence
South Korean data-centre firm Elice fell 32% on its Kosdaq debut and dragged US peers lower; the midterm-polling explanation is unverified, with coverage blaming AI valuation doubts.

A South Korean data-centre company lost almost a third of its value on its first day of trading on 20 October, a pointed result for an offering that had drawn heavy demand from investors beforehand. Elice, a cloud and AI data-centre operator, closed its Kosdaq debut at 61,500 won, 32.04% below its 90,500 won offer price, according to Bloomberg and CNBC. The shares fell as much as 38% at the open.

The numbers are therefore confirmed, but the story is not the one first suggested. The headline that prompted this piece, from Benzinga, tied the drop to US midterm polling. No coverage found by this desk makes that link, and the company is a Korean listing, not a US one. The polling explanation should be treated as unproven.

The debut

Elice raised about 201 billion won (roughly $151 million) in its offering. Bloomberg describes the first-day fall as the worst on record for a Korean IPO raising more than $100 million. The oversubscription claim in the original tip was not independently confirmed here, though Korean book-building for tech listings is routinely heavily covered, which makes a 32% first-day loss more striking, not less.

By the close, the damage had narrowed from the opening low of 38%. That is still a loss of roughly 29,000 won on every share allocated at the offer price.

Contagion, and what the coverage actually blames

The selling did not stay in Seoul. CNBC reported that US-listed 'neocloud' providers slid in sympathy, with CoreWeave down about 4% and Nebius about 5%. The reasoning offered by analysts was about the economics of the AI build-out, not politics: one described a shift from 'build at any cost' to asking who is paying, and for how long.

That reading fits other recent deals. Nvidia-backed Firmus pulled a planned A$5.5 billion Australian listing this month after failing to draw enough support, reportedly after a price cut from A$11 to A$8.25. In the US, Accelevation priced in September at $18, below its marketed $20 to $24 range. Three data-centre offerings in as many months have met resistance on price.

The policy-risk question

The midterm angle is not absurd on its face. Data-centre power demand, local permitting and electricity prices are live political issues in the US, and a change in congressional control could raise the odds of tighter scrutiny. But that is an argument about US-listed names. Nothing found links Elice's Korean investors to American polling, and no named poll or source for a possible Democratic sweep accompanied the claim.

Until someone ties the sell-off to polling with evidence, the cleaner explanation is valuation: investors are charging more for exposure to AI infrastructure whose returns depend on customers' spending.

What to watch

The next test is whether CoreWeave and Nebius recover the day's losses, and how the next data-centre deal prices. Any further withdrawn or downsized offerings would strengthen the valuation reading. A named poll, or a company statement citing policy, would be needed to support the political one.
