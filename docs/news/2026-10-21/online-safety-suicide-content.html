Regulators urged to act on suicide content after death of girl, 14

In one sentence
Calls are growing on Ofcom to show the Online Safety Act can keep suicide content from children after another 14-year-old's death, though details of the new case are unverified.

Another family is grieving a 14-year-old girl, and the same question is being put to the UK's online safety regulator: can the law stop suicide-related material reaching children? The details of this latest case were not available to verify at the time of writing. No inquest finding, named platform or Ofcom response has been confirmed, so what follows is the legal and political context rather than a finding about this death.

What the law now requires

The Online Safety Act received Royal Assent in October 2023. It gives Ofcom powers to enforce duties on platforms to tackle illegal content and to protect children from harmful material. Encouraging or assisting suicide is a criminal offence, so it falls under the illegal-content duties. Content that is legal but harmful to children, including material about suicide and self-harm, falls under separate child-safety duties.

Those duties have been phased in through Ofcom's codes of practice, and they are still young. Ofcom has had little time to show what enforcement looks like against a large platform, and critics say the evidence so far is thin. One bereaved parent has argued in print that Ofcom's Children's Safety Codes are too cautious and risk-averse to prevent deaths like her daughter's.

The precedent

The case that shaped the Act is Molly Russell's. She died in November 2017, aged 14. A 2022 inquest concluded she died from an act of self-harm while suffering from depression and the negative effects of online content. The coroner's Prevention of Future Deaths report went to social media firms and the government. It urged lawmakers to consider age verification, an independent regulator for platform content, and a review of parental controls.

Molly's death was not the last. In 2024 a coroner sent a prevention-of-future-deaths report to Ofcom and others after the inquest into Isabella Shere, also 14, which concluded in suicide. Ofcom also opened an investigation in April 2025 into a suicide website linked to deaths in the UK. Each case adds to the argument that the regime must show results, not only publish codes.

What is at stake

The pressure on Ofcom is practical. It can fine companies and seek court orders to restrict access to services, but those tools only matter if they are used. The questions campaigners are likely to press are:

  • Speed of action. How quickly Ofcom opens an investigation when harmful content is reported.
  • Age checks. Whether current age-assurance requirements keep under-16s away from the most dangerous material.
  • Takedown standards. Whether platforms must remove suicide content within a defined time, rather than relying on their own judgment.

Without a new fact, this debate is largely a repeat of the one that followed Molly Russell's death. A coroner's finding naming specific content or a platform would change that. It would give Ofcom a concrete case to answer, and could prompt a formal response, which coroners can require within 56 days of a prevention-of-future-deaths report. That response, and any enforcement step Ofcom takes against a named service, is the next thing to watch.
