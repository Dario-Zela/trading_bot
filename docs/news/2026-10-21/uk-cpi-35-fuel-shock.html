UK inflation rises to 3.5%, above forecasts, as Gulf war lifts fuel prices

In one sentence
UK CPI inflation rose to 3.5% in September, above forecasts, on Gulf-war fuel costs, leaving the Bank of England to weigh an energy shock against its easing path.

UK consumer price inflation rose to 3.5% in the year to September, the Office for National Statistics reported on Wednesday, up from 3.1% in August and 2.9% in July. The reading was above forecasters' expectations, according to the initial reports of the release. Motor fuel is the chief culprit, and the cause sits far from Westminster: the war in the Gulf and the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz. The question now is what a central bank does with inflation it did not cause and cannot easily influence.

The number

Three consecutive monthly increases have taken headline CPI from 2.9% to 3.5% since July. Transport is where most of the movement sits. Transport inflation rose to 6.6% from 4.6% a month earlier, and motor fuel prices were 29.8% higher than a year ago, according to MoneyWeek's coverage of the release. Restaurants and hotels were the largest downward contributor, which means the rise came from energy and fuel rather than from broad strength in the price of services.

A consensus figure for the month was not available in the reports reviewed, so the size of the miss cannot be stated precisely. It is, though, a miss in the direction the Bank of England has been bracing for. Its own projection puts inflation averaging about 3.75% in the fourth quarter of 2026 and near 4% in early 2027. Deutsche Bank also expects roughly 4% by early next year. On those paths, 3.5% is a way station rather than a peak.

The energy chain

The pump price is the most visible link between the war and household budgets, but not the only one. The Ofgem price cap rose 4% on 1 October, taking the typical annual household bill to £1,723. Cornwall Insight, the consultancy, expects a further rise of about 16% in early 2027. Brent crude has been back above $100 a barrel. None of those increases is captured in full in the September figure, so the pass-through into the CPI basket has further to run.

The physical picture remains contested. This edition also reports Iran's claim that a tanker was hit by a mine in Hormuz, set against the US Secretary of State's assertion that flows are close to pre-war levels. Diesel, meanwhile, has stayed elevated as Washington presses refiners and European partners over supply. A durable fall in pump prices depends on which of those accounts proves closer to the truth.

The Bank's dilemma

The conventional view is that central banks look through a one-off energy price shock: it lifts the price level, but it also squeezes real incomes and weakens demand, which eventually pulls inflation back down. The view holds only if the shock is temporary and if it does not leak into wages and into what firms and households expect prices to do next. After the inflation of 2021–23, the Monetary Policy Committee has little appetite for testing that assumption.

That is the dilemma. A supply-driven rise argues for patience, since higher rates cannot lower the price of crude. But a run of upside surprises, with inflation heading towards 4%, makes it harder to justify further easing, and harder to explain a cut to a public that is watching the cost of filling a tank. The more plausible near-term outcome is that any easing is delayed rather than reversed, though that is an inference from the Bank's own projections rather than a stated policy position. This report does not have the current Bank Rate or the date of the next decision in front of it, and neither is asserted here.

Gilts and the wider bill

The market channel is the one that reaches households fastest. If investors push back the timing of rate cuts, or price a higher path for inflation, gilt yields tend to rise. Those yields set the cost of fixed-rate mortgages, which are priced off swap rates closely tied to gilts, and they feed straight into the government's debt-interest bill. A weaker pound would add to imported inflation, though sterling's reaction to the release was not available at the time of writing, and neither were yield moves on the day.

The fiscal angle is awkward for the Treasury. The Chancellor has ruled out a Capital Gains Tax rise ahead of the Budget, while the Conservatives dare Labour to cut spending instead. Higher borrowing costs narrow the room for either approach.

What to watch

The next data point is the October CPI release, due in November, which will show whether fuel is still pushing the index up or whether the September jump was the leading edge of a plateau. After that comes the Bank's next rate decision and its updated inflation projections, which will show whether the 3.75% fourth-quarter forecast stands. Before either, the January energy price cap announcement will say how much of the expected 16% rise is confirmed. The path of Brent, and what happens in Hormuz, will matter more to all three than any domestic variable.
