Trade deals: India and Mexico said to be close, Canada tariffs face a new lawsuit

In one sentence
A US importer has sued over the 50% tariffs on Canadian goods under Section 338 while Ottawa offers no deal timetable, and India and Mexico talk up deals that remain unsigned.

A US food importer has sued President Donald Trump over the 50% tariffs on Canadian goods. Ottawa, meanwhile, says only that a trade deal will come "at some point." The suit was filed on 20 October. It lands as Canadian ministers defend the aerospace sector after Trump's remarks about Bombardier.

The lawsuit

The plaintiff is Gellert Global Group, a New Jersey food importer. It filed in the US Court of International Trade. It asks the court to declare the tariffs unlawful and to order refunds of duties already paid.

The case turns on the statute, not the rate. The administration imposed the levies under Section 338 of the Trade Act of 1930. That provision lets a president impose tariffs of up to 50% on countries found to discriminate against US commerce. According to the complaint, as reported by CNBC and Al Jazeera, it has not been used to levy tariffs in the 96 years since it was enacted. The suit also argues that Section 338 has been superseded by Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, which lays out a more procedural route to trade penalties.

The plaintiff's counsel is the Liberty Justice Center. It is the same group that challenged Trump's "reciprocal" tariffs. On 20 February, the Supreme Court ruled that he could not use emergency powers to impose those duties. That history is why Section 338 is being tested: the earlier legal basis is gone, and this is the replacement.

The suit covers a wide range of Canadian products, including dairy, furniture, clothing and wine. The White House has not commented publicly. An administration official told The Canadian Press on background that the president's actions would be defended vigorously. The case is at its earliest stage. A ruling is not imminent, and nothing in the filing changes what importers pay today.

Ottawa and Bombardier

The legal challenge arrives without a negotiating track to sit beside it. Ottawa's message is that a deal will come "at some point," which gives no timetable. Reporting this autumn describes talks as having deteriorated in recent weeks, and Canada's retaliatory list covers nearly $20bn of 700 different US goods.

Aerospace is the sharpest edge. In September, Trump said Bombardier should not be allowed to sell in the US unless its planes are built there. Earlier, in January, he threatened a 50% tariff on Canadian-made aircraft and decertification of new Canadian planes. He tied that threat to Canada's refusal to certify certain Gulfstream jets. The US is Bombardier's biggest market for business jets, and large buyers such as NetJets depend on its supply. Canadian ministers have responded by stressing protection of the sector. Neither the tariff rate nor the legal basis of any aircraft measure has been specified in the material reviewed here.

India and Mexico

The other trade threads are moving on official optimism more than signed text. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in late May that a US-India deal could come in "weeks, not months," and told a House hearing on 3 June that the sides were "a few weeks away from being able to conclude." Four and a half months later there is still no signed agreement. A report from 18 October described the deal as stalled "down to the last 1%." It cited legal uncertainty over the tariff plan after court challenges to the reciprocal duties, as well as friction over energy imports.

Mexico has also described its negotiations as close. This desk could not independently confirm the latest "very close" remark. Past statements are a poor guide: in October 2025 Economy Minister Marcelo Ebrard put the talks at about 90% complete, and steel and aluminium tariffs remained unresolved.

What ties them together

The common problem is authority. Several of these negotiations were built on tariff powers the courts have already narrowed. Section 338 is the next test of whether a president can set rates on this scale without Congress. A counterparty weighing a concession has reason to ask whether the tariff it is negotiating against will survive.

The next concrete marker is procedural: the government's response in the Court of International Trade, and whether the court moves to expedite. On the diplomatic side, the test is simpler. India's deal either gets a signature or becomes another slipped timeline.
