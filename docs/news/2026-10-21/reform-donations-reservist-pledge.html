Reform faces questions over legality of large donations as Farage pledges reservist tax cut

In one sentence
A single Guardian report says a senior Reform UK figure doubts the legality of some large donations, raising a possible Electoral Commission question that remains unproven.

A senior figure inside Reform UK is reported to have questioned whether some of the large donations the party has accepted are lawful. If the reporting holds up, the party's finances become a matter for regulators as well as a political argument. The account rests on a single outlet, the Guardian, and the details published so far are thin. There are no named individuals, no amounts and no dates. The article also carries no on-record quotes that this edition has been able to confirm. Reform has not, in the material available, acknowledged any internal doubt.

A story with a history

The claim lands on ground that has already been disturbed. Reform has spent the autumn defending its funding. In recent weeks it received two donations of £36 million each, from the cryptocurrency billionaires Ben Delo and Christopher Harborne, a combined £72 million. Nigel Farage has called them "100% compliant with the law today". He has not said whether they would survive the cap on donations that the government has proposed, which ministers want to apply retrospectively.

Other episodes have added to the pressure. Reform has been reported to the Metropolitan Police and the Electoral Commission over claims of illegal donations. Channel 4 News broadcast undercover footage of party figures discussing how to handle foreign-sourced money. Two people, the long-standing Farage aide Dan Jukes and the head of policy James Orr, were suspended pending an internal inquiry. Mr Jukes has denied wrongdoing. Mr Farage is also under investigation by the parliamentary commissioner for standards over a £5 million gift, which he says he was right not to declare.

Against that background, a report of doubt from within the party matters more than an outside critic's complaint would. Opponents are expected to question Reform's money. A senior insider doing so would suggest that the disagreement is not only between the party and its critics.

What the law asks

UK parties may accept donations above £500 only from "permissible donors", broadly individuals on the electoral register, UK-registered companies carrying on business here, and a small number of other bodies. Parties must report larger gifts to the Electoral Commission and take reasonable steps to check where the money comes from. Breaches can bring fines and, in serious cases, referral to prosecutors. The question for any large donation is therefore not its size. It is whether the donor was eligible and whether the party checked.

That is why an internal doubt, even an unquantified one, would be significant. It would bear on whether the party's checks were adequate, which is the test the Commission would apply. For now the claim is not substantiated enough to show any breach.

The reservist pledge

The news cycle around the party also included a policy offer. Mr Farage pledged to scrap a tax affecting armed forces reservists, according to a BBC Politics headline. The specific tax, its cost and how it would be funded have not been confirmed, and searches found no earlier Reform policy on the subject. The pledge fits the party's recent emphasis on defence and the cost of living. It also comes after Reform scaled back its 2024 promise of £90 billion in tax cuts to an "aspiration", with savings to come first. A costed version of the reservist measure would show how far that retreat has gone.

What to watch

The next developments are in the regulators' hands. The Electoral Commission has not said whether it will act on the latest report. If it opens an inquiry, or if the Guardian or others publish names and amounts, the claim moves from a political talking point to a compliance question. Parliament's handling of the proposed donation cap will matter too, since a retrospective rule would test the very gifts now in dispute.
