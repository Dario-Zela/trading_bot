Musk weighs merging Tesla and SpaceX

In one sentence
Reports that Musk is weighing a Tesla–SpaceX combination raise hard questions about valuation, related-party governance and who speaks for Tesla's minority shareholders.

Elon Musk is reported to be weighing a combination of Tesla, the Nasdaq-listed carmaker, with SpaceX, the privately held rocket and satellite group. Both companies have him as chief executive. No deal, board process or regulatory filing has been cited in the reporting. But the idea has circulated since at least late January, when Bloomberg reported that a Tesla tie-up was among the options discussed around SpaceX. The reports say some investors have pushed for it. Musk and the companies did not comment publicly on that report.

The mechanics are unusual. In most mergers a buyer and a seller bargain at arm's length. Here the same person would sit on both sides of the table, and the price of one party could not be read off a screen.

Pricing a private company against a public one

Tesla trades continuously, so its equity value is observable every second of the session. SpaceX has no such reference point. Its value is established in periodic private share sales and tender offers, which are set by a narrow group of buyers and sellers, and it publishes no audited accounts that Tesla's public holders can read.

Any exchange ratio would therefore rest on judgment: a forecast of SpaceX's launch and satellite revenues, a view on what its other ventures are worth, and a discount or premium for the fact that its shares do not trade. Each of those inputs moves the answer, and a small change in the ratio shifts value between the two groups of shareholders by large sums.

The companies already do business. Reporting has noted that SpaceX bought nearly $700 million of Tesla Megapack battery systems over two years to support data centres in Tennessee. Tesla's energy-storage manufacturing has been floated as a possible help to SpaceX's plans for space-based computing. Such links give a merger a commercial story. They also show the companies already transact with each other, which is the kind of dealing that related-party rules exist to scrutinise.

Who protects the minority

Musk held roughly 13% of Tesla in 2025 filings, a figure that should be checked against the latest proxy. That is far short of majority control, but large enough to make him the dominant voice in any vote. His stake in SpaceX is understood to be much larger.

The standard remedy for a conflicted deal is procedural. Independent directors form a special committee, hire their own advisers, negotiate hard and can walk away. Many deals also condition approval on a majority of the shares not held by the interested party. Courts in Delaware, where Tesla was incorporated until it moved to Texas in 2024, have long examined such transactions closely. The reincorporation matters here: Texas law gives shareholders somewhat less room to sue than Delaware's, and how a Texas court would treat a conflicted merger is untested.

The skeptics' case is practical. Legal experts quoted in coverage said valuation, shareholder protections, governance and stock-conversion structure would make any transaction extremely difficult. Tesla's board has also faced scrutiny over its closeness to Musk, which would make a credible special committee harder to convene. The earlier Bloomberg report treated a SpaceX tie-up with xAI as the more realistic option because the technological fit is clearer, while a Tesla combination faced regulatory and structural hurdles.

What a precedent would mean

The stakes reach beyond these two companies. Founder-led groups with a listed company and several private ones have a standing temptation to fold them together when it suits the founder. If a Tesla–SpaceX deal went through on a process that outside investors judged adequate, it would become a template. If it were challenged and lost, it would mark the limits of what boards can approve.

Tesla holders would also take on risk they cannot easily measure: the price paid, the quality of the private books, and the question of whether a car and energy company with a market valuation set by public investors should become a vehicle for a space business whose finances they cannot inspect.

Nothing has been proposed formally, and the companies could remain separate. The first concrete markers would be a filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, or an announcement that Tesla's board has formed a special committee. Either would show whether independent directors are in control of the process or only ratify it. Until then, the story is a report of an idea, and the trading price of Tesla shares carries whatever probability investors assign to it.
