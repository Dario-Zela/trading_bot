Egg freezing in sport: insurance with no guarantee

In one sentence
Egg freezing is marketed to athletes as career insurance, but it guarantees no baby, and sport's contracts and maternity protections have not caught up.

Egg freezing is increasingly pitched to elite sportswomen as career insurance: bank the eggs now, keep competing, start a family later. The pitch is easy to follow. The guarantee behind it is not there. Freezing preserves a chance of pregnancy, not a pregnancy, and sport's contracts and rulebooks have been slow to deal with the difference.

What the procedure does, and doesn't

The odds depend heavily on age at freezing. UK regulator HFEA figures for 2014–2016 put the chance of a birth at about 27% for eggs frozen under 35, against 13% for eggs frozen after that. The HFEA has also found that age at thaw matters little; age at freezing is what counts. These figures are several years old, so current national data should be checked at hfea.gov.uk.

Clinic numbers are broadly similar. London Women's Clinic reported an overall live birth rate of 26% among patients who returned to use their eggs, and only 14% of those who froze came back at all. Lister Fertility Clinic quotes pregnancy rates of 40% for under-35s, 30% for 35–40 and 16% above that. These are single-centre figures and not directly comparable with the national data.

The cycle itself involves hormone stimulation and a retrieval procedure, which sits awkwardly in a training calendar. Tennis player Sloane Stephens, who has frozen her eggs twice rather than step away from the tour, has said the first cycle left her heavier and that she returned to competition too soon. One clinic's website says there is no evidence retrieval harms long-term strength or endurance; that is the clinic's own claim.

Where governance lags

Some bodies are moving. Spain's football federation has partnered with a Madrid clinic to offer egg freezing and fertility treatment through 2029 to national-team players, referees and staff. Basketball's Sue Bird, who froze eggs in 2019, has pushed for fertility cover in the WNBA's collective bargaining agreement. Netball's Geva Mentor has called freezing a security blanket that lets her aim to play towards 40.

The objections are pointed. Athlete Ana Peleteiro has reportedly argued that federations should concentrate on maternity protection and childcare instead. The worry is that a voluntary perk hardens into an unspoken expectation: that a woman who has not frozen her eggs is choosing to put her career at risk.

That is the governance gap. A freezing benefit can look generous while leaving the harder questions open: whether a pregnant player keeps her contract and pay, whether her ranking or selection is protected, and what clubs owe her if treatment affects her season. Few clubs or governing bodies have published answers.

What to watch

The test is whether fertility benefits arrive with maternity protections attached rather than instead of them. The next round of collective bargaining in the women's leagues, and any federation that follows Spain's lead, will show which way it goes.
