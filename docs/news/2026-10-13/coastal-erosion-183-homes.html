Coastal erosion has claimed at least 183 English homes in 16 years

In one sentence
A reported tally of at least 183 English homes lost to coastal erosion in 16 years sharpens the question of who funds adaptation, though the data behind it is unverified.

A tally reported by the Guardian puts the number of English homes lost to coastal erosion at no fewer than 183 over 16 years. The figure turns a series of local losses, in Norfolk, Yorkshire and elsewhere, into one number that can be set against national policy. The count is a floor, as the "at least" implies. The underlying dataset and how it defines a "lost" home were not available for this article, and no official national tally of homes lost could be found. The figure should be read as a reported estimate until that method is published.

What the count does and doesn't show

Losses on this scale are concentrated in a few places. The soft cliffs of Norfolk and the Holderness coast of Yorkshire retreat fastest. Happisburgh, in Norfolk, has been reported to have lost around 35 homes in two decades, with its frontage receding by as much as six metres a year in bad spells. Hemsby, also in Norfolk, has about 35 homes at risk, a number reported as likely to rise towards 90.

A national total of 183 would therefore mean a small number of communities carry most of the loss. It would not mean a uniform national trend. Whether the figure counts only demolished or collapsed homes, or also those abandoned ahead of the sea, changes what it tells a reader. That distinction is the main thing the source needs to clarify.

The count also looks backwards. Projections look further ahead, and they are larger. The campaign group One Home estimates that almost £600m of coastal homes in England could be lost by 2100 under current policy, using Environment Agency erosion mapping. An older, unpublished Environment Agency analysis, reported by a campaign body, put the century-scale loss in England and Wales at about 7,000 homes. That figure could not be checked against an official source and should be treated with caution. Even so, 183 homes in 16 years sits at the low end of what those projections imply for the decades ahead.

Who decides, and who pays

The policy framework is older than the losses. England's coast is divided into 20 Shoreline Management Plans, drafted by coastal groups between 2006 and 2012. Each stretch is assigned one of three policies: hold the line, managed realignment, or no active intervention. The plans are non-statutory, but planning guidance treats them as the evidence base for coastal decisions. Where the policy is "no active intervention", homeowners have no automatic right to a sea wall and no general compensation scheme to fall back on, as campaigners point out.

Government money has begun to move towards adaptation, though not towards defence. The Coastwise project in North Norfolk has received more than £15m, on the condition, as reported, that it cannot be spent on sea walls or gabions. It is examining selective buyouts and the replacement of mobile homes. A separate £36m fund is reported to help households in Norfolk and Yorkshire adapt and relocate. The Environment Agency has concluded that some soft-coast communities in eastern England will need managed retreat.

The gap is between those pilots and a settled national rule. A loss count gives councils and residents a figure to cite, but it does not say who bears the cost of a home that is no longer insurable or saleable before it falls into the sea.

What to watch

The nearest test is Defra's commitment to review national Shoreline Management Plan policy by 2026, informed by an Environment Agency refresh of the plans. That review will show whether "no active intervention" stretches get a funded route out, such as buyouts or relocation grants, or stay as lines on a map. Publication of the data behind the 183 figure, including its definition of a lost home and its list of locations, would also let it be checked against the Environment Agency's own erosion mapping.
