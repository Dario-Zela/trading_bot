A UK creator earns £120,000 a year making viral videos

In one sentence
A UK creator reportedly earns £120,000 from viral videos, but the figure says more about dependence on platform algorithms than about what creators typically make.

A UK creator is reported to earn about £120,000 a year making viral videos. That is roughly four times the median full-time UK salary, and it comes from clips that an audience decides, in a few hours, whether to watch. The figure comes from a BBC Business profile. This piece has not independently verified it, and it does not name the creator or give a breakdown of where the money comes from.

A number like that makes a good headline. It is a weak guide to how the wider creator economy works.

One income, many landlords

A salaried worker has one employer and a contract. A video creator usually has several income streams, each controlled by someone else. Typical sources are:

  • a share of platform advertising revenue;
  • paid brand partnerships;
  • affiliate links;
  • merchandise;
  • subscriptions or tips.

Of these, only the last two or three sit close to the creator's own audience. The rest depend on a platform's recommendation system choosing to show the work to strangers, and on advertisers being willing to sit beside it.

That is the real economic point of a £120,000 year. It is a gross figure for a business with no guaranteed customers. The creator pays for their own equipment, editing time, tax and pension. They also carry the risk that a change to a feed's ranking rules, a shift in payout terms or a demonetisation decision cuts reach overnight. Platforms generally change these rules without negotiating with the people who rely on them.

What a profile cannot show

A single success story tells readers little about the distribution. Creator earnings are widely understood to be heavily skewed: a small group at the top earns well, and a long tail earns little or nothing. The BBC profile, as summarised for this piece, does not supply the survey data or payout rates that would show where £120,000 sits in that spread. Without them, the figure is an outlier or a typical top-tier result, and a reader cannot tell which.

Volatility matters just as much as the average. A viral video is, by definition, not repeatable on demand. Income from a hit can arrive in a rush and then fade, so a creator earning £120,000 in a good year may have no reliable way to forecast the next one. Mortgage lenders and landlords tend to prefer steadier evidence of income.

What to watch

The useful follow-up is data rather than another profile. That means platform payout rates per thousand views, published by the platforms or inferred from creators' disclosures, and large-sample creator surveys that report medians as well as means. Until those are set beside the headline figure, £120,000 is best read as proof that the ceiling is high, not that the floor is safe.
