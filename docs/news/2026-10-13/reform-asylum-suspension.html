Reform UK pledges to suspend the asylum system for up to five years

In one sentence
Reform UK says it would suspend asylum for up to five years, a pledge that needs ECHR withdrawal and primary legislation and is aimed at squeezing Labour on immigration.

Reform UK has pledged that, if it wins the next general election, it would suspend the British asylum system for up to five years. Nigel Farage announced the policy in Kent on Monday. It is a promise from an opposition party and carries no legal force. The next election is not due until 2029, and the plan would need legislation, and a good deal more, to take effect.

What Reform says it would do

According to reports of the announcement, Reform would stop granting refugee status or humanitarian protection during the suspension. It would also review refugee grants made in the previous five years. It would end legal refugee routes and resettlement schemes. Ukrainians who arrived after Russia's full-scale invasion in 2022 would be exempt.

The most striking element concerns the backlog. Reports say roughly 90,000 outstanding asylum and humanitarian-protection appeals would go unheard, with claimants marked for deportation. The tribunal system that hears those appeals would be abolished. Outlets differ on whether the 90,000 figure counts claims or appeals, so the number should be treated with care until Reform publishes detail.

Farage framed the policy as a matter of security. "The country is currently facing a national security emergency and the first duty of government is to protect its citizens," he said. Reform also repeated its pledge to end small-boat crossings within the first 100 days of government.

The legal problem

The pledge only works if Britain leaves the legal framework that governs asylum. Reform says it would withdraw from the European Convention on Human Rights, repeal the Human Rights Act 1998 and disapply the 1951 Refugee Convention. That is the real content of the proposal. Suspending the system is a consequence of that decision, which is far larger than any asylum rule.

Charities have called the plan unworkable, and critics say it is legally untenable. The reason is that the commitments it would override are not only domestic. Leaving the ECHR would also have consequences for other treaty arrangements, including those that underpin the Good Friday Agreement. Any government attempting it would need primary legislation, would face a hostile House of Lords and would meet sustained court challenges. This article could not confirm Reform's own account of how it would handle those obstacles.

Why it lands on Labour

The pledge matters less as a blueprint than as a marker. Immigration remains the axis on which Reform pushes the larger parties, and the policy forces Labour to answer on ground Reform has chosen. Labour's reply, as reported, was to call it "a clear sign of desperation". I could not trace the wording to a named spokesperson.

Labour's own position is awkward. Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood has pursued a tightening of asylum rules, framed as restoring order so that Britain can keep offering sanctuary. That drew open dissent from Labour backbenchers, some of whom said it echoed Reform's rhetoric. Richard Burgon called it a desperate attempt to triangulate with Reform. A harder pledge from Farage leaves the government squeezed from both sides. Moving further right risks more revolts in its own ranks, while holding its line invites the charge that it is too soft.

What to watch

The first test is whether Reform publishes costings and legal advice, which would show if this is a commitment or a campaign line. The second is whether ministers rebut the policy on its legal merits or simply on tone. Both will be visible in the party conference season and in Mahmood's next statements on the asylum backlog.
