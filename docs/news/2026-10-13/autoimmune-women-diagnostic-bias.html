Women more likely to be told autoimmune conditions are psychological

In one sentence
A Cambridge and UEA survey of 6,600 people found women with autoimmune disease report being given psychological explanations more often than men, and waiting longer for diagnosis.

Women with autoimmune conditions are more likely than men to report having been told their symptoms were psychological, according to a survey-based study from the University of Cambridge and the University of East Anglia. The same women also reported waiting longer for a correct diagnosis. The findings rest on what patients say they were told, not on clinical records, and that limits what they can show.

What the study found

The work was led by Dr Melanie Sloan, a researcher in Cambridge's department of public health and primary care. Shaista Tayabali, a patient with an autoimmune condition, is a co-author. It surveyed more than 6,600 people with autoimmune conditions. Most were women and most were in the UK. It was reported as published in The Lancet's eClinicalMedicine. Secondary reports are inconsistent on the journal name, and this article has not seen the paper itself.

The headline figures, as reported:

  • 43% of women said they had been told their condition had a psychiatric cause, against 33% of men.
  • 34% of women said their symptoms had been misdiagnosed as psychosomatic, meaning triggered or worsened by stress or anxiety, against 22% of men.
  • After adjusting for age, disease group, continent and household finances, women were about 46% more likely to report a psychiatric explanation and about 74% more likely to report a psychosomatic one.
  • The most common time to diagnosis was under three months for men and one to two years for women. Women also reported more healthcare consultations.

The conditions differed by sex. Almost a quarter of the women had autoimmune thyroid disease, meaning Hashimoto's or Graves', and one in seven had systemic lupus. Among men the most common were rheumatoid arthritis, vasculitis and antiphospholipid syndrome. Reports of being wrongly given a different physical diagnosis were slightly higher among men.

"Psychological or mental health explanations can be given too soon," Sloan said, adding that women "may be particularly vulnerable to these types of misdiagnoses".

Tayabali said that "as women we still often have a harder time having our symptoms believed".

How far the evidence goes

Several cautions apply. The data are self-reported, so they record perceptions of consultations, not verified diagnoses. A respondent who recalls being told it was stress may be remembering accurately or not, and the survey cannot say whether the advice was clinically wrong at the time.

The sample is also not representative. Because it was mostly female, UK-based and drawn from people who chose to respond, those with bad experiences may be over-represented. The male subgroup size and the full statistical methods were not given in the coverage reviewed.

The link to delay is an association. Different conditions have different diagnostic difficulty, and the men and women in the sample had different mixes of disease. The adjustment for disease group helps, but does not settle why the timelines diverged. The researchers themselves note that broader diagnostic challenges affect both sexes.

The overlap in symptoms is a plausible mechanism. Tiredness, low mood, brain fog, bloating, muscle pain and rashes are common in autoimmune disease and are also easy to attribute to stress, which clinicians describe as diagnostic overshadowing. Earlier smaller studies, including a 2025 paper in the journal Lupus and several case series, describe the same pattern. The new study is notable chiefly for its size.

What happens next

Professor David D'Cruz, a consultant rheumatologist at Guy's Hospital in London who was not an author, said clinicians must remain open-minded when symptoms persist or span several body systems. The practical test is whether the finding is reproduced with clinical records, where timings and the wording of consultations can be checked against what patients recall. The full paper, with its methods and confidence intervals, is the next thing to read.
