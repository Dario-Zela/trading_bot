Fed's Hammack wants more restraint, says neutral is likely higher

In one sentence
Cleveland Fed's Beth Hammack wants tighter policy, thinks neutral is likely higher, and says higher rates won't deter hyperscalers — implying AI capex may be less rate-sensitive than assumed.

The Federal Reserve's most prominent rate hawk this week argued for tighter policy. She also argued that the biggest spenders in the economy may not respond to it. Beth Hammack, president of the Cleveland Fed, said on Monday that policy needs more restraint, that the neutral interest rate is likely higher than commonly assumed, and that higher borrowing costs "won't dissuade hyperscalers" from spending.

Hyperscalers are the large cloud and AI infrastructure operators whose data-centre building has become a main driver of US business investment. If Hammack is right about them, a tighter Fed would fall mainly on other parts of the economy.

What Hammack said

According to FXStreet's account of her remarks, Hammack said she is hearing "early warning signs" of a possible problem with inflation expectations. She said the Fed needs to "cool the economy at the margins", and that without action "market conditions would change". The live question, she said, is "how fast to make policy more restrictive".

She did not give a target rate, a number of increases or a date, and the report carries none. The reported shape of her preference is smaller adjustments that come relatively quickly, rather than fewer, larger moves spaced further apart. The logic is that frequent small steps let the committee watch inflation expectations as it goes, without a single large move that might overshoot.

On the neutral rate, the level at which policy neither stimulates nor restrains, she said it is "likely higher" but hard to pin down. She added that her own projection is among the higher ones on the Federal Open Market Committee. That matters because the neutral rate is the yardstick for whether the current stance is tight at all. A higher neutral estimate means the same policy rate is less restrictive than it looks, which strengthens the case for going further.

She also described Treasury yields as "modest" by historic standards. And she said a return to 3% mortgage rates would probably mean something had gone wrong in the economy.

The hyperscaler claim

The remark most likely to draw attention was brief: "Higher interest rates won't dissuade hyperscalers, but might influence firms in other industries." The report gives no further detail. It does not say what evidence she relies on.

There is a plausible basis for the view. Hyperscaler capital spending has been planned around demand for AI computing, not around the cost of money. Moody's has estimated that US hyperscaler capex will top $700bn in 2026. The same analysis noted that spending on that scale is eroding the companies' historically strong free cash flow and pushing them to borrow more. Heavier borrowing makes them more exposed to rates, not less, so Hammack's claim is an assertion rather than a settled fact.

The interpretive point is narrower. If the largest investment wave is insensitive to rates, tightening works through a smaller set of channels. Smaller firms, housing and consumer credit take more of the strain, while the AI build-out carries on. That would make policy less efficient at cooling demand, and it may be part of why she argues for more restraint, not less.

The labour-market caveat

Hammack was not one-sided. She said the Fed does not want to threaten the jobs side of its mandate. She cited low-income workers who first described trading down and are now "juggling what bills to pay". Her case for tightening rests on inflation expectations, not on a view that households are comfortable.

What to watch

The test is whether other FOMC members endorse a higher neutral rate, since the committee's published projections are where that debate becomes visible. Traders pricing the rate path will also want the next inflation readings and any further Fed commentary on the pace of adjustment. Investors will also watch the next round of hyperscaler capex guidance, which will show whether spending really is deaf to the cost of money.
