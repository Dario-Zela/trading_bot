Oil risk premium stays as US-Iran talks stall and supertankers leave a Gulf hub

In one sentence
Stalled US-Iran talks and attacks on Gulf shipping keep oil risk elevated, with Fitch's base case now a Hormuz reopening deal only in Q1 2027.

The price of oil still carries a premium for the chance that one of the world's main export routes stays impaired. Talks between Washington and Tehran have stalled, Iran has called the US response to its peace terms "inadequate", and supertankers have been leaving a Gulf transfer hub. The ratings agency Fitch has pushed back its expected resolution. Its base case is now a durable deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz in the first quarter of 2027.

The evidence for this article is thinner than the subject deserves. The Fitch timing comes from a secondhand report of the agency's commentary, and the tanker movements come from a headline. Neither has been checked against Fitch's own note or against ship-tracking data. The analysis below works within that limit.

A forecast that keeps moving

The Q1 2027 date is the latest of several. In March, Fitch's framework treated a base case of about $70 a barrel as consistent with only a temporary disruption, and it put Brent in a $100–$120 range if closure persisted. By June its base case assumed reopening around the end of July, after an effective five-month closure. Brent was then expected to average $100–$110 a barrel in May–July, ease to about $80 in August and drift towards $70 afterwards.

That path did not happen on schedule. A June interim deal, announced with promises that the strait would reopen within days, has not delivered a durable settlement. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araqchi, has said Tehran will not start talks while Washington breaches that interim agreement. Reports also cite Iran's push for international recognition of its control over the strait, including the right to levy transit fees, as a sticking point.

The Fitch shift is a change in the date by which the risk is expected to clear, and the sources reviewed give no new Brent forecast attached to it. Older Fitch price numbers belong to older timelines and should not be read across.

Prices have swung with the headlines

Brent has traded in a wide band this year, from above $115 when talks deadlocked to below $80 on hopes of a deal. In late August it was near $86 after Iran and Oman discussed a temporary shared navigation corridor through the strait. Moves of 2–5% on a single day of diplomatic news are the pattern.

A risk premium in this setting is the gap between what oil would cost if flows were assured and what it costs when they are not. That gap does not need a closed strait to persist. It needs only the possibility that insurers, shipowners and charterers keep treating Gulf passage as hazardous. Attacks on Gulf shipping, and tankers repositioning away from a transfer hub, feed that caution directly. They raise freight and insurance costs and make cargoes harder to schedule, whatever the headline crude price does.

What a 2027 date implies

If the base case is right, the premium is not a feature of the current negotiating round. It would span the northern winter, when heating demand adds to the strain on supply, and run well into next year. The heating-oil squeeze on British households, covered elsewhere in this edition, is one place where that would show up.

Import-dependent economies are the most exposed. Their fuel bills rise when the premium holds, and their currencies and budgets carry the cost. Producers outside the Gulf benefit from higher prices, but the benefit is uneven and can be offset by weaker demand.

There is also a counter-scenario. Fitch has argued previously that reopening would produce an oil market surplus and a sharp price correction. A later deal would delay that correction, not cancel it. The longer the delay, the more the price depends on how much supply the market can find elsewhere in the meantime.

What to watch

The first test is whether Iran and Oman complete the shipping-lane arrangement, which Tehran has described as being in its final stages while insisting that the US must still meet other conditions. The second is whether Washington offers a revised response to the peace terms Iran has called inadequate. The third is Fitch's own next commodity update, which should show whether the Q1 2027 timing comes with a new Brent assumption or just a later date on the old one.
