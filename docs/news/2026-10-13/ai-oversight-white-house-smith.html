White House reportedly to absorb AI testing officials as Microsoft's Smith says AI is less regulated than milk

In one sentence
The Information reports the White House will absorb AI testing officials to fold safety evaluation into decisions; it is unconfirmed, while Microsoft's Brad Smith says AI is less regulated than milk.

A report that the White House plans to take in the government's AI testing officials is, for now, only a report. The Information says the Trump administration intends to move staff from the body that tests AI models into the White House. The stated aim is to fold AI safety evaluation into decision-making. The version reaching this desk came second-hand, via Benzinga. It has no named official, no date and no formal document attached. The White House has not confirmed it, and nothing here should be read as settled.

What the report says

The claim is narrow. Officials who now work in a standalone testing body would be relocated into the executive office. The report does not say, in the form seen here, how many people would move, what happens to the body they leave, or when.

The reasoning is organisational, and it is worth spelling out. A testing body that sits apart produces evaluations of AI models as technical findings, and others decide what to do with them. If the testers sit inside the White House, their results become an input to the same people who set AI policy. The test and the decision would no longer be separated by an institutional boundary.

That cuts both ways, and the report doesn't say which way is intended:

  • Faster clearance, closer scrutiny. Findings that reach decision-makers directly could speed up the way models are cleared or questioned.
  • Blurred accountability. Evaluators who work for the people they advise may find it harder to publish an unwelcome result. If a model is cleared, it becomes less clear whether the call belongs to the testers or the political staff above them.

Neither outcome follows automatically. Both depend on details of the move that are not yet public.

Smith's milk line

The same day brought a rhetorical gift to anyone arguing that AI oversight is thin. The BBC reports that Microsoft president Brad Smith says AI is less regulated than milk. The comparison is pointed because milk is one of the most heavily regulated foods: it is tested, labelled and inspected along the whole chain from farm to shelf.

The BBC frames the remark as a contribution to the debate over AI rules in the UK and the EU. It is notable who is saying it. Smith leads a company that sells AI products and has a large stake in how they are regulated. A Big Tech executive calling his own sector under-regulated is a position, not an observation. It may reflect a preference for clear, predictable rules over a patchwork, but that reading is an inference and the remark itself does not establish it. The triage material does not include Smith's full argument, so what he would put in place of the current rules is not covered here.

Why the two stories sit together

The two items are not connected by any known link. Nobody has said the Smith remark was prompted by the White House report, or the reverse. They do pose the same question from different directions: who checks AI systems before and after release, and to whom do they answer?

Milk is regulated through standards and inspections with an outside authority behind them. A testing function absorbed into the executive office would be a different design. It would give the government a closer view of model risks, but it would also place that view inside the political machinery rather than beside it. Whether that counts as more oversight or less depends on whether the findings stay independent once the evaluators are down the corridor from the decision-makers.

What to watch

The first marker is simple: an on-record statement from the White House or the agency concerned, or a named official who will stand behind the account. Without it, the story remains a single-sourced report. If it is confirmed, the details that matter most are what happens to the testing body's published work and whether its results will still be released publicly. On Smith's side, the thing to watch is whether the UK or EU debate he is joining produces a concrete proposal for pre-release testing.
