Publicly owned rail is popular, but are the trains on time?

In one sentence
Public ownership of UK train operators is spreading fast, but ORR data does not yet show clear punctuality gains, and LNER's record since transfer is mixed.

Britain is buying back its railway one franchise at a time. By the government's own count, eight in ten of the passenger journeys that Great British Railways will eventually oversee are now run by publicly owned operators under the Department for Transport's holding company, DfT Operator. The harder question is whether those trains are running on time. On the evidence available so far, the answer is not yet clear.

A programme moving faster than its evidence

The East Coast Main Line was the first big test. It went into public hands in June 2023, when LNER replaced Virgin Trains East Coast. Since then the list has grown. West Midlands Trains, which runs London Northwestern Railway and West Midlands Railway, transferred on 1 February 2026. Govia Thameslink Railway, which runs Southern, Thameslink, Great Northern and Gatwick Express, followed on 31 May 2026. Chiltern Railways was scheduled for 20 September and Great Western Railway for 13 December. I could not confirm that the Chiltern transfer has happened. Ministers expect all DfT-contracted services to be transferred by the end of 2027.

Above the operators sits the Great British Railways plan, announced in May 2025. It would put track and train operations in England under one public body.

The pitch to the public is simple: unify the system and it will work better. The government has said in Parliament that publicly owned operators generally do better on punctuality and cancellations than privately run ones. It has pointed to Northern and TransPennine Express, whose cancellation score it says improved by 34%. Those are government claims, and they compare public operators with those not yet transferred. They are not a before-and-after test of the same lines.

What the regulator's figures show

The Office of Rail and Road is the independent check. Its release for January to March 2026 says punctuality and reliability both improved on the same quarter a year earlier. I could not extract the headline percentages from the release itself. For comparison, the January to March 2025 baseline was 68.1% of trains on time, which the ORR defines as arriving within one minute. On the more lenient measure of arriving within three minutes, 85.9% of trains were on time.

The operator-level picture is less tidy. Rail Magazine reported that LNER's three-minute punctuality fell from 75.8% in April 2025 to 71.1% in February 2026, following a timetable change. One summary of the ORR release, which I could not reconcile with the quarterly headline, said punctuality had worsened over the full year to March 2026 for most operators. So the quarterly headline and the longer record are not telling the same story, and a single good quarter proves little.

There is also a measurement problem. The ORR has shifted its emphasis from the one-minute measure to the three-minute one. One commentator argued that this change alone lifts headline punctuality from around 66% to about 84%. Comparisons that span the change need care.

What would settle it

A fair test would compare each transferred operator's ORR figures, on one consistent measure, before and after transfer, and set them against a control group of operators still in private hands. The newer transfers, West Midlands and Thameslink among them, have too short a record for that. The ORR's next quarterly release, covering October to December 2026, is the first likely to include several operators with a meaningful period under public ownership. Until then, public ownership is popular, but the timetable has not yet shown that it works better.
