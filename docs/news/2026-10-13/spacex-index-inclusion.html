SpaceX is up 22% in two months, and index inclusion would be historic

In one sentence
SpaceX is up about 22% in two months, but under S&P's published rules it can't enter the S&P 500 before mid-2027 and would also need sustained profits.

SpaceX shares have risen about 22% over the past two months and are running ahead of the S&P 500, according to a Benzinga report. The rally is real, but it isn't the main story. The bigger question is whether the S&P 500 would take the stock at all, and whether its rulebook or the company's size and fundamentals would decide that.

No inclusion has been announced. On the rules S&P Dow Jones Indices has published, SpaceX is not eligible today.

What the rulebook says

The flagship S&P 500 is not a pure ranking by market value. A company needs a seasoning period of 12 months after listing. It needs positive reported earnings, measured as the sum of the latest four quarters and also the latest quarter. It also needs a minimum public float. A committee then picks from the eligible names, so being big and eligible does not trigger automatic entry.

SpaceX listed in June 2026, according to coverage from that period. Reports said it was targeting a valuation of about $1.75 trillion. The Benzinga brief does not give a share price or an index level. Those figures should be checked against exchange data and are not reproduced here.

The rules were tested in the spring. On 30 April S&P DJI floated a consultation on megacap listings. It proposed halving seasoning to six months and waiving the profitability and float tests for very large companies. On 4 June it dropped the idea. It kept the 12-month period and said size alone would not waive the profitability and float requirements. It did loosen entry to its broader Total Market indices, which are less widely tracked.

Other index providers went the other way. Nasdaq changed its rules so that large new listings can join the Nasdaq-100 after 15 trading days. FTSE Russell allows entry after five trading days. S&P's choice was a deliberate divergence.

Why it still matters

The delay is partly a matter of timing. Twelve months from a June 2026 listing points to mid-2027 at the earliest. SpaceX reported a net loss of about $4.9bn on revenue of about $18.7bn for 2025, according to the same coverage. Until it shows four positive quarters, the earnings test stands in the way regardless of the calendar. Both figures come from secondary reports and should be read as such.

If the stock clears those hurdles, the effect on the index would be large. A company valued near $1.75tn would be among the biggest names the benchmark has added. Index funds would have to buy a large block, and everyone else in the index would shrink slightly in weight. The index is already top-heavy in a few large technology and AI-linked names, as the desk noted in its piece on the AI trade's financing and concentration test. Adding a space and satellite company would put one more big theme into the benchmark, even if the theme is a different one.

The design question is whether the rules or the fundamentals would be doing the work. S&P's seasoning and profitability tests exist to keep speculative, newly listed companies out of a benchmark that trillions of dollars track passively. A company that clears them has earned its place under S&P's own terms. Had S&P waived them for size, the index would have been choosing by market cap alone, which is what the Nasdaq and FTSE Russell changes amount to.

What to watch

The first marker is SpaceX's quarterly results. Four straight profitable quarters, with the latest also in the black, is the gate. The second is the 12-month anniversary of the listing. After that the S&P index committee has discretion, and its announcements typically come a few days before changes take effect. Any fresh S&P consultation on seasoning would also be a signal, since the June decision was a rejection and not necessarily a final word.

Until then, the 22% gain is a price move in a stock that major index funds hold only through the faster-moving indices. The benchmark most investors mean by 'the index' has not changed its terms.
