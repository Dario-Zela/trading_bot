Bessent hits back at Warren's 'Trumpflation' tracker

In one sentence
Treasury Secretary Bessent called Senator Warren's 'Trumpflation' tracker a 'statistical sleight of hand' and answered with a Treasury chart, in a dispute over start dates and baselines.

The US Treasury Secretary has answered a Democratic senator's cost-of-living dashboard with a dashboard of his own. Scott Bessent responded on 9 October to the "Trumpflation" tracker that Senator Elizabeth Warren's Banking Committee office launched two days earlier. He called it a "statistical sleight of hand". The exchange is mostly about where each side chooses to start the clock, and neither side has published new data.

The tracker

Warren is the ranking Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee. Her office says the tool shows cumulative inflation of 5.2% since December 2024 and the average household paying about $3,388 more as a result. It lists rises in electricity, petrol, groceries, health care, rent and child care. The committee says it will also follow wage growth, job growth and household debt.

One detail has drawn criticism. According to Political.org, the tracker measures real wage growth over a narrower window that begins in February 2026, at the start of the Iran war. Starting there rather than at the beginning of the administration changes the picture, and that choice is the main target of Bessent's attack.

The rebuttal

Bessent answered with a Treasury chart titled "Trump Affordability Tracker: The Full Picture". Per Political.org, it makes two claims:

  • Inflation-adjusted weekly wages are up 1.5% since January 2025, against a 3.9% decline over Joe Biden's full term.
  • Annualised core inflation is 2.5% under Donald Trump, against 4.7% under Biden.

Bessent also said Warren's dashboard "conveniently ignores the Bidenflation", according to the same report. The quote is truncated in the source.

Reading the dispute

The two sets of figures do not contradict each other as much as they first appear to. Warren's headline number is a cumulative rise in the price level since late 2024. Bessent's is an annualised rate of core inflation, which excludes food and energy, set against a four-year Biden-era baseline. A price level can keep rising while the annual rate of increase slows. Households tend to judge their finances by the level, in particular what groceries and electricity cost now. Economists generally prefer the rate.

This is not the first clash between the two on the subject. Warren pressed Bessent in May on grocery costs, when her office said monthly grocery inflation had reached a nearly four-year high, and she has questioned his remarks that Americans "feel good" despite weak consumer sentiment.

This account rests largely on Political.org, a lesser-known outlet. I found no confirmation from a wire service or from Treasury's own site. The Warren figures come from her committee's press release. The Bessent figures were not checked against Treasury's chart or against Bureau of Labor Statistics data.

What next

The next test is the Labor Department's consumer price index release for September, when official numbers will show which framing the monthly data favours. Warren's office has said the tracker will be updated, and Treasury has not said whether it will do the same with its own chart.
