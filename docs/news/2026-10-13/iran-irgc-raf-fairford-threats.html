IRAN · FAIRFORD · By C. Tanaka · 2026-10-13 · 4 min read

Burnham blames Iran's Revolutionary Guards for threats to RAF Fairford; ambassador summoned

In one sentence
Burnham says Iran's Revolutionary Guards almost certainly directed a suspected plot against RAF Fairford; Miliband summoned the ambassador, but public evidence is thin and no one is charged.

Britain has publicly accused Iran's Revolutionary Guards of being behind a suspected plot against RAF Fairford, the Gloucestershire air base used by US forces. The government has summoned Tehran's ambassador and imposed sanctions. The accusation turns a security incident at an allied base into a direct dispute between London and Tehran. The public evidence is thin, however, and much of the case rests on assessments the government has not published.

What the government has said

Andy Burnham told the Commons that the threat to Fairford was "almost certainly directed" by the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC), and that it was "highly likely it was coordinated in Iran". Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband then summoned the Iranian ambassador, Seyed Ali Mousavi. The BBC quoted Miliband saying: "Britain will not tolerate threats on our soil." He added that Tehran's attempts to undermine UK and partner security through criminal networks and proxy groups "are unacceptable and will not be tolerated".

The government has also sanctioned two Iranian nationals. The BBC identified one as the head of the regime's counterespionage operations. Burnham promised further sanctions and a crackdown on the criminal networks and funding channels that Iran is alleged to use. Al Jazeera reported that he also linked an incident at RAF Molesworth to Iran. Other outlets did not carry that claim, so it should be treated with caution.

What happened at the base

The underlying events are modest compared with the rhetoric. On the morning of 27 September, five British citizens were detained near Fairford on suspicion of terrorism and explosives-related offences. The Associated Press reports that police eventually arrested seven men. Police found gasoline, an accelerant, in three vans linked to five of the suspects, but no explosives. All have been released on bail and no one has been charged.

US officials told Reuters they suspected an Iranian-linked plan for a drone attack. That is a significant claim, but it is attributed to unnamed officials and has not been set out publicly in detail. Ministers have not explained how the government concluded that the IRGC directed the plan, as opposed to inspiring or paying for it. Nor have they said what the detainees' alleged role was.

That gap matters. Prospect magazine has questioned whether the evidence in the public domain shows Iranian direction. Most other outlets reviewed did not raise this scepticism. The government can answer it by publishing more, or by pointing to intelligence it will not release. So far it has taken the second course.

Tehran's reply and allied backing

Iran denies involvement. Mousavi said Tehran had no part in the matter and was willing to cooperate. He also called on London to provide documents supporting its claims, according to the BBC. Iranian state media made the same denial.

London has some diplomatic cover. A joint statement from 29 countries, including the UK and the US, accepted Britain's findings. It said threats against people in signatory states were unacceptable, and that the signatories would act to "uncover, investigate, thwart and prosecute" hostile acts. It also said the incident showed the IRGC's willingness to carry out attacks outside Iran. Such statements are partly political. Governments often endorse an ally's attribution without independently verifying the evidence behind it.

Why Fairford matters

Fairford is a British-owned base that the US Air Force uses, and it has long been a flashpoint over the extent of British involvement in American operations. A threat against it sits where two questions meet. One is how safe the UK is from hostile-state activity on its own soil. The other is how exposed Britain is when it hosts allied forces during a standoff with Iran. Attributing the threat to the IRGC puts the first question at the centre of the story and keeps the second in view.

The government has moved quickly to show it takes the threat seriously. Summoning an ambassador and imposing sanctions are measured steps, well short of any military or economic escalation. They also leave room to de-escalate if the evidence weakens.

What to watch

Three things will show how far this goes. The first is whether police bring charges against any of the bailed men, since a prosecution would force evidence into open court. The second is the further sanctions Burnham has promised, and whether they reach IRGC-linked entities rather than individuals. The third is whether ministers publish more of the case, or whether Parliament's security committees press for it. Until then, the attribution rests on the government's word and its allies' endorsement.
