Islanders blame data centres for water shortages

In one sentence
Residents of Indonesia's Batam island blame new data centres for dry taps, but officials cite drought and no one has published the water-use figures that would settle it.

Residents of Batam, an Indonesian island that Jakarta wants to turn into a digital hub, say the data centres rising around them are taking the water that should reach their taps. Officials say the cause is a long dry spell. Nobody has published figures that would settle the question, according to a BBC report on the dispute.

About 300 people recently entered a compound at Nongsa Digital Park carrying empty plastic bottles and jerry cans. It was the latest protest, following others in August and September. Dewi Susanti, a resident of Bakau Serip, told the BBC: "Not a single drop has come through our taps for the last three months." Her household now relies on tanker trucks, whose water she described as dirty, cloudy and smelly. Her husband waits up overnight for the supply before leaving for work, she said, and money meant for rice now goes on water drums.

A island with no groundwater to spare

Batam has about 1.3 million people and no natural freshwater source of significance. It depends on rain captured in six artificial reservoirs, so a weak rainy season hits supply quickly. Simulations by the environmental group Akar Bhumi Indonesia suggest the Nongsa reservoir could run dry within a month without rain.

That is the backdrop to a rapid build-out. Authorities have approved 10 data centres, six of them in Nongsa Digital Park. Two are operating, one is under construction and at least six more are under review. Official figures put investment on the island at more than $6.7bn (£5.1bn) as of mid-2026. In April, Singapore's DayOne signed a deal with an Indonesian state utility for a data centre of about 450MW, and in June DayOne, Nvidia and Firmus Technologies announced a 360MW "AI factory". Official estimates put Batam's data-centre power demand above 1,100MW by 2030, enough for nearly a million homes.

What is and isn't established

The residents' claim is an attribution, not a finding. Batam's water utility says it is working to improve supply but has not said whether data-centre demand is a factor; it has said it would consider cloud seeding if the shortage continues. BP Batam, the agency overseeing trade and industry, blames a prolonged dry spell and denies that water meant for homes is being diverted to data centres.

The numbers that would test either account are missing. How much water Batam's data centres use is unclear. Agus Hasan, a professor at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology, told the BBC that reliable data on data-centre water use in Indonesia is scarce, partly because companies treat it as proprietary. He argued such facilities should be built "rationally, transparently, and responsibly."

Outside estimates give a sense of scale, not a verdict. US research suggests a typical data centre uses about 300,000 gallons a day, roughly what 1,000 US households consume. Malaysia's central bank has estimated that a 50MW facility could use water equal to 2,200 households a day. Both are far from Batam-specific measurements, and cooling designs vary widely. The BBC contacted DayOne, Sinar Primera and Gaw Capital; it reported no responses.

The authorities have acted as if the concern has some weight. After the August and September protests they told data-centre firms to build their own desalination plants rather than draw on reservoirs. That is an instruction, not evidence that the firms were drawing on them, and it is unclear whether any plant has been built.

A pattern in the region

Batam is not the first place in the region to face the question. Singapore paused data-centre construction in 2019, citing land and energy constraints, and lifted the pause in 2022 with efficiency standards. Across the strait, Johor in Malaysia approved projects worth tens of billions, drew complaints over noise, dust and water shortages, and has since moved to regulate the facilities' water and electricity use.

Whether Batam's shortage is mostly drought, mostly demand, or an old infrastructure problem with a new target, the dispute shows the same gap: communities on water-scarce sites cannot see how much the plants take. The next markers are whether the desalination instruction is enforced, whether the utility or BP Batam publishes consumption data, and whether the reservoirs recover when the rains arrive.
