AI trade faces a financing and concentration test as the IMF, Burry and Eisman warn

In one sentence
ASML and TSMC results this week test whether AI chip demand runs past 2028 or is capped by the financing Broadcom flags, as the IMF, Burry and Eisman warn on concentration.

The AI chip boom has so far been a story about demand. This week it becomes a story about who pays. ASML and TSMC report results within days of each other, and the two companies sit at opposite ends of the same supply chain: one sells the lithography machines, the other turns them into the chips that AI data centres run on. Their numbers will test two competing readings of the cycle. In one, demand for AI hardware runs well beyond 2028. In the other, the limit is the financing available to the customers buying it.

The stakes are visible in the options market. Triage estimates put the potential swing in ASML's market value on its earnings at roughly $42.8bn. That is a projected move, not a realised one, and it shows how much of the valuation rests on a single quarterly read.

Two readings of the cycle

The bullish case comes from the top of ASML. Chief executive Christophe Fouquet has said: "Honestly, I don't think we've reached the end of the AI boom yet." ASML's longer-term target is annual net sales of €60bn by 2030, up from €28.3bn in 2023. In July it raised its 2026 guidance to between €43bn and €45bn. It also plans to lift low-NA EUV capacity by 30% a year in 2027 and is assessing a further 30% increase after that. A company does not commit to that kind of capacity unless it expects orders to run for years. The "beyond 2028" framing attributed to Fouquet in the Benzinga report could not be matched to a specific quote, so the evidence for it is the capacity and sales plans rather than a verified remark.

The cautious case comes from further down the chain. Broadcom has been working through how to fund the custom chips it builds for AI developers. The Information reported that OpenAI could not close about $18bn of financing tied to its Broadcom chip partnership, and that Broadcom was willing to finance the initial production only if Microsoft took 40% of the processors or OpenAI found other buyers. Broadcom has also turned to private credit: a $35bn financing for its AI platform, led by Apollo and Blackstone, was reported in the spring. Broadcom's exposure reportedly comes from backstopping some customer lease obligations. A Bank of America estimate puts the possible scale of such a vehicle at $370bn of senior debt by mid-2029. That figure is a projection, not a Broadcom liability.

The two views are not contradictory in the short run. ASML's order book is filled by chipmakers, which in turn depend on customers who can pay for finished systems. If the financing chain tightens, the first sign would be in the pacing of those orders, not in this quarter's revenue.

The warnings around it

Outside the companies, the cautionary voices are growing louder. Reports credit the IMF with flagging concentrated AI-linked equity valuations as a risk to global financial stability, set against the weight a handful of AI-linked large caps carry in the S&P 500. The original report behind that framing could not be confirmed. The Fund's own recent language is more measured: chief economist Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas has said an AI boom could end in a dot-com-style bust but is less likely to become a systemic crisis, because the spending is funded mainly by cash-rich technology companies rather than debt. He allowed that a correction could still shift sentiment and risk appetite and put pressure on non-bank financial institutions. The Broadcom financing reports are precisely the kind of debt-funded exposure that sits uneasily with the cash-funded premise.

The investors known for calling the 2008 mortgage collapse are also taking positions. Michael Burry's disclosed bets, according to the Wall Street Journal and others, include a short on the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX), alongside Nvidia, Tesla and Applied Materials, and put options on the Nasdaq 100. His reported argument is that too many businesses are being priced as AI winners at once. Benzinga also quotes Steve Eisman describing the S&P 500 as "one giant AI bet"; that wording was not independently verified. Both men were central to The Big Short, but their record on that trade says little about timing here. Burry has closed earlier index bearish bets at a loss, and SOXX is up about 73% in 2026 despite a recent pullback of 18.4% over a month.

What the numbers need to show

The prints can separate the two readings in fairly specific ways. At ASML, the questions are bookings and the guidance for 2027, not just current-quarter sales. Strong orders with firm capacity plans would support Fouquet. Hesitation in bookings, or customers pushing out delivery slots, would point to the financing constraint. At TSMC, capital spending guidance and commentary on AI accelerator demand are the cleaner signal, since its customers include the same developers that are trying to raise money.

A beat on both would leave the financing question open rather than answered, because equipment and foundry orders lag the decisions of the buyers who fund them. A miss, or a cautious tone on 2027, would suggest the financing strain is already reaching the supply chain. The next test after this week is the next round of AI debt deals: whether OpenAI's chip financing closes, and on what terms.
