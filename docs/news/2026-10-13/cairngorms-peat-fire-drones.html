Drones hunt fires smouldering beneath the Cairngorms

In one sentence
Thermal drones are being used to find peat fires smouldering unseen in the Scottish uplands, a method earlier trials suggest works but still needs wider field proof.

Peat fires are among the hardest to fight because the fire can be out of sight. After a moorland blaze has been beaten back at the surface, heat can keep working through the dry peat beneath. It can smoulder for days or weeks and then break out again. In the Cairngorms area of the Scottish uplands, thermal drones are now being used to find those buried fires. Ground crews cannot see them, and a hotspot can sit just a few steps away without showing on the surface.

The account of the Cairngorms work comes from BBC Technology. The details available to The Bot Tribune on the number of aircraft, the operators and the area surveyed are limited. This piece therefore leans on the wider evidence about how the method works.

Why peat burns differently

Peat is partly decayed plant matter built up over thousands of years. When it is dry enough it will burn without flame, in a slow, oxygen-starved combustion that can travel below the surface and through root layers. Smoke may thin and the visible fire may die, but the ground stays hot. Crews who walk over such a site risk stepping into a burning pocket. They also risk declaring the fire out too soon.

The stakes are not only local. Upland peat stores a great deal of carbon, and burning it returns that carbon to the air. Degraded peatland is already a source of emissions rather than a store, so a fire that is allowed to smoulder adds to the problem. Finding it quickly is a land-management question and a climate one.

What the drones add

A thermal camera records heat rather than light. Flown over a burn scar, it shows warm patches that a person on foot would not notice. A crew can then be sent to the exact spot to dig out the peat and soak it.

The approach is not unique to Scotland. In the Peak District, United Utilities has used heat-seeking drones to find lingering hotspots after the Dovestones and Tintwistle moorland fires. Crews are given GPS coordinates for each hotspot. The company has said these can reignite even after rain. Academic work points the same way. A 2019 pilot study from Liverpool John Moores University, published in the journal Drones and based on work on Indonesian peatland, found that drone-mounted thermal cameras could detect subterranean peat fires. It also found that a simple temperature threshold could flag likely hotspots automatically. The authors called for further field testing. Rescuers in Ukraine have also reported using thermal drones over peat bogs, where they identified underground fires with surface temperatures of 55 to 200 degrees across about 70 hectares.

Limits and what comes next

Drones are not a complete answer. Thermal readings can be confused by sun-warmed rock, by wet ground and by the time of day. Flying in upland weather means wind, cloud and the rules on operating beyond line of sight. A survey also shows where heat is, not how deep the fire goes, so crews still have to dig.

The larger question is whether thermal surveys become routine after upland fires, rather than a trial used by a few operators. That depends on evidence from seasons like this one. The tests are how reliably the drones find fires that crews would otherwise miss, how many reignitions follow a drone-cleared site, and whether the cost and the training fit into fire-and-rescue and landowner budgets. Results from the Scottish deployments, set against the earlier English and academic findings, will show whether the method is ready to be standard practice.
