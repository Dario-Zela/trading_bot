BBC finds documents falsified to evade tax on Abramovich's wealth

In one sentence
A BBC Panorama investigation alleges Deloitte helped backdate documents to hide Abramovich's wealth and avoid tax; he denies wrongdoing and the claims are untested.

A BBC Panorama investigation, published on 12 October with the Bureau of Investigative Journalism, alleges that the accountancy firm Deloitte helped falsify company records so that Roman Abramovich's fortune could be hidden and tax avoided. The claims rest on a leak of thousands of documents. Abramovich denies any wrongdoing, and Deloitte Cyprus says it acts in compliance with the law. The allegations are the broadcaster's reading of old paperwork. No court or tax authority has tested them.

They matter beyond tax. The UK sanctioned Abramovich in March 2022 as an associate of Vladimir Putin. Sanctions enforcement depends on being able to establish who owns what. If the paperwork behind a fortune was rewritten to say something untrue, then the paperwork an investigator is handed is a weak guide to what a sanctioned person owns.

What the documents are said to show

The material comes from files on Abramovich's finances and on offshore companies administered from Cyprus between 1995 and 2022. It has been shared with partners including the Guardian, OCCRP and Paper Trail Media, as part of the Cyprus Confidential project run by the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists. Deloitte owned a Limassol corporate services firm, MeritServus, until 2005. Panorama says the two continued to work closely afterwards.

The programme sets out three cases.

Amiscan Investments. Draft 2003 accounts prepared by Deloitte Cyprus listed $672m (£508m) of assets. They included a West Sussex mansion, a London flat, a south of France estate, helicopters, private jets and boats, among them the $147.5m yacht Pelorus. A 2006 Cypriot transparency law meant such accounts would have to be made public. According to Panorama, the assets were instead shown as belonging to a parent company in the British Virgin Islands, with the transfer backdated three years to 2003. Deloitte Cyprus reviewed the backdated papers and called them "adequate" for taking the assets off the books. When the 2003 accounts were signed in 2006, about $75,000 of assets remained.

Kravin Investments. Kravin held almost $2bn in the bank in 2003 and earned substantial interest, which should have been taxed in Cyprus. An August 2005 MeritServus email to Abramovich's Russian associates described a plan devised after a meeting with Deloitte. It said the 2003 and 2004 financial statements would have to change. A Russian associate replied "I agree". Backdated documents then suggested Kravin had held the money only for its BVI parent, so no Cypriot tax would be due. A September 2005 Deloitte email, Panorama says, set out the changes to bank balances, interest income and profit and loss that this would require.

Electus Investments. Electus Cyprus held more than $10bn. MeritServus proposed moving beneficial ownership to a same-named BVI company, backdated to 20 November 2003. Emails from 2006 acknowledge that the Cypriot company owned the fortune, yet signed papers said otherwise. The Cypriot accounts showed assets of about $145,000.

Denials, and what remains unproven

Abramovich's lawyers say he did not evade tax or conceal his wealth. They say Panorama has no evidence that he knew of, authorised or directed any falsification, that he was "not involved in the day-to-day operation" of the companies, and that relying on professional advice was "entirely proper and legitimate". They also described a Jersey investigation into his assets as "undertaken for political purposes". Deloitte Cyprus said it could not comment on specific allegations because the documents are more than 20 years old and no longer accessible to it.

That gap in the chain is the one to watch. The emails reported by Panorama are addressed to his associates and advisers. Whether they establish that he knew is a separate question from whether the records were altered.

Outside voices were harder on the scheme. Andrew Mitchell KC called it "straightforward" tax evasion and said that, had it happened in England and involved millions, HMRC would probably consider prosecution. Lord Sikka, of the University of Sheffield, said misstating transaction dates and failing to publish true and fair accounts breached a statutory duty. Christos Christofides, a Cypriot MP, said that if tax was evaded, Cyprus must recover it and hold those responsible to account. The conduct described is from 2003 to 2006 and falls mainly under Cypriot jurisdiction, and Panorama reports no statement or action from HMRC.

The sanctions question

The UK sanctioned Abramovich in 2022 and he was forced to sell Chelsea FC. He has reportedly lived in Turkey, which did not apply sanctions. In Jersey, £5.4bn of his assets remain frozen under a criminal investigation that has run for four years. He denies the allegations made against him there. Panorama reported in 2022 that he made his fortune through a corrupt 1990s deal over a Russian state oil company, which he has said is false.

Sanctions regimes work from records: company registries, accounts, trust deeds. The case Panorama describes is one in which the records were allegedly made to understate what existed. Vladimir Milov, a former Russian deputy energy minister, argues that Western legal, financial and PR firms have helped oligarchs whitewash their reputations. That is a political claim and not a finding. But if the Panorama account is borne out, enforcers cannot treat a corporate document trail as evidence of ownership without checking it against independent sources.

The next tests are practical. One is whether HMRC, Cypriot authorities or the Jersey investigators say they will examine the leaked material. Another is whether Deloitte faces regulatory inquiry over work it can no longer produce records for. A third is whether any of the documents are challenged as incomplete or inaccurate. Abramovich's lawyers have already begun to do so, on the question of his own knowledge.
