Healey faces a £35bn Budget gap as growth upgrades and council debts add to the squeeze

In one sentence
A think tank puts the Chancellor's unfunded commitments at about £35bn against thin headroom, so the 28 October Budget must choose between tax rises and more gilt supply.

Chancellor John Healey goes into his first Budget with a funding problem that, on one think tank's reckoning, comes to about £35bn. That is the sum he would need to find to honour spending commitments already made. BBC reporting attributes the figure to a think tank. The triage notes do not name the think tank or break the total down by department, and the Treasury's reply is not yet on record. The number is therefore a single-source estimate and should be read that way.

The arithmetic of closing a gap that size is limited. The Chancellor can raise taxes, cut spending elsewhere or borrow more. Most of the pressure lands on the first and last of those, and the last of them is a gilt-market question.

The size of the gap

The £35bn sits inside a range of estimates now in circulation. Capital Economics has put the cost of pledges on defence, social care and council housebuilding at £46bn to £63bn by 2030, or 1.5% to 2% of GDP. A figure of £35bn is lower than that, though the two may not measure the same thing: the think tank's baseline and time horizon have not been published in the material available.

The cushion against any such bill is small. The Office for Budget Responsibility's headroom against the fiscal rules is reportedly between £10bn and £15bn, down from £23.6bn earlier this year. The Resolution Foundation has said recent pledges could squeeze it to as little as £8bn. Healey has committed to the fiscal rules, and the Budget is due on 28 October.

On those numbers the headroom covers perhaps a third of a £35bn shortfall at best. Whatever is left must come from somewhere.

Taxes or gilts

The choice between the two routes matters differently to households and to investors.

Tax rises close a gap at no cost to gilt supply. They also reduce household income and are politically costly. Extra borrowing avoids both, but it adds to the amount of debt the Debt Management Office must sell. Larger remit numbers tend to weigh on prices at the longer end of the curve, where demand from traditional buyers such as defined-benefit pension schemes has been thinner than it used to be.

The gilt market is also sensitive to credibility as well as volume. A Budget that fills the gap with optimistic assumptions invites a higher risk premium; one that fills it visibly, with taxes that survive scrutiny, tends to do the opposite. Investors will be reading the OBR's forecast as closely as the Chancellor's speech. The headroom figure that comes out of it will show how much of the £35bn has been absorbed by rules and how much by new money.

A growth upgrade with a catch

The forecast backdrop is mixed. BBC UK reports that the latest forecast upgrades near-term UK growth but warns that higher energy costs and interest rates will weigh on 2027. The triage notes give no figures, forecaster or release date, so the size of the upgrade cannot be stated here.

The structure of the warning still matters. An upgrade that fades in 2027 raises receipts now and lets the Budget look easier than it is. If the downgrade follows, the headroom the Chancellor reports in October could be reversed by the spring. Tax revenues forecast against a weaker 2027 would also leave less room for borrowing to stand in for tax.

The higher-rate warning bears directly on debt costs. Gilt issuance priced at higher yields adds to the interest bill, which is itself a drain on headroom.

Councils' SEND debts

A further call on the Treasury comes from local government. English councils are warning that debts on special educational needs and disabilities provision will reach £10bn by 2028, in a Guardian Politics report that describes the deficits as 'catastrophic'. The headline number could not be matched in other reporting. The County Councils Network projects £13.4bn by March 2028, and the OBR has forecast up to £14bn by 2027-28. Different studies appear to measure different groups of councils or different dates.

The mechanism is the same in each. The deficits are currently kept off councils' main balance sheets under a statutory override due to end in March 2028. The Local Government Association's survey found 79% of responding councils cannot set a balanced general fund budget in 2028-29, and the OBR has estimated that moving the liability onto central books could cost £6.3bn in 2028-29 with no offsetting savings identified. The OBR has also said the change would very likely lead many authorities to issue section 114 notices, which are formal declarations of effective insolvency.

That cost falls outside the £35bn as described, but not outside the Chancellor's problem. A decision on the override is a spending decision, and it competes for the same headroom.

What to watch

Three things settle the question. The first is the think tank's own paper, which should say what the £35bn includes and over what period. The second is the Treasury's response. The third is the Budget on 28 October, together with the OBR's forecast: the split between tax and borrowing will show up in the Debt Management Office's revised gilt remit that follows. If the remit rises materially, the gap will have been closed in the gilt market; if the Budget leans on taxes, the adjustment falls on households and the bond market gets its answer more quietly.
