UK student 'super app' runs on servers owned by a US-blacklisted Chinese firm

In one sentence
ZyMix, a beta messaging and payments app aimed at UK students, uses a Tencent European arm, and Tencent is on a US Pentagon military-company list, the Guardian reports.

A messaging and payments app pitched as the UK's first "super app" for Gen Z has a contract with a European arm of Tencent, the Chinese technology group the US Defense Department has placed on its list of Chinese military companies. The Guardian reported the arrangement on Tuesday. The app is called ZyMix, and it is aimed at university students.

The detail matters because of what the app does. ZyMix bundles chat, payments and community features, so it handles the kind of data (who a student talks to, what they say, how they spend) that people tend not to think about until it leaks. The app launched last week and is still in beta. It is published by a UK company, Flicknmix, which calls itself "British born". No user numbers have been reported.

This account rests on a reprint of the Guardian's story. The original could not be checked directly, and no regulator or university response has been found.

What the privacy policy said

The app's original privacy policy named "Tencent (Frankfurt)" as a place where user content and messages were stored, according to the Guardian. After the paper made inquiries, the policy was rewritten and the reference removed.

The company's account is that its agreement is with Tencent Cloud Europe BV, which it uses for auxiliary services. It describes the Tencent and Twilio services as used solely for performance testing. The revised policy says personal data is held mainly in the UK and EU, with some providers elsewhere, including the US. It adds that EU-hosted auxiliary providers receive only encrypted messages and limited technical data.

ZyMix says the beta complies with UK data, privacy and financial rules. "As a newly launched startup," it said, it is updating its privacy notice as it develops its services.

The gap between the two versions is the issue. The first policy said messages were stored with Tencent. The second says only encrypted messages and limited technical data reach EU providers. Which description is accurate is not established in the reporting.

The blacklist, and its limits

Tencent was added in January 2026 to the Pentagon's Section 1260H list, which names Chinese military companies. The listing describes Tencent as indirectly affiliated with the People's Liberation Army. Tencent calls the listing a mistake. It says it is not a military company, that the listing does not affect its business, and that it will work with the Defense Department to resolve it.

The list has narrow legal effects. The Defense Department is barred from contracting with listed entities, but wider sanctions do not follow automatically. The listing does not make a UK company's use of Tencent's cloud unlawful. Whether the arrangement is wise is a separate question, and it is the one the critics are raising.

The questions it raises

Fraser Sampson, the former biometrics and surveillance camera commissioner, called the "commercial confidentiality" argument suspicious. He said that changing a policy on the fly is never reassuring. The reference is to a security consultant, Tarique Ghaffur, who declined to name ZyMix's suppliers on those grounds. Sam Dunning of the UK-China Transparency NGO questioned whether the company took due care before launch.

There are other details. ZyMix has advertised Mandarin-speaking roles in the UK. A WeChat recruitment post says the product, R&D, design and testing team is based in Greater China. The company is also offering £200 to £400 a month to campus brand ambassadors.

None of this shows that student data has been accessed by anyone. The reporting establishes a supplier relationship, a policy that changed after press questions, and a development team based in China. It does not show misuse.

What to watch

The next markers are practical. One is whether the Information Commissioner's Office opens an inquiry, which has not been reported. Another is whether universities, whose campuses ZyMix is targeting, say anything about the brand-ambassador scheme. The third is whether the company publishes a fuller supplier list and data-flow description when its notice is finalised. Separately, Tencent's attempt to get off the Pentagon list would remove one part of the controversy, but not the question of where students' messages are kept.
