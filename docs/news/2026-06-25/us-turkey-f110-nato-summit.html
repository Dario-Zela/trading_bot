White House clears F-110 engines for Turkey days before NATO summit, signalling alliance reset

Turkey's TAI KAAN fighter jet during ground tests at its Ankara facilities, March 2023 — the aircraft the F-110 engines are built to power. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The White House approved 80 GE F-110 engines ($700M+) for Turkey's KAAN fighter days before NATO's Ankara summit, overriding Congress to signal an alliance reset.

The announcement was timed with obvious purpose. Days before NATO leaders convene in Ankara for their annual summit — the first to be hosted on Turkish soil in more than two decades — the White House has cleared the sale of 80 General Electric F-110 turbofan engines to Turkey, lifting a transfer that Congressional opposition had held up for months. The move marks the most substantive normalisation of the US–Turkey defence relationship since Ankara's 2019 acquisition of Russian air-defence equipment set the two allies on a collision course.

The package is worth more than $700 million. The engines — the F110-GE-129 variant — will power Turkey's first indigenously developed combat aircraft, the KAAN, a twin-engine stealth fighter that completed its maiden flight in February 2024 after years of development by Turkish Aerospace Industries (TAI).

A decade of friction

The F-110 question is inseparable from a longer story of NATO's most fractious bilateral relationship. In July 2019, Turkey took delivery of Russia's S-400 Triumf air-defence system, and Washington responded by ejecting Ankara from the F-35 Joint Strike Fighter programme — a partnership in which Turkish companies had been Tier 3 contributors, supplying components worth hundreds of millions of dollars annually. The security concern was direct: an S-400 radar operating near an F-35 could gather signature data that Russian engineers would exploit to improve the missile's kill probability against the aircraft elsewhere.

In December 2020, CAATSA sanctions landed on Turkey's Presidency of Defence Industries (SSB) — a blanket ban on US export licences that compounded Ankara's isolation from American military hardware and gave Turkey's defence establishment an urgent reason to build indigenous alternatives.

KAAN was the answer, at least in part. The programme, which TAI launched in 2016, was originally conceived as Turkey's fifth-generation platform for the 2030s. The S-400 crisis stripped away the comfortable assumption that American engines would be freely available, and Congress's informal review process — through which lawmakers can flag objections to proposed foreign arms sales — became the chokepoint. Ten F-110 engines slipped through under an earlier, smaller approval by late 2025, enough to power a prototype fleet. The batch of 80 now approved represents the volume required for a credible production programme. Turkey has spoken of eventually acquiring up to 250 KAANs; without the F-110 as a bridge, that schedule would collapse. A domestic engine, the Tusas TF35000, remains years away — Block 30 and 40 aircraft are not expected to use it until around 2032.

The calculus before Ankara

The decision to move now — overriding a pointed, if non-binding, objection from Representative Gregory Meeks, the ranking Democrat on the House Foreign Affairs Committee — reflects a deliberate White House calculation: warm NATO's most complicated eastern flank before the cameras roll in Ankara on 7–8 July.

Meeks had raised objections during the informal Congressional review, citing Turkey's continued possession of the S-400, unresolved tensions with Greece, and Turkey's role in Syria. His objections, like all Congressional holds on foreign arms sales, carry no legal force when an administration chooses to proceed. The State Department is expected to issue a formal notification to Congress in the coming days.

The ground for the decision was laid well before the summit. Ambassador Tom Barrack — a longtime Trump ally confirmed to the Ankara post — said in December that the Trump-Erdogan relationship had produced "the most fruitful conversations we have had on this topic in nearly a decade." Vice President JD Vance has separately indicated that a review of the longstanding F-35 ban was under way. Trump himself said, with characteristic looseness, that he was "probably going to do something that will make them very happy."

The engine clearance is the first concrete deliverable from that groundwork. Erdogan, for his part, has described the Ankara summit as one of the most significant international events in Turkey's recent history — a claim that becomes more plausible with each US concession made in the fortnight before it opens.

What the engines don't settle

For all its diplomatic weight, the F-110 deal leaves the harder questions intact. Gönül Tol, director of the Turkey programme at the Middle East Institute in Washington, put it plainly: "Acquiring the engines is certainly important for Turkey, but it is also the lowest-hanging fruit for a US administration that has made far more ambitious promises to Ankara, including Turkey's return to the F-35 programme." She called F-35 reinstatement "the real test" of whether the bilateral reset is substantive or merely atmospheric.

That test has no clear timeline. US law bars F-35 transfers to any country operating Russian S-400 systems, and Turkey's S-400s remain operational. Reversing the exclusion would require either Congressional action to amend or waive the restriction, or Turkey dismantling its S-400 inventory — a step Ankara has discussed in principle but declined to take.

Critics on the right are also uneasy. The Foundation for Defence of Democracies argued this week that NATO should press Turkey to choose between genuine alliance solidarity and its simultaneous pursuit of BRICS membership bids and a proposed Sunni security framework involving Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, Egypt and Qatar — moves that, in their view, amount to hedging against the alliance rather than deepening it. A transactional approach that rewards Turkey with hardware in exchange for summit hospitality, the argument runs, papers over structural divergences rather than resolving them.

The engine sale secures the table setting. What happens in Ankara in two weeks — and whether Trump's bilateral with Erdogan produces a concrete path on the S-400 disposal question that has blocked a genuine defence reset for nearly seven years — will determine whether the reset has any durable architecture beneath it. The next data point arrives on 7 July.
