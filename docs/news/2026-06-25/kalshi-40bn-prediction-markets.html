Prediction markets go institutional: Kalshi in talks to raise at $40 billion valuation

Tarek Mansour, co-founder and chief executive of Kalshi, photographed at Web Summit 2021. He confirmed this week the company is considering an IPO, not before 2027. — Web Summit / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Kalshi is in talks to raise at ~$40 billion — nearly double its May 2026 figure — as CFTC approval turns prediction markets into a regulated institutional asset class.

A prediction market exchange that barely existed as a regulated entity four years ago is in talks to raise capital at a valuation of roughly $40 billion — a figure that would place it among the most valuable private fintech companies in the United States. The Financial Times reported Wednesday that Kalshi is in active fundraising discussions at approximately that level, nearly doubling the $22 billion valuation the company reached just six weeks prior when it closed a $1 billion Series F round led by Coatue Management.

A deal, according to people familiar with the matter, could close as early as the third quarter of 2026.

From Courtroom to Regulated Exchange

Kalshi's current position is the product of a years-long legal campaign. The company received its CFTC designation as a regulated designated contract market in November 2020 — making it the first exchange licensed to trade event contracts on U.S. political and economic outcomes. But the license came with constraints, and the platform spent much of the following years in litigation with the regulator over exactly what it could list.

The turning point came in 2024, when a federal court in Washington ruled that the CFTC had exceeded its authority in blocking Kalshi from listing contracts tied to congressional election outcomes. The platform relaunched those markets almost immediately. In January 2026, CFTC Chairman Michael Selig withdrew a Biden-era proposed rule that would have banned political and sports event contracts outright — effectively clearing the regulatory runway the sector needed to attract institutional capital.

The structural shift that followed was rapid. In March, Kalshi was approved for institutional margin trading. It signed a partnership with ARK Invest and secured integrations with CNN and CNBC as real-time probability infrastructure for broadcast coverage of political and economic events. The company now commands more than 90% of U.S. prediction market activity by volume.

The Numbers Behind a $40 Billion Ask

Kalshi's valuation trajectory — $5 billion in October 2025, $22 billion in May 2026, and now $40 billion as a target — reflects metrics that have moved in the same direction. Monthly trading volume crossed $21.1 billion in June, more than double the $9.7 billion combined on Polymarket. Annualized volume has risen from roughly $52 billion a year ago to approximately $178 billion today.

The revenue picture is equally striking: annualized net revenue has tripled from approximately $735 million in December 2025 to more than $2 billion now — up from just $25 million as recently as December 2024. Much of that growth is sports-driven; sports contracts account for roughly 89% of Kalshi's volume. But the institutional signal is harder to dismiss. Institutional trading on the platform grew 800% in the six months ended in early May 2026, suggesting a phase change from retail-dominated participation to professional-market activity with considerably higher average transaction sizes.

A $40 billion valuation on $2 billion in annualized revenue implies a roughly 20x revenue multiple — aggressive for any fintech, and notable given that Kalshi is not yet profitable. The pricing embedded in that figure assumes that event-contract markets are early in a structural expansion into mainstream financial infrastructure, not approaching a ceiling.

For context: rival Polymarket, which operates through a decentralised structure outside U.S. regulatory jurisdiction, was last reported to be seeking funding at $15 billion. The $25 billion gap between the two implied valuations is, in effect, the market's price on a CFTC license. The unregulated platforms that preceded both — Augur, Intrade — either collapsed or were forced to close. Regulatory compliance has become the moat, and investors appear willing to pay for it.

The Wall Street Alignment

The mechanics of Kalshi's eventual IPO preparations reveal something about the platform's current leverage. Chief executive Tarek Mansour confirmed this week that a public listing is under active consideration, though not before 2027 at the earliest. "A company of our financial profile with the rate of growth that we're seeing, that sort of conversation has to happen," Mansour told CNBC.

What is less standard is the condition Kalshi has reportedly attached to that process: investment banks that want the IPO mandate must first integrate their own trading platforms with Kalshi's exchange, giving their institutional clients direct trading access before any advisory paperwork is signed. The Series F investor roster — Coatue, Sequoia Capital, Andreessen Horowitz, IVP, Paradigm, and Morgan Stanley — already suggests that top-tier institutional conviction exists. But requiring banks to deliver volume as a precondition for a mandate is a posture that makes sense only from a position of considerable leverage.

Mansour has described the long-term ambition as to "financialize everything and create a tradeable asset from differing opinions" — a scope that, if taken seriously, positions event contracts not as a curiosity adjacent to derivatives markets but as a new instrument class for pricing any form of uncertainty. That would include the kind of tail-event and macro-uncertainty pricing that algorithmic systems currently model through proxies: implied volatility, credit spreads, political-risk indices.

The remaining variables before that thesis is tested: whether the $40 billion round closes in Q3 as expected and who leads it. More structurally, the CFTC's new regulatory framework for event contracts — currently under White House review — will determine how much of Kalshi's regulatory moat survives broad-based competition from any institution that decides to stand up a rival compliant exchange. That rulemaking, not the fundraise, is the decision that will settle whether $40 billion is a floor or a ceiling.
