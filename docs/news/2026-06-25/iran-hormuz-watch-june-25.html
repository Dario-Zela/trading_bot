Iran-Hormuz: mines intact, 72 tankers transiting, oil at $70 as diplomacy advances

NASA MODIS satellite image of the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile-wide chokepoint through which roughly 20–30% of the world's seaborne oil passes. — NASA / Terra satellite (MODIS) via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Iranian mines remain in the Strait of Hormuz but 72 tankers crossed in 24 hours; oil fell to $70 as diplomacy advances and gasoline stocks surprised to the upside.

Iranian mines laid during the recent conflict remain in the Strait of Hormuz, US Energy Secretary Chris Wright confirmed this week — yet the waterway is moving oil again. Wright posted on Wednesday that 72 ships carrying roughly 20 million barrels had transited the strait in the preceding 24 hours, a figure he described as a full restoration of pre-conflict flows. The claim carried the optimism of a mission-accomplished moment. The mines tell a more complicated story.

Wright himself acknowledged that complete normalisation requires demining — a process he estimated at "a few weeks of effort" — while stressing that a full cleared channel is not strictly necessary for traffic to proceed. US naval escorts through Oman's territorial waters in the southern passage have effectively neutralised Iran's ability to interdict commercial ships, he said, adding bluntly that "Iran will not have the ability to close the Strait of Hormuz going forward." The assertion is backed by force rather than treaty. Iran's mines remain where they were laid; the US military presence is what makes them, for now, irrelevant to commerce.

The 72-ship figure is Wright's own count and sits well above estimates from maritime analytics firm Kpler, which tallied around 20 transits the same period, while CENTCOM reported 55 merchant ships on a recent day. Pre-war traffic ran to 50–100 daily crossings. That gap between official optimism and independent tracking is worth noting: the strait is moving, but arguments about what "normal" means are doing some heavy lifting.

Oil takes the stairs down

Markets drew their own conclusions. West Texas Intermediate futures briefly dipped below $70 per barrel on Wednesday — touching a session low of $69.63, the first sub-$70 print since early March — before settling at $70.34. Brent crude fell 4.3% to $73.74, its lowest level since before the US-Israeli strikes on Iran began in late February. Both benchmarks have now nearly unwound the entire conflict premium.

Two forces combined to push prices lower. First, diplomatic progress: Trump publicly stated that Iran is making "very big concessions" in ongoing nuclear talks, raising expectations that Iranian crude will return to global markets in volume. A 14-point memorandum of understanding has been signed, though nuclear inspection arrangements remain contested — the IAEA's chief said timing of site visits is "not essential," a formulation that satisfied neither hardliners in Washington nor Tehran. Second, a demand signal cut against the seasonal script: the EIA's weekly report showed US gasoline stockpiles rising by 2.1 million barrels against a forecast draw. That miss — in peak summer driving season — suggested the demand side of the ledger is softer than the calendar might imply.

The two headwinds compounded each other. Supply fears ease; demand disappoints. The result is crude that has surrendered most of what geopolitics gave it.

For markets, the open question is not whether the strait is functioning but whether it stays that way without a formal agreement. Mines do not deactivate themselves, escorts are a US policy choice, and the nuclear talks still have months to run. The next concrete marker: Iran's willingness to accept IAEA inspectors, which diplomats expect to become the central sticking point when talks reconvene.
