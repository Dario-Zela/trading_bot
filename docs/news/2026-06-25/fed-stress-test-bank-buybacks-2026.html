All major US banks clear the Fed stress test; buyback and dividend announcements expected imminently

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building, Washington D.C., headquarters of the Federal Reserve System — Wikimedia Commons / AgnosticPreachersKid / CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
All 32 US banks cleared the Fed's 2026 stress test after absorbing $708B in modelled losses, triggering a wave of buybacks and dividend hikes led by JPMorgan's $50B repurchase program.

The Federal Reserve released its 2026 annual stress test results on June 24, finding that all 32 large US banks held sufficient capital to absorb losses under a severe hypothetical recession. The clearance removes the last regulatory hurdle before banks can execute dividend increases and share repurchase programs — and within hours of the announcement, the largest institutions had already lined up at the window.

$708 Billion in Hypothetical Losses, and Still Standing

The Fed's 2026 severely adverse scenario was not a gentle probe. It assumed unemployment cresting at 10 percent, residential home prices falling 30 percent and commercial real estate values collapsing 39 percent — a composite shock designed to stress balance sheets across every major exposure type. Under those conditions, the 32 banks collectively absorbed more than $708 billion in projected losses: roughly $200 billion from credit cards alone, $160 billion from commercial and industrial loans, and $75 billion from commercial real estate.

The system held. Aggregate common equity tier 1 capital ratios declined by just 1.6 percentage points, ending the scenario well above the regulatory minimums. Every institution individually cleared its floor.

"Today's results underscore the strength of the banking system," said Vice Chair Michelle W. Bowman, who is simultaneously overseeing a broader restructuring of the Fed's supervisory posture. "As we work to increase the transparency and accountability of the stress test, public feedback will help us continue to improve and instill greater confidence in the stress test and its results."

The result also carried a structural footnote: in February, the Fed voted to freeze stress capital buffer requirements at their current levels until 2027, while it incorporates public feedback into its loss-estimating models. That means banks face no immediate increase in the additional capital cushion they must hold above baseline minimums — a meaningful ceiling removed before any capital-return calculation.

The Payout Wave Arrives

The cascade of announcements that followed the 4 p.m. release illustrated how tightly compressed demand for capital return had become.

JPMorgan Chase moved first and largest. The bank unveiled a $50 billion common share repurchase authorization — effective July 1 — alongside a 10 percent increase in its quarterly dividend, from $1.50 to $1.65 per share, pending board approval in the third quarter. Goldman Sachs raised its quarterly dividend 11 percent to $5.00 per share, a figure that represents a 25 percent increase relative to the prior year's level. Morgan Stanley took its dividend up 15 percent to $1.15 per share and reauthorized a $20 billion buyback program without a fixed expiration date.

Wells Fargo matched Goldman's 11 percent dividend lift, moving to $0.50 per share quarterly, while BNY Mellon led the group on percentage terms with a 19 percent increase — from $0.53 to $0.63 per share — starting in the third quarter.

Bank of America and Citigroup were more measured. BofA raised its quarterly dividend to 24 cents from 22 cents; Citi moved to 53 cents from 51 cents. Both banks flagged discrepancies between their internal models and the Fed's projections — BofA's own loss estimates ran substantially higher than the regulator's, driven partly by disagreement over how the near-zero rate environment in the scenario would affect its large bond portfolio. Citi, by contrast, projected better capital outcomes than the Fed did. Both institutions cleared the test regardless, and neither cited the model gap as a constraint on payouts.

A Regulatory Tailwind With a 2027 Horizon

For equity markets, the significance extends beyond the individual announcements. Share buybacks mechanically reduce float and support earnings-per-share figures — they are, at scale, a source of sustained demand for the underlying stock. JPMorgan's $50 billion authorization alone, deployed over multiple quarters, represents consistent buying pressure in one of the index's highest-weighted names.

The stress capital buffer freeze adds a further layer of clarity. Banks entering the capital-return season know that the cost of holding excess capital will not rise unexpectedly before 2027. That certainty encourages larger and longer-dated programs rather than cautious, one-quarter increments — which is why the Morgan Stanley reauthorization carries no expiration date at all.

The macro backdrop is not uniformly favorable. Barclays cut its S&P 500 target this week with an explicit recession warning, and questions about credit quality in commercial real estate — the sector that took the sharpest hypothetical hit in the stress scenario — have not disappeared. The Fed's own model assumed a 39 percent CRE price decline for a reason.

What the stress test establishes is a floor, not a ceiling. It confirms that, under the regulator's methodology, the banking system can absorb a severe shock without breaking. It does not predict that no shock is coming. The buyback announcements now in train will be the market's near-term signal for how much conviction bank management teams actually have — whether the programs are loaded front-end or paced conservatively, and whether they are supplemented by debt repayment or run parallel to it.

The specific numbers to watch arrive in July, when boards formally approve the dividend changes and execution timelines for repurchases become visible in open-market activity reports.
