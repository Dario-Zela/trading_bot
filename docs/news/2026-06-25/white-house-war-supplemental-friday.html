White House war supplemental due Friday, testing GOP unity on Ukraine, Middle East and ethanol

The US Capitol, where Speaker Johnson controls the supplemental's path to the floor and a fracturing Republican conference awaits its arrival — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
The White House filed an $87.6bn Iran war supplemental Wednesday, bundling E15 ethanol and farm aid into a package that will test a Republican conference already cracking on foreign spending.

The White House did not wait for the end of the week. OMB Director Russell Vought delivered the administration's long-anticipated supplemental spending request to Speaker Mike Johnson on Wednesday — a day ahead of the Friday deadline that Republican congressional leaders had been told to expect — and the figure at the top of his seven-page letter read $87.6 billion.

That number, drawn almost entirely from the costs of Operation Epic Fury, the American air campaign against Iran now entering its fifth month, lands in a Republican conference that has spent the spring publicly fracturing over the costs of American military engagement abroad. The supplemental's early arrival signals that the White House expects a fight — and intends to start it before opposition can fully organise.

The Weight of the Ask

The bulk of the request — roughly $67 billion — is earmarked for the Defense Department: $21 billion for munitions replenishment, $17.3 billion for readiness and operational expenses, $21.1 billion for classified programmes, $5.1 billion for cybersecurity and autonomous systems, and $2.4 billion for drone procurement. That figure is substantially lower than the $200 billion Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth floated in March, a concession to political reality even before the bill reached the Hill.

The remainder covers agricultural assistance ($11.1 billion, including Florida storm recovery), the Ebola response in Central Africa ($1.4 billion), Penn Station renovation in New York ($1 billion), and Washington D.C. construction projects ($500 million). Year-round renewable fuel sales — a reference to E15 ethanol — appear as a policy rider, with hemp regulation revisions tucked alongside.

Vought's cover letter urged Congress to act "as soon as possible." Democratic Senator Chris Murphy of Connecticut immediately declared the package "designed to repel Democratic votes" and "designed not to pass" — a signal that the administration is counting on Republican-only passage. That makes the internal arithmetic of the conference the only arithmetic that matters.

Public sentiment offers little cover to fence-sitters. Polling puts support for the Iran war's cost at 24 percent — a number that concentrates the mind of any member facing a competitive district in November.

A Conference Under Strain

The supplemental arrives in a conference already visibly cracked. The day before Vought's letter landed, four Republican senators — Lisa Murkowski, Susan Collins, Bill Cassidy and Rand Paul — voted with Democrats on a war powers resolution demanding congressional authorisation for continued operations against Iran. Senator Cassidy, in a direct exchange with the president, offered the bluntest public assessment on record: "This was supposed to last four weeks. It's lasted four months."

The House has its own fractures. On June 4, eighteen Republicans broke with leadership to pass a Ukraine aid package 226-195, employing a discharge petition — a parliamentary tool that bypasses the Speaker entirely — to force the vote. The bill authorises $8 billion in loans to Kyiv, imposes new sanctions on Russian oil and banking, and extends a military lend-lease programme. It exposed the distance between Speaker Johnson's alignment with the White House on Ukraine and the preferences of a meaningful slice of his own caucus.

House Foreign Affairs Committee chairman Brian Mast had declared in May that "you will not see a US security supplemental to Ukraine for $6 billion, $60 billion, or any other amount." His members answered three weeks later with a floor vote that said otherwise.

Ukraine does not appear as a line item in the $87.6 billion supplemental — the Iran war's costs dominate it entirely. But that June 4 vote sets the temperature in which the Iran package must be debated. Republicans who crossed the aisle for Kyiv may find it difficult to embrace a supplemental that omits Kyiv while spending lavishly on an air campaign with 24-percent approval. The implicit question for the coming weeks is whether the administration's foreign-spending coalition holds, or whether two distinct sets of Republican dissenters — Ukraine hawks on one side, fiscal sceptics on the other — compound each other.

For European NATO allies, the arithmetic is already familiar. European military spending jumped 14 percent to $864 billion in 2025; Germany exceeded the 2-percent-of-GDP threshold for the first time since the Cold War, reaching 2.3 percent. That rearmament, however, rests on an assumption that Washington's security commitments will hold. The Pentagon's recent notification to Congress that it intended to divert $750 million in NATO-provided Ukraine funds toward US military stockpile replenishment has not gone unnoticed in European capitals. Each congressional crack in the Republican foreign-affairs consensus makes that assumption slightly harder to sustain.

Corn Country's Backdoor

Among the supplemental's less-discussed provisions is language authorising year-round sales of E15 — gasoline blended at 15 percent ethanol rather than the standard 10 percent. Under current rules, E15 sales are restricted during summer months because the higher ethanol content evaporates faster in heat, contributing to ground-level smog. The White House argues that full-year authorisation could save consumers roughly 25 cents per gallon and eliminate more than $20 billion in annual fuel costs at a moment when Iran-related supply disruptions have kept pump prices elevated.

The House passed a standalone E15 bill — H.R. 1346 — in May. The Senate stopped it: the chamber's 60-vote procedural threshold is a reliable graveyard for biofuels legislation, where the split between corn-state senators and refining-industry allies maps neatly onto the bill's opponents and supporters.

By embedding the year-round authorisation in a supplemental spending package, the White House sidesteps both the EPA's standard rulemaking process and the Senate supermajority requirement. It is a clean illustration of a broader playbook: use must-pass emergency vehicles to advance agricultural priorities that cannot survive regular legislative order. Whether it helps or hurts the supplemental's floor prospects depends on which Republicans the E15 rider converts from votes into defections — refiners' allies in Texas and along the Gulf Coast are not natural allies of the corn belt on fuel-blend policy.

What Comes Next

The supplemental now sits with a Republican-controlled Congress that has rarely looked less cohesive on foreign affairs. Speaker Johnson must decide whether to schedule a floor vote before the August recess — knowing that every day of delay allows opposition to organise and midterm anxiety to deepen.

The Senate faces harder arithmetic. With Democrats already signalling opposition, Republicans need near-unanimity — yet four senators just voted against the Iran war on procedural grounds, and the E15 rider will produce its own Republican dissent from refiner-state members. The NATO summit, which just saw the White House clear F-110 engine sales to Turkey as a gesture of alliance repair, adds a diplomatic clock: allies are watching whether the supplemental clears, and what its final dollar amount says about how long Washington intends to sustain its operational tempo across two simultaneous theatres. The administration will get its answer before the August break — or the waiting will become its own answer.
