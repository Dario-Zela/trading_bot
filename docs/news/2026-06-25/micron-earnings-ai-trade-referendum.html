Micron earnings tonight are the AI trade's near-term referendum

Micron Technology's San Jose facility, one of the company's key US R&D and engineering campuses — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Micron's Q3 blowout — $41.5B revenue, $50B Q4 guidance, 2026 HBM sold out — hands the AI infrastructure bulls their clearest data point of the year.

Micron Technology reported its fiscal third-quarter results after the bell on Tuesday, and the numbers landed well clear of even the most bullish projections. Revenue hit $41.5 billion — up 346 percent year-over-year and $6 billion above the Street's consensus. Adjusted earnings of $25.11 per share beat forecasts by more than 22 percent. Gross margins reached 84.9 percent, the highest in the company's history. And then came the guidance: a fourth-quarter revenue target of $50 billion, against analyst expectations of roughly $43 billion. Shares rose 14.6 percent in extended trading, touching $1,199.52.

For a sector that had spent recent weeks questioning whether AI infrastructure spending was about to stall, this was an unambiguous answer — for now.

The memory market that didn't blink

High-bandwidth memory is not a product that features in most consumer electronics conversations. It is, however, the component sitting between AI accelerators and the compute performance that makes large language models viable. Every Nvidia GPU sold into a data centre needs stacked HBM; so does every custom accelerator that hyperscalers are increasingly building in-house. The supply of this memory — painstaking to manufacture, reliant on through-silicon vias and advanced packaging — has been the primary constraint on AI infrastructure deployment for two years running.

Micron's position in that market is not dominant: SK Hynix holds roughly 62 percent of global HBM supply, with Micron at 21 percent and Samsung trailing at 17. But Micron has been gaining fast, and its HBM4 generation — built for Nvidia's forthcoming Vera Rubin platform — is reportedly ramping at twice the pace of the previous HBM3E line.

What Tuesday's results confirmed is that the demand side has not wavered. Micron's entire 2026 HBM allocation is sold out under fixed-price contracts. The company disclosed 16 strategic customer agreements representing roughly $100 billion in minimum contracted revenue. Data centre revenue exceeded $25 billion in the quarter alone — a pace that annualises to more than $100 billion. CEO Sanjay Mehrotra told analysts the company "currently does not have line of sight as to when memory supply will be able to catch up with increasing demand," and that current market tightness is "locked in to persist beyond calendar 2027."

One of the 16 strategic agreements is with Anthropic. Announced three days before the earnings call, the deal spans HBM and DRAM supply, memory architecture co-design, enterprise deployment of Claude across Micron's operations, and a strategic investment in Anthropic's $65 billion Series H round. The deal matters beyond its commercial terms: it means all three global HBM suppliers — Micron, SK Hynix, and Samsung — are now infrastructure partners for one of the leading AI labs. The supply chain is tightening around the demand.

Wall Street's unresolved argument

Before Tuesday's numbers arrived, markets were not aligned on what to expect — or what to make of the AI equity rally's durability. The S&P 500 had slipped 0.10 percent to 7,358 on Tuesday, with the Nasdaq down 0.43 percent to 25,477, as investors pulled back ahead of the print. The caution was not irrational: semiconductor valuations have been running well ahead of earnings for long stretches of the past two years, and any signal of demand softening would ripple quickly across the cohort.

Cathie Wood's ARK Investment Management made its own read explicit. On June 23, the day before results, ARK purchased more than $9.6 million in Amazon shares across three funds, 23,603 Alphabet Class C shares at approximately $346.08 apiece, and 21,226 Tesla shares for roughly $8.6 million. The buying represented a reversal from months of ARK reducing exposure to Magnificent Seven names — a reallocation that ARK now appears to be unwinding as the view hardens that AI deployment will broaden from chipmakers into cloud, search, and autonomous systems.

The counterweight is not absent. Barclays, which raised its year-end S&P 500 target to 7,800 in the same week, accompanied the upgrade with an explicit note that the second half would be uncomfortable — flagging renewed inflation risk, a more restrictive Fed path, and the reality that earnings and AI capex visibility "must do more of the work as Fed support fades and positioning is less able to absorb disappointment." Raising a price target while warning that the cushion is gone is not a contradiction; it is a precise description of a market priced for continued execution with diminishing tolerance for misses.

Micron did not miss.

What the read-through means

The Micron results don't settle the structural-versus-cyclical argument permanently, but they do push the burden of proof firmly onto the pessimists — at least through the next earnings cycle. A $50 billion quarter, if achieved, would represent sequential revenue growth of roughly 21 percent and imply that AI infrastructure capex is not decelerating in any way the memory supply chain can detect.

The clearest near-term signal to watch is Nvidia's next quarterly report, where HBM pull-through demand will either validate or complicate Micron's forward guidance. Also worth tracking: SK Hynix's planned US listing, which would bring Micron's closest HBM rival onto American exchanges and sharpen the competitive read-across for investors who want direct exposure to both sides of the supply equation. The HBM4 allocation cycle — how Nvidia splits next-generation orders between Micron and SK Hynix — is where the market share story will be won or lost heading into 2027.
