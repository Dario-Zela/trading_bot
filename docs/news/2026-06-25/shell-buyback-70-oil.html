Shell shares lag as $70 crude tests the sustainability of its buyback pledge

Shell Centre on the South Bank, London, headquarters of the oil major whose buyback pledge is under renewed scrutiny at $70 crude. — Reading Tom / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Shell shares fell as crude hit $70, testing whether its 17-quarter, $3bn-per-quarter buyback streak survives a falling oil price and a $16.4bn acquisition simultaneously.

Shell's shares fell for a fourth consecutive session Wednesday, lagging the broader equity market by more than two percentage points, as Brent crude sank to around $70 a barrel — roughly a third below its April peak — and analysts weighed in on whether the oil major's capital-return machinery can keep running at that price.

The stock closed around $79.66 on Monday and slid further through the week, having now lost some 9% over ten trading days. The S&P 500 has been roughly flat over the same stretch. Energy funds are taking the brunt of the commodity move: Brent averaged $117 a barrel in April, then $107 in May, and now threatens to settle in the low seventies — a compression of nearly 40% in two months.

The buyback arithmetic

Shell has delivered at least $3 billion in quarterly share repurchases for 17 consecutive quarters, a record it has deployed as a central part of its investment pitch since CEO Wael Sawan took the helm in 2023. The company has also guided investors to expect distributions — dividends plus buybacks — equivalent to 40–50% of cash flow from operations, up from a 30–40% range previously.

Sawan himself has stated publicly that the dividend breakeven sits at $40 a barrel, and the buyback breakeven at $50 a barrel. On that arithmetic, $70 oil leaves a margin. But margins compress when you are simultaneously absorbing a $16.4 billion acquisition: Shell agreed in April to buy Canadian producer ARC Resources in its largest deal since the BG Group takeover a decade ago, issuing shares and cash to ARC shareholders at roughly a 20% premium to the trailing average.

The ARC deal requires at least 66% approval from ARC shareholders at a July 14 vote — and it was precisely that pending shareholder meeting that forced Shell to pause the current $3 billion repurchase tranche effective June 12, under securities law restrictions. So the 17-quarter streak is, technically, already on hold. Whether it resumes cleanly on July 15 depends partly on whether oil has recovered, partly on how the post-ARC balance sheet looks.

Analysts turn more cautious

BNP Paribas moved Shell to Neutral from Outperform, and Morgan Stanley cut from Overweight to Equal Weight in recent weeks, each citing a combination of valuation concerns following the stock's year-to-date rally to a high of $94.90 in early April, medium-term growth questions, and now — with the price having retraced sharply — the added uncertainty of the commodity move.

Shell's Q4 2025 earnings already signalled the pressure: profits came in at $3.26 billion for the quarter, the weakest since Q1 2021, as lower realised prices, higher taxes and elevated operating costs squeezed the result. Full-year operating cash flow fell 22% to $42.9 billion.

The company managed to sustain buybacks through that soft patch — cash delivery, Sawan noted, "remained solid" even in a softer macro. But Q4 was $70-ish oil with a relatively clean balance sheet. The forward question is whether $70 oil plus a $16 billion acquisition plus a paused buyback resume amounts to a different kind of stress.

With a Zacks consensus EPS estimate of $2.51 for the next quarter and a forward P/E around 8.4x, Shell is not expensively valued in absolute terms. The issue is that the buyback programme — not just the dividend — has been the multiple-expansion driver. If investors price in a structurally lower buyback pace, the re-rating runs in reverse.

What to watch

The immediate catalyst is July 14: the ARC shareholder vote and the scheduled end of the buyback suspension. A clean close restores the repurchase programme and lets Shell argue the pause was mechanical, not financial. A delay — or any sign the company needs to redirect cash toward ARC integration costs — would sharpen the sustainability question.

Beyond that, the oil price trajectory matters more than any single quarter's earnings. The US Energy Information Administration's June outlook projects Brent averaging around $105 in the near term, suggesting current levels are seen as a dip rather than a new normal. Markets are less sure: the peace-deal signals from Washington and Tehran, combined with rising Strait of Hormuz tanker traffic, have reordered supply expectations faster than the demand side can compensate.

If crude holds near $70 into August, Shell's Q2 results — due in late July — will arrive at precisely the moment analysts are reassessing whether the $50 buyback breakeven is a floor or a guide.
