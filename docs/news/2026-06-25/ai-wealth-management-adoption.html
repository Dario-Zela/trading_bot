AI adoption in wealth management rises fast, but most clients still insist on a human adviser

A financial adviser meeting with a client — the kind of relationship that AI adoption data suggests most investors still insist on preserving. — Whoisjohngalt / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
62% of retail investors now use AI investment tools, but 71% still want a human adviser in the loop — complicating wealth management's disruption story.

Nearly two-thirds of American retail investors now use artificial intelligence to inform investment decisions — a figure that would have seemed implausible three years ago, when generative AI tools were largely confined to enterprise pilots. A March 2026 survey of 938 U.S. investors conducted by Investing.com put the number at 62%, with nearly a quarter saying they rely on AI tools regularly. The trend holds across geographies: analytics firm Bridgewise puts global retail adoption at roughly 80%. Whatever the precise baseline, the direction is unmistakable.

But adoption and trust are different things. And the wealth management industry is learning that the second variable is doing far more work than the first.

A persistent trust deficit

The 62% headline figure covers a lot of ground. Most AI-using investors are doing something relatively modest: 62% screen stocks or assets, 35% use AI to parse financial news, 34% generate investment ideas. Fewer than a quarter apply it directly to portfolio decisions. A significant share of that adoption number, in other words, is people asking ChatGPT to summarise an earnings call — not delegating their retirement savings to an algorithm.

That distinction goes some way toward explaining why adoption hasn't produced the disruption the industry was braced for. Only 28% of retail investors trust AI-generated investment recommendations. A full 54% say they verify AI analysis against other sources before acting on it. And just 26% say they would choose an AI-powered investment platform; 40% would actively avoid one.

Among high-net-worth individuals, 60% are not comfortable with AI-only investment recommendations, per industry data collated across several recent surveys. The trust gap has proved surprisingly durable — it has outlasted several generations of technological improvement without narrowing much.

The reasons clients give cluster around a few themes: fear of incorrect recommendations (39% in the Investing.com study), concern about AI-driven market herding, and a harder-to-quantify sense that financial decisions involving tax, estate planning, and life transitions require contextual judgment that models don't reliably supply. One certified financial planner cited by advisors' group Money Quotient described finding significant AI-generated tax errors that "would have cost my client hundreds to thousands of dollars" — an anecdote that circulates widely enough in advisory circles to be shaping professional sentiment.

On the advisor side, the posture is more pragmatic than defensive. According to a T. Rowe Price survey of 182 advisors, 83% are now using AI at least monthly. Capgemini's 2026 World Wealth Report found that 76% of advisors want AI specifically to automate routine operational tasks, which currently consume 41% of their working time. The aim is to recover capacity for the work clients demonstrably still value: behavioral coaching through market volatility, estate and tax coordination, and relationship-building that requires empathy and accountability.

That repositioning is showing up in market structure. Hybrid models — AI-assisted portfolios with human oversight — captured 60.7% of robo-advisory revenue in 2024 and represent the fastest-growing segment. The original robo-advisor pitch — pure algorithmic management at rock-bottom fees — has largely been folded into larger platforms rather than displacing the advisory relationship outright.

The implication for fintech strategy is pointed. If the client-facing value proposition was always going to be augmentation rather than replacement, the disruption thesis that drove billions in venture capital toward algorithm-only platforms was targeting the wrong thing. The wedge was never cost — it was trust, and that turns out to require a person in the loop.

Capgemini's survey found that only 17% of high-net-worth individuals describe their current advisory experience as seamless and personalised — a significant gap that represents the actual competitive prize. How regulators handle disclosure requirements around AI-generated advice, particularly on fiduciary standards, will determine whether hybrid platforms or traditional RIAs are better placed to close it. The SEC has yet to issue definitive guidance on the matter.
