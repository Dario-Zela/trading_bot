Reform UK and Labour locked near-level in polls as Farage's ratings sag

Nigel Farage's personal ratings remain deeply negative, but Reform UK's poll numbers haven't followed them down. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK holds at 23-24% and Labour at 25-26% even as Farage's net approval sits near -15%, keeping UK politics — and gilts — unsettled ahead of November's Budget.

Nine weeks into Andy Burnham's premiership, British politics has settled into an uneasy stalemate: Labour and Reform UK are running within touching distance of each other in the polls, and neither man at the top — Burnham or Nigel Farage — seems fully able to convert his party's position into personal credit.

The latest BritPolls poll-of-polls has Labour on 25-26%, Reform UK on 23-24% and the Conservatives trailing on around 20%. That is a closer race than the headline gap suggests once margins of error are factored in, and it has barely moved since Burnham took over from Keir Starmer on 20 July. What has moved is the story underneath the numbers.

A bounce that isn't quite a bounce

Burnham arrived in Downing Street with real momentum — he'd forced a by-election to get into the Commons, then won the Labour leadership unopposed after Starmer quit in the wake of dire May local election results. Ipsos and Opinium both have him as the only major party leader with a positive net approval score, running as high as +14 in early September, well above the -9 he carried into the job. YouGov's more recent read puts it lower, at +7, with unfavourable views climbing six points in a month — the shine coming off, if not gone.

Farage's numbers tell almost the opposite story. His net approval sits at roughly -15% on BritPolls' tracker, and as low as -26 in Opinium's 9 September poll — deeply negative by any standard, and worse than Kemi Badenoch's flat zero or Liberal Democrat leader Ed Davey's -3. Yet Reform's vote share hasn't budged. Voters who dislike Farage personally are, in large numbers, still willing to tick the Reform box — a decoupling that has puzzled pollsters and unsettled a Labour operation banking on personal toxicity to do some of its electoral work for it.

Markets, still watching the gilt

The fragmentation isn't cost-free. Thirty-year gilt yields pushed toward three-decade highs during the Starmer-to-Burnham transition in May and June, and sterling shed roughly 3% as investors priced in the risk of a fiscally looser government. Yields have eased back since Burnham's arrival but remain elevated heading into Rachel Reeves's autumn Budget, still expected in late November. A polling deadlock that persists this close to a major fiscal statement keeps that risk premium alive rather than resolving it.

Both sides now have set-piece moments to make their case. Reform's October conference leaned hard into a "First 100 Days" pitch — a promised immigration emergency, an end to Channel crossings, and tax cuts funded by scrapping net-zero commitments — while facing its own credibility test after a Channel 4 sting on party contractors' polling practices forced two resignations. Labour's conference season and the Budget itself will be the next real tests of whether Burnham's personal goodwill can be turned into party numbers, or whether Reform's floor proves as durable as it has all year.
