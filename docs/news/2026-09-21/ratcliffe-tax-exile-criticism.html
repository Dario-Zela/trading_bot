Man Utd owner Ratcliffe loses 'moral high ground' after tax exile move, says Labour chair

Sir Jim Ratcliffe, the Ineos founder and Manchester United co-owner, pictured in 2013. — Science History Institute / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Labour chair Bridget Phillipson said Man Utd co-owner Jim Ratcliffe "loses the moral high ground" by criticising UK taxes while living as a tax exile in Monaco.

Sir Jim Ratcliffe has spent the past fortnight telling Britain it is "on the slide." On Sunday, a member of the government told him exactly where he could put that opinion.

Bridget Phillipson, Labour's party chair and a senior cabinet minister, used a BBC appearance to dismantle the credibility of the Manchester United co-owner's recent broadside against UK economic policy. Ratcliffe, the Ineos founder who has been a tax resident of Monaco since 2020, told the BBC that high taxes and immigration were driving Britain into decline and that he had lost confidence in the country's direction. Phillipson's response was blunt.

"You'll forgive if I do take with a pinch of salt what someone who is a tax exile has to say about our country."

She went further, telling Sunday with Laura Kuenssberg that Ratcliffe "loses the moral high ground" by "making these kinds of pronouncements while choosing to make decisions, that he is within his rights to make, to become a tax exile." She added, pointedly, that he was also "a big supporter of Brexit" — the policy whose economic fallout he now appears to be criticising from the French Riviera.

A billionaire's complaint, badly timed

Ratcliffe's Monaco move is old news — it has been estimated to save him upward of £4bn in tax since he relocated in 2018 and formalised his residency two years later. What turned a familiar gripe about Britain's tax burden into a genuine political flashpoint was the venue: Ratcliffe made his comments as part-owner of a football club whose staff have spent the past year absorbing some of the most aggressive cost-cutting in the Premier League.

United have cut around 450 jobs since Ratcliffe took operational control, closed the staff canteen at Old Trafford, and stripped back free lunches for everyone outside the first-team squad — reportedly leaving non-playing staff at the Carrington training base with soup and bread as the standard offering. Bonuses and Christmas parties have also been trimmed. Ratcliffe has defended the programme by pointing to the numbers: he says United are now "the most profitable club in the world," a turnaround driven almost entirely by cost discipline rather than any uptick in on-pitch form or revenue.

The optics problem

The juxtaposition is the story. A co-owner who has personally structured his affairs to minimise UK tax exposure is simultaneously overseeing a regime in which club staff lose subsidised lunches to protect the bottom line. Phillipson's line about losing "the moral high ground" lands because it names that contradiction directly, without needing to relitigate the underlying tax debate.

It is not the first time Ratcliffe's public statements have generated blowback disproportionate to their content. Earlier this year he drew criticism, and an apology, after remarks about immigration were characterised as suggesting parts of Britain had been "colonised" — comments that prompted condemnation from supporters' groups and a rebuke from Number 10. A Manchester United Muslim supporters' group renewed calls this week for him to step back from the club, citing the pattern of remarks.

Ratcliffe's camp has not directly rebutted Phillipson's "tax exile" framing, and Ineos declined to add further comment beyond the original BBC interview, in which Ratcliffe also criticised UK energy policy — calling the failure to invest further in North Sea oil and gas "insanity" — and said a British "culture of envy towards wealth" was pushing rich residents abroad.

For a club still explaining soup-and-bread catering to its own employees, having its co-owner hand ammunition to a government minister is not the kind of headline United's communications team will have wanted heading into the international break.

What's next

Chancellor Rachel Reeves delivers her Budget in the coming weeks, the backdrop against which Ratcliffe's tax comments were made and Phillipson's rebuttal was pitched. Any further Old Trafford cost-cutting announcements between now and then will be read, inevitably, against Monaco.
