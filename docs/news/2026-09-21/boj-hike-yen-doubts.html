BoJ's split hike to 1.25% leaves yen unconvinced

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda, whose press conference remarks left investors doubting the pace of further tightening. — Cabinet Public Affairs Office, Government of Japan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
The BoJ hiked to 1.25% but a 7-2 vote — split by two Takaichi-appointed dissenters — sent the yen lower on doubts the tightening cycle will continue.

The Bank of Japan raised its policy rate a quarter point to 1.25% on Friday, the highest level since 1995, and the yen promptly sold off. That is not how central bank tightening is supposed to work.

The mechanism was buried in the vote count. Seven of the BoJ's nine board members backed the hike, but two dissented — Toichiro Asada and Ayano Sato, both academics appointed to the board this year by Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi. Asada argued core inflation still hadn't reached the 2% target, making a pause more appropriate than a hike. Sato said price and activity trends hadn't meaningfully accelerated. Markets had priced a hike as near-certain; what they hadn't priced was a board this divided over doing it.

The yen fell through 157 to the dollar and the Nikkei rallied more than 1% — a stocks-up, currency-down combination that normally accompanies a dovish surprise, not a rate increase. Governor Kazuo Ueda didn't help matters at the press conference. Asked whether faster or back-to-back hikes were on the table, he hedged: "That depends on how price conditions develop... we shouldn't rule anything out," adding that underlying inflation "hasn't exceeded 2% yet, and we want to keep it that way." Investors who wanted a commitment to a steeper path got a shrug instead. "They've just clearly underwhelmed versus expectations here," said Ray Attrill, head of currency strategy at National Australia Bank. "One of the more staggering aspects of it was that they couldn't even get the unanimous vote for that."

Two governments pulling in opposite directions

The split vote isn't happening in a vacuum. Takaichi has favored looser policy to protect government spending plans, and her two board appointees now form a visible, if outvoted, bloc against tightening. On the other side, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has leaned on Tokyo publicly to hike faster and support the yen — pressure Japan's finance minister has reportedly called "scary" in its bluntness. Ueda is threading both: hiking enough to satisfy Washington's complaints about a weak yen, while pacing it slowly enough not to provoke open conflict with the prime minister who appointed part of his own board.

For carry trades and JGB positioning, the read-through is that the policy rate number overstates the BoJ's actual resolve. A central bank whose own board can't agree on step four of a tightening cycle is not a central bank the market should assume will deliver steps five and six on schedule — regardless of what the headline rate says. The next test comes at the BoJ's October 29-30 meeting, where a second Takaichi-appointee dissent, or a third, would confirm this was the start of a pattern rather than a one-off.
