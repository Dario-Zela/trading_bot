UKRAINE WAR · By C. Tanaka · 2026-09-21 · 6 min read

Ukraine's largest-ever drone strike hits Moscow's main refinery as oil holds near $104

The Kapotnya refinery complex in southeastern Moscow, which supplies roughly 40% of the capital's fuel. — A. Savin, Wikimedia Commons (Free Art License)
In one sentence
Ukraine fired over 1,000 drones at Russia overnight, hitting Moscow's Kapotnya refinery — 40% of the capital's fuel supply — as Russians finished voting in parliamentary elections.

Just after midnight on Sunday, as the final ballots were being counted in Russia's parliamentary election, Ukraine launched the largest drone barrage of the war against Moscow. Russia's defence ministry said it shot down 1,110 drones nationwide; Moscow's mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, said more than 450 were aimed at the capital alone — and enough got through to set the city's largest oil refinery on fire.

The Kapotnya plant, a Gazprom Neft facility in Moscow's southeastern industrial belt that supplies roughly 40% of the capital's fuel, took a direct hit on its ELOU-AVT-6 distillation unit — the piece of equipment that turns crude into usable fuel. Andrei Vorobyov, governor of the wider Moscow region, said two people were killed in the strikes and about 20 wounded; Moscow's airports briefly halted flights as debris and smoke drifted over the city. For energy traders parsing the geopolitical risk premium already baked into crude prices, it was the latest and largest data point in a pattern that keeps repeating: Ukraine's long-range drones are no longer a nuisance to Russian refining capacity, they are shaping it.

A record barrage, timed to the ballot box

The scale set this attack apart. Sobyanin called it the largest strike on Moscow since the full-scale invasion began in February 2022, surpassing even the June assault that had already knocked out a large share of Kapotnya's capacity and left Reuters reporting the plant would not be back to full operation until 2027. That this second, heavier wave landed on the closing day of a three-day vote for all 450 seats of the State Duma was not lost on Russian officials. Sobyanin said the timing was "clearly planned with the aim of disrupting the elections." Kyiv did not dispute the timing, only the framing: President Volodymyr Zelensky, addressing the strikes obliquely, warned that "if Ukraine burns, your Moscow will burn too," a line consistent with Kyiv's stated strategy of matching Russian strikes on Ukrainian energy infrastructure with strikes on Russia's.

The vote itself produced no surprises. With most precincts counted, the ruling United Russia party was tracking near 57.5% of the party-list vote, comfortably retaining Kremlin control of the chamber alongside token opposition from the Communists, the nationalist LDPR and other parties that vote in lockstep with the government on the war. Final results are not due until 25 September, but the outcome was never in doubt — which is precisely why the drone campaign's timing reads as a message aimed less at Russian voters than at Russian markets and Russian morale.

Why a refinery fire moves an oil price

Brent crude was trading near $104 a barrel on Monday, having eased back after four straight sessions of declines from a September peak above $108. Even so, the benchmark remains up more than 10% for the month — a rally driven initially by the collision of the Iran war's disruption of Strait of Hormuz shipping and Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline outages, and now compounded by a second front: the steady degradation of Russia's domestic refining capacity.

That distinction matters to traders. Hormuz risk is about crude oil failing to reach the market. The Kapotnya strikes are about refined fuel failing to reach Russian pumps — diesel, petrol and jet fuel that Moscow's own population and military logistics depend on. Refinery outages don't reduce Russia's crude exports in the way a blocked strait would; Russia can still ship barrels abroad. But they do squeeze the country's domestic fuel balance, a dynamic that has already forced Moscow to extend a gasoline export ban through the year and has pushed retail fuel prices higher in Russian regions far from the front line. Energy analysts watch refinery-utilization data as a leading indicator precisely because it captures how much of a producing country's own supply chain has been knocked offline — a distinct signal from crude-price benchmarks, and one that has been trending steadily worse for Russia since Ukraine's long-range drone campaign intensified this year.

An escalating, not stabilizing, infrastructure race

The pattern here is cumulative rather than singular. Kapotnya has now been hit at least twice within roughly a week, on top of the June strikes that forced a partial shutdown lasting months. Ukraine's Fire Point FP-1 and other long-range platforms — some fitted with warheads resembling the domestically produced Flamingo cruise missile, according to fragments examined by Ukrainian military analysts — have extended Kyiv's reach deep into Russian territory, including strikes on a Shahed-drone production plant in Tatarstan more than 1,200 kilometres from the border. Each strike compounds the last: a refinery that loses its AVT distillation unit cannot simply reroute crude to an undamaged line, because the AVT unit is the first and only entry point for converting crude into anything usable. Rebuilding that capacity under wartime sanctions, with limited access to Western refining technology, is measured in months, not days.

For oil markets, that compounding effect is the story underneath the headline price. A single Hormuz disruption can ease once diplomacy or naval escort routes reopen a shipping lane, as they partly have in recent weeks. A refinery sector being dismantled piece by piece does not un-happen; each additional unit taken offline raises the floor on how quickly Russian fuel supply can recover even after a ceasefire, real or notional. That is the calculation now sitting alongside Hormuz risk in every trading desk's model of where Brent goes next: not just how bad the next shock might be, but how much of the damage already done is permanent.

What to watch

Russia's energy ministry will likely disclose within days how much of Kapotnya's roughly 12-million-tonne annual capacity is offline, and whether the AVT-6 unit can be patched enough for partial output before winter demand peaks. Traders will also watch whether Ukraine sustains this tempo — a second attack of this scale within a week suggests intent to keep pressure on refining infrastructure through the autumn — and whether Moscow responds by widening its own strikes on Ukrainian energy assets, a tit-for-tat dynamic that has defined this phase of the war. The next Brent data point that matters is less the daily settlement price than the weekly Russian refinery-utilization figures, which will show whether Kapotnya's damage is a temporary dip or the latest step down in a slower, structural decline.
