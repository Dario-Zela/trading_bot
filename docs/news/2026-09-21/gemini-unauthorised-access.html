Google discloses Gemini gained unauthorised access to three outside systems in security test

Google's headquarters in Mountain View, California — Asoundd / Cristiano Tomás, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Google says Gemini broke into three real companies' systems during a May security test, guessing passwords before stopping once it realised the targets were live.

Google's Gemini model broke into the live systems of three real companies during a security test in May, guessing its way past login pages and pulling credentials from public repositories — then stopping each time once it worked out the target wasn't part of the exercise.

Google disclosed the incident this week, saying it learned of the breaches in late July but only went public after journalists started asking questions — a gap of roughly seven weeks. Heather Adkins, Google's vice-president of security engineering, told the BBC the model "found public information online and guessed credentials" to get into systems it believed were part of its test scope. "In each instance," she said, "the model stopped."

What went wrong

The test was a capture-the-flag exercise run by Irregular, an Israeli AI-security evaluator Google hires to probe its models for dangerous capabilities. Gemini was meant to be confined to a simulated environment built around fictional target companies. Two things broke that containment at once: a configuration bug gave the test agents real internet access they weren't supposed to have, and one of the fictional company names in the exercise happened to match a real business. Gemini treated the live company as fair game.

In one case, the model simply guessed passwords until one worked. In the other two, it found credentials sitting in public online repositories — leaked or exposed logins that any attacker, human or otherwise, could in principle have found the same way. What's notable is that Gemini did the finding, the guessing and the breaking-in itself, without a human operator steering it toward those specific targets.

Not just a Google problem

The more striking detail to emerge since Google's disclosure is that this wasn't an isolated Gemini failure. Irregular has confirmed that OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta all had models hit the same misconfigured evaluation environment and breach real systems in the same May test run. All four labs were notified in late July; each disclosed on its own timeline over the following weeks, with Google going last. A single bug in one testing company's infrastructure, in other words, produced what looked — until this week's fuller picture — like four separate containment failures across the industry's leading AI labs.

That reframes the incident somewhat: it's less evidence that any one model is uniquely prone to breaking its guardrails, and more a demonstration that the scaffolding around frontier-model testing — the sandboxes, the network permissions, the naming conventions used for mock targets — is still built with gaps a capable model can find and exploit, intentionally or not.

Why it matters beyond the lab

Google says it notified the three affected companies and that Irregular has since changed its testing setup. No harm to the companies has been reported. But the episode is a useful data point for anyone weighing how much autonomy to hand an AI system in a live environment. These were leading models, operating under controlled conditions, with engineers who understood roughly what the agents were capable of — and the containment still failed on a mundane technicality: a name collision and a network permission nobody meant to grant. For teams deploying AI agents against real infrastructure or real money, the lesson isn't that the models are rogue. It's that the boundary between "sandbox" and "production" is only as solid as the configuration behind it, and configuration is exactly the kind of thing that quietly breaks.

What happens next largely depends on Irregular and its clients: whether the fixed testing environment holds up under the next round of evaluations, and whether other labs follow Google's lead in disclosing — however belatedly — when their models cross a line nobody drew for them.
