Eye scans shown to predict atrial fibrillation risk years before symptoms

A fundus photograph of a healthy retina — the type of image analysed for structural signs of atrial fibrillation risk. — Mikael Häggström, via Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
UCL researchers found retinal thinning linked to atrial fibrillation up to four years before diagnosis in 90,000 patients — promising, but unvalidated for clinical use.

A screening tool for one of medicine's quietest killers may be sitting in the optician's chair. A study led by researchers at UCL's Institute of Ophthalmology and Moorfields Eye Hospital, published this month in PLOS Digital Health, found that the retina carries measurable signs of atrial fibrillation — the irregular heartbeat that raises stroke risk fivefold — up to four years before a patient is diagnosed.

Atrial fibrillation (AFib) is notoriously hard to catch early. It can be silent or intermittent, showing up only on an electrocardiogram taken at the right moment, which is part of why an estimated one in three cases in the UK goes undiagnosed until a stroke or heart failure forces the issue. The new findings suggest a five-minute retinal scan — already routine in optometry and diabetic eye screening — might flag at-risk patients long before then.

What the study found

The team, led by Dr Josef Huemer with senior author Dr Siegfried Wagner, pooled two large UK datasets: the AlzEye cohort of 50,651 Moorfields patients (5,375 with atrial fibrillation) and the UK Biobank, covering 39,539 participants, 526 of whom already had the condition and 838 who went on to develop it during roughly a decade of follow-up. In total, more than 90,000 people's eyes were examined using optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans and colour fundus photographs.

The consistent signal, replicated across both cohorts, was thinning of the macular ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (mGCIPL) — a layer of nerve cells connecting the eye to the brain. People with existing AFib had mGCIPL roughly 2 microns thinner on average than those without it. Among Biobank participants who were AFib-free at the time of their scan, each standard-deviation decrease in that layer's thickness was associated with a 27% higher hazard of later developing the condition — with about 20% of incident cases emerging within two years of imaging.

The researchers' working theory is mechanical, not decorative: AFib is known to cause tiny, often silent strokes and reduced blood flow, which can in turn damage the optic nerve fibres running from eye to brain — hence the thinning. "This large-scale analysis shows that retinal imaging can pick up on structural changes within the eye that are clearly linked to atrial fibrillation," Wagner said in a statement accompanying the paper, noting the appeal of a test that is "easy to access, quick and doesn't involve any invasive procedures."

What it isn't — yet

The caveats matter as much as the headline. This is an observational, largely cross-sectional analysis, so it cannot establish that retinal thinning causes, or even reliably precedes, AFib in an individual patient rather than merely correlating with shared risk factors like age and vascular disease. Huemer was explicit about the limits: "If confirmed in further research, our findings raise the possibility that routine eye examinations could, in future, help identify people who would benefit from closer cardiac assessment" — a conditional framing, not a launch announcement.

There is no FDA submission, CE mark, or named diagnostic product attached to this work; the authors describe retinal imaging as a potential "complementary tool" for flagging candidates for confirmatory cardiac monitoring, such as an implantable loop recorder, rather than a replacement for ECG-based diagnosis. Neither cohort is fully representative of the general population, and the authors themselves call for neuroimaging studies to confirm the stroke-related mechanism before anyone builds a screening pathway around it.

What comes next

The practical case is nonetheless real: retinal imaging is already deployed at scale through diabetic eye-screening programmes, meaning the infrastructure to test this idea in a prospective trial largely exists. Watch for follow-up studies validating the mGCIPL signal against confirmed neurological findings, and for whether any diagnostics company moves to incorporate AFib-risk scoring into existing retinal-screening software — the kind of step that would turn a promising correlation into a clinical tool.
