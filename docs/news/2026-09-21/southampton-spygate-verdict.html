Southampton's Eckert awaits imminent FA verdict in Spygate case

Tonda Eckert watches from the touchline during a Southampton away fixture at Charlton Athletic, November 2025. — Timmy96 / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Southampton already lost promotion over its spying scandal; the FA now decides within days whether manager Tonda Eckert personally faces a ban.

Southampton's Championship promotion is already gone, sacrificed to a scandal that has become the sport's reference case for institutional cheating. What remains unresolved is whether the man who ran it should lose his job too — and English football's authorities are, at last, about to say.

The Football Association is expected to deliver its verdict on Tonda Eckert within days, most likely during the current international break, closing out a case that has hung over Southampton's St Mary's for four months. Eckert, the club's head coach, was charged in July with three breaches of FA rule E3.1 — misconduct — over what an EFL disciplinary panel called "a contrived and determined plan from top down" to spy on rival training sessions.

What Southampton admitted

The facts are not in dispute; Southampton conceded them outright. Between December and May, staff sent by Eckert observed or filmed closed training sessions belonging to three promotion rivals — Oxford United, Ipswich Town and Middlesbrough — each within 72 hours of a fixture, in violation of EFL regulations. The scheme unravelled on 7 May, when a Southampton analyst, William Salt, was spotted filming Middlesbrough's session at Rockliffe Park from behind a tree. Southampton beat Middlesbrough in the play-off semi-final regardless, but an independent panel ruled that junior staff had been "pressurised" into doing observations "that Mr Eckert and the senior coaches wished them to do," and threw the club out of the final before it had kicked a ball at Wembley. Hull City took their place against Middlesbrough. Southampton also picked up a four-point deduction for this season and, this month, saw co-owner and director Rasmus Ankersen resign.

Eckert has not disputed his role. "I've asked for forgiveness for what I've done. I've apologised publicly," he said over the summer, adding that the club's punishment was "unheard of in the history of sports." Chief executive Phil Parsons has made a similar case for clemency on the individual charge, arguing the institution has already absorbed enough pain: "It's going to be really tough to take if we get another punishment, on top of being thrown out of the play-offs, four points this season, and potentially more. We lose money not being in the Premier League, but businesses lose money. We had to spend a huge amount on legal fees."

The enforcement gap

That argument points at the real tension in the case. The EFL's toolkit only reaches clubs — points, expulsions, fines — which is why Southampton, not Eckert, absorbed the promotion penalty even though the panel found the plan came from the top down. The FA's rule E3.1 exists precisely to close that gap, letting the governing body sanction the individual who directed the misconduct, up to and including a ban from the game. But there is no real precedent for a case like this: an admitted, organised, multi-club spying operation run by a head coach, rather than an on-pitch flashpoint or a betting breach. Reporting around the club suggests the FA is weighing options between a fine, a touchline ban, and something more severe, with a lengthy suspension seen as the scenario that would force owner Dragan Šolak to move Eckert on mid-season despite Southampton's strong league form since.

Eckert, for his part, has stopped short of predicting an outcome. Asked in September whether he expected a decision during the international break, he said simply: "Yes." Asked if he expected a ban: "Let's see, it's a situation that has dragged on for a long time."

What's next

Southampton return from the break away at Wrexham before a far sharper test of the club's mood: the South Coast derby at St Mary's against Portsmouth on 11 October. The FA has given no fixed hearing date, but club officials expect clarity before then — meaning Eckert, one way or another, should know his fate before he next sends a team out at home.
