STANDING WATCH · IRAN · By C. Tanaka · 2026-09-21 · 4 min read

Iran ceasefire holds for a ninth day as Tehran warns of 'painful' response

Tehran's Azadi Tower, a symbol of the capital where officials have warned of a 'painful' response to any resumption of US strikes. — Bernard Gagnon / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Iran's ceasefire held a ninth day, but Tehran's military warned of retaliation if US strikes resume, as a UN mission found war crimes by both sides.

Nine days without an Iranian missile or drone crossing into Israeli, American or partner airspace is, by the standards of this war, a long time. It is also, as of Day 203, the entirety of the good news.

The pause holds for a straightforward reason: neither side has fired first since September 8–9, when Iran's last confirmed launch struck Muwaffaq Salti air base in Jordan. But the quiet is not a settlement. It is an interval between rounds, and both sides spent this week making sure everyone understood that. Iran's Khatam al-Anbiya Central Headquarters said over the weekend it had been informed the United States, "with the backing of some regional countries," was preparing to resume strikes — and warned that any new attack would trigger "sustained retaliation against US bases and interests," with regional states that cooperate with Washington "considered complicit." It is the harder edge of a threat Tehran has made before: Parliament Speaker Mohammad-Bagher Ghalibaf has said any future aggression would meet "a faster, more intense and more painful response," declaring the era of proportionate retaliation over.

A ceasefire with no signatures

What exists between Iran, the United States and Israel is less a negotiated ceasefire than a mutual pause that has outlasted expectations — the same fragile shape as the truce reached in June, which collapsed within weeks under tit-for-tat tanker strikes and ballistic exchanges. Trump has said Washington is nearing the end of the war, with direct talks reportedly under way; Tehran's public posture, by contrast, has stayed defensive and conditional, built around the word "if." If the US resumes strikes. If regional states host new operations. Nothing in the record suggests either side has offered the other a durable exit, only a shared interest — for now — in not being the one who restarts it.

That asymmetry matters for markets. Brent crude fell to $102.41 on September 18, down for a third straight session, as Saudi Arabia said it had moved 2.8 million barrels a day through the Strait of Hormuz over the prior week, against roughly 700,000 barrels a day in August when the strait was effectively closed. That recovery is the clearest sign the ceasefire is real in its effects even if it isn't real on paper — and the clearest reason a single resumed strike could reverse it overnight.

The UN's parallel verdict

Complicating any eventual negotiated exit, the UN's Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Iran published findings on September 17 concluding there are "reasonable grounds to believe" the United States committed war crimes in a pair of February 28 strikes — one on a primary school in Minab that killed more than 150 people, around 120 of them children, the other on a sports complex and residential area in Lamerd that killed 22 civilians with tungsten-pellet munitions. The same mission documented what it called crimes against humanity by Iran's own government during the 2025–2026 protest crackdowns: at least 221 children killed, mass arrests, a nationwide internet blackout lasting more than five months, and dozens of executions since March. Its summary was blunt: Iranian civilians are "caught between gross human rights violations and crimes against humanity committed by their own Government and the nation's deadly conflict with the United States and Israel."

The White House dismissed the findings on September 18 as "unserious nonsense," insisting Iran was the only party committing war crimes. That reflexive rejection, paired with Tehran's own record under the same report, means the mission's work is unlikely to produce leverage for a settlement anytime soon — it has instead given both governments a fresh reason to distrust whatever forum eventually tries to end this.

What to watch

The next test is not diplomatic but military: whether the Trump administration acts on what Iran says it has been told — a resumption of strikes — or lets the pause run past the two-week mark. A single confirmed strike in either direction would be the fastest route back to the Hormuz bottleneck that shut in nearly three-quarters of the strait's normal flow in August, with the crude-price reaction to match.
