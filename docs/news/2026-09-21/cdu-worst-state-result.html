Merz's CDU suffers worst-ever state result as AfD surges in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern

Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who called the CDU's Mecklenburg-Vorpommern result 'a disaster' after the party fell below the 5% threshold for the first time in its history. — European Union / Sophie Hugon, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The CDU fell below Germany's 5% threshold for the first time ever as the AfD won Mecklenburg-Vorpommern outright with 38%, prompting Merz to call it a 'disaster.'

The Christian Democratic Union has lost state elections before. It has never, in the party's 80-year history, lost so badly that it failed to clear the 5% threshold and vanished from a state legislature entirely — until Sunday night in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern, the rural, lake-studded state that stretches along Germany's Baltic coast.

The CDU took 4.9% of the vote, according to the final count from the state election authority, down from 13.3% in 2021 and enough for zero of the Landtag's 71 seats. The Alternative for Germany, campaigning on migration and economic grievance under regional leader Leif-Erik Holm, won outright with 38.2% — its strongest result in any German state to date. Chancellor Friedrich Merz, who leads the CDU nationally as well as the federal coalition in Berlin, did not try to soften it. "We cannot sugar coat the result in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern," he told reporters after calling an emergency meeting of party leadership on Sunday evening. "It is a disaster."

A state remade

The scale of the collapse is worth sitting with. The CDU's 8.4-point drop erased all 12 of its seats in one stroke, and turnout climbed to 78.1% — up more than seven points from 2021 — meaning the AfD didn't just consolidate a shrinking electorate but pulled in voters who had previously stayed home or backed someone else. Its 32 seats mark an 18-seat gain and a 21.5-point swing, the kind of shift that in most democracies would put a party in government. It won't here: incumbent premier Manuela Schwesig's Social Democrats finished a close second at 35.5% (29 seats, down from 39.6% in 2021), and with the Left on 6.5% and the Greens on 5.7%, a red-red-green coalition holding 39 of 71 seats remains the most likely outcome. The new Sahra Wagenknecht party, BSW, narrowly missed the threshold itself at 4.8%.

That arithmetic matters because it is the only thing standing between Holm's AfD and power in Schwerin. Since 2018 the CDU has operated under a formal "resolution of incompatibility" ruling out any coalition or cooperation with the AfD at any level of government — the so-called firewall, or Brandmauer. It held again on Sunday. But a party that can win 38% of the vote in a state and still be locked out of office by a coalition of its rivals is a standing argument, made nightly on the AfD's own campaign materials, that the mainstream is rigging the outcome rather than winning it.

Squeezed from both flanks

Mecklenburg-Vorpommern is not an isolated data point. On September 6, the AfD won 43.8% in neighbouring Saxony-Anhalt — the strongest showing by any party in that state since reunification — while the CDU fell to 17.2%, its worst result there on record. An exit poll that day found just 9% of voters approved of Merz's performance as chancellor. And on the very day Mecklenburg-Vorpommern voted, Berlin held its own state election in which the CDU was pushed into second place, down to roughly 20% from 28.2% in 2023 — not chiefly by the AfD, which placed third, but by the Left, which surged to around 25% from 12%. The pattern across all three votes is the same: the CDU is not merely losing to the far right in its eastern strongholds, it is losing altitude everywhere, to whichever party is positioned to catch the protest vote.

What it means for Berlin

The federal coalition — CDU/CSU and SPD, formed after the February 2025 election and in office since that May — was already unsteady before Sunday. A summer scandal involving the surrogacy arrangements of former Union parliamentary leader Jens Spahn forced his resignation in July, and the cabinet reshuffle that followed was handled badly enough to trigger, for the first time, open talk within the CDU of replacing Merz roughly 14 months into his chancellorship — with North Rhine-Westphalia's Hendrik Wüst and Bavaria's Markus Söder both circulating as names. The SPD, for its part, is using the moment to press for renegotiation of pension and long-term care reforms, and some Union lawmakers have floated withholding budget votes in retaliation — the kind of leverage play that, unchecked, is how coalitions actually fall apart, rather than through any single dramatic rupture.

Merz insisted Sunday that he intends to stay and that the economic reforms his government has pushed will continue regardless of the result. No state goes to the polls again until 2027, when Bremen, Schleswig-Holstein, North Rhine-Westphalia, Saarland and Lower Saxony are all due — leaving the federal coalition's autumn budget negotiations and the pension and care talks the SPD has demanded as the nearer-term test of whether Sunday's result stays a regional embarrassment or becomes the trigger for something bigger in Berlin.
