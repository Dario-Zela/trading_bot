Only 160 apprenticeship starts in eight months on Labour's flagship growth scheme

Bridget Phillipson, education secretary when foundation apprenticeships launched, at her Downing Street cabinet appointment — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
Labour's foundation apprenticeship scheme has produced 160 starts against a 30,000 target, prompting warnings its skills revolution is failing.

Labour came to office promising a “skills revolution.” Eight months into one of its flagship vehicles for delivering it, the revolution has produced 160 apprentices.

That is the total number of young people who have started a foundation apprenticeship — the paid, entry-level scheme ministers unveiled as a centrepiece of their growth and opportunity agenda — between August 2025 and April this year, according to official figures reported by the Guardian. The government's own ambition is 30,000 such starts by the end of this parliament. At the current run rate of roughly 20 a month, it would take well over a century to get there.

A scheme built for a real problem

Foreman apprenticeships were designed to answer a genuine crisis: nearly a million 16- to 24-year-olds in England are not in education, employment or training, and apprenticeship starts for under-25s have fallen by around a third since the Conservatives introduced the apprenticeship levy a decade ago. Sir Keir Starmer announced the new scheme, alongside a wider overhaul of the levy into a “growth and skills levy,” as part of what the government called radical skills reforms — paid jobs for young adults carrying a minimum of eight months' structured training, meant to be an accessible first rung for those the system has been failing.

The shortfall was surfaced not by opposition researchers but by the Fabian Society, a think tank generally sympathetic to Labour. Its report, Levying Up, warns that the government's skills revolution risks outright failure and argues the apprenticeship levy system — inherited and only partially reformed — has failed on every metric it was meant to improve: growth, employer skills needs and social mobility alike. “The route into an apprenticeship does not work,” said Joe Dromey, the Fabian Society's general secretary.

Numbers that undercut the pitch

The 160 figure sits awkwardly against a backdrop of overall apprenticeship growth: total enrollments in England are up around 9% this academic year, past 300,000. But that headline masks the trend the government said it most wanted to fix — starts among under-25s have kept falling even as the system as a whole expands, and the one programme built specifically to reverse that has barely registered.

The Fabian Society's prescription is a £311m-a-year regional apprenticeship fund, financed by devolving 5% of an expanded levy and letting employers use the money to cover up to half an apprentice's wage costs — roughly £4,700 per placement. Alan Milburn, the former Labour cabinet minister now reviewing youth worklessness support for the government, and Greater Manchester mayor Andy Burnham, who has pushed his own technical education overhaul, are both circling the same territory: that national levy design is too rigid and too slow to reach the young people it targets.

Why it lands beyond skills policy

Apprenticeships were never going to move the growth dial on their own. But foundation apprenticeships were pitched as a low-cost, high-visibility proof point — a scheme government could stand up quickly and point to as evidence its growth mission was more than rhetoric. A number this far below target does the opposite: it invites the question of whether Whitehall can execute the smaller, simpler parts of its agenda at all, which is an uncomfortable frame to carry into arguments about the bigger, harder ones — welfare reform, planning, the industrial strategy.

The Department for Education says it remains committed to increasing youth apprenticeship starts. The near-term test is whether Milburn's worklessness review, due to report in the coming months, forces a redesign rather than a restatement of the same target — and whether the Treasury, mid-Budget squeeze, is willing to fund the kind of wage subsidy the Fabian Society says the scheme actually needs.
