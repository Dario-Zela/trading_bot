US-China talks open in New York on trade and AI ahead of Trump-Xi summit

Donald Trump and Xi Jinping at a bilateral meeting during the 2017 G20 summit in Hamburg — the two leaders meet again this week for the second time in 2026. — White House photo by Shealah Craighead / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US and China opened pre-summit talks in New York proposing an AI safety hotline while explicitly keeping chip export controls off the table, days before Xi's Washington visit.

Eight hours in a conference room at JPMorgan Chase's Manhattan headquarters on Sunday produced no signed agreement, no joint statement, and — by design — no resolution of the hardest question in the US-China relationship: how far Washington will go in restricting the flow of advanced chips to China. What it did produce was a clearer picture of the opening terms both sides intend to carry into the Oval Office this week, when Xi Jinping lands at Joint Base Andrews for his first state visit to Washington since 2015.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng led the session, joined by US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer. Bessent called the talks "very successful" afterward; Chinese state media described them as "candid, in-depth and constructive." Both descriptions are the standard vocabulary of pre-summit diplomacy, but the substance underneath was more concrete than usual, touching trade, rare earths, and — for the first time in this format — a proposed AI safety mechanism.

The AI carve-out

The headline proposal to come out of New York was American, not Chinese: Bessent floated a notification system under which Washington and Beijing would flag AI-related incidents that rise to the level of a national security concern — the diplomatic equivalent of a hotline for algorithmic accidents. "Moving from opaque to more transparency between the number one and the number two AI powers in the world is very important," Bessent told reporters, framing the goal as "a shared vision of common goals and common threats." The mechanism, still a proposal rather than an agreement, would also touch cooperation on monitoring AI-directed cyberattacks, with US officials floating the idea that American and Chinese AI labs could self-regulate and share information to head off such attacks.

What the mechanism pointedly does not touch is more instructive than what it does. Greer told reporters that US export controls on advanced AI chips and semiconductor manufacturing equipment were explicitly excluded from the talks — a firewall Washington drew deliberately, separating the diplomacy of AI safety from the harder-edged instrument of chip policy. That distinction matters to anyone pricing semiconductor exposure into the summit: it signals the administration intends to keep export controls as a standalone lever, not something to be traded away as part of a broader AI-cooperation gesture. The timing sharpens the point. Just a day before the New York meeting, Washington accused six Chinese AI firms of copying restricted models and circumventing chip export controls — the kind of allegation that tends to harden a negotiating position rather than soften it.

Taiwan, Iran, and the ticking clock

Beneath the AI headline sat the older, harder business of the relationship. The talks were meant to prepare the ground for a US-China trade truce — struck in Busan last October, capping US tariffs on Chinese goods at roughly 20% after a tariff war that briefly pushed levies into triple digits — that expires November 10. Negotiators also revisited the flow of Chinese rare-earth magnets and critical minerals, which US officials say remains insufficient despite Beijing's earlier commitments to ease restrictions; on that front, delegates reported no visible progress. Geopolitics hung over the room without being the formal agenda: Taiwan and the fate of sanctioned Iranian oil flows were raised alongside the economic issues, officials said, underscoring that the trade talks and the broader strategic relationship are no longer treated as separable tracks.

Greer described Sunday's session as groundwork for "a successful summit" — Trump and Xi meet Thursday and Friday in Washington, the second encounter between the two leaders this year after Trump's visit to Beijing in May. The itinerary is heavy with ceremony: an arrival at Joint Base Andrews on the 23rd, a Rose Garden military review and state dinner on the 24th, tea and a National Archives visit on the 25th. Xi is skipping the UN General Assembly, happening in New York the same week, to make the trip — a signal of how much weight Beijing is putting on the Washington leg alone.

What the opening positions tell you

The shape of Sunday's talks — AI dialogue advancing, export controls deliberately walled off, critical minerals stalled, Taiwan and Iran acknowledged but unresolved — suggests Washington is trying to bank a symbolic win on AI governance without loosening its grip on the chip supply chain that underpins it. For markets watching semiconductor and AI-linked names into the summit, that combination is the tell: a warmer diplomatic register on AI safety paired with an unmoved position on the export controls that actually constrain Chinese chip access. The truce's November 10 expiry is the next hard deadline; what Trump and Xi say about extending it on Thursday and Friday will matter more than anything signed in Manhattan.
