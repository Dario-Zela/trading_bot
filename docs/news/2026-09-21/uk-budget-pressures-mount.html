UK inflation, spending overshoot and Iran fallout squeeze Reeves ahead of Budget

The Bank of England, whose rate-setting committee is weighing a fuel-driven inflation spike against a slowing growth outlook. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
War-driven fuel inflation, a £5.1bn spending overshoot and surging gilt yields have roughly halved Chancellor Healey's fiscal headroom ahead of the 28 October Budget.

Six weeks before Chancellor John Healey stands up to deliver his first Budget, the numbers he needs to make add up are moving in the wrong direction — and not for reasons his own department can control.

Inflation is accelerating, government spending is running hotter than forecast, and gilt yields have surged far enough to strip out roughly half the fiscal headroom he inherited. The common thread running through all three, ministers now argue openly, is the war between Israel, the United States and Iran and its knock-on effect through energy markets. "What's happening in the Middle East is hitting inflation, it's hitting growth, it's hitting borrowing costs," Healey told the Financial Times this month, framing his 28 October Budget as one that will need to preserve a "buffer against uncertainty" rather than spend it away.

A war premium in the inflation print

The most immediate evidence sits in the August inflation figures the Office for National Statistics published this month. Headline CPI rose to 3.1%, up from 2.9% in July and the sharpest annual rate in five months, with CPIH — the measure including owner-occupier housing costs — at 3.3%. The driver was unambiguous: transport prices, and motor fuel specifically. Petrol rose 9.1p a litre over the month to 161.3p; diesel rose 14.2p to 181.8p. Annual fuel inflation jumped to 23.0%, from 15.5% the month before, pushing the transport component of CPI to 4.6%.

Core inflation, by contrast, held at 2.6% and food inflation eased to 1.3% — its lowest since 2021. That split matters: it suggests the current overshoot is being driven by an external energy shock layered on top of an otherwise cooling domestic price picture, rather than broad-based demand pressure. For the Bank of England, already navigating its own balancing act after last week's decision to hold rates at 3.75% while scrapping long-dated gilt sales, a fuel-driven inflation spike complicates the case for the rate cuts markets had been pricing in in 2026.

Spending is already running ahead of plan

The second pressure point predates the current bout of Middle East volatility but is being amplified by it. Data compiled through the summer show central government spending running £5.1bn above the profile set out in the OBR's March 2026 forecast, even as receipts came in £2.8bn ahead of expectations on stronger corporation tax and PAYE income tax. Borrowing in the first four months of the fiscal year reached £56.7bn — £2.3bn above the monthly profile consistent with that March forecast, even after accounting for the year-on-year improvement.

That matters because Rachel Reeves, before she was replaced as Chancellor by Healey in a July reshuffle under Prime Minister Andy Burnham, built her fiscal framework — a current-budget surplus by 2029/30 — on a headroom estimate of roughly £26bn. Deutsche Bank's UK economist Sanjay Raja estimated this month that if the recent bond sell-off holds, that cushion is now closer to £13.8bn before any new spending commitment is even weighed. Almost all of the deterioration, on his calculation, traces to higher government interest costs rather than new spending decisions — a direct consequence of 30-year gilt yields climbing to 5.89% in September, their highest since 1998, and 10-year yields sitting near 5.3%.

Ministers stop pretending it's routine

What has shifted in recent days is less the economic data than the political candour around it. Labour chair Bridget Phillipson told Sky News that Donald Trump's on-and-off military campaign against Iran had disrupted global oil supplies in a way that was now visibly complicating Healey's Budget arithmetic, describing "very real challenges... particularly where it's come to the conflict we've seen in the Middle East and the impact that that's been having on businesses and on families close to home." The OBR itself flagged as much in its spring update, warning that the conflict, which escalated as it was finalising that document, "could have very significant impacts on the global and UK economies." That caution has since been partially borne out: the OBR trimmed its 2026 growth forecast to 1.1% from 1.4%, even as it nudged up 2027 and 2028 estimates.

The read-across for markets is that three separate pressures — energy-driven inflation, a spending base already running hot, and a bond market repricing UK sovereign risk — are compounding rather than offsetting each other, at precisely the moment the Treasury needs flexibility. Sterling and gilts have both been trading with a visible tail-risk premium tied to Middle East headlines, distinct from the domestic fiscal story that dominated coverage of Reeves's tenure.

What happens next

The next hard data point is the OBR's pre-Budget forecast, expected in the first half of October, which will show whether the £5.1bn spending overshoot and the gilt move translate into a formal downgrade to the headroom figure. Healey has signalled tax rises are now "near-certain" rather than a fallback option, and traders will be watching the 28 October Budget itself for whether he opts to rebuild the buffer through revenue measures or absorb a thinner margin against future shocks. Either way, the war in Iran has moved from background risk to a line item ministers are prepared to name out loud.
