US midterm early voting begins amid cost-of-living strain and Iran war fatigue

An "I Voted" sticker, the small ritual marker of a midterm cycle that opened six weeks early this year — Dwight Burdette / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Early voting opened in Virginia, Minnesota, South Dakota and soon Illinois, with cost-of-living anger and Iran war fatigue set to decide who controls Congress on November 3.

The line outside a community center in Fairfax County, Virginia, started forming before the doors opened. Some voters were there for the coffee-and-doughnuts civic ritual of it; others said, more bluntly, that they wanted to be first in line to say something about their grocery bill.

In-person early voting opened on September 18 in Virginia, South Dakota and Minnesota, with Illinois following on September 24 — the first concrete data points in a midterm cycle that both parties agree is being fought on two fronts: the price of eggs and the price of a war in Iran that most voters say they never wanted prolonged. North Carolina began mailing absentee ballots on September 4. By Election Day on November 3, 47 states plus Washington, D.C. will have offered some form of voting before the deadline itself — up from 24 states in 2000, a two-decade expansion of the early-voting window that now means a meaningful share of the electorate will have already voted before the final debate, the final ad buy, or the next economic data print lands.

The affordability primary

Ask pollsters what's driving the electorate and the answer arrives almost embarrassingly uniform. A Reuters/Ipsos poll fielded August 28–31 found 47% of registered voters naming cost of living as the single most important factor in their vote — and three in four said their personal cost of living is heading in the wrong direction. Other surveys echo the ranking even when the wording shifts: inflation and affordability consistently outpoll immigration, crime and even the Iran conflict itself as the top-line concern, a pattern pollsters say has held since 2024 and shows no sign of softening as the campaign enters its final seven weeks.

That matters because the party seen as better on the issue tends to benefit from it, and polling has tilted toward Democrats on cost-of-living questions even as the generic ballot stays close. Affordability has become the dominant theme in Americans for Prosperity's grassroots surveys of the most competitive Senate races, according to the group's internal tracking — a signal that even Republican-aligned pollsters see the issue cutting against incumbents of both parties in high-inflation districts.

War fatigue, not war support

The second thread is harder to poll cleanly, because it isn't really about foreign policy so much as exhaustion. An NBC News survey found 62% of voters saying the Iran conflict "has not been worth it," and 57% saying they'd be less likely to back a candidate who supported it. Reuters/Ipsos put approval of the strikes at just 35%, with only 17% expecting Middle East stability to improve over the coming year. Even within the president's own coalition, support has eroded on economic grounds: a Politico poll found just 37% of Trump backers still supported the war if it meant higher prices at home, down from 50% in May.

Strategists on the right describe it privately as a turnout problem rather than a persuasion problem — the war hasn't converted Republicans into Democrats so much as it's made some of them tired enough to stay home. That distinction matters enormously in midterms, where turnout differentials, not vote-switching, usually decide close races. A nine-day-old ceasefire in Tehran has eased the most acute anxiety, but pollsters say the fatigue it left behind is stickier than the news cycle that produced it.

What's actually on the ballot

Strip away the mood music and the mechanics are stark. Republicans hold a three-seat House majority, meaning Democrats need a net gain of just three to retake the chamber — a bar the Cook Political Report's ten Democrat-held and eight Republican-held toss-ups suggest is within reach. The Senate math is tougher: Democrats need four net pickups against a map where 22 of the 35 seats up are currently Republican-held, with North Carolina's open seat, Georgia's Jon Ossoff and Michigan — after Gary Peters' retirement — drawing the heaviest spending. Forecasters have put Democratic Senate odds as high as 65%, though most describe the chamber as a genuine toss-up rather than a lean.

Either chamber flipping would reshape the fiscal calculus markets have been pricing since the summer: tax policy, spending bills and the regulatory agenda all run through whoever holds the gavel come January. Early-voting turnout numbers — typically reported county-by-county within days — will be parsed by both campaigns and by markets alike for whether the affordability complaint is translating into actual ballots, or whether it remains, as it so often does in September, a poll answer that doesn't yet have a candidate attached to it.

The next real signal lands in early October, when the first wave of early-voting return data — party registration of who's actually requesting and casting ballots — starts to firm up turnout models ahead of the November 3 count.
