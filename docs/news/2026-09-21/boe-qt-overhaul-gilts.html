BoE holds rate at 3.75% but scraps long-dated gilt sales, yields still push toward 5.3%

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which held Bank Rate at 3.75% and overhauled its gilt-unwind timetable on September 17. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
BoE held rates at 3.75% and scrapped long-dated gilt sales to unwind its £488bn QE book by 2034, but gilt yields near 5.3% show fiscal and inflation risks still dominate.

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee did the easy part on Wednesday: it left Bank Rate at 3.75% for a sixth consecutive meeting, a decision so well-flagged that sterling barely blinked. The harder, more interesting decision was buried in the accompanying minutes — a rewrite of how the Bank unwinds the £488bn government bond portfolio it built up over more than a decade of quantitative easing.

The MPC voted 6-3 to hold rates, with chief economist Huw Pill joined by external members Catherine Mann and Megan Greene in voting for an immediate quarter-point rise to 4%. That is the largest hawkish dissent of this cycle, and it reflects an inflation backdrop that has deteriorated since July. CPI rose to 3.1% in August, and the Bank now expects it to climb toward 3.75% by the fourth quarter and above 4% in early 2027 — pushed higher by the Middle East conflict's effect on crude and refined energy prices, which the Bank says remain "more volatile and higher than pre-conflict" levels.

Governor Andrew Bailey has been unusually blunt about where that leaves policy. "It is likely policy may have to tighten," he told reporters, an odd thing for a central bank governor to say in the same breath as holding rates steady — but it captures the Committee's position: on hold now, leaning toward a hike later if the inflation overshoot doesn't fade on its own.

The QT overhaul

While the rate decision changed nothing, the Bank's approach to quantitative tightening changed a great deal. The MPC voted unanimously to set out, for the first time, a full timetable for reducing its gilt holdings to zero — but on far gentler terms than the market had been bracing for.

Of the £488bn of gilts still on the Bank's balance sheet, £222bn maturing by 2034 will simply be allowed to run off as bonds mature. Another £146bn maturing between 2035 and 2049 will be sold — but gradually, worth roughly £20bn a year. And £120bn of the longest-dated paper, maturing in 2049 or later, will be kept indefinitely to back the Bank's banknote issuance, rather than sold at all. Combined with maturities, that puts total annual balance-sheet runoff at about £46bn, down from £70bn over the past year, with the full unwind of the active-sale portion stretching out to 2034.

More immediately, the Bank has paused all gilt sales until April 2027 while it consults with the Treasury on a structural change to how it offloads bonds: selling directly to the Debt Management Office at market prices, rather than running its own auctions into the open market. That is a technical-sounding shift, but it matters — DMO sales avoid the auction-day price impact that has periodically rattled the long end of the curve since QT began in 2022.

This is the part of the decision markets had been waiting for. Long-dated gilt sales have been a recurring source of indigestion for a curve already strained by heavy government borrowing, and scrapping them outright — rather than merely slowing them — is a tacit admission that the previous unwind pace was creating more financial-stability risk than the Bank wanted to carry into an uncertain fiscal year. Thirty-year yields fell 11-12 basis points on the news, and 10-year yields eased around 8 basis points, the sharpest single-day gilt rally in months.

Yields still near two-decade highs

That relief needs context. Gilts had sold off hard in the fortnight before the meeting, with the 10-year yield striking 5.295% on September 10 — the highest level since August 2007 — as oil prices pushed back above $100 a barrel and reignited fears that imported energy costs would keep UK inflation elevated well into next year. Even after Wednesday's QT-driven rally, yields remain only a few basis points below that 19-year high, and 30-year gilts are trading near levels last seen before the 2008 financial crisis.

The gap between what the Bank is signalling and what the market is pricing tells its own story. A slower, gentler QT unwind is by construction a dovish, supportive-for-bonds move — it removes a chunk of forced seller flow from the long end. Yet gilts have barely responded relative to where they sat a month ago, because the QT relief is being offset by two other forces the Bank cannot directly control: an inflation path that its own MPC dissenters think warrants tightening, not easing, and a fiscal backdrop where Chancellor Rachel Reeves is heading into a November Budget with headroom that independent estimates put well below the roughly £22.7bn she banked at the last fiscal event, some analysts reckoning it has been cut by a third or more by higher borrowing costs alone.

In other words, the Bank of England has done what it can on the supply side of the gilt market. It cannot do anything about the demand side, where investors are still charging a premium for UK duration risk tied to persistent inflation and a debt-to-GDP ratio that shows no sign of falling.

What the pivot signals

Taken together, the rate hold and the QT rewrite point to a central bank trying to run two policies at once: keep monetary policy tight enough to lean against an inflation overshoot, while making sure its own balance-sheet operations don't add unnecessary stress to a gilt market already digesting heavy fiscal issuance. That the QT changes needed a unanimous vote — in a Committee that split three ways on rates — suggests financial stability concerns now command broader consensus than the inflation debate does.

The practical upshot for traders is a gilt curve that should see less forced long-end supply than previously modelled, a modest positive for duration positioning, but one that will keep being tested by the same inflation and fiscal pressures that pushed yields to 5.3% in the first place. The next real test comes in two stages: October's CPI print, which will show whether the energy-driven inflation pickup the Bank flagged is materialising as forecast, and Reeves's Budget in November, where the market will find out whether the Treasury's borrowing plans validate the fiscal risk premium currently priced into gilts — or make it worse.
