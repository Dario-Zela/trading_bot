Ashtead Technology shares sink 15% as Middle East delays hit subsea projects

An offshore installation vessel of the type used in subsea energy projects — the segment Ashtead Technology services from bases including Aberdeen and Abu Dhabi. — Flickr user penwin, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Ashtead Technology's shares fell 15% after it cut 2026 guidance on Middle East project delays, part of a wider wave of Iran-war-linked UK profit warnings.

Ashtead Technology Holdings shed more than 15% of its market value on Thursday after the Aberdeen-based subsea equipment specialist told investors that projects earmarked for the second half of 2026 have been postponed until 2027 — the latest and largest sign that the Iran war is now cutting directly into UK corporate earnings, not just sentiment.

The group, which rents out remotely operated vehicles, survey gear and mechanical tooling to offshore energy operators from bases in Aberdeen, Abu Dhabi, Singapore, London and Houston, said full-year revenue is now tracking roughly 5% below the market consensus of £214.2m, with adjusted EBITA seen around 15% below the £59.2m analysts had pencilled in. Year-end leverage is expected to hold near 1.3 times — a balance sheet the company is at pains to describe as resilient, even as the order book slips.

A warning that was already flagged

This is not entirely a surprise. In a July 15 trading update, chief executive Allan Pirie had already told the market that hitting full-year expectations depended on "an improvement in the Middle East conflict and the absence of significant disruption to project schedules." Neither condition has been met. Thursday's statement confirmed that a number of Middle East projects slated for the second half of this year have now slipped a full financial year, with additional slippage in Europe and the Americas that the company attributes to broader economic uncertainty and vessel-scheduling changes.

Ashtead's Abu Dhabi base is not incidental — the Gulf has been one of the fastest-growing markets for its ROV and asset-integrity work serving offshore oil, gas and wind infrastructure. When operators pause capital projects in the region, as many have since fighting broke out, the delay shows up first in Ashtead's order book, several quarters before it might appear in a major oil company's own numbers.

Part of a wider pattern

Ashtead's warning lands amid a broader run of profit alerts that EY-Parthenon's UK tracker has linked to the conflict, which began on February 28. The firm's Q2 data found 59 profit warnings from listed UK companies in the three months to June — up from 55 in the first quarter — with 53% citing policy change and geopolitical uncertainty, the highest share in more than 25 years of the survey. Travel and leisure was the worst-hit sector, with seven warnings in the quarter, the most since 2022, as airlines including easyJet flagged weaker bookings and higher jet fuel costs. EY-Parthenon partner Jo Robinson noted that warnings are "increasingly concentrated in sectors facing rising costs, cautious consumers and tighter credit conditions."

Subsea services sit outside that travel cluster but share the same transmission mechanism: a regional conflict that was, for months, treated by equity markets as a contained tail risk is now working its way through capital spending decisions in energy, tourism and adjacent supply chains. Ashtead's exposure is a reminder that the read-through is not limited to airlines and tour operators — any UK-listed firm with meaningful Gulf project revenue is carrying similar risk into its 2026 numbers.

The next test comes at Ashtead's full-year results, expected early next year, when the market will look for whether the postponed Middle East work has firmed up for 2027 or slipped further. Until the ceasefire — now nine days old — proves durable enough to unfreeze deferred capital projects, subsea and energy-services names with Gulf exposure are likely to stay on watch lists alongside the travel stocks already absorbing the hit.
