Lagarde pushes back on automatic ECB response to oil shocks

ECB President Christine Lagarde, who told euro-area finance ministers in Dublin that rates will not move automatically in step with energy prices. — European Union 2023 / European Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Lagarde told euro-area finance ministers in Dublin that ECB rates "do not move in lockstep" with oil prices, pushing back on bets on automatic further hikes after the deposit rate reached 2.5%.

Two weeks after raising the deposit rate to 2.5% — its second hike since June — the European Central Bank is trying to make sure markets don't extrapolate a third, a fourth, and a fifth.

Speaking to euro-area finance ministers gathered in Dublin for an informal Eurogroup meeting, ECB President Christine Lagarde pushed back on the assumption that further oil-driven inflation would automatically trigger more tightening. "Interest rates do not move in lockstep with the price of energy," she told reporters, arguing that an energy shock cuts both ways — it lifts prices, but it also saps growth and consumption, which pulls in the opposite direction for policy.

A shock the ECB didn't choose

The backdrop is the war between Israel and Iran, now in its ninth week of an uneasy ceasefire, which has kept Brent-linked crude prices elevated near $104 a barrel and Strait of Hormuz shipping risk on every trading desk's screen. Eurozone headline inflation hit 3.3% in August, its highest since September 2023, with Spain — heavily exposed to energy import costs — running at 4.5% while Germany sat at 2.9% and France at 2.7%. That divergence is itself a headache for a central bank setting one rate for nineteen countries.

The ECB's response so far has been two quarter-point moves: the first on 11 June, its first hike in three years, and the second on 10 September, which took the deposit rate to 2.5%, the main refinancing rate to 2.65% and the marginal lending facility to 2.9%. Lagarde called the September move a "no-brainer" — unanimous, and, by the bank's own account, unsurprising. What has surprised traders is the accompanying forecast: the ECB now expects headline inflation to average 3.0% this year before easing to 2.5% in 2027 and 2.1% in 2028, an upward revision from June that had markets pricing close to another percentage point of tightening by late 2027.

Drawing a line between shock and structure

Lagarde's Dublin remarks were an attempt to cap that repricing. The distinction she drew — between a commodity-price shock and the underlying, stickier inflation the ECB actually targets — is the same one she made in March, when she said the Iran-driven energy shock was "no repeat of 2022," the year eurozone inflation peaked near 10%. Her argument now is that renewed energy-supply disruption raises prices directly, but only becomes a monetary problem if it feeds through into wages and services via what she has called "indirect and second-round effects." Until that hands off, a mechanical hike-for-every-oil-spike reaction risks over-tightening into a slowing economy — the ECB's own September projections flagged weaker growth alongside the higher inflation path.

For a Eurogroup already nervous about bond-market volatility — Lagarde used a Bloomberg interview earlier in the week to urge "calm" amid the turmoil — the message was as much about anchoring expectations as about the rate itself. Finance ministers arrived in Dublin weighing whether the deteriorating situation in the Gulf warranted revising their own economic assessments; Lagarde's framing gives them, and traders, a rule of thumb: watch core and services inflation, not the oil tape, for the next move.

What's next

The ECB's next scheduled rate decision falls in late October, by which point another month of Hormuz shipping data and eurozone core-inflation readings will have landed. Markets currently price further tightening into 2027 rather than a near-term hike, a bet that hinges on the ceasefire holding and energy prices not lurching higher again — the same fragile assumption underpinning Lagarde's distinction between a shock and a trend.
