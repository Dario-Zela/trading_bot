Meta launches legal challenge against Ofcom's Online Safety Act classification

The Royal Courts of Justice in London, where Meta's fee dispute with Ofcom is set for a hearing on 13–14 October. — David Castor (Wikimedia Commons, public domain)
In one sentence
Meta has filed a second legal challenge against Ofcom this year, contesting the Category 1 tagging of WhatsApp and Instagram after already suing over how the regulator calculates fees.

Meta has gone back to court against the regulator that oversees Britain's internet, this time to contest the label that puts WhatsApp and Instagram in the most heavily regulated tier of the Online Safety Act. Lawyers for the company served notice of the appeal last week, its second legal action against Ofcom this year — and the clearest sign yet that the UK's flagship internet law is heading for a prolonged fight in the courts rather than a quiet rollout.

The classification in question is Category 1, the top rung of a system Ofcom uses to decide how hard it leans on a platform. Landing there brings extra transparency reporting, tighter rules on fraudulent advertising, more user controls, and fuller record-keeping on illegal content — obligations designed for the biggest, most consequential services, but ones Meta argues don't fit neatly onto WhatsApp, a largely private messaging app, in the way they might fit a public social feed.

Two fronts, one regulator

This is not Meta's first swing. In May, the company filed a separate judicial review at the High Court over how Ofcom calculates fees and fines under the Act — a dispute with genuinely large numbers attached. Ofcom can fine a company up to 10% of its "qualifying worldwide revenue," not just what it earns in the UK. Meta reported roughly $201 billion in global revenue last year, which puts its theoretical maximum penalty near $20 billion for a single breach — a figure Meta's lawyers call disproportionate and beyond what Parliament intended when it wrote the law. Mrs Justice Collins-Rice granted permission for that case to proceed in March, and it's due for a substantive hearing at the High Court on 13 and 14 October.

The Computer and Communications Industry Association, a US trade body whose members include Meta, Google and Amazon, has said it intends to apply to intervene in that fee case, calling it a matter with "wider potential impact on the sector." Epic Games — still smarting from its own long-running disputes with app-store gatekeepers — is expected to seek similar standing. On the other side, the 5Rights Foundation, a children's online safety charity, is lined up to back Ofcom's approach.

The new Category 1 appeal, filed through a separate tribunal process, adds a second track to the pressure campaign. Meta insists the two challenges aren't an attack on the legislation itself. "These are not challenges to the law itself, but how Ofcom has interpreted it," a company spokesperson said, adding that Meta is doing "like many others in the industry, including TikTok, Roblox, X and Quora" — a normal feature, in its telling, of a new regulatory regime settling into place.

A regulator under siege

That list of companies is doing some of the heavier lifting in this story. Roblox and Quora are separately contesting their own Category 1 designations. TikTok and X are suing over how much user data Ofcom can demand from them. Ofcom's own officials have acknowledged the strain: Oliver Griffiths, one of the regulator's directors, said the organisation is "operating in a highly litigious environment" and has to be careful about overreach — "if we run unnecessary risks, we are going to get absolutely clobbered legally by deep-pocketed individuals and firms."

That's a striking admission from inside a regulator that Parliament built with real teeth — the power to fine, and in extreme cases to have services blocked in the UK. It suggests Ofcom is now calibrating its enforcement not just against the letter of the Online Safety Act but against the risk of losing in court to companies with far deeper legal budgets than the regulator itself.

Why the tier matters

Category 1 was never meant to be a rubber stamp. It exists because Parliament decided that a handful of platforms — by reach, by risk, by influence over public discourse — warrant closer scrutiny than the rest of the internet. Knock WhatsApp out of that tier, or force Ofcom to redraw the line, and the practical effect is fewer services facing the toughest obligations, at exactly the moment the Act is meant to be biting down.

The fee case carries a more immediate financial edge. Ofcom has said it plans to send its first invoices to regulated firms this month, calculated under the worldwide-revenue formula Meta is now contesting. A ruling against the regulator wouldn't just cost Meta money it might otherwise pay — it would force Ofcom to redesign how it funds enforcement of the entire Act, potentially shifting costs onto UK taxpayers or smaller UK-based services instead of global platforms.

What unites both cases is the argument that Ofcom has stretched its statutory remit past what the legislation actually authorises — a technical-sounding claim with a very unglamorous but consequential payoff: if courts agree even partially, other platforms now sitting quietly inside Category 1 will have a template to follow, and the regulator's authority to set its own rules of enforcement gets narrower with each ruling against it.

What's next

The High Court hears the fee dispute on 13 and 14 October, with the Category 1 classification challenge expected to follow a separate tribunal timetable in the coming months. Ofcom has continued issuing first invoices under the current fee formula in the meantime, meaning money is already changing hands on rules that may not survive the autumn's court dates. Whichever way the October hearing lands, it will be read across the industry as a signal for how much room the UK's online safety regime actually has to enforce its own tiers.
