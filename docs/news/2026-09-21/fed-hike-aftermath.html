Fed's Warsh delivers quarter-point hike, two more priced in for 2026

Kevin Warsh, who became the 17th chair of the Federal Reserve in May 2026, delivered his first rate hike as chair on September 16. — Federal Reserve (official portrait, public domain)
In one sentence
The Fed hiked rates a quarter point to 3.75%-4.00%, its first increase since 2023, as new Chair Kevin Warsh called inflation 'too high for too long' and futures priced two more hikes this year.

The Federal Reserve raised interest rates on Wednesday for the first time since 2023, ending a three-year hold and putting new Chair Kevin Warsh's hawkish instincts on record less than four months into the job.

The Federal Open Market Committee voted unanimously to lift the benchmark federal funds rate by a quarter point to a range of 3.75%–4.00%. It is a small move in absolute terms — a single step after years of the Fed sitting still — but it reverses the direction of travel entirely, and it does so under a chair handpicked by a president who has spent months publicly demanding lower rates, not higher ones.

"Inflation has been too high for too long," Warsh told reporters after the decision, adding that policy is "not yet restrictive."

That second phrase is doing the real work. It tells markets the Fed does not regard 4% as a ceiling.

Three months, three data points

Warsh, who took the gavel in May after Jerome Powell's term ended, spent his first Jackson Hole speech in late August laying the groundwork for exactly this move, warning that inflation was "concerning" and that the central bank might have "work to do." By the September meeting, he pointed to three things that had shifted since the Fed's last gathering: a labor market that stayed resilient rather than cracking, a summer of inflation prints that ran hotter than the Fed's 2% target, and price pressure that officials increasingly tie to the ongoing fallout from the Iran conflict rather than a fading, transitory effect.

The unanimous vote — all twelve members, no dissents — is itself a signal. Rate decisions during regime shifts often split a committee between officials anchored to the old consensus and those pushing for change. This one didn't. The Fed's quarterly dot plot backs that up: 16 of 18 officials penciled in at least one further hike this year, with four expecting two.

What markets are now pricing

Fed funds futures moved quickly to reflect the harder line. Contracts tied to the December meeting were pricing the policy rate near 4.2% by the close on September 18 — consistent with two additional quarter-point hikes before year-end, on top of Wednesday's move. Odds of that full two-hike path sat near 37% in the days after Warsh's remarks, with the remaining probability split between one more hike and a pause.

For a bond market that had spent the better part of three years betting on cuts, that is a meaningful repricing. It also puts the Fed on a collision course with the White House: Warsh's decision came despite repeated public calls from President Trump and administration officials for the opposite, a tension that has already drawn comparisons to past fights over central bank independence.

What it means for positioning

A hiking Fed under a chair still building his inflation-fighting credibility is a different regime than a Fed on hold waiting to cut. Higher-for-longer assumptions embedded in equity valuations, carry trades, and duration positioning now have official confirmation rather than just speculation. Algorithmic strategies built around a 2026 easing cycle are the ones most exposed to repricing; those already leaning toward higher real rates and dollar strength gained validation on Wednesday.

The next test comes at the Fed's late-October and December meetings, where the dot plot's implied path will either be confirmed or walked back depending on how September and October inflation data land. Until then, futures markets — not FOMC statements — are the fastest read on whether Warsh's hawkish debut holds.
