US extends pause on China shipbuilding probe to January 9, 2027

In one sentence
USTR has proposed extending its pause on China-linked port fees to January 9, 2027, two months past the November 9 expiry, a short-dated extension that leaves the truce unresolved.

The United States is putting off, for a second time, the port fees it designed to punish China's dominance of global shipbuilding. The Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) has proposed extending the suspension of its Section 301 action on China's maritime, logistics and shipbuilding sectors through January 9, 2027. That is two months beyond the current expiry on November 9, 2026.

The proposal is not yet final. It appears in a Federal Register notice scheduled for publication on October 26, which asks for public comment on a pause running from November 10, 2026 to January 9, 2027. Comments are due by 5 p.m. ET on October 30 under docket USTR-2026-0694. One trade outlet reported the notice as issued on October 23, but the Federal Register text describes it as a request for comments, so the formal extension has yet to be adopted.

How the pause got here

The fees took effect on October 14, 2025, under a Section 301 action that grew out of a 2024 petition from the United Steelworkers and other unions. They were suspended on November 10, 2025, for a year, as part of the trade deal Presidents Trump and Xi struck in South Korea. They were therefore in force for less than four weeks. In that window Matson reported about $6.4m in charges, while COSCO, China's largest state-owned carrier, faced estimated annual US charges of around $1.5bn.

Beijing mirrored the arrangement. It had introduced its own special port fees on vessels with a US connection on October 10, 2025, and suspended them in the same truce. Whether China will extend its side of the pause has not been confirmed in the reporting reviewed.

Treasury announced it first

The sequence is unusual. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on September 23 that the two countries had agreed to extend the Busan agreement from November 10, 2026 to January 10, 2027, after meeting Vice Premier He Lifeng. A Treasury announcement does not change a USTR fee schedule, though. Only a new USTR notice can, and trade press noted in late September that the fees would revert on November 10 unless one was issued. The proposed notice closes that gap, and it sets the end date a day earlier than Bessent's. The pause would also continue to cover certain duties on ship-to-shore cranes and cargo-handling equipment.

USTR says it is "continuing to engage with China with the goal of obtaining the elimination" of the practices at issue. That is the language of a negotiation that has not finished.

Who wants what

Shipping lines and more than 200 trade groups asked for an extension, warning that reinstated fees would raise costs and strain supply chains. Others want the opposite. Senators Mark Kelly and Elizabeth Warren have written that "Section 301 port fees are critical to revitalizing U.S. shipbuilding." Some maritime unions also want the fees reinstated.

The two-month length points to how provisional the truce is. A full-year extension would suggest a settled deal. A stub period tied to a Treasury-level date suggests both sides prefer to keep talking without committing to terms. Bessent himself said Washington wants a broader agreement with Beijing, not a string of smaller ones. The shipping fees are one of the few levers the US holds that bear directly on China's industrial strategy, and they are being held in reserve.

What to watch

The comment window closes October 30, and USTR must then publish a final notice before the current pause lapses on November 9. If it does, carriers and importers can price freight without the fees through the first week of January. The next test comes then, either another extension or a broader deal with Beijing. Shipping advisers such as NorthStandard are telling clients to keep budgeting for the charges and to allocate liability for them in charter contracts.
