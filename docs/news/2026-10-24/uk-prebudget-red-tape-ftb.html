Pre-Budget UK pledges: up to £800m of red-tape savings and a £140K cap on first-time buyer loans

In one sentence
The Treasury says its red-tape package will save firms up to £800m, though the figure is an unaudited estimate that includes tariff relief, while reported first-time buyer caps of £110,000 and £140,000 await Budget confirmation.

The Treasury has put a number on the cost of paperwork. Five days before the Budget, John Healey, in the run-up to his first as Chancellor, said a package of reporting and regulatory changes would save businesses about £800m. The figure is a Treasury estimate. No independent costing or timetable has been published, and the headline sum is not all red tape. The same week, reports said the government's first-time buyer scheme would carry a household income cap of up to £140,000. That figure is a London ceiling, and it has not been confirmed.

The two items are unrelated in substance. Both are pre-Budget positioning, and both are open to the same test: how much of the number survives contact with the detail.

What the £800m is made of

The Treasury's own arithmetic, as reported by the Press Association, shows how the total is built. The largest single item is simpler company reporting, put at £290m. Easing duplicate reporting for asset managers is put at £105m. Cutting red tape in home buying and selling is put at £116m, a saving that accrues to firms in the transaction chain. Those three come to £511m. Reports describe the administrative element as more than £600m a year.

The remainder is not paperwork at all. Up to £155m comes from extending suspended import tariffs on more than 250 products, covering chemicals, plastics, automotive and food and drink. The suspension was due to lapse in December and is being extended by a year. A tariff suspension is a tax measure. Counting it beside form-filling lifts the total, and it carries an "up to" qualifier of its own.

The package sits under a wider commitment to cut the administrative cost of regulation by 25% by the end of this Parliament. Further measures are due in the Budget on 28 October. Healey said firms were tired of form-filling and unnecessary paperwork. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the aim was "getting government off their backs".

Reaction: welcome, with a warning

The British Chambers of Commerce welcomed the direction. Kate Shoesmith, its director of policy, backed clearer guidance, simpler reporting and fewer duplicate requests, and supported the tariff extension. She also set the saving against the chamber's own measure of cost growth. Its Policy Cost Stack estimates that a representative 50-employee SME faces about £827,000 more in annual policy-driven costs than in 2016, a rise of over 70%. On that basis the headline saving roughly matches the extra annual cost borne by a single mid-sized firm. Shoesmith's warning was that the government must not add new business costs while it removes old ones.

The Conservatives took the opposite line. Shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith said Labour had admitted that red tape was hurting business, and pointed to its 330-page Unemployment Act as a source of new burdens.

The fiscal backdrop limits how generous the Budget can be. Borrowing came in at £24.5bn last month, above forecasts. Healey is expected to raise taxes on banks and has ruled out a rise in capital gains tax, citing growth. A saving that is mostly estimated, and partly a tariff pause, sits alongside tax rises that are not.

The housing end of the Budget

The other pre-Budget leak concerns Your First Home, the scheme under which the state would provide a 20% interest-free equity loan on new-build homes, leaving buyers to find a 2.5% deposit and a lender to cover the rest. Developers are expected to contribute, but the size of their contribution and the length of the interest-free period have not been confirmed.

The income cap has moved. Ministers were reported to have considered £90,000 outside London and £110,000 in the capital. The Times reported the levels would rise to £110,000 outside London and £140,000 in London, on the view that the lower caps excluded too many middle-earning couples. Teachers and nurses were cited as examples. A financial adviser, Samuel Mather-Holgate, described the lower threshold as "cliff-edge policy": two public-sector salaries could put a couple over the line while still leaving a home out of reach.

The cap decides the scheme's reach. A higher ceiling widens the pool of buyers for participating housebuilders and for lenders holding the remaining 77.5% of the purchase price. No figures for their exposure have been published, and none of the thresholds has been confirmed.

What to watch on 28 October

The Budget documents should settle three things. The first is whether the Treasury publishes a line-by-line costing of the £800m, split between administrative savings and the tariff measure, and whether the Office for Budget Responsibility endorses it. The second is which further regulatory measures are named, since the three itemised ones account for under two-thirds of the headline. The third is whether the home-buyer income caps are confirmed at £110,000 and £140,000, and what developers are asked to pay. Both the savings claim and the scheme's reach are, for now, a ceiling rather than an outcome.
