Arrest after fire at Hitler's Eagle's Nest retreat

In one sentence
A fire destroyed much of the Eagle's Nest above Berchtesgaden on Friday night, and police provisionally arrested one person while arson remains unconfirmed.

The Kehlsteinhaus, the mountain-top lodge above Berchtesgaden that English-speaking visitors know as the Eagle's Nest, caught fire on Friday evening. Police have provisionally arrested one person. They have not said whether that person started the fire.

The alarm was raised at about 21:15. By the time the first crews reached the summit at 21:52, German reports say, the whole building was already burning. Fire brigades from six municipalities attended, along with mountain rescue, the Bavarian Red Cross and police, about 200 people in all. They were still working on Saturday morning.

A hard place to fight a fire

The site made the job harder. The house stands on a ridge at the top of a narrow mountain road, and heavy fire engines struggled to get up it. Crews had to set up an extra water supply. No injuries have been reported, and rescuers searched the building as a precaution. As far as is known, nobody was inside.

How much of the building survives is not yet known. Officials have not said which parts of the historic structure can be saved.

What police have said

Police in Upper Bavaria confirmed that they detained one person shortly after the fire began, on a provisional basis, and gave no further detail. The newspaper Bild reports that the person is a motorcyclist who was heading towards the building as rescuers approached. Bild also quotes a police spokesperson as saying arson is suspected. The Traunstein criminal police are leading the inquiry under the prosecutor's office.

The reports are careful on one point. Whether the person in custody caused the fire has not been established, and no cause has been confirmed. A provisional arrest is a holding measure, and it can be followed by a charge or by a release.

The building

The Kehlsteinhaus was finished in 1938 as a gift to Adolf Hitler for his 50th birthday and was used for Nazi Party receptions. He is recorded as visiting only a handful of times. It survived the war and has run as a mountain restaurant and tourist stop since 1952. Visitors ride a dedicated bus up the mountain road, then a brass-lined lift through the rock, and the season normally runs from May to late October. The building is owned by the Free State of Bavaria.

It has long sat awkwardly between two uses, as a scenic summit café and as one of the best-known surviving buildings of the regime. That tension will matter if the police inquiry finds the fire was deliberate and points to a motive. At the moment there is nothing to say about motive.

What to watch

The next developments are whether prosecutors charge or release the detained person, what the fire investigators conclude about the cause, and what a structural survey finds once the site is safe to enter. Bavaria will then have to decide whether and how to repair a building it owns.
