Labour sets Green councillors a deadline to disown Zionism policy

In one sentence
Labour has given Green councillors in about 18 councils seven days to reject a conference motion declaring "Zionism is racism", or lose their power-sharing deals.

Labour has told Green councillors across England and Wales that local power-sharing deals will end unless they publicly reject a motion passed at the Green Party conference this month declaring that "Zionism is racism". The demand has turned a national row over one party's policy into a test of whether local administrations can survive a dispute that has little to do with bin collections or planning.

The ultimatum

Party chair Bridget Phillipson wrote to Labour council groups that govern jointly with the Greens, asking them to seek reassurance that their Green partners condemn the motion. Reports of the letter, dated around 15 October, say the Greens were given seven days to respond. If they did not, Labour said it would end its arrangements.

Reports differ on how many councils are affected. Labour's own figure, as reported, is 17 in England and one in Wales. Other outlets have given 18 or 19. The seven-day window would have closed around 22 October. The reports reviewed do not say whether any Green group has complied, or whether any coalition has yet been broken up, so that outcome remains unconfirmed.

Sheffield as the test case

Sheffield is the most closely watched example. Labour has 26 councillors there and the Greens 19, and the two run the council jointly. Labour's group leader, Fran Belbin, wrote to her Green counterpart, Angela Argenzio, urging a public rejection of the motion "in order for our joint administration to continue".

Argenzio has said local Green councillors do not conflate Zionism with Judaism, nor anti-Zionism with antisemitism. She also warned: "If Labour chooses to destabilise Sheffield City Council over this, Labour owns the consequences." The Greens argue that it is undemocratic for one party to tell local parties what to do and whom they can work with.

A national dispute pushed down to town halls

The motion has already drawn fire at the top of politics. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has called it antisemitic, and the equalities watchdog has written to the Green Party to examine the policy's impact. Green leader Zack Polanski abstained in the conference vote, which gives local Greens an obvious line to take: that they did not write the policy and are not bound to defend it.

That is what makes the ultimatum awkward for both sides. Labour is asking councillors, who in most cases had no hand in the motion, to disown it on the record. Refusal risks the loss of working administrations. Compliance would put Green councillors at odds with their own conference, in a party that has recently prided itself on member-led policy.

The Liberal Democrats, who also share power with Greens in some places, said it was for their local councillors to decide whether to end coalitions. That leaves Labour as the party using the issue most forcefully as a lever.

What to watch

The first signal is whether Green groups issue statements distancing themselves from the motion, and whether Labour groups carry out the threat or quietly extend the timetable. Sheffield, where the margin between the parties is narrow, is likely to show first. How the Greens handle the equalities watchdog's inquiry will also shape how far local groups feel able to separate themselves from the conference decision.
