Telecom ETF's record run of lagging the S&P 500 is pinned on SpaceX

In one sentence
A small telecom ETF is on a record run of trailing the S&P 500, and one outlet blames SpaceX, but no subscriber, pricing or filing evidence has been shown for that link.

A small exchange-traded fund that tracks American telecom companies has lagged the S&P 500 for longer than at any point in its history, according to Benzinga. The outlet's explanation is that SpaceX is hammering telecom stocks. The streak is a matter of price data. The explanation is an inference, and the report as summarised offers no evidence for it.

The streak

The fund is IYZ, the iShares U.S. Telecommunications ETF. Benzinga describes its current run of underperformance relative to the S&P 500 as its longest on record. The length of the streak and its start date were not available in the material this desk reviewed, and neither could be checked against price history. Neither is stated here.

The context makes a long streak unsurprising. The S&P 500 has been near a record high, but the gains have been narrow: 60% of its stocks sit in a bear market. An index lifted by a small group of very large companies is a hard benchmark for a defensive, income-oriented sector. A telecom fund can trail it for a long time without any single villain.

The SpaceX claim

The causal story is that satellite connectivity, led by SpaceX's Starlink, is eroding the position of incumbent carriers. The mechanism is plausible. Starlink sells broadband to homes that terrestrial networks reach poorly. Its direct-to-cell service, offered in partnership with T-Mobile, connects ordinary handsets to satellites. SpaceX has also agreed to buy wireless spectrum from EchoStar, which suggests it intends to compete in mobile on its own account.

Plausibility is not evidence, though. For the claim to hold, three things would need to show up in the data:

  • Subscriber losses at the large carriers in rural or fixed-wireless markets, where satellite broadband competes most directly.
  • Pressure on pricing or average revenue per user, which would appear in quarterly results.
  • Analyst or management commentary in filings that names satellite providers as a cause rather than a risk factor.

The Benzinga report, as summarised for this desk, cites none of these. It also leaves open the usual rival explanations for a telecom fund's weakness: interest-rate moves, heavy capital spending on networks, debt loads, and the pull of technology stocks on the benchmark. Those would hurt telecoms whether or not a single satellite was launched.

The fund's narrowness matters too. IYZ is a modest vehicle holding a limited number of names. A handful of company-specific events can drive its relative return. The record belongs to this fund. It does not by itself describe the sector.

What would settle it

The test is in the carriers' own numbers. The coming round of quarterly results from the big American wireless and cable companies will show whether net subscriber additions, churn and pricing are moving in the way the satellite thesis predicts. If management teams start naming satellite competition in their explanations, the market's story will have found its evidence. If they point to other factors, the streak is more likely a story about the S&P 500's concentration than about a rocket company.

Until then, the fund's record run is a fact, and SpaceX is a hypothesis about its cause.
