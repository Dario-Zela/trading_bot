Texas sues OpenAI and Altman over alleged harms to users

In one sentence
Texas AG Ken Paxton has sued OpenAI and Sam Altman personally, alleging ChatGPT misled users and harmed them, in a test of state consumer-protection law against AI makers.

Texas has taken OpenAI to court, and it has named the company's chief executive as a defendant. Attorney General Ken Paxton filed suit on Friday, 23 October, in Collin County district court. It accuses OpenAI and Sam Altman of misleading Texans about ChatGPT and of harming them, children above all. The case tests whether a state can use ordinary consumer-protection law against a generative-AI maker, and whether it can reach the person running the company as well as the company.

What Texas alleges

The complaint opens with a 2015 remark by Altman: "AI will probably most likely lead to the end of the world, but in the meantime, there'll be great companies." It goes on to say that OpenAI did not adequately warn users about ChatGPT's risks or about its accuracy. It also says the company knew of the dangers and pressed on in the race to dominate AI.

Paxton's office lists a series of harms. They include mass shooters being aided in deadly rampages, vulnerable people being steered toward suicide, professionals suffering public humiliation, and users losing critical-thinking skills. The office also says minors have become addicted to a tool that "fakes human compassion to collect their data with no parental oversight."

Press reports say the claims rest on the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices–Consumer Protection Act. They also cite the state's SCOPE Act, a children's online safety law, with one report saying that claim centres on missing age verification. This article is based on the press release and news coverage. The complaint itself has not been reviewed, so the detail of each claim and the evidence behind the headline harms are not yet known.

Why Altman is named

The suit seeks to hold Altman personally liable for alleged violations and for statements he made about OpenAI's products. Corporate officers are usually shielded from liability for what their companies do. Consumer-protection suits are a common exception when an executive directs or takes part in the conduct, and Texas appears to be relying on that route. Whether a court will treat Altman's public statements about safety as the kind of conduct that creates personal liability is the most novel question in the case.

The state wants a broad injunction requiring OpenAI to block minors from ChatGPT. It also asks for court-appointed, third-party evaluator approval of any new models. One report puts the monetary relief sought at more than $1 million, a modest figure beside the injunction request. If the court granted the injunction, a judge would in effect be setting conditions for releasing AI models.

A pattern, and a politician

The suit fits a pattern. In the past year Paxton has sued or settled with Meta, TikTok, Snapchat and Roblox under state consumer-protection laws, arguing their safeguards for children were inadequate. Those cases used familiar legal tools against social platforms. Applying them to a chatbot that generates its own answers is less settled, because it is unclear whether ChatGPT's output is the company's own conduct or something closer to a user's.

The politics are plain. Paxton is running for the US Senate, and the Texas Tribune describes the suit as the latest in a run of actions against large companies. The Tribune also notes that Elon Musk, who co-founded OpenAI and left in 2018 after a dispute with Altman, is the largest single donor to Paxton's campaign, having given nearly $200 million. Musk later founded the rival xAI. His own 2024 suit against OpenAI and Altman, seeking $1.5 billion, was dismissed by a federal judge. Nothing in the reporting shows Musk had any role in the Texas filing, but the overlap is the kind a defence team will raise. The state's outside counsel includes Ashley Keller and Tony Buzbee, who represented Paxton in his 2023 impeachment proceedings.

OpenAI's response

An OpenAI spokesperson said the company has worked with experts to reduce risks and add safeguards. The spokesperson added: "Governments have an important role to play in setting robust safety standards for AI." OpenAI said it is willing to work with Texas and other states on policy that applies across the industry. The company has not yet responded to the legal claims in court.

What to watch

The first signal will be OpenAI's response in Collin County. A motion to dismiss that attacks the claim against Altman personally, or moves the case to federal court, would show where the company thinks the weak points are. Beyond that, other state attorneys general are pursuing OpenAI too. If courts accept that a chatbot's outputs and its makers' safety claims fall under existing deceptive-practices law, states can regulate AI through enforcement without waiting for Congress. If they reject it, the pressure shifts back to legislators.
