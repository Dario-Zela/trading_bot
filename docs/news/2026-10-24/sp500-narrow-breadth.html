S&P 500 near a record while 60% of stocks sit in a bear market

In one sentence
The S&P 500 sits near a record while about 60% of its stocks are in a bear market, and Michigan sentiment, at 48.0 (est. 46.3), shows households remain gloomy.

The S&P 500 is close to a record high. Most of the stocks inside it are not. According to Benzinga, about 60% of the index's constituents are in a bear market, a state that is usually shorthand for a fall of 20% or more from a recent peak. The report does not give the threshold it used or the date of the measurement, so the 60% figure is best read as a rough guide to the proportions and not as a precise count.

The two facts fit together arithmetically. The S&P 500 is weighted by market value, so a small number of very large companies can lift the index while a majority of its members fall. The index level reports on the former. A headline near a record says little about the latter.

A level that hides a split

Breadth, meaning how many stocks take part in a move, is the usual check on an index rally. When most stocks rise with the index, the rally is broad and the headline level is a fair summary of the market. When a minority does the lifting, the same level describes a much narrower event.

The Benzinga figure points to the second case. If roughly three in five stocks are down 20% or more while the index sits near its high, the stocks that are rising must have gained enough, and be large enough, to cover the losses of the rest. The triage material does not name those stocks or quantify their share of the index, so the concentration is an inference from the arithmetic. It is not a documented list.

The practical consequence concerns risk. An investor who holds a broad basket of stocks, or who judges the market's health by the index headline, may see a calm picture. The experience underneath can be very different for the median stock. Index funds that track the S&P 500 are themselves weighted toward the biggest names, so they carry the narrowness inside them. The index's own level is a poor gauge of how widely the market is holding up.

A related story in this edition, on the telecom ETF's record run of lagging the S&P 500, shows another way a handful of names can set the index's direction while a sector fund trails.

Households are not celebrating

The other data point of the day points the same way. The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment gauge rose to 48.0, against a consensus estimate of 46.3. That is a beat, and an improvement on the prior reading.

It is also a low number in absolute terms. A reading of 48.0 is not a recovery; it is a smaller degree of gloom. Consumers are still reporting far less confidence than the index level alone would suggest. That gap is consistent with the breadth picture: if most stocks are in bear markets, the wealth effect of a record index is likely to be felt by a narrower slice of households than the headline implies. The data do not prove that link, since sentiment responds to prices, jobs and politics as well as to share prices. But the two readings point in the same direction.

The foreign precedent

The split also raises the old question of what, if anything, policymakers do when a broad swathe of the market is falling. One much-cited answer comes from China, where the state has repeatedly stepped in as a buyer. Prior coverage records that Central Huijin, a unit of China's sovereign investment vehicle, bought exchange-traded funds and blue-chip stocks as the so-called national team during the July 2015 crash. In September 2024, the central bank and financial regulators announced support measures, including state-linked ETF purchases, after a prolonged equity slump.

These are background points from earlier reporting and were not re-verified for this article. The Benzinga item that prompted the comparison could not be checked, and no current figures on ETF flows or specific Beijing actions are confirmed. What can be said is structural. China's tactic relies on a state-directed buyer with the ability to deploy capital on instruction. The United States has no equivalent. Its closest counterparts, the Federal Reserve and the Treasury, work through rates, liquidity and credit and not through buying equity ETFs. Whether the tactic could transfer is therefore as much a legal and institutional question as a market one. It is also a different problem. Beijing's interventions answered broad routs, while the American split is a narrow index high on top of a broad decline.

What to watch

The test of the divergence is whether it closes, and the direction it closes in matters. Either breadth improves, with the laggards catching up, or the leaders give way and the index converges downward on the majority. The next Michigan sentiment release will show whether the move to 48.0 was the start of a trend or a one-month wobble above a very low base. In the market itself, the measures worth following are the share of S&P 500 members trading above their 200-day moving averages and the performance of the equal-weighted index relative to the cap-weighted one. Both would register a change in breadth before the headline level did.
