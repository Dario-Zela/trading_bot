Storm Austen cuts power to thousands in the south-west as Burnham warns of a 'winter of greater flooding'

In one sentence
Storm Austen has cut power to thousands in the south-west while Andy Burnham warns of a 'winter of greater flooding', but no official forecast has yet been cited to back the claim.

Storm Austen has left thousands of homes in south-west England without power, and the political reaction has been quicker than the technical detail. Andy Burnham has warned of a "winter of greater flooding" and said he has stockpiled tinned food and bottled water. The remark has put a plain question to ministers and local authorities: are they preparing the public as seriously as some officials say they are preparing themselves?

The evidence behind that question is thin, and the facts below mark where it runs out.

What is known

The Guardian reports that Storm Austen caused widespread power outages across the south-west, attributing them to strong winds and rain. The reporting available for this article gives no household count beyond "thousands". It also gives no peak gust speeds, no restoration times, and no statement from the network operators. Those figures would show whether this was a routine autumn disruption or something more serious. Storms of this kind regularly bring down lines across the region, and on the information so far Austen fits that pattern.

Burnham's remarks come from reporting by the Guardian and BBC Politics. The headline wording is "winter of greater flooding", and the stockpile is described as including tinned food and bottled water. The exact quote, the setting, and the quantities and timing of the stockpiling have not been independently checked here. They should be read as reported, not as established.

What is not known

The central gap is the forecast basis. A warning that this winter will bring more flooding than usual carries weight if it rests on Environment Agency or Met Office outlooks. It carries much less if it is a politician's judgment of the mood. The material available contains no flood-risk figures from either body, no seasonal outlook, and no government response. Nor is there any sign of a flood-defence failure linked to Austen.

That shapes how the story should be read. For now it is a political story: a prominent figure has chosen to talk publicly about household resilience. It becomes a preparedness story only if the claim is matched by official forecasts and by evidence about the state of defences, warning systems and grid resilience.

Why the stockpile matters

The detail of the tinned food gives the warning its force. Official advice in the UK has long encouraged households to keep basic supplies for emergencies. A senior politician saying they have done so turns that advice into a personal example. It also invites the follow-up that critics will press: if the risk is serious enough to act on privately, what is being done publicly, and who is paying for it?

Storm Austen supplies the immediate test of that argument. Power cuts and flooding are linked in practice, because pumping stations, communications and care services all depend on electricity. How quickly supplies are restored, and whether any flooding follows, will say more about resilience than any one politician's pantry.

What to watch

Three things would settle the matter. First, network operators' final figures on customers affected and the time taken to reconnect them. Second, any Environment Agency or Met Office outlook for the winter, which would show whether the "greater flooding" claim has official backing. Third, a response from ministers on flood-defence funding and emergency planning. Until those arrive, the warning remains a claim to be tested, not a forecast to be relied on.
