Ofcom admits online safety regime has not yet protected children

In one sentence
Ofcom's own data show no significant fall in children meeting harmful content since the UK's child-safety duties began, and it says the Online Safety Act may need changing.

Ofcom, the body charged with policing the UK's Online Safety Act, has said in its own data that the law has not yet made children's online lives measurably safer. Its first report on harmful content, published on 22 October, found "no significant change in the proportion of children encountering harmful content over the first nine months after the children's safety duties came into force in July 2025". The Guardian led its coverage with the admission. The debate has moved from whether platforms are complying to whether the approach works.

What the report found

Ofcom's survey research put the share of 11- to 17-year-olds who saw harmful content in a four-week period at 73%. The regulator called the finding concerning. It also said it had seen no new evidence that the severity of harm to children had changed.

Two caveats apply. The data cover only the first nine months of the duties. Ofcom said they do not capture the period after it stepped up pressure on Facebook, YouTube, Snap, Roblox and TikTok. And this is survey evidence of what children report seeing, not a count of enforcement actions or removed content, so a flat line does not show that nothing has changed behind the scenes.

The report's one policy recommendation was narrow. Ofcom advised adding body-stigma and depression content to the Act's priority categories of harmful content, and suggested no other changes. It would be for the Secretary of State to adopt that.

A regulator that was already hedging

The report did not come out of nowhere. In September, Oliver Griffiths, Ofcom's group director for online safety, told the House of Lords Communications and Digital Committee that the regulator was "underwhelmed" by the overall effect so far. Mark Bunting, its director of strategy delivery, said there was "a long way to go".

Ofcom's account of what has worked is specific and limited:

  • All of the ten largest pornography services now have age gates.
  • Three-quarters of the top 100 services have either introduced age checks or blocked UK users.
  • Age checks rose 23-fold across a sample of 32 companies.
  • Progress has been slower on the big services children actually use. Ofcom has won commitments from X on removing hate and terrorist content, and from Meta and Snap on grooming risks. It is investigating whether TikTok's age-inference technology missed a "significant proportion" of children. TikTok says it is "confident" it meets its obligations.

Bunting also pointed to a design problem. Platforms can add child protections without having effective ways to identify which users are children. The Act's "safe harbour" can deem a company compliant if it follows Ofcom's codes, even where identified risks remain inadequately addressed. Ofcom suggested the legislation itself may need changing.

Resources, sequencing and the under-16 ban

The regulator's room to act is also constrained. The Act passed in 2023, and the duties for the largest "categorised services" are not expected to be fully in force until around mid-2027. Ofcom plans to publish a revised roadmap by the end of 2026.

Meanwhile, the government announced in June that it plans to ban under-16s from major social media platforms from next year. Ofcom has moved staff onto implementing that ban, and has asked ministers for more resources in a business plan submitted in July. Griffiths said delivering both the Act and the ban is manageable if properly resourced and sequenced, but warned that without extra staff and funding "something will have to give".

The Children's Commissioner for England, Dame Rachel de Souza, told the same inquiry that children felt the Act had made "absolutely no difference". She was reportedly furious that Ofcom had not shared companies' risk assessments with her office. Griffiths said Ofcom had received no formal request and that existing law requires a company's consent before such material goes to a third party. That is a small dispute, but it shows how little outside scrutiny the system allows.

Critics on the free-speech side read the same evidence differently. They argue the Act has age-gated a wide range of content without improving child safety, and that the regulator is seeking to broaden it anyway.

What to watch

Ofcom has the powers to act. It can fine companies up to £18m or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is higher, and can ultimately seek court orders blocking persistent offenders in Britain. The question now is whether it uses them against the largest services, where the exposure data suggest little has shifted.

Three markers will show which way this goes. The first is the revised roadmap due by the end of the year. The second is the outcome of the TikTok age-inference investigation. The third is the Lords committee's inquiry, which could produce the first formal case for amending the Act, including its safe-harbour provision. Other governments looking to the UK as a model for child-safety rules will be watching how Ofcom answers its own numbers.
