Serbia's election too close to call between Vučić and the student movement

In one sentence
Serbia votes on 25 October in a parliamentary election that both Vučić's party and the student-led list say they can win, with no reliable polling and fairness concerns.

Serbia votes on Sunday in an early parliamentary election that pits the ruling Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) against a student-led list born of almost two years of street protest. The contest is being described as too close to call, though no result exists yet and published polls disagree. Two corrections to the framing are needed. This is a parliamentary vote, not a presidential one. And Aleksandar Vučić is no longer president. He resigned on 27 September so that he can be appointed prime minister if his party forms the next government.

The question is whether a movement that began as grief and anger can win under rules it does not control.

How the vote came about

The protests trace to November 2024, when a concrete canopy collapsed at the railway station in Novi Sad and killed 16 people. Students blamed corruption and negligent oversight of public works. They occupied university buildings and held rallies that grew into a broad civic movement. It has no formal leader, and that has been both a strength and a puzzle for conventional politics.

That movement has now put forward its own ballot list, the Students' List. Its supporters say they want to turn street pressure into votes. "Not everything is going to be great after Sunday, but it will be much, much better," supporter Lidija Miladinović told the Associated Press.

Vučić has governed for 14 years. His second presidential term would have ended in May 2027, and he is barred from a third. Moving to the premiership lets him keep power, and reporting describes the resignation as a planned manoeuvre. It also triggers an early presidential election, in which a candidate close to him is widely expected to do well, according to one pre-election analysis. He has also allied with the Serbian Radical Party, led by Vojislav Šešelj, a convicted war criminal. Vučić has cited Vladimir Putin's stated hope that he becomes prime minister: "It is no coincidence that Vladimir Putin said he hopes that I will be the prime minister."

What "too close" means

About 6.4 million people are eligible to vote for 250 seats, and a list needs 3% to enter parliament. Observers expect high turnout and expect most smaller parties to fall below the threshold. That matters for the arithmetic. Votes for failed minor lists are wasted, and they could decide whether the SNS bloc or the students come out ahead in seats.

The AP reports that polls have shown varied results and that both camps claim to be ahead. No reliable shares are available, so "neck-and-neck" rests on that disagreement and on the fear the government clearly feels. It is not a measured dead heat.

The playing field is the other uncertainty. The monitoring group CRTA has cited a "narrowing of freedoms and deepening of inequality" and more than 100 violent incidents during the campaign. Reporting also points to government influence over much of the media, and to allegations of intimidation and surveillance. The 2023 election was followed by protests alleging fraud. The EU and international monitors have raised concerns about the safety and fairness of this vote.

Analyst Aleksandra Krstić was blunt about the downside scenario. "I do not see a possibility that he (Vucic) would concede defeat," she told the AP.

What is at stake

Serbia is an EU candidate country, and Brussels has criticised democratic backsliding under Vučić. A narrow student win, or a narrow SNS win under contested conditions, would both bring that file back into focus. The first would test whether an incumbent will hand over power. The second would test how loudly the EU is prepared to question a result.

The immediate signals are the turnout figures and preliminary results on Sunday night, followed by the monitors' verdict. Then comes the harder part. With 250 seats and a 3% threshold, one side may still need partners to govern. If the margin is thin, coalition talks and any challenges to the count could take weeks to settle.
