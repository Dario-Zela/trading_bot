Briton faces separation from baby son as Sweden orders deportation

In one sentence
A British photographer faces deportation from Sweden and separation from his 16-month-old son, a German citizen, and is appealing to the higher migration court.

A British photographer who moved to Sweden after Brexit has been ordered to leave the country, a decision that would separate him from his 16-month-old son. Simon Montgomery is appealing to Sweden's higher migration court. His case turns on a narrow question: whether a father who shares custody, but whose child sleeps mostly at the mother's home, is providing "actual care" under EU free-movement rules.

The case

Montgomery's son is a German citizen through his mother, Montgomery's former partner. The couple separated soon after the birth in 2025. They have a legally recognised joint-custody arrangement, agreed through the municipal family law service. Montgomery says he sees the boy three days a week. He expects that time to grow as the child stops needing night-time breastfeeding, and he says overnight stays are about to begin.

Montgomery arrived after Brexit, so he cannot rely on the withdrawal agreement's residence protections for Britons who were already living in the EU. He applied instead to stay as the father of an EU citizen, using the free-movement rights that follow from his son's nationality. He filed the application when the boy was six months old.

The Swedish Migration Agency rejected it in December 2025. It found no evidence of "actual care", because the boy still stayed overnight with his mother. It also said Montgomery could keep seeing his son as a visa-free visitor from abroad.

The migration court upheld both the refusal and the deportation order this month. It said the state's interest in regulated immigration outweighed his interest in protecting his private and family life. The ruling rested mainly on his failure to show that he met the conditions for residence as the family member of an EEA citizen.

The arguments on appeal

Montgomery's lawyer says the lower court read "actual care" too narrowly. The boy lives mainly with his mother, but the lawyer argues that is a feature of a child's first years, not a measure of the father's involvement. Forcing Montgomery to leave would destabilise a child in a critical attachment phase, the lawyer says. The lawyer adds that the mother has no family network to call on when she is ill or has appointments. The appeal also invokes Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protects family life. It raises the child's rights under EU law and international conventions as well.

Montgomery has said of the visitor suggestion that, because he can enter visa-free, authorities say it "shouldn't interrupt my parenting ability". He rejects that. "I am not here because I want to be in Sweden," he has said. "I am only in Sweden to be with my son." He also notes that "everything in Sweden is about the children's right to both parents".

The order has a practical cost beyond the legal one. His grandmother is 98 and lives in the UK, but he fears that if he leaves he will be blocked from returning to his son. He described the choice between parenting and visiting her as "almost like torture in some ways".

What to watch

The case is individual, and nothing in the reporting shows a change in Swedish policy. Its interest lies in the gap it exposes. A child's EU citizenship gives a non-EU parent a route to residence, but only if that parent can show hands-on care, and a shared-custody arrangement with a baby who still sleeps mostly at the mother's home may not meet the test. The higher migration court's decision will show whether it reads "actual care" strictly or weighs the child's interests more heavily. The Swedish Migration Agency had not responded to requests for comment at the time of reporting, and the German government said it could not comment on the case.
