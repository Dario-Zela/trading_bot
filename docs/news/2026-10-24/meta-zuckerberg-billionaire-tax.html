Meta agrees to help Zuckerberg cover a possible California billionaire tax

In one sentence
Meta gave Zuckerberg non-voting shares he can sell, including to pay a possible California wealth tax, in return for an eight-year residency pledge; the tax vote is Nov. 3.

Meta has arranged for Mark Zuckerberg to be able to raise cash against a possible California billionaire tax. The arrangement leaves the company's own balance sheet largely untouched, and what it gives up is control.

The terms, set out in regulatory filings and reported by the BBC, Reuters and the Associated Press, are narrower than the headline suggests. Meta's board agreed to create a new class of non-voting shares. Zuckerberg, who keeps majority ownership, can sell them "for any reason", including paying a wealth-tax bill. The sellable stock represents 10% of his overall economic stake in Meta. In exchange he commits to remain a California resident for eight years, unless "material unforeseen circumstances" intervene.

What is on the ballot

The measure, which voters decide on Nov. 3, would impose a one-time 5% tax on the net worth of residents worth more than $1 billion. It applies to anyone whose primary residence was in California on Jan. 1, 2026, so moving after that date does not obviously avoid it. Most of the proceeds would backfill federal healthcare cuts. Forbes puts Zuckerberg's net worth above $220 billion, which implies a liability above $11 billion.

Zuckerberg had told the board he was considering selling his Palo Alto home and moving. The residency pledge is the company's answer to that.

Who pays

The question is whether corporate money is quietly absorbing a personal tax. On the disclosed facts, the answer is mostly no, with caveats.

Meta is not writing a cheque. Zuckerberg sells shares he would otherwise be unable to sell without giving up votes, so the cost of the tax falls on him and, through dilution, on other shareholders. Meta says he gets no additional pay, and argues that if the tax passes he would effectively be paying to serve as CEO while living in the state. Reuters reports the agreement would stay in force even if the measure fails.

The filings do not give the number of shares, a price, or the board's vote. They do not explain how the non-voting class affects existing holders beyond the company's statement that Zuckerberg's control is unchanged. Meta also says the shares serve other purposes. CFO Susan Li told analysts on an earnings call that the structure "will expand our funding tools and give us flexibility to invest behind the unprecedented momentum", pointing to AI infrastructure, employee compensation and acquisitions.

The BBC reported that Meta's shares fell more than 7% after the announcement, a drop it put at over $100 billion in market value. Several factors can move a stock around an earnings release, so the fall cannot be attributed to this arrangement alone.

Reaction and what follows

The policy's backers read the deal as evidence that a billionaire exodus is overstated. Suzanne Jimenez of SEIU United Healthcare Workers West called it "the nail in the coffin to months of scare tactics claiming that billionaires are fleeing California." Opponents, including Gov. Gavin Newsom, argue the tax will still push wealth out of the state. The anti-tax committee, Building a Better California, has raised more than $333 million against $32.7 million for the Yes on 40 campaign.

The precedent is the part other founders and lawmakers will study. The template is a liquidity mechanism tied to a residency commitment. It is not a subsidy. A state drafting a wealth tax may find that such structures blunt its effect on the illiquid holdings of founder-controlled companies without removing the liability. Nov. 3 settles whether the tax exists. The detail still missing is what the non-voting class costs other Meta shareholders.
