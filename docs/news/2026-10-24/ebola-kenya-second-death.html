Second Ebola death in Kenya as Africa's health chief warns of continent-wide outbreak

In one sentence
A 65-year-old woman became Kenya's second Ebola death as Africa CDC warns the Bundibugyo outbreak is the most geographically extensive on record.

Kenya's health ministry announced on Friday that a 65-year-old woman had died of Ebola. She is the country's second fatality and a close relative of the first patient, who died this month. She had developed symptoms while in quarantine, and she died in Nairobi that evening. The death comes as Africa's top public-health official has described an outbreak of unusual geographic reach. Whether regional containment is keeping pace with the virus is now an open question.

A caution on sourcing: the "continent-wide" warning in the triage summary could not be matched to a verbatim statement. The nearest documented language is from Jean Kaseya, director-general of Africa CDC, who has called the outbreak the most geographically extensive in history, spanning several countries across three African regions.

What happened in Kenya

The first case was a 40-year-old Kenyan man who had been living in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. He reached Kenya from Uganda on October 3, tested positive on October 5 and died the same day, according to reports carried by the Associated Press. Kenya announced the case on October 6.

The woman who died on Friday was placed in quarantine as a contact. By the ministry's account she developed symptoms on October 13, was admitted to an infectious-disease unit the same day and received supportive care. A 39-year-old man from the same family had fallen ill in quarantine a day earlier, and Kenya confirmed his case on October 14.

The ministry puts the confirmed total at nine cases and two deaths. The other seven patients are in two isolation facilities. Officials said all cases belong to one cluster of known contacts and that there is no evidence of community transmission. That is the ministry's assessment, and it has not been independently verified.

Contact tracing identified 135 contacts of the first patient. Of these, 118 were quarantined and eight tested positive: four health workers and four close relatives. The infection of four health workers is a reminder that hospital transmission, not only household transmission, is part of this cluster. Contacts who complete the 21-day quarantine without symptoms are due to be released from Saturday.

The strain involved is Bundibugyo, one of the rarer Ebola species. It has no approved vaccine or specific treatment, so care is limited to supportive measures such as hydration and symptom management.

The regional picture

The Kenyan cluster is a spillover from a far larger epidemic. A US CDC report cited by Contagion Live recorded 9,235 confirmed cases and 4,444 confirmed deaths of Bundibugyo disease as of October 15. Of those, 9,212 cases and 4,441 deaths were in the DRC. The outbreak is the second-largest Ebola epidemic on record. Kaseya has put the DRC's lethality rate near 50 per cent.

The virus has crossed borders repeatedly. Uganda reported 20 confirmed cases earlier this year and was declared Ebola-free in August. A doctor who returned to France in June tested positive, and the virus did not spread further there. Kenya is the fourth country with a case linked to the DRC epidemic. Nigeria has now confirmed a fifth: a woman married to a Swiss diplomat who tested positive in Abuja after returning from Uganda. Switzerland's foreign ministry says she did not visit Congo. Nigeria's health minister, Muhammad Pate, said her case "appears to be closely related" to the Kenyan one, though the source of infection is unverified. Africa CDC said the first patients in both Kenya and Nigeria passed through Entebbe airport on October 3.

The route is the troubling part. Kenya's first patient reportedly travelled from the DRC to Uganda by road and then flew to Nairobi, even though the DRC–Uganda border has been closed since May. Patrick Amoth, Kenya's director-general for health, said a meeting with DRC and Uganda officials and Africa CDC would examine how the man passed through three countries undetected. Uganda's government media chief, Alan Kasujja, publicly questioned how Kenya let him through its airport. That public sparring suggests the surveillance gaps are shared and not yet agreed upon.

After Nigeria's case, Kaseya appealed for immediate and flexible funding, diagnostics, trained responders and stronger community surveillance. Earlier this year, pledges of about US$498.8 million were announced at an Africa CDC meeting in Kampala. The sums needed have grown with the epidemic.

What to watch

Kenya's own test is narrow and measurable. If no new infections appear as the 21-day quarantine windows close, starting this weekend, the ministry's single-cluster account will have held. A new case outside the listed contacts would undercut it.

The larger test is cross-border. Two airport transits on the same day produced cases in two countries, so the findings of the DRC–Uganda–Kenya review of the first patient's movements matter more than any one national count. The questions are whether exit screening at Entebbe and entry screening at Nairobi are tightened, and whether further imported cases surface in the coming weeks. Some governments have already acted: the US now bars travellers who have been in the DRC in the past 21 days from boarding US-bound flights.

Figures here come from ministry statements relayed by news agencies and are likely to be revised.
