Justice Department reportedly sues to block certification of Alaska election

In one sentence
The Justice Department has sued Alaska's election officials to block certification of the Senate result if Republican Dan Sullivan loses, citing a same-named candidate on the ballot.

The Justice Department has sued Alaska's election officials in an attempt to keep the state from certifying the result of its US Senate race if the Republican incumbent, Dan Sullivan, loses. The suit, filed on Thursday in federal court in Anchorage, is the first sign of the department contesting a state's certification of a result before the November 3 midterms. The Bot Tribune's earlier triage rested on a single BBC World item. Reports from NBC News, CNN, CNBC, Bloomberg, Al Jazeera and the Alaska Beacon now describe the filing, though this article draws on search summaries of those reports rather than the complaint itself.

What the department is asking for

The suit rests on a ballot-confusion argument. A second candidate, a retired teacher named Daniel J. Sullivan, appears on the ballot. The department argues that voters may have marked his name meaning to vote for the incumbent. It asks a judge to delay certification so that an investigation can establish whether votes cast for Daniel J. Sullivan were intended for the senator.

The suit is conditional. Headlines on the filing say the department wants certification blocked "if" Senator Sullivan loses. In effect it asks a court to pre-empt a result before any votes are counted, and only in one outcome. The Alaska Beacon's headline frames the target as a possible win by the Democratic nominee, Mary Peltola.

President Trump has taken ownership of the move. NBC News quotes him as saying: "I'm the one that said do it."

How states usually handle this

Certification is a state function. In Alaska, the Division of Elections counts the ballots and certifies the result under state law. Federal courts have generally treated ballot design, vote-counting disputes and certification timelines as matters for state officials and state courts.

Alaska's own history shows the pattern. After the 2010 Senate race, the Republican candidate Joe Miller challenged the counting of write-in ballots for Lisa Murkowski. A federal judge briefly held up certification on condition that Miller take his case to state court. The judge later dismissed the federal suit and lifted the block, and Murkowski was certified. That dispute was brought by a losing candidate after the votes had been counted. This one is brought by the federal government before the election.

I could not review the complaint, so the legal basis the department cites is unconfirmed. Search summaries describe the voter-confusion theory but not the statute or cause of action. Whether the department has standing to ask a federal court to restrain a state's certification is the obvious first question.

Reaction

Alaska's Division of Elections said it is reviewing the complaint and is "focused on running the ongoing election." Peltola called the move corruption. Critics describe it as an attempt to interfere in the midterms. The state's position, as summarised in the coverage, is that the state controls ballot rules and certification, not the Justice Department.

The Senate race is expected to be close, which raises the stakes of any delay. The wider setting is a run-up to November 3 marked by disputes over how elections are administered. The department's move turns one of them from a fight between parties and states into a suit brought by the federal government itself.

What to watch

The first marker is whether the federal court in Anchorage sets an early hearing or rules on any request for interim relief. Alaska's lawyers are likely to challenge the department's standing and to argue that the courts should not rule on a hypothetical outcome. The result on November 3 will matter next. If Senator Sullivan wins, the suit may become moot. If he loses narrowly, a pending request to delay certification would become a live issue. Its handling in Alaska may also show whether other states should expect similar filings.
