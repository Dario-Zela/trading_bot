White House reshuffles Canada tariffs ahead of midterms as Mexico deal is called 'very close'

In one sentence
Trump swapped products on the 50% Canada tariff list from October 30, easing some goods Maine growers use; the White House cites Canadian retaliation, and the political motive is inferred.

The White House has rewritten part of the list of Canadian goods that carry a 50% tariff. It took some products off the list and put others on, and the changes take effect October 30. That is four days before the November 3 midterms. The goods removed include plywood, some paper and some machinery. The one Senate race the changes are most plainly tied to is Susan Collins's in Maine.

Whether this is trade policy or electoral targeting depends on which document is read. The government's paperwork gives one account and the political reporting gives another. The two are not strictly in conflict.

What the proclamation does

President Trump signed the order on October 23 under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930. A White House fact sheet describes it as a refinement of tariffs first announced on July 20 and modified on September 8. Section 338 is rarely used. The fact sheet says it lets the president "suspend, revoke, supplement, or amend" earlier proclamations when the public interest calls for it. The order modifies Proclamation 11048, which concerns motor vehicles, as amended.

The fact sheet gives the following changes:

  • Removed from scope: plywood sheets, some paper products, certain machinery and refrigeration equipment, and certain packing crates and boxes. Reuters also lists fresh and dried seaweed.
  • Added to scope: certain valves, carpets, waste products and unspecified "others." Reuters's longer list includes paints, shampoos, cocoa powder, wooden coffins, ice cream sticks, air-conditioning machines, chest freezers, archery equipment and canoes.
  • Coverage: the duties apply whether or not a good qualifies under the US-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). They stack on top of Section 232 tariffs.

The fact sheet states no rates and no import values for the swapped categories. How much trade each side of the swap covers is therefore not public, and it is not clear whether the change loosens or tightens the tariff overall. The 50% rate is reported by Reuters for the underlying measure.

The stated rationale and the political one

The official reasoning is about Canada. The fact sheet says Canada walked away from a near-final trade deal this summer and imposed retaliatory tariffs on about $20bn of US exports. It calls the measures an offset for Canada's "discriminatory treatment of U.S. commerce" and says only China and Canada have retaliated rather than negotiate. White House spokesperson Matthew Middleton described the new order as "refining the scope" of the earlier tariffs.

The political reporting is more specific. Reuters says Collins, a Republican, had urged the administration to address tariffs hitting Maine companies and farmers. She said the relief would help blueberry, potato, apple and seaweed producers, and that many growers rely on Canadian equipment and packaging that are unavailable from US suppliers or costly to replace. She faces Democrat Troy Jackson in a race polls show as very close. Jackson accuses her of enabling the "chaos" of the trade war and of wanting credit for the relief. Senator Ron Wyden, a Democrat, called the move an attempt to save Collins's career.

The evidence for a Maine motive is a public request from the senator, the timing, and the fit between her list and the exempted goods. Seaweed, packing crates and farm machinery are close to what she described. Some of the exemptions, such as plywood and paper, are broader than any single state. Nothing in the White House text mentions Maine or the election. The motive remains an inference.

The same Reuters report links a second favour to a tight race. Trump dropped a threatened ban on Bombardier, which employs about 1,500 people in Wichita, Kansas, after Kansas lawmakers including Senator Roger Marshall pressed him. Marshall is also seeking re-election. Reuters notes Republicans may need to hold close Senate seats in Maine and Michigan to keep a slim majority. Michigan Republican Mike Rogers, who has previously called for an end to the trade war, praised the new tariffs on Canadian waste.

What the talks look like

The cluster also carried a presidential remark that a Mexico deal is "very close" and that Canada is "working hard" for one. Only the Canada half is confirmed. Reuters reports Trump said Friday that Canada is working on a deal. Ottawa's trade minister, Dominic LeBlanc, said Canada is assessing the change and is willing to work toward a more mutually beneficial relationship. That is a cautious statement, not an announcement of talks.

The Mexico remark could not be verified in this reporting. The fact sheet does not mention Mexico beyond the USMCA reference, and the Reuters report does not mention Mexico talks. No terms, figures or dates have been published for a Mexico agreement. A presidential "very close" is a statement of intent, and it should not be read as a deal.

The record on Canada points the other way. By Reuters's account, Trump imposed 50% tariffs on $20bn of Canadian imports in August after talks collapsed, and Canada answered in September with tariffs of 15% to 50% on $20bn of US goods. The fact sheet describes a Canada that is still retaliating, while the president says it is working on a deal. Both statements came from the same White House within days of each other.

Why it matters

For importers, the practical effect is a changed cost base from October 30. Buyers of Canadian plywood, certain paper and packaging, and machinery lose the 50% duty. Buyers of valves, carpets and waste gain one. The absence of published rates and trade values makes it hard to say who gains more.

The legal question is separate. Section 338 is seldom invoked, and the fact sheet leans on its amendment power to justify month-to-month adjustment of the tariff list. A power that lets the president add and remove product lines by proclamation, with no stated rates, can be used for fine-tuned rewards and penalties. Whether it is used that way is a question the Maine example raises but does not settle.

The next data points are dated. The changes take effect October 30. The Maine and Michigan Senate races are decided on November 3. Ottawa's formal response to the order, and any retaliation or relief it announces, would show whether the Canada talks are real. A published Mexico text would be the first evidence for the other half of the remark.
