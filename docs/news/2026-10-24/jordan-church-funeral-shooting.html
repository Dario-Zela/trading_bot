Seven shot dead by gunmen at church funeral in Jordan

In one sentence
Gunmen killed at least seven people and wounded 34 at a Greek Orthodox funeral in Karak, Jordan; the king called it terrorism, but motive and perpetrators remain unknown.

Gunmen opened fire on mourners at a Greek Orthodox church in southern Jordan on Friday, killing at least seven people and wounding 34 more. King Abdullah II called it a terrorist attack and promised the "harshest retaliation". The kingdom has seen little violence of this kind in recent years, and officials have not yet said who was responsible or why they struck.

What happened

The attack took place in the village of Hammoud, in Karak governorate, about 110 miles south of Amman. A funeral procession was under way when gunmen got out of a vehicle and began shooting. They then stormed the church compound. Footage reviewed by news organisations shows two men in casual clothes with pistols and a third carrying a long gun.

One eyewitness, quoted by the Jordanian outlet Al-Mamlaka, said between 150 and 200 people were in or near the church. He said the gunmen fired continuously for about five minutes before fleeing. Most of the victims, he said, were between 50 and 70 years old.

Interior Minister Mazen Al-Faraya gave the toll of at least seven dead and 34 wounded at a press conference. Other reports put the number of wounded as high as 40, citing the health minister, so the figures may still change. The reports consulted do not name the person being buried.

Who was responsible

Al-Faraya said "a group of extremists" carried out the attack. He did not say whether they had links to any known organisation, and no group has claimed responsibility. Security forces have launched a manhunt.

That leaves open the question the angle of this story turns on. The target was a Christian congregation at a funeral, which points toward an attack on the community as such. But a local or family feud has not been ruled out in public, and officials' use of the word "extremists" is not the same as an established link to a wider network. Until suspects are identified, those readings stay open. The attackers' choice of a gathering of mostly older mourners, and the use of a vehicle and a mix of handguns and a long gun, say little by themselves about who they were.

The king wrote that "every Jordanian life is precious" and pledged to act forcefully against those threatening the country. Lebanese President Joseph Aoun condemned what he called a "heinous terrorist attack", saying terrorism has no religion or homeland.

Why it stands out

Karak has a significant Christian minority, and Jordan has long presented itself as a stable country with a tradition of coexistence among its faiths, even as it sits beside Syria, Iraq, Israel and the occupied West Bank. Mass-casualty shootings are rare. The closest precedent in Karak is from December 2016, when gunmen ambushed police in a series of attacks, including at the Crusader castle that draws tourists. Ten people were killed, seven of them officers, and Jordanian forces killed the attackers after a long standoff.

The regional backdrop is tense. This week's edition also covers Netanyahu's statement that the army is ready for a large-scale operation in the West Bank and US strikes on Iran. Nothing reported so far connects the Karak shooting to either, and it would be a mistake to assume a link.

What to watch

The first marker is whether the gunmen are caught and identified. A claim of responsibility, or evidence of ties to an organised group, would make this a story about Jordan's security. If the suspects turn out to be local and the cause personal or tribal, it would be a grim but contained crime. Officials have also promised further details from the investigation, and the death toll could rise, since many of the wounded are elderly.
