Japan weighs counting energy and AI spending toward 3.5% defence target

In one sentence
Tokyo is reportedly weighing counting energy and AI outlays toward a US-sought 3.5%-of-GDP defence target, which could raise the headline figure without adding military capability.

Japan is weighing whether to count spending on energy and artificial intelligence towards a defence target of 3.5% of GDP, a way of reaching the number Washington wants without a matching increase in military capability. The idea was reported by Nikkei Asia and relayed by Benzzinga. Tokyo has not confirmed it, and no government document or figures have been published.

The number Washington wants

The target has been rising on Japan's horizon for a year. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi pledged in October 2025 to reach the existing goal of 2% of GDP, set in 2022, two years early. The cabinet then approved a supplementary budget that took fiscal 2025 defence-related outlays to roughly ¥11 trillion, including an initial budget of about ¥9.9 trillion. Defence Minister Shinjiro Koizumi said the 2% level had been achieved. That supplementary budget still needed parliamentary approval.

The Trump administration has pressed for far more. Kyodo reported that Washington privately asked for 3.5% of GDP, the level NATO members agreed to as their core defence goal. Takaichi said after talks with Trump that there had been no exchange of numbers. Reports in September 2026 said Tokyo thought 3.5% unlikely within five years and that Takaichi had become wary of tying policy to a GDP-based figure. Those reports came through secondary summaries, and I could not check them against Nikkei's original.

What counts as defence

The reclassification would be less a break than an extension of an existing debate. Kyodo reported last year that the government was considering counting programmes to protect energy facilities, transport and communications networks, along with shipbuilding, disaster prevention and police work on sensitive information. Japan's 2022 strategy already allows a category of related spending on coast guard work and infrastructure alongside the core defence budget.

NATO's formula is the closest precedent. Its 3.5% target is for core military spending, with a further 1.5% allowed for broader security-related investment such as infrastructure and resilience. Counting energy and AI would push Japan's definition into the second category, or beyond it.

Why the definition matters

GDP is a large denominator, and Japan's debt is heavy. A 3.5% target would more than double current levels, so the funding is a real problem. Tokyo has struggled to secure stable revenue even for the ¥43 trillion five-year build-up announced in 2022. Redefining what counts would let ministers report progress without finding all of that money, and without adding to the number of ships, missiles or troops.

The risk is to the figure's credibility. A headline ratio is useful to allies only if it says something about capability. Energy security and computing capacity can have real military value, but they are also commercial and civilian investments that would be made anyway. If they are relabelled, the ratio rises and deterrence does not. Other US allies under the same pressure could also copy the approach, which is why the precedent matters beyond Tokyo.

What to watch

The test is whether the Japanese government confirms the plan and publishes a definition. Takaichi has promised to revise Japan's key security documents by the end of 2026, and that revision is where any new accounting would appear. Washington's reaction will show whether it accepts a broad count or insists on a narrower one.
