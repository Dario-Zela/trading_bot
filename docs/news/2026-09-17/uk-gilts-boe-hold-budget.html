UK FISCAL SQUEEZE · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-17 · 6 min read

Gilt yields near 6% as BoE holds, inflation surprises, and Budget tone hardens

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who has begun signalling 'tough decisions' ahead of the 28 October Budget as gilt yields climb. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields near 6% — a 28-year high — have erased the FTSE 100's dividend income edge just as inflation, BoE caution and a harder Budget tone collide.

The number that mattered most in London on Thursday was not the Bank of England's rate decision — a hold at 3.75% that most traders had already priced in — but a quieter figure ticking over in the gilt market: the 30-year UK government bond yield, brushing against 6% for the first time since 1998. Twenty-eight years ago Tony Blair was a year into his first term and the Bank of England had only just been handed independence. The comparison alone tells you how markets are framing the moment.

Three things are colliding at once — a hotter-than-expected inflation print, a central bank holding its nerve despite a geopolitical wildcard, and a government edging from cost-of-living reassurance toward austerity language ahead of an October Budget. None of them is decisive on its own. Together, they have pushed long-dated UK borrowing costs up faster than anything happening in equities, and that is starting to reshape how income investors think about British assets.

The gilt market gets there first

The 30-year gilt yield touched 5.89% in early September and has hovered close to 6% since, a level last seen when Bank Rate itself was in double digits. The 10-year yield, more closely tied to the Treasury's day-to-day borrowing costs, sits near 5.3% — its highest since the run-up to the 2008 financial crisis. Britain sold £4.25 billion of 30-year debt in early September at a yield of 5.82%, the most expensive such sale since 1998.

The consequence for equity investors is direct and measurable. The FTSE 100's forward dividend yield sits around 3.4% for 2026. Against a 10-year gilt yielding roughly 5.3% and a 30-year approaching 6%, the income premium that has long been part of the case for holding UK blue-chips — get paid more to own the riskier asset — has essentially vanished. Bloomberg data puts the FTSE 100's yield advantage over gilts at its narrowest in 19 years. For a pension fund or income-seeking retail investor, a gilt now pays more than a basket of dividend-paying stocks, without the equity risk attached. That is the mechanical story behind a rotation out of income equities and into fixed income that fund managers have been describing since early September.

Inflation gives the hawks their moment

The timing compounds the problem. UK consumer prices rose 3.1% in the year to August, up from 2.9% in July and the first reading above 3% since March. Motor fuel costs, pushed by the Middle East conflict's effect on oil prices, jumped 23% year-on-year; heating oil rose 13% month-on-month. Core inflation, stripping out energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, held steadier at 2.6%, and the services measure stayed at 3.4% — evidence that the headline jump is energy-driven rather than a broad-based re-acceleration, but evidence that will not fully reassure a Monetary Policy Committee already split over how much weight to give it.

That data landed two days before the Bank's Thursday decision, and it shaped the argument inside the MPC without changing the outcome. The Committee voted 8–1 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75%, the sixth straight meeting without a change. The lone dissenter pushed for a hike rather than a cut — a reminder that the debate inside the Bank has moved from "when to ease" to "whether to tighten further," a reversal from where the conversation stood a year ago.

Bailey's conditional warning

Governor Andrew Bailey used the post-decision briefing to flag the scenario the Bank is most worried about: a further escalation in the Middle East conflict driving a sustained energy price shock. Under the Bank's more severe modelling, a prolonged war pushing oil toward $100 a barrel could lift UK inflation to around 6% — roughly triple the Bank's 2% target — and would likely require what the Bank has termed a "forceful" policy response, potentially taking Bank Rate back above 5%.

That is a tail-risk warning, not a forecast, and Bailey has previously described the range as "paths where we don't have to increase rates and paths where we do." But tail risks are exactly what gilt markets have been pricing since the Middle East fighting widened. With oil already trading above $101 a barrel, the scenario is not abstract — it is the difference between the Bank's central case and its downside case narrowing in real time.

Downing Street changes its tone

The fiscal side of the story has moved just as fast. Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who took office in July, has begun talking openly about "tough decisions" ahead of Chancellor John Healey's first Autumn Budget on 28 October — a marked shift from the government's earlier cost-of-living messaging. The reason is arithmetic as much as politics: rising gilt yields have cut the Treasury's fiscal headroom from roughly £23.6 billion to something closer to £13 billion, according to Institute for Government analysis, before a single new spending commitment is made.

Burnham has repeated a manifesto pledge not to raise income tax, National Insurance or VAT, which removes the three largest revenue levers from Healey's toolkit and points toward narrower, targeted measures — wealth, assets, or property have been floated — to close the gap. Healey has declined to rule out tax rises, telling reporters only that government borrowing costs are at "historic highs." The market has already drawn its own conclusion: gilt strategists increasingly treat an October tax rise as close to certain, which is itself part of what is keeping long-dated yields elevated. The bond market, in effect, is negotiating with the Treasury before the Treasury has published a single line of the Budget.

The read-through

What ties these threads together is a divergence in how fast different parts of the UK market are pricing risk. Gilts are moving on fiscal credibility and energy-driven inflation math, and moving hard — a 28-year high in borrowing costs is not a subtle signal. Equities, so far, have been comparatively becalmed, still leaning on the assumption that dividend income offers a cushion. That assumption is now testably weaker than it has been in nearly two decades. If the gap between gilt yields and FTSE dividend yields stays this narrow into the Budget, the rotation already visible in fund flows has room to extend, and UK equity valuations — particularly among the high-yield financials, utilities and telecoms that make up a large share of the FTSE 100's income — may have to adjust to compete for the same pool of capital gilts are now attracting on their own.

The next fixed points are 28 October, when Healey's Budget will either validate or undercut what the gilt market has already priced in, and the following inflation print due in mid-October, which will show whether August's energy-driven spike was a one-off or the start of the run toward 4% that some forecasters, including Deutsche Bank, now expect around the turn of the year.
