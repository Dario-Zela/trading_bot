Nike adds LVMH's Alexandre Arnault to board amid turnaround struggles

Alexandre Arnault, LVMH's Moët Hennessy deputy CEO, at the University of Oxford in 2022. — Sedentaire / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Nike named LVMH's Alexandre Arnault to its board as the sportswear giant's stock sits near a five-year low amid a stalled turnaround.

Nike has put a Frenchman with a taste for luxury handbags and Champagne on a board still trying to figure out how to sell more sneakers.

The sportswear company said Alexandre Arnault, deputy chief executive of LVMH's Moët Hennessy wines and spirits arm, has joined its 12-member board of directors, effective September 15. It is the kind of appointment that reads, on paper, like a hedge — bringing someone who has spent his career polishing premium brands onto the board of a company whose core problem is that its mass-market positioning has gone stale.

Arnault, 34, is not a stranger to fixing brands that have lost their shine. He ran RIMOWA, the German luggage maker, for four years after LVMH bought it, then spent four more as an executive vice president at Tiffany & Co. overseeing product, communications and industrial operations following LVMH's $15.8 billion acquisition of the jeweler. Before that he cut his teeth at McKinsey and KKR. He also sits on LVMH's own board and has previously held director seats at Carrefour, Birkenstock and Moncler — a résumé built almost entirely around turning tired names into desirable ones again.

"Alexandre has earned a reputation for helping iconic global brands evolve, innovate and grow in a changing, complex marketplace," Nike's executive chairman, Mark Parker, said in the announcement. Chief executive Elliott Hill was more direct about the fit: Arnault's "experience across innovation, digital transformation and brand building will be an asset as we continue to strengthen our connection with consumers."

A turnaround still short on turns

Hill needs whatever help he can get. Nike shares have fallen roughly 38% this year and were trading near $36 heading into the announcement — a five-year low for a stock that once traded above $170. Fiscal 2026 revenue came in essentially flat at $46.4 billion, while profit slid nearly 3.5%. China, once a growth engine, has now logged eight straight quarters of declining sales, including a 17% slump in one recent quarter as discretionary spending there softened.

Hill, who came out of retirement last October to take the top job, has framed his response as a "Win Now" strategy: stripping back reliance on legacy franchises like the Air Force 1 and Dunk, rebuilding relationships with wholesale partners Nike had spent years pushing away in favor of direct sales, and putting more staff on the ground in cities such as Shanghai and Beijing. He has acknowledged, in his own words, that progress is taking "longer" than expected, even as he insists the company's "direction is clear." Twenty months in, investors are still waiting for the numbers to agree with him.

What one seat can and can't do

It is worth being plain about what a single board appointment does and does not accomplish. Arnault will not set product strategy, run marketing campaigns, or renegotiate wholesale terms — that is Hill's job, and the board's role is oversight, not execution. What Nike gets is a credentialed voice in the room who has watched, up close, how LVMH extracts pricing power and scarcity value from brands that could easily have drifted into commodity status. Whether that perspective translates into board-level pressure on Nike's own premium and lifestyle lines remains to be seen, and won't show up in a quarterly print any time soon.

The market's read was accordingly muted — this is a governance story, not an earnings one, and traders treated it that way. Nike does not report again until its fiscal first-quarter results later this year, when Hill will face the same question he has fielded all year: whether "Win Now" is producing anything more than press releases.

What's next

Nike's next earnings report, expected in the coming weeks, will show whether Hill's wholesale and city-focused push has arrested the China slide. Arnault's own committee assignments — and whether he pushes for changes to Nike's premium and lifestyle strategy — will be worth watching in the company's next proxy filing.
