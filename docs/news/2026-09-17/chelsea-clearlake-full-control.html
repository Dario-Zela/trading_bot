Clearlake takes full control of Chelsea in £950m buyout of Boehly and Walter stakes

Stamford Bridge, Chelsea's home ground, now under Clearlake Capital's sole operational control. — Lachlan Fearnley / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Clearlake Capital paid £950m to buy out Todd Boehly and Mark Walter's Chelsea stakes, valuing the club at roughly £5bn.

Chelsea Football Club has a single owner for the first time since Roman Abramovich sold up in 2022. Clearlake Capital, the Santa Monica private equity firm that has run the London club alongside a rotating cast of American co-investors for four years, has bought out chairman Todd Boehly and Dodgers owner Mark Walter for a combined £950 million — a deal that prices the whole club at roughly £5 billion, including debt, more than double what the Boehly-led consortium paid Abramovich three years ago.

Boehly and Walter each held about 12.8% of Chelsea. Clearlake already controlled 61.5%. Adding their stakes takes the private equity firm's holding to around 87% — Swiss billionaire Hansjörg Wyss, the fourth member of the original consortium, is staying on with his own 12.8% slice, according to Chelsea's statement, which described him as remaining 'an important stakeholder and partner.' It is not, strictly, total ownership. But with Wyss a passive investor and Clearlake now holding every operational lever, 'full control' is the accurate description of what changed hands.

From consortium to control

The 2022 takeover was sold, at the time, as a partnership: Boehly the public face and chairman, Clearlake's Behdad Eghbali and José Feliciano the money and the votes, Walter and Wyss along for a substantial ride. It rarely worked that way in practice. Eghbali, who sits on Chelsea's board and several other Clearlake portfolio companies, was widely reported to hold the real day-to-day sway even as Boehly did the press conferences and stood pitchside. The tension surfaced most visibly in the club's managerial churn — five permanent head coaches since the takeover — and came to a head in 2024, when Boehly was said to have opposed Clearlake's decision to move on from Mauricio Pochettino. Disagreements over whether to redevelop Stamford Bridge or build anew added to the friction. By 2024, according to reporting at the time, both camps were separately exploring buying the other out.

Clearlake won that argument by attrition rather than drama. In a joint statement, Eghbali and Feliciano struck a conciliatory note: 'Todd has been an important partner throughout our ownership of Chelsea, and we thank him for his time and contribution as Chairman. He will always be a part of the Chelsea story and family.' Boehly, in his own statement, called it 'an honor to serve as chairman' and said he was proud of what the ownership group had built. Chelsea's statement was blunter about what happens next: 'There will be no changes to the day-to-day operations, leadership or strategy at the Club.'

Why Walter sold, and why now

Boehly's exit reads as the resolution of a long-running boardroom dispute. Walter's is harder to separate from what else has been happening to him this year. The Guggenheim Partners chief executive is under federal criminal and civil investigation in Manhattan, with prosecutors and the SEC examining roughly $20 billion in transactions between two insurers he controls — Delaware Life and Clear Spring Life and Annuity — and other businesses tied to him, over whether the relationships were properly disclosed. His firm, TWG Global, has said there was 'no fraud.' Walter has also just agreed to sell his majority stake in the Los Angeles Lakers to Joshua Kushner and Bob Iger, barely a year after buying the team, for a reported 25% premium on his purchase price — a deal that looks, in hindsight, like the first domino in a broader liquidity push. Selling out of Chelsea, a club whose politics he had less influence over than Boehly did, follows the same logic: raise cash, exit disputed or scrutinized positions, simplify the balance sheet while regulators are watching.

Private equity's football problem — and appetite

The deal lands the same year Clearlake's own investors have started asking pointed questions about the firm's sports habit. Feliciano is separately closing in on a roughly $3.9 billion purchase of Major League Baseball's San Diego Padres, and Eghbali has called sports 'a mainstay investment category for investors in the decades to come.' Some limited partners in Clearlake's buyout funds see it differently — Bloomberg reported in April that the Chelsea and Padres deals had stoked investor unease about a private equity shop drifting from its technology and industrials roots into trophy assets with no obvious exit multiple. A football club generates matchday and broadcast revenue, not the recurring cash flows PE models are built around, and Chelsea has no realistic path to a near-term sale given the size of the buyer pool for a £5 billion asset.

That is, in a sense, the real story here. Clearlake did not just buy out two feuding partners; it removed the two shareholders most likely to force a sale, a change of strategy, or public disagreement, and did so at a valuation more than double the Abramovich-era price. Whether that bet pays off depends on questions no closing statement answers: what Financial Fair Play-style profitability rules the Premier League and UEFA will tolerate from a fully leveraged private equity owner, and what Chelsea's results on the pitch look like once there is no longer a chairman to blame when things go wrong. The next test arrives domestically before Christmas, when the January transfer window opens and the market gets its first look at how a single-owner Chelsea actually spends.
