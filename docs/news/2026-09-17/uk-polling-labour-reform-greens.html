Labour's lead over Reform narrows nationally as Greens surge in the cities

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who succeeded Keir Starmer in July, now presides over a Labour lead that has all but evaporated in national polling. — Alecsandra Dragoi / Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Labour's poll lead over Reform has shrunk to under 2 points as Reform dominates industrial towns and the Greens surge in cities, squeezing Labour from both sides.

Two months ago, Andy Burnham inherited a Labour Party with a working Commons majority and, on paper, years to govern before facing voters again. What he did not inherit was a stable poll lead. The latest PollCheck seven-poll average, published this week, puts Labour on 25.6%, Reform UK on 23.6% and the Conservatives on 20.3% — a Labour margin over Nigel Farage's party of under two points, comfortably inside any reasonable margin of error.

A lead that keeps shrinking, then recovering, then shrinking again

The volatility has a specific cause. Reform's party conference this month coincided with a donations row: two senior aides stood down after being filmed discussing ways round Britain's foreign-donation rules, and the Liberal Democrats, Labour and the Greens all called for a police investigation. The Metropolitan Police has said it will merely "assess" the material rather than open a formal inquiry, and Farage insists the party has "broken no laws." The episode briefly widened Labour's advantage — Opinium had Burnham's party three points clear in early September, its best showing since he took over — but the bounce has already faded. By the middle of this month the two parties were back within statistical touching distance, exactly where they stood before the scandal broke.

That pattern — a governing party unable to convert an opponent's bad week into durable advantage — is the more telling signal than either single data point. Labour's underlying support has not moved much all year; what has moved is the composition of the opposition to it.

Reform's map looks nothing like a national swing

Regional polling from BritPolls shows why a two-point national gap understates Reform's reach. The party is running at 35–38% across the East of England, 33% in the Midlands, 32% in Yorkshire and 31% in the North East — post-industrial, Leave-voting seats that backed the Conservatives in 2019 and Labour before that. In England's big cities, by contrast, Reform barely registers. That concentration matters more than the topline share under first-past-the-post: a party can poll in the low twenties nationally and still take a large block of seats if that support clusters efficiently, which is precisely the geography Reform has built.

Meanwhile, Labour is losing its other flank

If Reform is squeezing Labour from the industrial towns, the Green Party is squeezing it from the cities. Green support in urban England has risen roughly 12 points since the 2024 general election and now sits within four points of Labour in those seats, according to figures cited by the Guardian and AOL — though neither has published the underlying fieldwork dates or sample sizes, and the claim should be read as directional rather than precise. It is corroborated, at least in spirit, by May's local elections, in which Labour recorded its worst London results since 1968 while the Greens, now led by Zack Polanski, took control of councils in Hackney and Waltham Forest and won two directly elected mayoralties. Some individual polls have shown the Greens overtaking Labour outright among voters under 50; treat those as outliers rather than trend, but the direction of travel is not in doubt.

Why a two-point poll gap is a markets story

The consensus trade since Labour's 2024 landslide has rested on a simple assumption: a large majority buys the government room to legislate without needing to bargain, which in turn makes gilts and sterling relatively insensitive to Westminster noise. A Labour Party being pulled apart at both ends — losing rural, post-industrial seats to Reform and progressive urban seats to the Greens simultaneously — is a very different proposition for investors than a single, clean two-party swing. It raises the possibility of a much smaller majority, or a Commons where budget votes require more actual persuasion, at the next election. Burnham's government does not face voters imminently, but nervousness compounds gradually: the gilt market has already shown it reacts to succession and stability questions well before any vote is cast, as it did around Starmer's resignation in June.

The next test of whether this is a real fragmentation or a September wobble comes with the next PollCheck update and any fresh regional breakdowns from BritPolls, plus whatever the Met decides to do — or not do — with the Reform donations file.
