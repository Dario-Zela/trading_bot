Leaked plans reveal prisoners set to 'double up' in prefab cells

HMP Wayland in Norfolk, one of ten category C prisons named in leaked plans to double-bunk inmates in prefabricated cells. — Chelb / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Leaked papers show ministers plan to double-bunk inmates in prefab cells built for one, as England's prisons run out of space with no long-term fix in sight.

England's prison system has run out of road, and out of the money and time it would take to build new road. Leaked emails, first reported by the Guardian, show the Prison Service has drawn up plans to squeeze a second inmate into hundreds of prefabricated cells built for one — steel-framed pods that were only ever meant to be a stopgap, and were designed for a single occupant.

The email, sent on 27 August by a senior Prison Service executive to governors at ten category C prisons, asked them to sign off installing second beds in so-called rapid deployment cells, or RDCs. Whitehall's arithmetic is blunt: doubling up bunks in these units would add 476 places to the estate almost overnight, at a fraction of the cost and time a new wing or prison would require. "Maximising available prison capacity is of high ministerial interest," the executive wrote, according to the leaked correspondence.

Pods built for one, now asked to hold two

The cells in question were never meant to be permanent. Rapid deployment cells first appeared at HMP Norwich in March 2023, pitched as a short-term patch for an overcrowding problem that was supposed to ease once new prisons came on stream. Roughly 800 of the steel-framed units have since gone up in the grounds of more than 15 prisons across England and Wales, each one measuring as little as 2.9 by 3.4 metres — smaller than a single parking bay — and fitted with a bed, a toilet and a shower for one person. They can be stacked two storeys high and are rated for a 15-year lifespan. Occupancy has typically gone to lower-risk prisoners, sometimes as a reward for good behaviour, precisely because the units were built for one person to live in with a measure of privacy.

The leaked email names specific targets: Wayland in Norfolk would take on 43 extra beds, Littlehey in Cambridgeshire 60, Erlestoke in Wiltshire 66, and Rochester in Kent 60, with the remainder spread across the other six category C sites. A Ministry of Justice spokesperson, responding to the disclosure, insisted no final decision has been made and that "cells are only doubled up where it is safe to do so" — while confirming the department is separately investing up to £4.7bn to create 14,000 new prison places over the coming years. That investment, however, is measured in years; the capacity problem is measured in weeks.

A system already over its own safety line

The numbers explain the urgency. England and Wales held 87,111 people in prison as of the most recent count, against a Ministry of Justice-defined safe and decent capacity of 81,616 — meaning the estate is already roughly 5,500 places over the line the department itself has drawn. Roughly a quarter of prisoners nationally are already sharing cells designed for one, frequently with an unscreened toilet a few feet from another man's bed. A Public Accounts Committee report earlier this year warned the justice system faces "total gridlock" without rapid action, noting that a 2021 pledge to add 20,000 places by the mid-2020s has proven "completely unrealistic": the places are running roughly five years late and 80% — about £4.2bn — over budget.

The response so far has been a patchwork of emergency measures rather than a settled plan. Former justice secretary Shabana Mahmood invoked Operation Safeguard, holding some prisoners overnight in police cells, and ordered recalled offenders released after a fixed 28 days to free up space. Mahmood has since moved to the Home Office; the justice brief now sits with Alex Norris, appointed Lord Chancellor by Prime Minister Andy Burnham after Keir Starmer's resignation in July. Norris inherits a problem that has outlasted three governments' worth of promises to fix it structurally — and the RDC memo suggests his department, like its predecessors, is choosing to manage the symptom rather than wait for a cure.

Governors and unions warn the numbers don't add up

The reaction from inside the system has been sharper than the ministry's careful public language. Mark Fairhurst, national chair of the Prison Officers Association, said his members could effectively down tools over the plan even though prison officers are barred by law from striking. "I know they would walk out on health and safety grounds if asked to oversee prisons which cannot cope with the numbers being proposed," he said. The Prison Governors Association has called for individual risk assessments at each site before any doubling-up proceeds, and the Howard League for Penal Reform has described the move as an act of institutional desperation rather than policy.

The safety argument is not abstract. A cell built for one person's bed, toilet and shower, with a second adult and a second bed added, changes the physical geometry of confinement — less space to de-escalate a dispute, less privacy at a toilet a few feet from a stranger, more bodies for the same number of staff to supervise. The Ministry of Justice's own 15-year lifespan estimate for the RDCs assumed single occupancy; doubling the population inside a fixed footprint was not the load the design was tested against.

What comes next

No governor has yet signed off on the doubling-up proposal, and the ministry says no final decision has been taken. But the underlying pressure — an estate roughly 5,500 places over its own safety threshold, with permanent capacity years away — does not go away if this particular plan is shelved; it resurfaces in the next memo. The Sentencing Bill working through Parliament, intended to divert more offenders away from custody, is the government's stated route out of the crisis, but the Public Accounts Committee has already flagged that HMPPS is leaning on that legislation arriving quickly and working as modelled — an assumption, not a guarantee. Until it does, watch for governors' responses to the RDC request, due back to the Prison Service in the coming weeks, and for whether Norris's department treats doubling-up as one-off triage or as the new baseline.
