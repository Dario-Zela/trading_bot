EU's 'Made in Europe' procurement rules threaten UK hopes for a Brussels reset

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, where the procurement overhaul originated. — Matthias v.d. Elbe / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
New EU procurement rules could reject bids with under 50% European content, leaving UK firms exposed just as ministers push for a Brussels reset.

The European Commission's overhaul of its public procurement rules, unveiled this month, gives national authorities in the bloc new powers to favour EU-based suppliers over rivals from outside the bloc — including, now, the United Kingdom. The timing is awkward for Downing Street: British ministers are simultaneously trying to persuade Brussels to loosen post-Brexit trade friction, and the procurement proposal moves in the opposite direction.

Under the draft rules, EU public buyers would gain the ability to reject bids where less than half their value originates in the EU or in countries with reciprocal procurement agreements, restrict some tenders to European firms outright, and apply scoring preferences to bids with higher European content at the award stage. Separate legislation under the same 'Made in Europe' banner, championed chiefly by France, could push local-content thresholds as high as 70% for some manufactured goods. Public procurement is not a rounding error: it accounts for roughly 15% of the EU's economic output, and the UK is Europe's largest single external trading partner.

A third country, formally

Because the UK has no procurement chapter in its 2020 trade agreement with the EU, British firms sit outside any preferential carve-out and are treated the same as suppliers from China or the United States once the rules bite. William Bain, head of trade policy at the British Chambers of Commerce, put the risk plainly this month: British businesses could "be squeezed out of supply chains, as well as facing higher costs and bureaucracy," particularly in automotive, aerospace, defence and medical equipment — sectors where UK suppliers are woven into EU manufacturing chains built up over decades. Bain also noted a possible upside: a single EU-wide procurement rulebook, if UK firms can qualify under it, would be simpler than the patchwork of national rules they navigate today. That silver lining depends entirely on where London ends up sitting relative to the new thresholds.

The European Commission has said the reform is not a blanket 'Made in Europe' quota and stops short of excluding all outside bidders. But discretion granted to national buyers cuts both ways: it lets governments favour EU suppliers when they choose to, without requiring uniform application across the bloc. For UK exporters, that ambiguity is itself a cost, since bidding into 27 procurement markets with variable local-content enforcement is harder to price than a single hard rule would be.

Colliding with the reset

The procurement push lands squarely inside the UK's attempt to rebuild ties with Brussels. Hamish Falconer, the minister now leading the reset effort, raised the issue directly with the EU's trade commissioner, Maroš Šefčovič, at their first meeting on 7 September, making UK exposure to 'Made in Europe' a named priority. Trade minister Anas Sarwar has separately urged UK business leaders to lobby their EU counterparts directly on the risks. An EU-UK summit is pencilled in for November, but officials on both sides have privately conceded the reset has lost momentum — a dynamic unhelped by unresolved sticking points such as the EU's push for a youth-mobility scheme roughly double the size the UK has offered.

The pattern echoes an earlier procurement dispute: the UK failed to secure acceptable terms to join the EU's SAFE defence-procurement fund late last year, walking away when the terms on offer proved unworkable. 'Made in Europe' risks becoming a second front in the same fight, this time across the far larger civilian procurement market.

What it signals

The substance here matters less than the sequencing. Brussels is not proposing to punish Britain specifically — the rules apply to any non-EU supplier — but their arrival just as London is asking for closer alignment undercuts the political case for a reset built on goodwill. It suggests EU industrial policy, driven by concerns about Chinese competition rather than by Britain at all, is being set with limited regard for the UK relationship, and that any exemption will have to be negotiated line by line rather than assumed.

Negotiations on the procurement regulation are expected to run into 2027, with a transition period pushing real-world application closer to 2029–2030 — leaving time, in principle, for the UK to argue for carve-outs before the rules take effect. The more immediate marker is the November EU-UK summit, where procurement access will test whether the reset can produce results beyond communiqué language.
