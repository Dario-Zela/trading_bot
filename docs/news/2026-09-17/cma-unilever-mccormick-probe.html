CMA opens Phase 1 probe into $44.8bn Unilever-McCormick merger

Unilever House, the company's Grade II listed Art Deco headquarters on London's Victoria Embankment. — Cnbrb / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The CMA has until 11 November to decide if the $44.8bn Unilever-McCormick foods merger needs a deeper Phase 2 competition review.

Britain's competition watchdog has opened a formal Phase 1 investigation into the £44.8bn ($44.8bn) combination of Unilever's foods business with McCormick & Company, giving itself until 11 November to decide whether the deal needs a deeper, months-long Phase 2 review.

The Competition and Markets Authority launched the inquiry on 16 September, following an informal consultation period that ran through late July and early August. Principal case officer Darren Gysi is leading the review, which will test whether the tie-up has a realistic chance of substantially lessening competition in any UK market — the standard threshold for Phase 1 merger cases.

The deal itself, announced in March, is one of the largest in food and consumer goods this year. McCormick is combining with the bulk of Unilever's foods division — Knorr, Hellmann's, Marmite, Pot Noodle and Frank's RedHot among the brands changing hands — to create a flavour-focused group with roughly $20bn in combined annual revenue. Under the terms, McCormick pays $15.7bn in cash, with Unilever shareholders ending up owning 55.1% of the combined company, McCormick shareholders 35%, and Unilever itself retaining a 9.9% stake it has said it will sell down over time. Completion is targeted for mid-2027, contingent on shareholder votes and regulatory sign-off in multiple jurisdictions.

Where the overlap bites

The clearest competition flashpoint is condiments. McCormick already owns French's, the dominant US mustard brand, and Unilever's UK portfolio includes Colman's, the 217-year-old mustard maker. Rather than wait for the CMA to flag the overlap, Unilever got ahead of it: in August it put Colman's up for sale, hiring Rothschild to find a buyer, and carved the brand out of the McCormick transaction entirely. A Unilever spokesperson said the move was taken "to proactively seek to address potential competition concerns from the planned combination of Unilever Foods and McCormick." Also excluded from the deal are Lipton drinks, the group's India, Nepal and Portugal operations, its lifestyle nutrition business and the Buavita juice unit.

What the clock means for Unilever stock

A Phase 1 clearance on or before 11 November would remove one of the last major hurdles to closing; a referral to Phase 2 would push the timeline deep into 2027 and reopen questions the companies thought they had pre-empted with the Colman's sale. Unilever shares have already given up roughly 14% since the deal was announced on 17 March, reflecting broader unease about the foods spin-off alongside the regulatory overhang. Not everyone reads that as a red flag: Bloomberg Intelligence's Diana Gomes has argued the cash-and-stock structure favours Unilever and keeps leverage contained, and J.P. Morgan retains a buy rating on the stock. The CMA can extend its Phase 1 deadline "in certain limited circumstances," but barring that, 11 November is the date that decides which of those two stories wins out.
