Von der Leyen floats Canada as EU's first 'associate member' amid US trade strain

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen with Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney — European Union, photo by Aurore Martignoni / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Von der Leyen proposed Canada as the EU's first 'associate member' during her State of the Union address — a status with no treaty basis yet, and one Trump immediately called a possible 'hostile act.'

European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen used the biggest set-piece of Brussels' political calendar to make an offer no EU leader has extended before: come inside the tent, but not all the way in.

Addressing the European Parliament in Strasbourg on Wednesday for her annual State of the Union speech, von der Leyen turned to a guest in the chamber — Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, the first foreign head of government ever invited to attend — and told him directly: "I would like to work with you on opening the door for Canada to be the first associate member of the EU." It is a status that does not exist in any EU treaty. Nobody, including the Commission itself, can yet say precisely what it would mean.

A trade relationship outgrowing its treaty

The backdrop is blunt economics. Roughly 70% of Canadian exports still cross the US border, a dependency that has curdled since Donald Trump returned to the White House with tariffs on Canadian goods and, more pointedly, repeated musings about Canada becoming the "51st state." Carney, a former central banker not given to hyperbole, has spent the past several months explicitly courting Europe as an alternative anchor, telling audiences in Brussels and elsewhere that Ottawa wants a "unique alliance" with the EU — while taking pains to say he was not seeking full membership.

Von der Leyen's answer went further than Carney had asked for. Canada and the EU already trade under the Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement (CETA), in force since 2017, but von der Leyen framed Wednesday's proposal as building on CETA toward what she called an "Alliance for the Future" — a partnership spanning intelligent manufacturing, artificial intelligence, cybersecurity, defence, energy and critical minerals and batteries, the last of which speaks directly to Canada's push to become a Western alternative to Chinese-dominated supply chains. "We share one ocean, one set of values, one way of seeing the world," she said, casting the move as evidence that, in an era of "a fracture in the international rules-based system," democracies can "choose with whom to work."

Ottawa is reportedly already negotiating entry into Erasmus+ student exchanges, Horizon Europe research funding, and mutual recognition of professional qualifications — smaller-bore integrations that would sit underneath whatever "associate member" ultimately comes to mean. Canada is also said to want an arrangement letting its citizens live and work in Europe without visas, a considerably bigger ask.

What "associate member" actually is — for now, mostly undefined

The phrase is not new to Brussels; it is new to Canada. German Chancellor Friedrich Merz floated "associate membership" earlier this year as a possible way-station for Ukraine on its path to full EU accession — a status offering a seat closer to the table without a vote at it. Applying the same label to a G7 economy on the other side of the Atlantic, with no accession ambitions at all, is a different exercise entirely, and it is one the EU's own treaties do not currently accommodate.

That is the crux of the problem facing anyone trying to assess the announcement: enshrining a genuine "associate member" status would require treaty change, which means unanimous agreement among all 27 capitals and ratification in each one — a bar that has stalled far less ambitious EU reforms for years. Existing halfway-house models offer only partial guidance. Norway and Switzerland enjoy deep access to the EU single market through the European Economic Area and a web of bilateral deals, but as rule-takers with no vote on the regulations they must follow. Whether Canada would get a seat at Commission meetings, access to EU budget lines, or any actual say in EU policymaking remains, in von der Leyen's own framing, a question for negotiation rather than an answer she brought to Strasbourg.

Washington notices immediately

If the proposal was a trial balloon, it did not stay aloft long before drawing fire. Speaking to reporters in Charlotte, North Carolina, hours after von der Leyen's speech, Trump dismissed the idea of Canada joining the EU's orbit as "laughable" and called Canada a "terrible trade partner," before turning the temperature up considerably: "If it's a good intention, that's fine. If it's a bad intention, we'll put very heavy tariffs." He suggested Washington could cut off trade with Europe "on many things" if it judged the move to be aimed at the United States.

The threat underscores exactly what makes the announcement significant even in its unfinished state. Von der Leyen did not need to spell out voting rights or budget contributions to send a signal — the signal was the invitation itself, extended in public, to the leader of a country the sitting US president has needled about annexation. Trump's tariff threat is itself a kind of confirmation that Brussels' gambit registered as intended in Washington, whatever shape the actual arrangement eventually takes.

What comes next

The two sides have set themselves a concrete date to start filling in the blanks: an EU-Canada summit in Montreal next month, where officials are expected to sketch out which parts of the "Alliance for the Future" — tech and defence cooperation, critical minerals, energy — can be agreed through existing legal mechanisms, and which would require the treaty change that a formal associate-member status implies. That distinction will determine how much of Wednesday's announcement is durable policy and how much is atmospherics aimed at Washington. Also worth watching: whether other EU capitals, several of which have their own reasons to avoid a fight over unanimity and precedent, embrace the idea as warmly as von der Leyen did from the podium, or quietly slow-walk it once the cameras are gone.
