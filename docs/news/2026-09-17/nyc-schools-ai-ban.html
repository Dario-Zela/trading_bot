New York City schools ban generative AI for all pre-K-8 students

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani, whose administration announced the AI moratorium alongside Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels. — Marc A. Hermann / MTA, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
New York City barred generative AI for 600,000 pre-K-8 students, the largest US classroom AI moratorium yet, and blocked chatbots for all grades.

New York's public schools reopened for the year on September 10 with a new rule bolted onto the usual list of first-day instructions: no AI. From 2-K — a pre-kindergarten track for two-year-olds — through eighth grade, roughly 600,000 children in New York City Public Schools are now barred from using generative AI tools in class. Chatbots, AI tutors, AI-graded worksheets — all of it, off-limits for two-thirds of the largest school system in the country.

Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced the policy on September 2, calling it a one-year moratorium rather than a permanent ban — though for the roughly 600,000 students it covers, the practical effect is the same for now. The district says it will also discontinue or disable the AI features in more than 38 previously approved classroom programs that don't meet new safety and oversight standards.

The restrictions go further for a narrower slice of technology: companion chatbots, and mainstream consumer tools like ChatGPT and Claude, are blocked for every grade, including high school. Screen time gets capped too — pre-K through second grade loses individual devices in class entirely, third through fifth grade is capped at 30 minutes a day, and middle schoolers get 45. Exceptions exist for assistive technology used by students with disabilities, for multilingual learners, and for computer-science and career-readiness programs.

Teachers, notably, are not covered by the same restrictions. They can still use approved AI tools to draft lesson plans, translate materials, or write parent communications — just not to grade work, monitor behavior, run counseling sessions, or make decisions about promotion and graduation.

A reversal under pressure

The policy is a sharp turn from where the district stood as recently as March, when it released initial AI guidance that was, in the chancellor's own words, seen as having "missed the mark." More than half the City Council signed a letter pushing for a pause, parent groups gathered thousands of petition signatures, and the administration froze new AI software purchases in July while it rewrote the rules. "This moratorium is a commitment to getting the future right," Mamdani said at the announcement. "The tech industry wants us to believe that A.I.-powered early education is not only inevitable, but necessary. We do not see it that way."

New York isn't acting alone. Norway barred generative AI from primary schools — grades one through seven — in June, with the ban taking hold at the end of August. Prime Minister Jonas Gahr Støre argued the tools let children "skip foundational steps in learning to read, write and do basic maths." The two policies differ in mechanics — Norway's is a standing ban, New York's a one-year pause with carve-outs — but both rest on the same wager: that classroom AI's costs to children currently outweigh its benefits, regardless of what the vendors selling it argue.

That wager is the part worth watching. A single 600,000-student school district doesn't move federal policy, and the ban doesn't touch how AI companies sell to adults, workplaces, or higher education. But it is a concrete data point against the industry's preferred narrative of inevitable, welcomed adoption — one of the country's most visible public institutions looked at the pitch and said no, at least for now. The moratorium runs for one school year; whether it lapses, tightens, or gets copied by other districts will depend largely on what the city's own review finds before next September.
