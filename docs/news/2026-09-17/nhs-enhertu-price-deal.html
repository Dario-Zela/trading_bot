NHS England strikes price deal giving wider access to breast cancer drug Enhertu

Structural diagram of trastuzumab deruxtecan, the antibody-drug conjugate sold as Enhertu — Fvasconcellos / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
NHS England will fund Enhertu for ~1,000 women a year with HER2-low breast cancer after a price deal, aided by a NICE threshold rise tied to the UK-US pharma trade pact.

For more than two years, women with a common and incurable form of advanced breast cancer in England watched a drug shown to buy them, on average, an extra half-year of life sit just out of reach — available across the border in Scotland, available in the United States, but blocked at home on cost grounds. That changed on Thursday. NHS England confirmed that Enhertu, known clinically as trastuzumab deruxtecan, will now be routinely funded for patients with HER2-low advanced breast cancer, following a price agreement between the health service, the drug's regulator and its manufacturers, AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo.[^1] NHS England estimates around 1,000 women a year will become eligible.[^2]

The headline framing — a cash-strapped health service finally forcing a price concession from a premium oncology drugmaker — is broadly right, but it understates how the deal actually came together, and that matters for anyone trying to read across to the next drug fight.

A rejection, reversed

HER2-low breast cancer is not a rare subtype. It describes tumours with a lower level of the HER2 protein than classic HER2-positive cancers — too low to qualify for older HER2-targeted drugs like trastuzumab (Herceptin), but, researchers discovered, still enough of a target for Enhertu's more potent design, which pairs an antibody with a chemotherapy payload delivered directly into cancer cells.[^3] In the pivotal DESTINY-Breast04 trial, patients given Enhertu after one or two prior lines of chemotherapy had a median progression-free survival of 9.9 months, against 5.1 months on standard chemotherapy, and a median overall survival gain of around six and a half months.[^4] Those are, by the standards of metastatic breast cancer trials, unusually large numbers, and they underpinned the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence's decision that the drug worked. NICE's objection was never clinical. It was price.

The Scottish Medicines Consortium approved Enhertu for Scottish patients in December 2023.[^5] NICE reached the opposite conclusion for England in March 2024, and again in July of that year, ruling that the drug's list price made it too expensive relative to NICE's cost-effectiveness threshold — the amount, roughly £20,000 to £30,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY), that the service has historically been willing to pay.[^6] Breast Cancer Now called it a "dark day"; the charity, together with campaign groups METUPUK and Make 2nds Count, spent the following eighteen months pressing NICE, NHS England and the two manufacturers to find a way through, arguing that patients were "dying waiting."[^7] Polling the charity commissioned found 82% of UK adults thought the drug should be funded.[^8] A confidential list-price discount, offered by AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo through NHS England's standard patient access scheme, narrowed the gap but never closed it.

What actually moved: not just the negotiating table

Here is the part of the story that complicates the tidy narrative of NHS muscle-flexing. What closed the gap was not simply harder bargaining by NHS England negotiators — it was a change to the yardstick NICE uses to judge value for money, itself a downstream effect of a UK–US trade deal on pharmaceutical pricing agreed in outline in December 2025 and finalised in the following months.[^9] Under that arrangement, Britain secured a zero-tariff exemption for pharmaceutical exports to the United States, and in return committed to raising NHS medicines spending as a share of GDP — from 0.3% toward 0.35% by 2028 and 0.6% by 2035 — and to softening the rebate rate drugmakers pay back to the Treasury under the voluntary VPAG scheme.[^10] As part of the same package, the government instructed NICE to lift its standard cost-effectiveness threshold from £20,000–£30,000 per QALY to £25,000–£35,000, effective this April, and to revise how it weighs quality-of-life benefits in its modelling.^11 Helen Knight, NICE's director of medicines evaluation, was explicit about the mix of factors involved: "a commercial solution, alongside recent changes to NICE's cost-effectiveness thresholds and the use of new methods for assessing quality-of-life, has meant this treatment can now be provided on the NHS."[^12] The specific discounted price agreed with AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo has not been disclosed, which is standard practice for these patient access scheme agreements, but is understood to be confidential and separate from the drug's list price.

That sequencing matters for how investors and health-policy watchers should read this. It is less a case of the NHS unilaterally extracting a concession through raw purchasing leverage, and more a case of a widened policy channel — bought, in effect, by a trade concession to Washington — combined with a incremental price discount meeting somewhere in the middle. The leverage genuinely shifted, but it shifted partly because government policy moved the goalposts, not only because NHS negotiators pushed harder against a fixed one. Health Secretary Yvette Cooper, who took up the post in July after a reshuffle, framed it in patient terms: "Enhertu can be life-changing and this means NHS patients will finally be able to benefit from it."[^13]

What it means for the next drug

For patients, the practical effect is straightforward. From Thursday, eligible women with unresectable or metastatic HER2-low breast cancer who have already had chemotherapy can be prescribed Enhertu through NHS England, ending the anomaly in which patients in Scotland had routine access and patients in England did not. Claire Rowney, chief executive of Breast Cancer Now, called it "a momentous day for thousands of people who will now have access to a drug that will give them the opportunity to gain precious time with loved ones."[^14]

For the pharmaceutical sector, the more durable signal is the mechanism, not the outcome. A higher NICE threshold and softer clawback terms — secured as part of a trade deal, not a health-budget decision — make it structurally easier for the next expensive oncology or rare-disease drug to clear NICE's bar. That is a tailwind for manufacturers' UK revenue assumptions, not obviously a sign of stronger NHS bargaining power on its own. What did shift decisively against manufacturers is reputational and political pressure: two years of visible campaigning, parliamentary questions and charity polling built a cost to inaction that outlasted several rounds of NICE rejection. Any drugmaker eyeing a UK cost-effectiveness fight now has to weigh not just the negotiating arithmetic but the prospect of a sustained public campaign eroding its position over multiple appraisal cycles.

The next test of this new equilibrium arrives with NICE's forthcoming reappraisals under the revised threshold, expected to bring three to five additional medicines into routine NHS commissioning each year — a small but watchable indicator of whether April's methods changes translate into faster, not just eventual, access.[^15]
