Holyrood pauses AI datacentre planning decisions pending government guidance

The Scottish Parliament building at Holyrood, Edinburgh, where MSPs voted to pause planning decisions on new AI data centres. — Darren J. Prior / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
MSPs voted to freeze planning decisions on new AI data centres until Scotland publishes national guidance, rejecting a full moratorium.

Scotland's parliament has told planning officials to stop deciding whether new AI data centres can be built — but stopped well short of banning them.

MSPs voted on Wednesday for a Labour amendment stating that "no decisions on planning or consenting" of AI data centre applications should be made until the Scottish government publishes national guidance for planning authorities on how to handle them. A rival bid from the Scottish Greens' Patrick Harvie for an outright moratorium on new applications being submitted at all was defeated. Only the Greens backed a full stop; the SNP and Labour lined up behind the softer option of a pause.

In one sentence: MSPs voted to freeze planning decisions on new AI data centres until Scotland publishes national guidance, rejecting a full moratorium.

The vote isn't binding on the Scottish government, and it doesn't touch projects already under construction. What it does is put a political marker down on roughly two dozen hyperscale data centre proposals now working through Scottish planning systems, at a moment when almost none of them have been decided.

Why planners are nervous

The numbers explain the anxiety. Around 24 hyperscale data centre projects have been proposed across Scotland — in Fife, Falkirk, Edinburgh, East Lothian, Inverclyde and the planned Lanarkshire AI Growth Zone. Two of the larger ones alone, a 600MW site at Auchtertool in Fife and a 300MW site at Larbert near Falkirk, would together draw roughly 900 megawatts, about a quarter of Scotland's current peak electricity demand. Campaign group Friends of the Earth Scotland has put the full pipeline's power draw at more than one and a half times peak demand nationally, alongside worries about noise, water consumption for cooling, and land use.

Hannah Mary Goodlad, the SNP's planning minister, told MSPs it was "highly unlikely" any decisions would be taken before Christmas — which is also the timeframe ministers have set for publishing the promised national guidance. In effect, the government is confirming a pause that was, in practice, already happening.

A vote that satisfies almost no one

For campaigners, the outcome landed as a disappointment dressed up as a win. Kim Pratt of Friends of the Earth Scotland said only a moratorium covering all hyperscale data centres, held in place until proper social and environmental safeguards exist, could actually protect communities, arguing that MSPs had "failed to reject data centre plans, fuelled by the greed of big tech." The group also disputes industry job claims, citing research suggesting a 100MW data centre supports as few as 15 direct operational jobs once running.

For the data centre industry, the vote is a milder outcome than a moratorium would have been, but not a clean bill of health either. Developers now face a parliament on record as uneasy about the sector, a government promising new rules whose content is still unknown, and — separately — a direction already in force requiring planning authorities to notify Scottish ministers of every new data centre application within seven days of validation, alongside mandatory environmental impact assessments for large-scale sites.

What it signals beyond Scotland

None of this stops a single data centre being built in England, Ireland or anywhere else hyperscalers are chasing capacity. But it is a data point in a wider pattern: governments across Europe are increasingly willing to treat AI infrastructure as something requiring active permission rather than default approval, even where the political appetite for an outright block isn't there. The SNP-Labour compromise — pause, don't prohibit — reads as an attempt to be seen managing the issue without picking a fight with an industry that governments elsewhere are actively courting.

The next concrete marker is the guidance itself, due by Christmas. Its contents — whether it sets binding limits on power and water use, or simply offers planning authorities a framework to weigh applications against — will do more to shape investment decisions in Scotland than Wednesday's vote did.
