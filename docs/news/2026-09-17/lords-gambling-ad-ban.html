Lords committee calls for comprehensive ban on gambling advertising

The House of Lords chamber at the Palace of Westminster, where the cross-party Liaison Committee's report was published. — UK Government / Wikimedia Commons (OGL v3.0)
In one sentence
A Lords committee wants a tobacco-style ban on gambling ads, but with no legislative teeth yet, it's political noise the market has already discounted.

The House of Lords does not often move markets, but on Wednesday its cross-party Liaison Committee did its best to unsettle a sector that has already had a rough year. In a follow-up report titled Gambling Harm — Time for Action, peers concluded that a comprehensive ban on gambling advertising — covering marketing and sponsorship as well as adverts — is "the most effective policy option" for cutting problem gambling, and urged the government to introduce one "as soon as practicable."

The comparison peers reached for was tobacco. Cigarette advertising was banned outright in the UK in 2002, and the committee's central argument is that gambling promotion should go the same way: tolerated as a legal activity, but no longer sold to the public as entertainment. "Governments have been far too passive in reacting to the transformative explosion of digital advertising," the report says, pointing to the rise of social-media influencers and betting-app push notifications as evidence that voluntary codes have been outpaced by technology.

The numbers behind the push

The committee puts the number of adults in Great Britain gambling in a way that counts as problem gambling at between 1.0 million and 1.5 million, citing harms ranging from debt and relationship breakdown to suicide. The industry, by contrast, spends more than £1 billion a year on advertising in Great Britain, a sum peers argue is disproportionate to any public-health safeguards currently attached to it.

Crucially, the ban as proposed is not absolute. The report carves out on-course advertising at horseracing and greyhound tracks — an industry that leans heavily on bookmaker sponsorship for funding — and asks the government to separately assess the risk profile of National Lottery advertising rather than folding it into the same restrictions automatically. Lord Foster of Bath, who sat on the original 2020 Gambling Industry Committee that this report follows up, put the logic plainly: a ban on most gambling advertising "would reduce gambling and therefore reduce problem gambling and the serious harm it causes."

A government caught between two promises

The report's sharpest line is aimed not at the industry but at Downing Street. Peers say ministers face "a fundamental tension" between Labour's 2024 manifesto commitment to reduce gambling harm and more recent government rhetoric about supporting the sector's growth — a tension sharpened by the fact that Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who took office in July after Keir Starmer's resignation, has spent years campaigning against betting sponsorship of sport as Mayor of Greater Manchester. Whether that history translates into legislation is unclear: this is a select-committee report, not a bill, and the government has made no commitment to act on it.

Why the market shrug is the right call

For Entain and Flutter, the news lands on ground that is already scorched. Flutter's US-listed shares are down roughly half from their level a year ago, a slide driven chiefly by the online-gambling tax increases in last year's Budget and mounting compliance costs rather than advertising rules specifically. Entain has separately set aside £585 million to settle a deferred prosecution agreement with HMRC and has been cutting jobs. Against that backdrop, a report from a committee with no power to legislate — however pointed its language — is unlikely to move valuations much further. The regulatory direction of travel for UK gambling stocks has been priced in for the better part of two years; this is another data point on that path, not a new one.

What would change the calculus is a firm government response. The Department for Culture, Media and Sport has not indicated when, or whether, it will formally reply to the Liaison Committee's findings, and any advertising restriction of this scope would likely require primary legislation and a lengthy consultation. Investors will be watching for the government's next Budget statement and any signal from DCMS on gambling reform timing — that, rather than this report alone, is what would actually reprice the sector.
