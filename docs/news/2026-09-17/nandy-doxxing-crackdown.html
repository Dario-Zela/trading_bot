Culture Secretary Nandy pledges crackdown on 'doxxing' after RNLI leak

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy, who has pledged to criminalise the publication of people's home addresses without consent. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Culture Secretary Nandy pledged a criminal offence for 'doxxing' after RNLI crew were named and addressed online, but no bill or timeline exists yet.

Two Royal National Lifeboat Institution volunteers helped bring around 140 migrants ashore near Portsmouth earlier this month after their dinghy was intercepted in the Channel. Within hours, anti-migrant protesters had published the crew members' names, photographs and home addresses online, branding them "traitors" and "collaborators." Sussex Police is now investigating, and the charity has opened a security review that includes advice to volunteers to avoid wearing RNLI uniforms off-station and permission to close lifeboat stations or shops if staff feel unsafe.

Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy has responded with a pledge to toughen the law against what she and others are calling "doxxing" — publishing someone's personal details, especially a home address, without consent and with intent to harass. She wrote to RNLI staff and volunteers saying she was "shocked and saddened" by what happened in Portsmouth and in a separate incident in Dover, and has said the government will act to make address-leaking of this kind a criminal offence.

"If people think it's acceptable to go around harassing people online and publishing their addresses and other personal attributes, they have another thing coming, because the government is going to act in this space," Nandy said.

A summit, and a no-show

Nandy summoned executives from major social media platforms to a meeting in Whitehall this week to press them on how quickly they remove doxxing content and what proactive steps they take to stop it spreading. Representatives from several platforms attended; X, owned by Elon Musk, did not send anyone and did not respond to the invitation, according to Nandy's office. She called the company's absence an "utter disgrace," adding that police officers and journalists covering the Portsmouth and Dover protests had also been targeted with similar tactics.

RNLI chief executive Peter Sparkes has condemned the abuse directed at volunteers who "selflessly give up their time to keep others safe," noting that families as well as crew members have faced threats. The charity, which relies on roughly 5,600 volunteer crew across more than 230 stations, has framed the leak as part of a wider pattern of digital harassment spilling into real-world risk for people doing unpaid rescue work.

What the pledge actually commits to

What Nandy has not done, so far, is publish a bill, a clause, or a timetable. "Doxxing" has no single legal definition in UK law today; existing protections are scattered across harassment, stalking and data protection statutes, and prosecutors already have some tools — including under the Protection from Harassment Act — to pursue people who publish addresses with intent to intimidate. What Nandy is describing is a more explicit, standalone criminal offence targeting the act of publishing someone's personal data to enable harassment, which would close gaps that currently require proving a broader pattern of harassment or malicious intent under existing statutes.

Until a bill is drafted and a legislative slot found, this is a ministerial promise rather than a change in the law. That distinction matters for anyone assessing what happens next: platforms and data brokers operating in the UK face no new obligations today, and any eventual statute would need to navigate familiar tensions between privacy protection and free expression — the same fault line that shaped earlier fights over the Online Safety Act.

What to watch

The practical test will be whether Nandy's department follows the RNLI meeting with a published consultation or draft clause, which is the usual next step before a bill gets parliamentary time. Sussex Police's investigation into the original leak, and whether it results in any charges under existing law, will also signal how much of this can already be prosecuted without new legislation. Until either lands, this remains a political response to a genuinely ugly episode rather than a regulatory shift with consequences for platforms.
