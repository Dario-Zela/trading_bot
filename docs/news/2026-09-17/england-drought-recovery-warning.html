CLIMATE · DROUGHT · By S. Whitfield · 2026-09-17 · 4 min read

Nearly three-quarters of England still in drought, with months of rain needed

Scar House Reservoir in the Yorkshire Dales, its retreating waterline exposing the walls of a farmstead submerged since the reservoir's construction. — Mhockey / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
England needs 120% of normal rainfall by April to end a drought now leaving reservoirs a fifth low, with four regions set to stay parched into spring 2027.

Nearly three-quarters of England remains officially in drought this week, and the exit is further off than officials had hoped. New Environment Agency modelling, presented to the National Drought Group on 15 September, finds that England needs rainfall equivalent to 120% of the seasonal norm over the next seven months — through to April 2027 — for every region to climb out of drought status. Recent rain has helped, but it has not come close to closing the gap.

This is England's third drought in five years, and the numbers behind it are stark. July was the driest on record; August was joint-hottest. Five heatwaves rolled through the summer, and demand for water climbed by up to 30% on the hottest days even as supply fell. Summer rainfall came in at just 64% of what would normally be expected, with the south and east of the country receiving barely half.

The reservoirs are the tell

Reservoir storage is the clearest single gauge of how deep the deficit runs, and it currently sits at 56.9% of capacity nationally — around a fifth below what is normal for mid-September, according to the Environment Agency's weekly assessment covering the week to 10 September. Twelve individual reservoirs or reservoir groups are now under half full. Groundwater levels and river flows, the slower-moving indicators that typically lag behind rainfall by weeks or months, are also depressed across most of the country.

"We're experiencing our third drought in five years, and the recent rainfall — while welcome — is not yet enough to replenish all the water we've used this summer," said Emma Hardy, the water minister. Philip Duffy, chief executive of the Environment Agency, has described the situation as "very serious." Temporary use bans remain in force across large parts of the country, affecting an estimated 30 million people.

Helen Wakeham of the Environment Agency put the underlying arithmetic plainly: recovery "is a very long process and only complete once reservoirs, rivers and groundwaters are back to normal" — not simply once the taps stop restricting garden hoses.

A patchwork recovery, region by region

The Environment Agency's central forecast — built on a Met Office outlook for a wetter-than-average autumn — has some regions bouncing back well before spring. Cumbria and Lancashire, along with Greater Manchester, Merseyside and Cheshire, could return to normal status as early as October or November. Most of the rest of the country would see what officials call a "fragile recovery" by December.

Four areas are the exception: Devon and Cornwall, East Anglia, the Thames Valley and the West Midlands are projected to remain in drought through to April 2027 even under that optimistic scenario. The Environment Agency puts the odds at roughly one in four that half of England is still in drought by next spring, though it rates the chance of a severe drought — on the scale of 1976 — at only around 1%, since none has occurred since then.

That regional split matters because it lands squarely on the growing season. A drought that persists into the Thames Valley and East Anglia — two of England's most intensive arable regions — through April narrows the window farmers have to prepare soil and establish spring crops before summer stress sets in again. Root vegetables such as potatoes and carrots have already shown variable yields this year, livestock farmers have been drawing down winter feed stocks early, and a bluetongue outbreak has been aggravated by the heat. Lower river flows also tighten the margin for industries that rely on water abstraction licences, including thermal power generation, which needs river or reservoir water for cooling.

The response, and what comes next

Government and regulators are treating this as a structural problem rather than a one-off. Ministers have announced a £65m drought relief package for farmers, including £15m earmarked for on-farm reservoirs, plus a further £100m for extreme weather response. Longer term, nine new reservoirs are planned in England by 2050, and changes to the Planning Act are intended to speed up approvals for the kind of infrastructure that took decades to build the existing network.

None of that changes the immediate picture. The National Drought Group is now meeting weekly rather than monthly, and its next assessment will turn on how much of the forecast autumn rain actually arrives — the Met Office's Sarah Davies has flagged a warmer, wetter autumn as the base case, but base cases in a drought this entrenched have a habit of underperforming. The real test comes in November and December, when this year's rainfall either starts closing the 120% gap or confirms that the driest regions are heading into a second consecutive dry spring.
