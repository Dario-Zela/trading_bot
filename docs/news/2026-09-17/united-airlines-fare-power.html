United Airlines says fare power is offsetting rising fuel costs

A United Airlines Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner — the carrier says fare increases are covering most of this year's fuel-cost surge. — Bill Abbott / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
United says fare hikes are offsetting most of a $6bn fuel-cost surge from the Middle East oil rally, with little sign yet of demand cracking.

United Airlines said this week that its pricing power is more than keeping pace with the surge in jet fuel costs triggered by the Middle East oil rally — a claim, if it holds, that cuts against the standard assumption that energy shocks squeeze airline margins until demand cracks.

The numbers behind the claim are substantial. Jet fuel in major U.S. markets has averaged $4.23 a gallon in recent weeks, up nearly 70% from levels before fighting between the U.S., Israel and Iran began on February 28 and disrupted an estimated fifth of global oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude, which was trading in the low $80s before the war, has been as high as $107.50 a barrel this month — a four-month peak — before easing slightly on Wednesday to around $105.83 after U.S. officials said a Saudi pipeline damaged in recent attacks would resume operations within days. United's own math: roughly $6 billion in additional fuel expense for 2026 against what it had budgeted in January, with second-quarter fuel costs alone up 84% year-over-year to $2.3 billion.

Against that, United has raised fares by more than 20% over the course of the year. In the second quarter, yields rose 12% and total revenue climbed 16% to $17.7 billion, enough for United to recover about half of the quarter's fuel-cost increase through pricing alone — a share management expects to rise to 80–90% in the third quarter and to full recovery by the fourth. Chief executive Scott Kirby told analysts that demand had shown "surprisingly little elasticity" in response to the higher fares, and the airline raised its full-year adjusted profit guidance to $9–$11 a share even as the fuel bill grew.

Why the pricing is sticking

Kirby's explanation goes beyond fuel. He points to the collapse of Spirit Airlines, which pulled meaningful low-cost domestic capacity out of the market, alongside rising labor, maintenance and airport fees that were pushing fares up independent of the oil shock. Fuel now accounts for 31.4% of industry operating costs, up from 25.4% a year ago, according to IATA figures, which separately project the average one-way fare climbing to $193 in 2026 from $180 in 2025. Kirby's bet is that the combination is structural rather than cyclical — enough, he says, to have permanently retired the era of $9 fares to Central America.

Not everyone on Wall Street is convinced the fare gains survive contact with more capacity. United and American are both adding international routes — United alone is launching ten new long-haul destinations — and skeptics argue that growth could eventually undercut the pricing discipline that has let carriers pass fuel costs through so cleanly. The test arrives with United's third-quarter results, expected in mid-October, which will show whether the 80–90% fuel-cost recovery materializes as guided, and whether bookings for the back half of the year hold up as new capacity lands.
