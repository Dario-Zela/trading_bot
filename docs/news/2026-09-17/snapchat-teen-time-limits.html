Snapchat CEO open to daily time limits for teen users

Snap co-founder and CEO Evan Spiegel, who told the BBC the company would be "willing to implement" daily time limits for teen users. — Steve Jennings / TechCrunch, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Snap CEO Evan Spiegel told the BBC the company would be "willing to implement" default daily time limits for teens, but set no timeline.

Evan Spiegel is not making any promises. But he is not saying no, either.

The Snapchat chief executive told the BBC this week that his company would be "willing to implement" default daily time limits for teenage users — a notable shift in tone from a platform that has spent years building parental-control features around the edges of its app rather than capping usage outright. Asked when such a limit might arrive, Spiegel offered nothing concrete. "That's something we've talked about internally," he said. "I don't have more to share today."

"I think that's a really good example of something that we'd be willing to implement... I think it is an important step forward in the industry in terms of really identifying industry norms."

Meta set the terms

The timing is not a coincidence. In late August, Meta settled a sprawling legal battle with US state attorneys general over allegations that it knowingly designed Facebook and Instagram to be addictive to young users, agreeing to pay up to roughly $17-18 billion and to build a default two-hour daily limit for teens into both apps, along with a midnight-to-6am access block unless a parent opts in.

Buried in that settlement is a mechanism aimed squarely at Meta's rivals. Of the total payout, about $12.7 billion is guaranteed. The remaining roughly $5.3 billion is conditional: Meta only has to pay it if YouTube and TikTok adopt comparable protections — a one-hour daily limit, night-mode restrictions and stronger age verification — and contribute a matching sum themselves. Meta has publicly called on the industry, including Snap, to follow its lead. Neither TikTok nor Google, which owns YouTube, has yet said what it will do.

Snap was not a party to the settlement and faces no such financial trigger. But it shares the same regulatory audience, and its user base — younger, on average, than Instagram's or Facebook's — makes it an obvious next target for the same argument: that platforms built for engagement need a built-in brake for minors.

What Snap already has

The company points to Family Center, a feature launched in 2022 that lets parents see who their teenager is messaging and how often, without reading the content of conversations. It is a visibility tool, not a limit — parents can watch usage climb, but nothing stops it. A default cap, of the kind Meta has now committed to, would be a different category of intervention: automatic, opt-out rather than opt-in, and enforced by the platform rather than delegated to a parent who has to go looking for it.

That gap is precisely what Spiegel's comments gesture toward closing, without actually closing it. "We want to figure out how we can be part of the solution," he told the BBC — language built for a moment when doing nothing is starting to look conspicuous, but committing to specifics is not yet required.

Why hedge rather than commit

For Snap, the calculation is straightforward. Time limits cut into the metric the company reports to investors every quarter — daily active users and time spent — at a moment when it is already fighting to hold user growth against TikTok and Instagram. Committing publicly, with no legal or financial gun to its head the way Meta had, would be giving away leverage for nothing. Signalling openness costs it very little and buys goodwill with regulators in the UK, EU and US who have Snap, alongside YouTube, Pinterest and Reddit, on their list of platforms to scrutinise over teen safety this year.

That is the pattern to watch: a statement of principle now, engineering and a launch date later — if at all. Meta needed a settlement to force the issue. Snap, so far, only needs a headline.

What's next

The next concrete marker is whether TikTok or YouTube respond to Meta's conditional offer with their own commitments; that would sharpen the pressure on Snap considerably, since "the whole industry moved except us" is a harder position to hold than "we're thinking about it." Until Snap names a mechanism, a threshold and a date, Tuesday's interview is a statement of intent, not a policy.
