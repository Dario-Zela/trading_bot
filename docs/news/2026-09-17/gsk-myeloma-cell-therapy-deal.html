GSK strikes deal on multiple myeloma T-cell therapy

GSK's global head office in Brentford, west London. — Ian Wilson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
GSK will pay up to $750m for a pre-clinical Chimagen T-cell therapy for multiple myeloma, its second such deal with the biotech and part of a wider UK pharma bet on cell therapy.

GSK has agreed to acquire a still-experimental blood cancer drug from a Chinese biotech, in a deal worth up to $750 million that extends the pharma major's push into next-generation cell and immune therapies for multiple myeloma.

The target is a trispecific T-cell engager developed by Chimagen Biosciences, a privately held San Diego- and Beijing-based biotech. Unlike bispecific engagers already on the market, the molecule is designed to bind T cells while simultaneously latching onto two separate tumour-associated antigens — an architecture GSK says should produce a deeper, more durable response with better tolerability than existing engagers. The programme has not yet reached the clinic; GSK and Chimagen expect it to enter Phase I trials in 2027.

"Today's deal secures a promising T cell engager and advances GSK's leadership goals in blood cancer," said Hesham Abdullah, GSK's senior vice president and global head of oncology R&D. "The agreement complements our existing portfolio in multiple myeloma, adding a new potential option to address the different needs of patients facing this complex disease."

A second bite at the same biotech

The terms disclosed by GSK are the customary mix of an unspecified upfront payment plus development and commercial milestones, totalling up to $750 million if the drug clears every regulatory and sales hurdle. That structure is designed to cap GSK's near-term cash outlay on an asset that is, by the company's own timeline, at least six years from any possible approval.

It is also the second time GSK has bought a pipeline asset off Chimagen's shelf. A 2024 agreement, worth up to $850 million, handed GSK CMG1A46, a dual CD19/CD20-targeted T-cell engager now in early-stage trials for B-cell malignancies. Chimagen's chief executive, Zhenhao Zhou, framed the follow-on deal as a natural extension of that relationship: "Working with GSK combines our precision T-cell engager programme with their global development and commercial capabilities to redefine care for multiple myeloma patients."

Multiple myeloma is the third most common blood cancer, with roughly 180,000 new diagnoses worldwide each year, and remains incurable despite a wave of newer antibody and cell therapies. GSK already sells Blenrep, an antibody-drug conjugate for the disease, giving the Chimagen asset a natural home in an existing commercial and medical infrastructure rather than requiring GSK to build a franchise from scratch. The company points to a US market for multiple myeloma T-cell engagers that it expects to exceed $10 billion by 2032 — the kind of figure that justifies a nine-figure bet on a pre-clinical molecule.

Part of a wider pattern

The deal lands amid a broader scramble among UK-listed pharma majors to buy their way into cell and T-cell therapy rather than rely on internal discovery. AstraZeneca has been the most aggressive: it paid up to $1 billion for Belgian biotech EsoBiotec, whose viral-delivery platform aims to engineer immune cells inside the body rather than in a lab, and separately acquired Neogene Therapeutics for its T-cell receptor technology. Both moves, like GSK's Chimagen deals, target haematological cancers where engineered T cells have shown some of oncology's most striking results — and some of its thorniest manufacturing and durability problems.

The read-across is that traditional small-molecule and antibody pipelines are no longer where either company sees its next decade of growth. GSK's chief executive, Luke Miels, has separately struck what the company calls its largest deal in more than a decade to add near-launch lung cancer assets, underscoring that oncology, not GSK's legacy vaccines and respiratory franchises, is where fresh capital is being deployed.

Markets, notably, were unmoved. GSK shares ticked up less than 1% on the news, trading around 1,869p against a previous close of 1,854.5p — a reaction consistent with investors treating this as a sensible, low-risk pipeline addition rather than a re-rating event. A same-day upgrade from Berenberg to "buy", citing GSK's late-stage portfolio and accelerating deal pace, did more to move the stock than the Chimagen transaction itself.

What's next

The absence of disclosed upfront figures leaves the market to judge the deal mostly on strategic logic rather than price. The next concrete marker is the Phase I start, expected in 2027 — until then, the asset remains a bet on architecture and antigen selection rather than clinical data. Investors will also be watching whether GSK's pattern of milestone-heavy, capped-risk biotech deals continues, or whether it eventually pairs its oncology ambitions with a larger, AstraZeneca-style acquisition of its own.
