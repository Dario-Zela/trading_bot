King acknowledges 'suffering of the past' as Caribbean tour begins

In one sentence
King Charles has opened a Caribbean tour acknowledging the 'suffering of the past', with slavery's legacy and reparations due at the Commonwealth summit in Antigua from 1 November.

King Charles has begun a tour of the Caribbean at a moment when the question of what Britain owes for slavery is no longer a footnote to royal travel. It is the agenda. The tour opens in The Bahamas, runs on to a state visit to Guyana, and ends at the Commonwealth heads of government meeting in Antigua and Barbuda. Reporting by BBC UK and the Guardian UK frames his remarks as an acknowledgement of the "suffering of the past", and says he is due to discuss slavery's legacy with Commonwealth leaders. That reporting has not been independently checked here, and the exact wording of his Bahamas remarks is not confirmed.

What the palace has said

The outline was set out in early October. Buckingham Palace said the programme would not shy away from "the darkest days of our past", while concentrating mainly on each country's present and future. Reports at the time were just as clear about what to expect from the visit: no apology on behalf of the British state, and no movement on money.

That is the government's line. Ministers rejected reparations again last month, after a Jamaican delegation filed a petition addressed to the King. The King acts on ministerial advice on these tours, so the gap between what he can say and what some governments want him to say is built in.

The itinerary

The schedule, as reported, runs like this:

  • The Bahamas, 27–29 October
  • Guyana, state visit, 29–30 October
  • Antigua and Barbuda, 1–4 November, for the Commonwealth summit (CHOGM), with the Queen

He is also reported to be due to speak at the summit itself. Reuters has said reparations are likely to feature prominently there.

A familiar form of words

The vocabulary is not new. In Barbados in 2021, as Prince of Wales, Charles called slavery an "appalling atrocity" that "forever stains our history". At the Commonwealth summit in Kigali in 2022 he spoke of his "personal sorrow at the suffering of so many" and of deepening his understanding of slavery's enduring impact. In 2024 in Samoa he told leaders the past cannot be changed, though he said it was possible to learn from it.

Each time, the language has stopped short of apology. Caribbean governments have noticed. Many want more than sorrow: an apology, funding for development and health, and a settled place for reparations in Commonwealth business.

Why the tour is watched

The backdrop is not friendly to royal set pieces. The 2022 tour by Prince William and Catherine, through Belize, Jamaica and The Bahamas, was widely criticised as tone-deaf on slavery. Barbados removed the monarch as head of state in 2021. Debate about following suit has simmered elsewhere in the region.

That is why a form of words matters. A royal acknowledgement, however carefully hedged, gives member states a reference point to cite when pressing their claims at Antigua. It does not bind the government that advises him. It would also be possible for the summit to take the sorrow as sufficient and move on, which is what critics fear.

What to watch

The King's Guyana remarks on 29–30 October come first, then his speech at the Antigua summit from 1 November. The test is whether the communiqué text on reparations moves, or whether the royal language is left to stand in for it.
