China's families now support more old people than children

In one sentence
With births at 7.92 million in 2025 and 323 million people over 60, China's shrinking families are absorbing elder-care costs that weigh on spending and strain pensions.

China's households are being asked to carry something their parents' generation did not: a growing number of elderly relatives and almost no siblings or children to share the load. Births have fallen so far, and the population over 60 has grown so large, that the cost of old age is shifting from the state and the extended family to the couple in the middle.

The official figures for 2025, published in January, show the scale. The population fell by 3.39 million to about 1.405 billion, the fourth consecutive annual decline. Births dropped to 7.92 million from 9.54 million in 2024, which was itself a record low, and deaths rose to 11.31 million. The birth rate was 5.63 per 1,000 people, the death rate 8.04. Some 323 million people were aged 60 or over, about 23% of the population, and 223.65 million were 65 or over.

The squeeze in the middle

The arithmetic is simple. The one-child policy, in force from 1979, produced a generation of only children. Many of them are now in their 30s, 40s and 50s, and each may have two parents and up to four grandparents to look after. The births of recent years, meanwhile, mean the next generation will be smaller still. A fertility rate reported at roughly 0.96 children per woman in 2025 is far below the level needed to keep the population stable.

A rough calculation from the published numbers puts the 60-plus population at about 38 for every 100 people aged 16 to 59. That is not an official dependency ratio, and the working-age band is not the same as the pool of people who actually pay into pensions. But it shows the direction. A couple with one child can end up supporting four parents and a child, with no brothers or sisters to split the costs.

Beijing's stated preference is for most older people to stay in their own homes, supported by family. That keeps the bill off public budgets and relies on filial duty to fill the gap. Researchers who study the generation of only children describe adults caring largely alone, juggling jobs, their own children and the physical and financial demands of looking after parents. Roughly a million families are estimated to have lost their only child, leaving them with no one to provide that care at all.

A later retirement

The state is also asking people to work longer. From 1 January 2025, China began phasing in a higher retirement age, to be completed over 15 years. The normal age for men rises from 60 to 63. For women it rises from 50 to 55 for those in factory and similar jobs, and from 55 to 58 for those in other roles. The change eases pressure on pension funds by keeping contributors paying in for longer and delaying payouts.

It also lands on the same households. Workers who stay on the payroll for a few more years have less time to provide unpaid care, which pushes families toward paid help or private arrangements that many cannot afford. The pension system itself is uneven. Urban employees receive far more than rural residents, and the funds that depend on current workers are weakest in regions where the young have left. Pension budgets are already under strain as large cohorts leave the workforce, according to reports on the 2025 data, though this article found no comprehensive published figure for the system's funding gap.

What it means for spending

The consequence for the economy is a less visible one. Household income that goes on medical bills, care workers or a parent's rent is not available for spending on cars, appliances, travel or brands. Households with elderly dependants also tend to save more as a precaution, since they expect further care costs and have little confidence in what the state will provide. That works against the government's stated aim of a growth model led by consumption rather than investment and exports.

The pressure is also unusually durable. Projections cited in recent reporting put the 60-plus population at roughly 400 million to 420 million by 2035, a rise of around 80 million to 100 million in a decade. Foreign firms that sell into China, from consumer goods to luxury and carmakers, are selling to a middle class whose discretionary budget is being drawn down by care obligations. Some companies in elderly care, medical devices and insurance stand to gain, but the largest consumer categories depend on the younger households under most pressure.

What to watch

Two things will show whether policy is changing course. The first is the annual population release from the National Bureau of Statistics each January, which will show whether births continue to fall from 7.92 million. The second is any move from Beijing to fund long-term care more directly, through public insurance or subsidised services, rather than leaving it to families. Until one of these changes, the household remains the main source of support for China's older people, even as households shrink.
