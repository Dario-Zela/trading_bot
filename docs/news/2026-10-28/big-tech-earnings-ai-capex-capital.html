Big Tech earnings test whether AI capex is becoming revenue, as capital and China's AI crash loom

In one sentence
Microsoft and Alphabet report this week, and investors will judge them on whether cloud growth justifies AI capex that the big four plan to push to about $725bn in 2026.

Two of the largest spenders on artificial intelligence report quarterly results this week, and the question hanging over both is less whether they beat forecasts than what the money is buying. Microsoft is expected to extend a run of double beats, meaning results ahead of expectations on both revenue and earnings, according to a Benzinga preview. Alphabet reports in the same week, with some bears arguing that its shares carry a technical advantage. The preview names AI capital expenditure and cloud growth as the central questions for both.

The triage material gives no consensus figures for either company's quarter. This piece therefore leaves out forecasts for revenue, earnings and cloud growth, and says so rather than guessing.

The spending the numbers have to justify

The scale of the outlay is documented in earlier reporting. After first-quarter results in the spring, a Financial Times compilation put the combined 2026 capital spending plans of Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta at about $725bn, up 77% from last year's record of $410bn. Alphabet's own 2026 capex guidance was $175bn–$185bn, well above consensus at the time. On the revenue side, Microsoft's Azure grew 40% in the quarter reported in April, ahead of consensus of roughly 39%, and the company guided to 39%–40% growth in constant currency for the following quarter.

Those are April figures and may since have been superseded. They set the terms of the test, though. Capex is a large share of the earnings growth priced into the major indices, so the market is looking for evidence that cloud revenue is rising fast enough to carry it. A beat on headline revenue and earnings answers little if capex guidance climbs faster than cloud growth. A steady Azure growth rate alongside a higher spending guide would pose the reverse problem. Neither company's quarterly figures settle the matter on their own. The pattern across several quarters does.

Who pays for the build-out

A second thread concerns where the money comes from. Benzinga frames the fundraising and listing plans of Anthropic, Meta and SpaceX as a test of whether public and private markets can supply enough capital for AI. The source gives no deal sizes, valuations, dates or investor names, and nothing in it shows that markets are strained. The framing is a hypothesis, not a finding.

It matters to the earnings week because the largest spenders fund much of their build-out from operating cash flow. If the returns on that spending disappoint, the cost of capital for the wider group of AI borrowers and issuers is likely to rise with it. Anthropic's listing filing is covered separately in this edition.

A warning from China, with caveats

The third thread is a sell-off in Chinese AI stocks. A Benzinga headline says they have lost $480bn in under four months. That figure could not be independently verified, and a search found no corroborating source for it. The surrounding context is better established. The South China Morning Post reported that Chinese AI stocks rose 65% in the first half of 2026 before bubble fears began to weigh on sentiment, and Bloomberg Law reported on June 30 that the year was going badly for Chinese equities after a strong run in 2025.

Two opinions are attached to the story. Michael Burry, the investor known for the 2008 "Big Short", is quoted comparing today's market to the eve of the dot-com crash. An unnamed commentator argues that the US shows the same signs as China's AI-stock collapse. Both are attributed opinions and neither is new reporting. Burry's bearish positioning is documented: SCMP reported in July that he had increased bets against AI-linked stocks, and a November 2025 report said he held put options on Nvidia and Palantir with more than $1bn in notional value. A bear who is positioned for a fall is not a neutral witness, and a drop in one country's stocks does not show that another's is next.

What to watch

The specific tests are concrete. For Microsoft, the signal is Azure growth against the 39%–40% guided in April, set beside any change in the spending outlook. For Alphabet, the signal is Google Cloud growth against capex guidance that was already $175bn–$185bn. The pattern the market is looking for is revenue growth that holds or accelerates while spending guidance rises more slowly. If cloud growth slows while spending guidance climbs, the sceptical case, including the China comparison, gets its first piece of hard US evidence.
