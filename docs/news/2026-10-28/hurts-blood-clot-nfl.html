Hurts played with a blood clot: what it means for the NFL

In one sentence
Jalen Hurts played a full game in London with a calf clot later found to be a DVT with lung emboli; the NFLPA wants an inquiry while the NFL says protocols were followed.

The premise has moved well past unverified. Jalen Hurts, the Philadelphia Eagles quarterback, played all 62 offensive snaps of a game in London on 11 October with a blood clot in his left calf. Within eight days that clot had been re-diagnosed as a deep vein thrombosis with clots in both lungs, and his season was over. The players' union wants an investigation, and the NFL says its protocols were followed.

What the Eagles say happened

The team's own account, as reported by the Philadelphia Inquirer, runs as follows. Hurts was cleated in the calf in a loss to the Rams on 4 October. He reported it to the training room on 8 October and practised fully on the 9th. After the flight to London, an ultrasound on the 10th showed what the team called a "superficial blood clot". Eagles medical staff and London doctors judged that he could play safely. They recommended a follow-up scan within 72 hours of the game and the flight home.

Hurts played, and the Eagles lost 19-17 to Jacksonville. The follow-up scan on 14 October showed "movement in the small blood clot". He was ruled out of the next game, against Carolina, and placed on injured reserve on the 17th. On the 19th a joint statement from the Eagles and his agent, Nicole Lynn, said further testing had found deep vein thrombosis and bilateral pulmonary emboli. Hurts is clinically stable, on blood thinners and barred from contact. The team expects a full recovery.

No fixed timeline for his return has been given. The earliest the injured-reserve rules allow is 22 November against the Steelers.

What the clearance process looks like

The league's case rests on process, not outcome. Dr Allen Sills, the NFL's chief medical officer, said flight decisions for players with clots depend on factors such as location and patient history, and that a panel of experts inside and outside the club weighs in: "This isn't just one doctor saying yay or nay." NFL officials spoke to the team's doctors and trainers and to the Cleveland Clinic London. Jeff Miller, an executive vice-president, said Hurts was evaluated at a hospital the day before the game. He disputed a report that Hurts had gone more than 30 hours without sleep, saying the player slept seven or eight hours.

Commissioner Roger Goodell said "the protocols were followed" and that he was "more worried about his health, ultimately". The league has also said the Eagles did not breach its injury-report policy and will not be punished.

The NFL Players Association is unconvinced. It says the league had told it no review of Hurts's care was planned, and it has asked for a full investigation. Goodell has since said the league would welcome a review. Reports differ on whether one has formally begun, and on its scope, who would run it and when it would report. Senator Lisa Blunt Rochester has also written to Goodell with questions and asked for a briefing.

The question that remains

The dispute is narrower than the headline suggests. Nobody has said the Eagles ignored a rule. The question is whether a rule that was followed is a good one: whether a superficial clot, found the day before a game and a long-haul flight from kick-off, should have been enough to clear a quarterback, and whether a protocol that leans on club doctors' judgement can be checked by anyone else. A superficial clot is usually a less serious condition than a deep one, and the Eagles say the initial reading was reasonable. The later diagnosis does not by itself show it was wrong. It does show how little room the original call left for error.

What to watch is whether the review becomes a formal one with a published scope, and whether the league's answer to Congress changes the clearance rules for players with clots. Hurts's own recovery will set the pace. He cannot resume contact football until he is off blood thinners.
