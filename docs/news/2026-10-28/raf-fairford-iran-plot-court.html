Two in court over alleged Iran-linked plot at RAF Fairford

In one sentence
Two 24-year-old men are charged under the National Security Act over RAF Fairford; police say the intelligence service involved is believed to be Iran's, which Tehran denies.

Two men have appeared at Westminster Magistrates' Court over an incident at RAF Fairford, the Gloucestershire airbase that US bombers had been using for strikes on Iran. Police say the foreign intelligence service one of them is accused of helping is believed to be Iran's. Charges are allegations, though. No court has yet tested whether Tehran was behind what happened at the base, and the circumstances remain largely unexplained.

What the men are charged with

Both men are 24 and both are charged under the National Security Act, which in 2023 replaced much of the UK's older espionage law.

Mohmed Abdulkarim, a British national from London, is charged with conduct likely to materially assist a foreign intelligence service between 31 August and 28 September. Police have said the country concerned is believed to be Iran. The wording matters. The offence is about conduct likely to help a foreign intelligence service. It is not a charge of acting as an Iranian agent, and the charge sheet does not by itself show direction from Tehran.

Nouman Essghir, a dual British-Iranian national, faces one count of entering a prohibited place for a purpose prejudicial to the UK, dated 22 August. He was first arrested outside the base on 27 September. Essghir was remanded in custody, and the case returns on 20 November at the Old Bailey.

The charges cover different periods and different conduct. Nothing in the public account links Essghir's alleged entry in August to the intelligence-service allegation against Abdulkarim, so the two should not be read as one single charged plot.

How the case unfolded

Five British men were arrested outside RAF Fairford in the early hours of 27 September. US bombers that had flown from the base against Iran were withdrawn on security grounds shortly afterwards. Six people remain on bail, four of them British nationals arrested outside the base that night, according to police.

Politics got ahead of the courts. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said earlier this month that activity at Fairford was likely to have been coordinated in Iran. Tehran's ambassador to Britain denied it. A prime minister's assessment, drawn from intelligence the public has not seen, is not evidence in a criminal trial, and the defendants are presumed innocent.

The bail decisions drew scrutiny. Assistant Commissioner Laurence Taylor, head of National Counter Terrorism Policing, defended them:

"We know the decisions made during our investigations can sometimes appear illogical to those on the outside."

He said they had led to "swifter progress, better evidence" and secured the charges. Police have not said whether further charges are expected against those still on bail.

Why it matters beyond the dock

Fairford is one of the principal UK bases for American long-range aircraft. That makes it a natural target for anyone seeking to disrupt, or simply watch, US operations against Iran. If the prosecution establishes the foreign-intelligence link, the case would show Iranian-linked activity reaching a British military site used by US forces, in the middle of a conflict in which Britain is a host rather than a combatant.

The stakes are practical. The withdrawal of the bombers shows how quickly a security concern can change US basing decisions. Any UK review of perimeter protection, vetting and policing around allied sites will draw on what emerges, and so will Washington's confidence in the arrangement. Britain has also accused Iran-linked actors of hostile activity before, and Tehran has consistently denied such claims, so the evidential bar here is higher than the political rhetoric suggests.

What the public does not know is still considerable: what the men are alleged to have done at or near the base, what was gathered, and who they were allegedly working for. Prosecutors will have to set that out. Much of it may come only at trial, and some of it may be heard in closed session if sensitive intelligence is involved.

What to watch

The next fixed date is 20 November at the Old Bailey, when Essghir's case is heard. The first thing to watch is whether the Crown puts a state link into open court, or leaves it in the charge wording alone. After that: whether police charge anyone among the six on bail, and whether the government announces any change to security around bases hosting US aircraft.
