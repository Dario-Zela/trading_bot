Insiders say Rosebank would not breach UK emissions targets

In one sentence
Unnamed insiders say approving Rosebank fits UK emissions targets, but the claim is unconfirmed and hinges on how downstream scope 3 emissions are counted.

Whether the UK can approve a large new North Sea oilfield and still hit its climate targets is a question that has dogged the Rosebank project for years. Unnamed government insiders now reportedly say the answer is yes. The claim, attributed to anonymous sources and carried by the Guardian's politics coverage, has not been confirmed on the record by any minister or department, and the timing of any decision has not been verified.

That caveat matters. An anonymous assurance is a signal about where thinking sits inside government. It is not a policy position, and it is not a legal determination.

What is being claimed

The reported assertion is narrow: that approving Rosebank would be compatible with the UK's emissions targets. No official has been identified as saying so, and the sourcing gives no detail on the method behind the view. That gap is the whole story. "Compatible" can mean very different things depending on what is counted.

Rosebank lies west of Shetland. Prior coverage names Equinor as operator, with Ithaca Energy as the main partner. Neither company's current stake or stated position has been checked for this article, and both should be confirmed before the shareholding is treated as settled.

Why the arithmetic is contested

The dispute turns on scope 3 emissions: the carbon released when oil and gas are burned by customers, rather than in extracting and processing it. Production emissions from a platform are a small share of the lifecycle total. Most of the climate impact of a field sits in the combustion of its output.

In June 2024 the UK Supreme Court ruled, in a case involving a Surrey oil site, that environmental assessments for fossil fuel projects must consider those downstream emissions. Rosebank's original consent was later challenged on that basis in the Scottish courts, and as I understand the record it was set aside, sending the decision back to ministers. The details of that ruling and the government's subsequent guidance on assessing scope 3 emissions should be checked against the primary documents.

This is where an "compatible with targets" claim becomes difficult to read. There are at least two ways it could be true:

  • A territorial argument. UK carbon budgets cover emissions released within UK borders. Oil exported and burned abroad does not count against them, so a field can be "compatible" in a narrow accounting sense whatever its global effect.
  • A substitution argument. Ministers could contend that domestic production displaces imports, which carry their own emissions, and that the UK will need some oil and gas through the transition anyway.

Neither argument removes the scope 3 question. Campaigners contend that the courts have required downstream emissions to be weighed in the decision, not set aside by accounting convention. A government that approves Rosebank on territorial logic alone would be inviting a fresh challenge.

What it means for producers and policy

For the companies involved, the stakes are practical. A project of this size involves years of capital commitment, and the licensing and consent history has already cost time. A clear, defensible route to approval would matter as much to them as the decision itself.

For policy, the claim touches the wider North Sea debate: whether new licences are consistent with net zero, how much weight energy security carries, and how far the transition can lean on a managed decline of existing fields. Government has previously signalled it would judge fields case by case rather than ban new development outright. A reported view that Rosebank fits within targets would suggest that case-by-case test leans toward approval.

What to watch

Three things would turn the report from rumour into policy. First, an on-record statement from the energy secretary or the department on the decision timetable. Second, the published scope 3 assessment and any updated environmental statement for the field, which would show how downstream emissions were counted. Third, the response of the claimants who won the earlier challenge, who have every incentive to test a new consent in court if the reasoning looks thin.

Until those appear, the insiders' claim tells readers what some in government believe, and little more about what has been decided.
