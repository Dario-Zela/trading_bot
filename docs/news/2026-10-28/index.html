WORLD · CHINA · By C. Tanaka

China's families now support more old people than children

China's population has now shrunk for three years running, 2022 through 2024, the first back-to-back declines in decades. Births in 2024 fell to about 9.54 million, a record low, and the country's households are feeling the effect in a new way: for many families, the number of elderly relatives they are expected to support now outweighs the number of children who can share that load.

For most of the post-1949 era, the state and the extended family divided the work of caring for retirees, and a large cohort of children meant the burden was spread thin. That balance is tilting. As fewer young people enter the workforce, pension funds that depend on contributions from current workers face a shrinking base, and more of the cost of old-age care is shifting onto individual households. Money that might otherwise go to spending or saving is increasingly committed to parents and grandparents.

Beijing began phasing in a higher retirement age on 1 January 2025, raising the normal age for men from 60 to 63 and for women from 50 or 55 to 55 or 58, depending on job type. The change is designed to ease pressure on pension funds, but it also keeps people in work for longer.

The squeeze matters for Beijing's plans to make consumption a bigger engine of growth, and for foreign companies that sell into China's domestic market. Watch for how household spending and savings rates respond over the coming year, and whether policy moves from pension reform toward direct support for family care.
