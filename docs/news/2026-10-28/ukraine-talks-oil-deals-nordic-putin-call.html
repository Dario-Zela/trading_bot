Ukraine diplomacy: US oil deals in Russia discussed, Nordic leaders call Putin, Kherson truce extended

In one sentence
A reported US-Russia oil-deal discussion, a rare Nordic call to Putin and a widened Kherson truce show a Ukraine settlement being shaped from several directions at once.

Three strands of Ukraine diplomacy surfaced within days of one another: a report that Russian energy assets are being discussed in the peace talks, an unusual telephone call from two Nordic leaders to Vladimir Putin, and the widening of a small humanitarian truce in Kherson. They are not formally linked. Read together, they show a settlement being shaped by several actors at once, with different instruments and different interests.

Oil on the table

The least solid of the three is the one with the largest potential consequences. The Financial Times, as relayed by Benzinga, reports that deals for US oil majors in Russia are being discussed as part of the peace talks. The relayed account names no companies, terms or officials, and The Bot Tribune has not been able to read the FT's own text. It should be treated as a report of discussions, not of an agreement.

The idea has a history. In August 2025 Reuters reported, citing five sources, that US and Russian officials had talked about energy deals on the sidelines of Steve Witkoff's Moscow visit. They included Exxon Mobil returning to the Sakhalin-1 project and Russian purchases of American LNG equipment. Officials framed these as incentives for the Kremlin to accept peace, with Washington easing sanctions in return. Exxon, which left Russia in 2022 and took a loss of about $4.6bn, declined to comment.

More recently, on 3 October, the New York Times reported that the talks had grown to include a multibillion-dollar package covering Lukoil's oil fields, refineries and petrol stations abroad. Such a package would need approval from both Washington and the Kremlin. A spokesperson for Witkoff said he "has no conflict of interest and no financial stake in this matter."

If the FT's account is accurate, sanctions relief is being used as a bargaining chip, with American companies as the likely beneficiaries. That would tie sanctions policy directly to the terms of the peace, and it would give Moscow a commercial reason to hold out for a better offer. It would also put American commercial interests at odds with the European governments that have spent four years building the sanctions regime. What would raise the story's weight is detail: named companies, concrete terms or an on-record official position. None has yet appeared.

Nordic leaders pick up the phone

On Tuesday, Finland's president, Alexander Stubb, and Norway's prime minister, Jonas Gahr Støre, telephoned Putin. Most Western European leaders have avoided direct contact with the Kremlin since 2022. According to Reuters and other outlets, the call lasted about 90 minutes, and the two men then spoke to Volodymyr Zelensky. It was reportedly coordinated with Kyiv. Stubb described the exchange as "frank and substantive."

The Kremlin said Putin criticised EU and NATO policies but agreed to stay in direct telephone contact. Its readout did not suggest any shift in Russia's stated positions. Sergei Lavrov played down the initiative, saying European states were merely seeking a place in future negotiations. Denmark's prime minister, Mette Frederiksen, was reported as sceptical, saying Europe should stick together rather than go solo.

The motive is easier to read than the outcome. Both countries border Russia and sit in NATO. Both have reason to want a say in a process increasingly driven from Washington, and a settlement that touched European security or sanctions would be decided partly without them. A call to Putin gives them a channel and a record. It also risks the divergence among Europeans that Frederiksen warned about, and that Moscow has long tried to exploit.

A truce that widened

The third strand is on the ground. A US-brokered ceasefire around Oleshky, a Russian-occupied town in Kherson region, took effect on Tuesday. It was designed to allow aid deliveries, demining of access roads and the evacuation of civilians. The White House had initially pointed to a start date of 29 October, while Ukraine's General Staff and Moscow's announcement gave 27 October. The 27th is the date on which the truce actually took effect.

The "extension" in this week's headlines is geographic rather than temporal. Melania Trump said the area covered had "expanded beyond Oleshky, within the Kherson region." Ukrainian officials said Russian shelling had stopped in Kherson city and the wider region. Ukrainian officials give the truce's end as 22:59 Kyiv time on 1 November. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said things were proceeding "according to plan." It remains a limited, time-bound arrangement, and the reports available so far describe no violations since it began.

Its significance is partly practical, since civilians in a besieged town can leave. It is also partly diplomatic, because it shows that Moscow and Kyiv can agree on a narrow, verifiable step with American mediation.

What ties them together

Each strand answers a different question: what Russia might be offered, who gets to speak to it, and whether either side will honour even a small restraint. The Kherson truce expires on 1 November, so the first test is whether it is renewed or lapses. The next is a round of Ukrainian, US and Russian talks reportedly planned for Abu Dhabi in November. If energy deals are on that agenda, the sanctions question will move from background reporting to a matter that European governments must either accept or contest.
