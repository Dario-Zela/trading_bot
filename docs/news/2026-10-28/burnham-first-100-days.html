Burnham faces crunch decisions in his first 100 days

In one sentence
Burnham marks 100 days as PM with high approval and popular giveaways, but the Budget, EU policy and his promised ten-year plan are now the real tests.

Andy Burnham reaches his hundredth day as prime minister today. He took office on 20 July, when Keir Starmer tendered his resignation to the King and Burnham, unopposed for the Labour leadership, was invited to form a government. The milestone is conventionally a moment for scorekeeping. The more useful question is what comes next, and the answer is that the easy part of his premiership appears to be over.

What the first hundred days delivered

The early record is one of popular announcements and a strong personal standing. Burnham said on arrival that his first instruction would be to end rough sleeping, and promised a ten-year plan for Britain later this year. Since then he has announced the removal of VAT from domestic electricity bills, a £2 cap on single bus fares from 2027, and business-rate relief for pubs, social clubs and live music venues, according to Brit Brief. The same account notes that some consumer measures, such as easier subscription cancellation and curbs on 'fake' discounts, were first announced under Starmer and merely brought forward.

He has also set up a prime ministerial office outside London, intended, in the words of one academic assessment, to 'rewire the state and devolve power'. The Conversation reports that he remains the most popular party leader in the country, with an approval rating of 46%. Critics are less impressed. One commentary piece argues that his policy outlines remain tentative, with little visible progress on living costs, housing or industrial renewal, and that the northern office has so far produced mostly symbolic results. Much of this is commentary rather than reporting, and should be read as such.

Where the pressure is likely to fall

Three decisions stand out, and none is of Burnham's choosing alone.

The first is the Budget. John Healey, reported to be chancellor, delivers his first fiscal statement with the government already committed to giving up revenue on electricity VAT and fares. Popular giveaways have to be paid for, and the gilt market will judge whether the sums add up; this paper's Budget preview sets out who wins and who pays. UnHerd has already flagged the Budget as the first real test of his standing, noting that he has yet to face a major political challenge.

The second is Europe. Burnham has reopened the argument over Britain's relationship with the EU, which reporting suggests divides his own party. Officials from London and Brussels are due to discuss freedom of movement, a subject on which any concession would draw fire from Reform on one side and Labour sceptics on the other.

The third is the ten-year plan itself. A prime minister who has promised a long-term strategy and a decentralised state will eventually have to say what is cut, what is built and where. Until then, the gap between the mood he has created and the policy he has published remains the main line of attack.

What to watch

The Budget is the nearest hard date. Gilt yields on the day, and the response of the Office for Budget Responsibility to the costed giveaways, will show whether the honeymoon survives contact with the arithmetic. Burnham's advantage is that the next general election is not expected for about three years. His difficulty is that a leader who has spent a hundred days being liked now has to start being decisive.
