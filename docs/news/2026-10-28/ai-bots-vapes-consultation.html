AI bots flooded the 2023 consultation on the disposable vapes ban

In one sentence
Researchers say 76% of 118,756 responses to the 2023 UK disposable vapes consultation were AI-driven fakes, which the government says it caught and excluded, but others may not catch theirs.

A UK government consultation on banning disposable vapes was swamped by automated, AI-generated responses, according to a study by University of Edinburgh researchers. The study was reported by the Guardian and published in the journal Tobacco Control. The consultation ran in 2023. The finding is only now surfacing, and it raises a plain question: if software can write most of the replies, what is a consultation measuring?

The numbers

The consultation drew 118,756 responses. The Department of Health and Social Care (DHSC) identified 76% of them, more than 90,000, as fraudulent using bot-detection methods. The department puts the figure at 90,835 automated responses, all of which it says were excluded from the decision-making process.

The fake responses were not random noise. Almost all of them went after the multiple-choice questions and opposed the plans to restrict disposable vape sales. In other words, they were built to tilt the apparent balance of public opinion against the policy.

The researchers describe it as the first documented instance of large-scale automated interference in a formal public health policy consultation. That is a claim about what has been documented, not about what has happened. The lead researcher, Dr Nason Maani, put the worry this way: what can't be caught isn't known about.

What the government says

A DHSC spokesperson said the department has robust controls to prevent manipulation of its consultations, and that those controls worked effectively in this case. On the evidence reported so far, that appears to be right. The fake responses were identified and removed, and the ban went ahead, taking effect on 1 June 2025.

That reading has limits. The department detected the flood because it was enormous and lopsided. The study's authors point out that consultations run with fewer resources, or by bodies without the capacity to screen submissions, may be at greater risk. A smaller, subtler campaign, with varied wording and spread across free-text answers, would be harder to spot. The study's own caveat is the sharper point: the method that caught this one tells nothing about what it missed elsewhere.

Why it reaches beyond vapes

Governments cite consultation responses as evidence. Ministers point to them to show a policy has support, or that objections were heard. That use assumes each response comes from a person. Cheap text generation weakens the assumption, because a single operator can now produce tens of thousands of plausible, differently worded submissions at little cost.

This case is not the first link between vaping and bots. A 2019 study found most of the posts promoting vaping on Twitter probably came from bot accounts. That was social media, not a formal consultation, but it shows the pattern is not new to this policy area.

Several details are not in the reporting available so far. They include who was behind the automated submissions, and how DHSC's detection worked beyond the label "bot-detection methods". Neither is clear from the coverage reviewed, and the Guardian report rests on the study and the department's statement.

What to watch

The practical test is whether other departments and regulators now say how they screen responses, and whether they publish the share they discard. The study's authors suggest agencies with open consultations are the ones to press. Disclosure of how many responses were removed, and why, would let outsiders judge whether the safeguards work.
