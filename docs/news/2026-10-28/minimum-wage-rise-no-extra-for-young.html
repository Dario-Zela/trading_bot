Minimum wage to rise, but 18-20s get no bigger increase

In one sentence
The government will raise the minimum wage but give 18-20s no larger rise, pausing recent convergence with the adult rate and leaving hospitality and retail with a relatively cheaper youth workforce.

The government is set to raise the national minimum wage, but workers aged 18 to 20 will not receive a larger increase than everyone else, according to BBC UK. That would end a pattern of recent years. The younger rate has been lifted faster than the adult rate, to close the gap between them. The change matters most to hospitality and retail, two sectors that employ many young people.

The BBC report is the only source for the decision. It gives no new hourly rates, percentage rises or start date, and it does not say whether the government is following a Low Pay Commission (LPC) recommendation. Those details are not reported here until they can be checked against the original.

The pattern being broken

The starting point is the April 2026 uprating. The National Living Wage, which applies to those aged 21 and over, rose 4.1% to £12.71 an hour. The 18-20 rate rose 8.5% to £10.85. The government said it accepted the LPC's recommendations in full.

The year before, the gap in the uprating was wider. The 18-20 rate rose 14.8% to £10.00, against 9.8% for the adult rate, according to the figures published at the time. The stated purpose was to narrow the difference between younger workers and the full adult rate. The government's own material has described a path towards extending the adult rate to 20-year-olds in 2027, and to 18 and 19-year-olds in 2028 or 2029. That path was explicitly "subject to economic conditions and Government policy towards young people at the time".

A rise with no extra for 18-20s therefore reads as a pause, and possibly a retreat, from that convergence. The reported decision does not say which.

Why hospitality and retail

The size of the gap is the point. The 18-20 rate sits roughly £1.86 below the adult rate, about 15% less. For an employer, hiring a 19-year-old instead of a 22-year-old is a saving on the wage bill. Each year the gap narrows, that saving shrinks and so does the incentive to hire young. Holding the gap steady in percentage terms, or letting it stop closing, keeps the saving in place.

That is the sense in which youth pay is being treated as a cost lever. The government wants to raise the floor for low-paid workers generally, and it appears unwilling to push the cost of employing the youngest staff up faster than the rest. Whether that is a judgment about youth unemployment, about business margins, or about the Budget arithmetic is not stated in the reporting available.

For listed UK retailers and restaurant groups, the effect is on the margin of the change. Their overall wage bills still rise with the headline rate. Without an extra boost to the 18-20 band, though, the increase is smaller than it would have been under the recent pattern for the staff on the lowest rungs. The scale cannot be estimated without the rates.

What to watch

Three things will settle the picture. The first is the actual figures: the 2027 adult rate and the 18-20 rate, and the percentage gap between them. The LPC's own consultation had estimated an adult rate of between £13.02 and £13.34, a rise of 2.4% to 5.0%. The second is whether the government confirms the LPC's recommendation or departs from it. The third is whether the 2027 and 2028 convergence dates are formally dropped or only deferred. The coming Budget, covered elsewhere in this edition, is the most likely place for detail on all three.
