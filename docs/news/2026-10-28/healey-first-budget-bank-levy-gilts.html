Healey's first Budget: who wins, who pays, and how gilts will read it

In one sentence
Healey's first Budget is previewed as taxing banks to fund targeted energy-bill help; gilts will judge it on headroom, not on who pays.

John Healey delivers his first Budget as Chancellor with the cost-of-living squeeze at the centre of the pitch. Pre-Budget reporting points to two linked measures: a tax rise on banks, and targeted help with energy bills. The Guardian has described the moment as a 'difficult' one. The question for households is who gets the relief. The question for the gilt market is who pays for it, and whether the sums leave enough headroom to be believed.

One caveat applies throughout. The measures below come from press previews, not from a Treasury document, and the previews differ on detail. Nothing here is confirmed until the Chancellor sits down.

What the previews say

The reported core is a swap. Banks would pay more, and poorer households would get help with energy costs over the winter. The Guardian has reported that the energy help would be 'targeted and temporary', aimed at the most vulnerable households rather than a universal cap or a VAT cut. One report names a larger Warm Homes Discount, currently a £150 rebate for households on certain benefits, as the likeliest vehicle. A cut in VAT on energy bills has also been discussed. City AM put the cost of that at about £1.7bn over a full year.

The reporting on the bank tax is less settled. Two routes are described. One reverses the 2023 cut to the bank corporation tax surcharge, which went from 8% to 3% under Jeremy Hunt. One account has the surcharge going back up to 8%, and a figure of about £2bn a year has been attached to that reversal. The other route raises the bank levy, a small percentage charge on bank balance sheets. These are different taxes. The surcharge falls on profit, and the levy falls on the size of the balance sheet. No rate, start date or yield has been confirmed.

The reported £15m pledge on tackling misogyny is a small social-policy line. It could not be checked against a primary source, and it is not material to the fiscal arithmetic.

Who pays

On the reported numbers, the bank tax is modest against the problem it is meant to solve. About £2bn a year is real money for lenders, and it is not much against a fiscal position that City AM reports is some £22bn short after spending commitments. The bank tax would cover the targeted energy support. It would not close the wider gap, so the other revenue measures matter. Commentators quoted in the City press describe tax rises more broadly as close to certain. A bank tax would therefore be the most visible rise in the package, not the biggest.

The FTSE 100 lenders are the direct exposure. A profit-based surcharge cuts earnings straight through, and a levy cuts them whatever the profit. The market has had weeks of pre-briefing, so much of any hit to bank share prices may already be in the price. What matters now is whether the final rate and any exemptions are better or worse than the leaks. The usual defence from banks is that a higher tax leads to wider lending margins or less lending. Whether that happens depends on how competitive the market is.

Households with energy bills benefit only if they meet the targeting test. The Chris Mason line from the BBC is that the Budget must go 'some way towards delivering' what the Prime Minister has promised. Targeted relief is cheaper than universal relief, and the gap between the two is where the political risk sits. Voters who miss the cut-off will notice.

How gilts will read it

The bond market does not care much who pays a given tax. It cares about three things: the size of the borrowing forecast, how much headroom against the fiscal rules remains, and whether the plan looks stable.

Headroom is the concern. Analysts cited in the previews say a recent rise in gilt yields has eaten into the margin, which is the reason for the tax rises. A Budget that funds relief with a tax that raises about £2bn does little to rebuild that margin. A reversal of the 2023 surcharge cut is also a revenue source with a political cost that is already priced in. A small, fully funded giveaway is the kind of package gilts tend to take calmly. The risk is a package that looks funded on paper but relies on optimistic receipts, or one that leaves the Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) forecasting thinner headroom than the Treasury says.

Sterling follows the same logic. A credible plan supports it, and a plan that raises doubts about the deficit path weakens it. Pensions are exposed through gilt yields, and mortgage pricing follows swap rates, which track gilts. A move in the 10-year yield after the OBR publishes its forecast would reach savers, pension funds and borrowers together.

What to watch

The first test is the Chancellor's speech: the bank tax rate and base, the energy-support design and its cost, and whether either is described as temporary. The second is the OBR forecast published alongside the Budget, which sets out the headroom against the fiscal rules and the borrowing path. The gilt market will price that document more closely than the speech. A third is the reaction from the large lenders, whose guidance on how they will absorb or pass on a new charge will show how much of it falls on shareholders.
