Why the data centre backlash is growing

In one sentence
Local objections to noisy, power-hungry data centres are multiplying, but the data show friction and relocation rather than proof that AI capacity is being stopped.

A data centre is, to a neighbour, mostly a very large windowless building that hums. The cooling fans and backup generators run around the clock, and the electricity supply it needs can match that of a small town. For years the objections were handled one planning meeting at a time. Increasingly they are being counted, and the tally suggests the fight has become a real constraint on where AI infrastructure gets built.

What residents are objecting to

The grievances are consistent from place to place: noise and power demand, often with water and wildlife added. The BBC reports that opposition to noisy, power-hungry data centres is spreading to more communities.

Recent local cases show the pattern.

  • Monash, Australia. On 5 October the council rejected a permit to expand a Centuria data centre. A councillor cited the jump in power demand to a 38MW facility. Council officers had said the noise assessment met state criteria, so the refusal was not a technical finding. Centuria is also seeking approval for a second centre on the same site.
  • Norwich, England. A 150MW application at Keswick is with South Norfolk Council. It sits near a protected wildlife area, which has added environmental objections to the noise complaints.
  • Poland. Hillwood Polska revised plans for its Reguły project after local opposition over water, electricity, noise and transparency.
  • United States. Councils in Naperville, Illinois, and Jacinto City, Texas, have rejected or opposed schemes. A California developer withdrew a large proposal after residents lined up to object. Lexington, Kentucky, has had a moratorium, due to expire on 31 October but open to extension.

These are individually small stories. They are chosen examples, not a dataset, and they show where objections arise, not how often they win.

Counting the cost

The broadest tally comes from Data Center Watch, a privately run tracker of US opposition. Its first report covered 2023 to March 2025 and found 16 projects worth $64bn blocked or delayed, of which about $18bn was blocked outright. Later quarterly figures, as reported by trade and tech outlets, are larger: at least 75 projects worth around $130bn in the first quarter of 2026, and at least 45 worth about $68bn in the second. The group says opposition succeeded in blocking or delaying two of every three projects it targeted.

Those numbers need handling with care. The tracker uses "delayed" broadly, to cover extended reviews, temporary permit denials, legal challenges and strong local opposition. It does not follow projects afterwards, so a scheme that was held up for six months and then built counts the same as one that died. Project counts for the same period differ between outlets reporting on it. The Uptime Institute has noted that cancellations tend to happen early, during land acquisition, zoning and planning, which is also when they are hardest to see.

So the evidence supports a narrower claim than "AI buildout is being stopped". Opposition is widespread and growing in volume. It is adding friction and, in places, forcing developers to change sites or specifications. The data do not yet show that it has moved the aggregate timetable of AI capacity.

Why it matters beyond the planning committee

The objections are not only aesthetic. Power demand is the link between a local meeting and the national picture. Utilities plan generation and grid upgrades around forecast load, and a large share of that forecast is data centres. When a council turns down a 38MW or 150MW site, the question for the grid is whether the load vanishes or reappears somewhere else. Developers can usually try another site, but good ones with grid access and fibre are scarce, and a move costs months.

That is the connection to the capital side. The companies discussed in Big Tech earnings test whether AI capex is becoming revenue are committing money on the assumption that capacity arrives on schedule. Delay does not cancel that spending, but it can push back when it earns anything, and it favours developers able to buy land already zoned and connected.

There is also a political lesson. Where councils have refused on power grounds against officers' technical advice, as in Monash, the objection is to the scale of the thing, not a fixable defect. Noise can be engineered down. A power appetite cannot.

What to watch

Three things would turn this from a nuisance into a constraint on the buildout. The first is follow-up data: whether delayed projects are eventually approved, relocated or abandoned. The second is whether utilities and grid operators start citing local refusals in their load forecasts. The third is the outcome of live cases. In the UK that includes the Keswick application in Norfolk, and in the US whether Lexington's moratorium is extended when it lapses on 31 October. A run of refusals in those places, instead of conditional approvals with noise limits attached, would be the clearest sign that the objections are changing where the servers go.
