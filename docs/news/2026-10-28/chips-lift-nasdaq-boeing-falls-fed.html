Chips lift the Nasdaq 100 as Boeing falls 10% ahead of the Fed

In one sentence
Chip stocks lifted the Nasdaq 100 on Fed decision day as Boeing fell about 10% for reasons not yet public, so the rotation reading is unproven.

US stocks closed with a split verdict on 28 October: semiconductors pulled the Nasdaq 100 and other major indices higher, while Boeing fell about 10%. Growth sectors beat defensive ones. The session fell on the second day of the Federal Reserve's October meeting.

The figures come from a single Benzinga report. That report gave no closing prices, no index levels and no reason for Boeing's fall. Searches run for this article found no independent account of the day, so the numbers below are approximate and the cause of the Boeing move is unexplained.

What moved

Chipmakers led the advance. Semiconductors carry a large weight in the Nasdaq 100, so a strong day for the group tends to lift the index whatever the rest of the market does. Growth sectors outperformed defensives, which is the usual signature of investors adding risk rather than hiding from it.

Boeing went the other way. A fall of roughly 10% in one session is rare for a mature industrial of its size. The company has had double-digit daily falls before, including in April 2025 when China announced retaliatory tariffs on US imports. Those moves usually followed an identifiable event: a tariff announcement, an accident, a charge on a programme or a labour dispute. Here the source names none. A drop that size normally comes with a company statement, a regulatory action or an earnings-style disclosure. None had been found by the time of writing.

Rotation, or two separate stories?

The pairing of a tech rally and a falling industrial invites a rotation story: money leaving defensive and cyclical names for growth. The sector breakdown fits that reading, but a single stock is thin evidence for it. If Boeing's fall came from something specific to the company, it says little about allocation and the rotation reading falls away. The chip rally and the Boeing drop may simply have coincided.

The test is breadth. A real rotation would show up in other industrials, in the defensive sectors and in the relative performance of equal-weight and cap-weight indices. The triage material does not include that data. Until it is available, the sector comparison is a description of the day rather than a trend.

The Fed in the background

The backdrop is the Fed. The FOMC meeting is scheduled for 27–28 October, as best recalled from the published 2026 calendar; this was not checked here. If that holds, 28 October is decision day. Futures on the Dow, S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 were reported edging higher before the session, with no size given. Novartis and HSBC were named as European stocks in focus, also without a reason.

Rates matter to this split because long-duration growth stocks are more sensitive to the path of borrowing costs than defensives. A chip-led rally into a rate decision suggests investors were not bracing for a hawkish surprise. The reaction to the Fed's statement and press conference is the first thing that would confirm or undercut that.

What to watch

The next data points are Boeing's explanation, which should come through a company filing or a statement, and whether the chip rally holds after the Fed. The mega-cap earnings that follow, covered elsewhere in this edition, will show whether the growth bid rests on revenue. If Boeing's cause turns out to be company-specific, the day was a strong tech session and an isolated industrial collapse, and the rotation story would not stand.
