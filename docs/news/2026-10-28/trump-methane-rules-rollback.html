Trump to relax methane leak rules for oil firms

In one sentence
The Washington Post reports the administration will relax methane leak rules that industry calls too costly, with some backers reportedly top Trump donors; details are unconfirmed.

The Trump administration plans to relax federal rules on methane leaks from oil and gas operations, according to a Washington Post report relayed by Benzinga. The report says industry executives argue the existing requirements are too costly. It adds that some of those pressing for the change are among President Donald Trump's biggest donors. The details of the rule and its timing have not been confirmed.

What is at stake

Methane is the main component of natural gas. It is also a potent greenhouse gas that traps far more heat than carbon dioxide over its first two decades in the atmosphere, then breaks down within roughly a dozen years. That makes it a lever for near-term warming. Cutting it is widely regarded as one of the cheaper climate measures available, because much of the escaping gas is a saleable product.

Leaks are hard to see. They come from valves, tanks, flares that fail to ignite and ageing low-output wells, and detection depends on inspection or monitoring equipment. Rules on leak detection and repair are therefore the main way regulators learn how much escapes.

Some of the earlier reporting on this subject points to the same pressure points. Coverage this year has described EPA plans to revise the 2024 Biden-era standards, with the agency claiming about $45 billion in regulatory savings. Reports have also described exemptions from monitoring requirements for smaller operators. Dates on some of these items are inconsistent, and they are not the Post story itself, so they should be read as context rather than confirmation of what the Post found.

The donor question

The regulatory-capture question rests on the reported overlap between those who want the rules loosened and those who fund the president. Earlier reporting has centred on Jeff Hildebrand, an oil executive and large Trump donor, and on low-producing wells that emit a lot of methane relative to their output. Environmental groups such as the Sierra Club have called such moves a giveaway to fossil fuel executives.

Political giving by regulated industries is legal and common, and it does not by itself show that a policy was bought. The test is documentary: whether the specific executives, the specific provisions they sought, and the text of the final rule line up. That is the evidence a reader should look for as the Post's reporting is followed up.

The cost argument is also contestable. Industry points to compliance bills. Critics point out that recovered gas can offset part of the expense and that the climate damage is not borne by the companies. Neither side's figures can be checked until the proposal is published.

What to watch

The next marker is the formal proposal and its regulatory impact analysis. That document should show the estimated emissions increase, the claimed savings and the comment period. Because the rules are a model for other climate regulations, litigation from states and environmental groups is likely to follow a final rule. Any disclosure of meetings between agency officials and the executives involved would bear directly on the capture question.
