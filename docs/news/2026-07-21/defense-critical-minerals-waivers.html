Trump tightens waiver rules for defense contractors on foreign critical minerals

Mountain Pass, California — the only rare-earth mining and processing facility of scale in the United States, and the domestic alternative contractors are now being pushed toward. — Tmy350 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Trump's new order forces defense contractors to prove they've exhausted alternatives before keeping waivers to buy banned Chinese minerals, effective January 1, 2027.

The Trump administration is closing a loophole that has let defense contractors keep buying critical minerals from China even while federal law says they shouldn't. An executive order signed Monday tightens the waiver process contractors use to sidestep restrictions on foreign-sourced materials in weapons production, and sets a hard deadline — January 1, 2027 — after which the Department of War will stop granting waivers under the old, permissive standard.

In one sentence: Trump's new order forces defense contractors to prove they've exhausted domestic and allied alternatives before they can keep buying banned minerals from China, with the old waiver standard expiring January 1, 2027.

What changes

Under existing law — 10 U.S.C. § 4872 — contractors have long been barred from sourcing certain minerals and materials from what the statute calls "covered nations," chiefly China, but also Russia, Iran and North Korea. In practice, waivers were easy to get: a company mostly had to show that a banned supplier was the cheapest or most convenient option. The new order, titled "Securing America's Defense Supply Chains and Ensuring Domestic Acquisition of Critical Materials," ends that. Contractors seeking a waiver now have to document where their materials actually come from, show they searched for alternatives, and lay out a plan to wean themselves off prohibited suppliers. Companies that can't make that case risk losing their contracts.

Peter Navarro, the White House senior counselor for trade and manufacturing, put it more bluntly: the order will "end the waiver racket" that let contractors and subcontractors route around the sourcing ban. "No more: 'we tried nothing and we're out of options,'" he said, describing the order as a form of "battlefield preparation" that lets the Pentagon map its dependencies before, rather than during, a conflict.

The mapping mandate

Beyond waivers, the order directs the Pentagon to build out a supply-chain mapping regime within 180 days, tracing designated weapons systems from raw material to finished platform. That means identifying not just where a prime contractor's inputs come from, but where its subcontractors' subcontractors source minerals, components and even software — a level of visibility the Pentagon has historically lacked below the top tier of its industrial base. Regulations to implement the mapping rules are due 90 days after the policy is finalized, and the Secretary must report progress every six months through January 2028.

Why it's hard to unwind

The practical bind is that China dominates the upstream of the rare-earths supply chain the Pentagon depends on: it accounts for roughly two-thirds of global rare-earth mining and about 90% of refining capacity, according to industry estimates. Domestic capacity is thin by comparison — Mountain Pass in California, run by MP Materials, remains the only rare-earth mining and processing site of scale in the U.S. The administration has been trying to build up alternatives in parallel; in February it launched a roughly $12 billion critical-minerals stockpile effort, and MP Materials shares have been among the beneficiaries of that broader push. But standing up domestic mining, processing and qualification takes years, not months, which is why the order gives contractors an 18-month runway rather than an immediate cutoff.

What it means for contractors

For prime contractors like Lockheed Martin and Boeing, the order raises the administrative burden and the cost of getting weapons systems built on time. Domestic or allied sourcing is typically more expensive and slower to scale than buying from an established Chinese supplier, and that tension lands squarely in the middle of a defense-spending buildup the administration itself is driving. Contractors now face a choice between eating higher costs and lead times to source domestically, or investing in the paperwork and diversification plans needed to keep a waiver alive under the tighter standard. Either path pushes procurement costs higher, with the bill likely showing up in future contract negotiations rather than in any single line item today.

The order also has spillover effects beyond defense. Rare earths and other critical minerals feed EV motors, wind turbines and semiconductor supply chains, so a policy push that reroutes demand toward domestic and allied producers — and away from Chinese processors — has knock-on implications for pricing and availability across those markets too.

What's next

The first concrete marker is the 180-day deadline for the Pentagon to publish its supply-chain mapping policy, due around mid-January 2027 — just before the waiver restrictions themselves take effect on January 1, 2027. Contractors and mineral producers will be watching how the Department of War defines "sufficient" proof of alternative sourcing, since that standard will determine how many existing waivers survive the transition.
