White House imposes sweeping new tariffs on Canadian goods, autos and alcohol

President Donald Trump signed three Section 338 proclamations imposing 50% tariffs on Canadian goods on July 20, 2026. — Official White House portrait, January 2026 / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump signed three Section 338 proclamations imposing 50% tariffs on Canadian autos, alcohol and dairy goods, effective in 30 days; Ottawa calls it a CUSMA violation and says it's merely matching prior U.S. moves.

Canada is about to find out what it costs to say no to Washington twice.

On Monday, President Donald Trump signed three proclamations under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, imposing an additional 50% tariff on a wide swath of Canadian goods — cars, wine and spirits, dairy products, and a long tail of items the White House fact sheet lists down to hockey sticks and cement. The duties take effect in 30 days. They stack on top of tariffs already in place, and the administration says they apply even to goods that would otherwise qualify for duty-free treatment under the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement.

Section 338 is a statute Washington rarely reaches for. It lets a president impose duties up to 50% — the maximum the law allows — on a country found to discriminate against U.S. commerce, without the months-long investigation process that trade remedies like Section 301 require. The White House used it here in one motion across three sectors at once, which is the detail worth sitting with: this isn't a single-industry dispute that escalated. It's a coordinated, multi-front tariff action against the United States' second-largest trading partner, timed to land as the two countries were supposed to be negotiating a renewal of USMCA, which lapsed earlier this summer.

The discrimination case, by the numbers

The White House frames all three proclamations around a single grievance: that Canada treats American exporters worse than it treats everyone else. The autos proclamation points to a 25% Canadian tariff, in place since April 2025, on U.S.-made vehicles that don't qualify for USMCA preference — a levy the administration says cut Canadian imports of U.S. motor vehicles by roughly 22%, or $5.6 billion, over the following year.

The alcohol proclamation tells a starker story. After Trump's earlier round of tariffs and his repeated suggestion that Canada become the "51st state," all but two Canadian provinces and territories pulled American liquor from government-run store shelves. The White House says that boycott cut U.S. alcoholic-beverage exports to Canada by about 81%, or $582 million, over the year through February. The dairy proclamation, meanwhile, revives a longer-standing U.S. complaint that Canada's cheese quotas are tighter for American producers than for the European Union, despite Ottawa having trade agreements with both.

Notably exempted: energy, potash, fish, critical minerals, and goods already covered under Section 232 national-security tariffs, including steel, aluminum and many auto parts. That carve-out matters for the auto sector specifically — it means the pain lands on finished vehicles and non-covered components rather than the full supply chain, though assemblers running cross-border production lines will still feel it.

Ottawa's answer

Prime Minister Mark Carney did not treat this as a bolt from the blue. In a statement issued the same day, he called the new duties "the latest in a series of unilateral U.S. trade actions" that he says began with tariffs violating CUSMA — Canada's name for the pact — and argued Ottawa's own counter-measures have simply matched what Washington imposed first. "Canada believes in the benefits of free and fair trade," Carney said, pointing to more than 20 new economic and security partnerships his government has signed since taking office, while adding that Canada "stands ready to engage intensively" to resolve the dispute.

That rhetoric of openness sits alongside continued retaliation on the ground. Ontario, Quebec and British Columbia have kept U.S. alcohol off provincial liquor-store shelves since last year, and Canada has said it will maintain matching counter-tariffs on U.S. autos, steel and aluminum. The White House fact sheet itself notes, pointedly, that only two of Washington's trading partners have chosen to retaliate rather than negotiate: China, and Canada.

What it means for pricing and positioning

The 30-day runway before the tariffs bite gives both sides a window to negotiate them away, and Section 338 actions have historically been used as leverage as much as policy — a lever Trump has pulled before with other partners and then eased once concessions materialized. But the mechanics matter for anyone pricing risk in the meantime. Auto assemblers with cross-border supply chains face a step change in landed cost on any vehicle or part not shielded by the Section 232 carve-out. Dairy and spirits producers on both sides of the border are staring at a second consecutive year of losing access to each other's shelves. And because the duties apply even to USMCA-qualifying goods, the usual hedge — proving North American content — no longer fully protects importers the way it has for the past several years.

The next marker to watch is not a data release but a negotiating one: whether Washington and Ottawa restart substantive USMCA renewal talks before the 30-day clock runs out, or whether the tariffs take effect as scheduled around August 20 and Canada answers with a fresh round of its own. Markets pricing sector-specific input costs — autos, spirits, dairy, building materials — have that date circled either way.
