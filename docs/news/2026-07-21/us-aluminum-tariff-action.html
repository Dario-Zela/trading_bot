Trump orders fresh adjustment to US aluminum import rules

An aluminum smelter complex in Washington state — the kind of US primary-production capacity the new tariff incentive is meant to expand. — brewbooks / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Trump's aluminum proclamation keeps the 50% Section 232 tariff intact but offers a 25% rate to firms that commit to building US smelters — with no application window yet published.

President Trump signed a proclamation on July 20 adjusting how the United States taxes aluminum imports — not by raising the headline tariff rate, but by offering to cut it in half for companies that agree to build metal-making capacity on American soil.

The mechanism, issued under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, leaves the baseline 50% tariff on most aluminum products untouched. What it adds is an opt-in discount: companies that submit an "onshoring plan" to the Commerce Department — committing to build, expand or refurbish a US primary aluminum smelter, with construction underway by January 20, 2029 — can import a matching volume of primary aluminum at half the standard Section 232 rate, roughly 25%, while their domestic project comes online. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is tasked with running the approval process; the White House fact sheet says benefits can be clawed back retroactively if a company misses its commitments, but has not set an application deadline or published start date for the program itself.

Why now

The administration's stated rationale is a supply gap: domestic demand for primary aluminum outstrips what US smelters can produce, so importers have been paying the full 50% rate on volumes no American plant is currently equipped to supply. The fact sheet also leans on national-security language, noting aluminum's role in armored vehicles, naval vessels, spacecraft and missiles — the same framing the White House used for the original Section 232 aluminum tariffs during Trump's first term and their reinstatement and escalation to 50% in June 2025.

The design echoes an incentive scheme the administration already runs for Canadian and Mexican aluminum producers, though officials say this version carries fewer sourcing and documentation requirements.

Market reaction

Equity moves were modest and split. Century Aluminum (CENX) rose as much as 3.2% on the news, while Alcoa (AA) — down roughly 40% over the prior month on separate pressures — pared its losses rather than rallying outright. The divergence tracks exposure: Century, together with Emirates Global Aluminum, is already building what it calls the largest single investment in US primary aluminum history, a 750,000-tonne-a-year smelter in Inola, Oklahoma, expected to more than double current domestic output and support over 1,000 permanent jobs. Century chief executive Jesse Gary said the proclamation makes "investments like Oklahoma Primary Aluminum" easier to justify. Alcoa, whose US capacity is more built-out already, has less obvious upside from a program aimed at new construction.

What's still missing

For traders pricing in the tariff's effect on input costs — sheet and extrusion buyers, autos, packaging, the broader industrial-metals complex — the proclamation is more signal than schedule. No application window has been published, no volume caps have been disclosed, and Commerce has not said how many onshoring plans it expects to approve or how it will size the "commensurate" import allowance against each project's capacity. Until those rules are published, the practical tariff exposure facing importers is unchanged: 50% remains the rate that applies absent an approved plan.

The next marker is procedural rather than dramatic — Commerce's formal solicitation for onshoring applications, which will show which companies intend to bid for the discount and roughly how large the resulting import carve-outs could get.
