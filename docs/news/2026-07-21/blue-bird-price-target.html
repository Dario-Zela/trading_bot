Blue Bird shares jump as Barclays lifts price target

In one sentence
Barclays lifted its Blue Bird price target to $85 from $75, but the real driver is an aging bus fleet and a backlog stretching into 2027.

Shares of Blue Bird Corporation (NASDAQ:BLBD) climbed 4.2% to an all-time high of $81.53 on Monday afternoon after Barclays raised its price target on the school bus manufacturer to $85 from $75, keeping its Overweight rating in place. The stock has now gained 74% since the start of 2026.

"Production trends, new awards, firm pricing, and ongoing acquisitions" support the outlook into 2026, Barclays wrote — the kind of note that reads as routine until you look at what's actually filling the order book.

On its face, this is one line in the daily churn of analyst notes: a bank nudges a target 13% higher, a stock ticks up, nobody breaks a sweat. Blue Bird is not a volatile name and Monday's move, while real, sits comfortably within a year in which the stock has nearly doubled. The more interesting question is why a company that makes yellow buses for a living has become one of the better-performing industrials on the board.

A fleet getting old, and a company getting paid for it

The answer starts with the age of the vehicles already on the road. Much of the US school bus fleet dates to purchasing cycles from a decade or more ago, and districts are now replacing units in volume — a dynamic Barclays flagged in noting that industry consolidation and an aging fleet have pushed average selling prices higher alongside a growing multi-year backlog. Blue Bird, based in Fort Valley, Georgia, has translated that into pricing power rather than just unit growth: first-quarter operating margin improved to 11.1% from 9.4% a year earlier, even as revenue of $352.6 million beat expectations.

Management has leaned into the moment. After a strong first half and the closing of the Micro Bird acquisition, Blue Bird lifted full-year guidance in May to roughly $1.75 billion in net revenue — up from an earlier $1.5 billion target — and about $245 million in adjusted EBITDA, up from $225 million. The company is now pointing investors toward a longer-run target of $2.5 billion in revenue at a 15%-plus EBITDA margin.

Electrification is a mix shift, not a mandate

Electric and other alternative-power buses now account for close to half of Blue Bird's sales mix, backed by an order backlog that stretches into 2027 and includes more than 900 firm EV orders as of the second quarter. That expansion has been underwritten in part by the EPA's Clean School Bus Program, a $5 billion, five-year pool of grant funding created under the 2021 infrastructure law that has already funded EV fleet purchases for districts from Arkansas to Kentucky.

What is notable is that the growth case no longer depends on that program surviving intact. The Trump administration has been reworking the Clean School Bus rules to widen eligibility beyond battery-electric buses to propane, compressed natural gas and other lower-emission alternatives — sanding down what had been a policy tailwind specific to EVs. Blue Bird's backlog has kept growing anyway, evidence that the replacement cycle itself, not a particular fuel mandate, is doing the heavier lifting.

What to watch

Barclays also flagged a constraint worth taking seriously: production capacity. Blue Bird's plants can only build so many buses a year, and a backlog stretching into 2027 means the company is now managing a supply problem rather than a demand one — a comfortable position, but one that caps how fast revenue can actually convert. The next test comes with fiscal third-quarter results, expected in August, where investors will be watching whether margins hold as EV mix keeps climbing and whether management raises full-year guidance a third time.
