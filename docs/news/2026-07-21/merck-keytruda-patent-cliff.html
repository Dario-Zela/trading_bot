Merck eyes life after Keytruda's patent cliff with new cancer drug combinations

In one sentence
Merck's Keytruda loses US patent protection in 2028 on roughly $29.5B in annual sales, and its ADC pipeline with Daiichi Sankyo and Kelun-Biotech is the untested bet meant to fill the gap.

Merck's Keytruda is the best-selling drug on earth — roughly $29.5 billion in sales last year, close to half the company's revenue — and for a large share of cancer patients worldwide, it or a drug like it has become the default second ingredient alongside chemotherapy. That dominance has an expiration date. Keytruda's core U.S. patents lapse in 2028, opening the door to biosimilar competition on a product analysts expect to peak near $36 billion that year before sliding toward $20 billion or lower by 2030.

What happens next is the question Merck's stock has been quietly re-pricing around all year. Unlike a normal patent cliff — a known date, a known drop — this one comes with genuine uncertainty about the replacement, which is why Barron's and others have zeroed in on it as a live catalyst rather than a settled fact.

The defense: buying time on Keytruda itself

Merck's first move isn't a new drug at all. In September the FDA approved Keytruda Qlex, a subcutaneous version of pembrolizumab that can be injected in one to two minutes rather than the roughly 30-minute IV infusion. Because it carries its own, later-expiring patents, Merck is trying to migrate patients onto Qlex before 2028 — the company has floated 30–40% adoption by then — which would shield that portion of the franchise from biosimilar substitution even after the original molecule goes generic. It's a rear-guard action, not a growth plan, but it buys years.

The offense: antibody-drug conjugates

The more consequential bet is technological: pairing Keytruda with antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which fuse a targeting antibody to a toxic payload so chemotherapy is delivered directly to tumor cells rather than the whole body. Merck has built this pipeline almost entirely through partnerships rather than in-house discovery.

With Daiichi Sankyo, Merck co-develops ifinatamab deruxtecan (I-DXd), a B7-H3-targeted ADC now in Phase 3 trials spanning esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and relapsed small-cell lung cancer, after the FDA lifted a partial clinical hold earlier this year. A related Daiichi collaboration, patritumab deruxtecan for EGFR-mutated lung cancer, hit a setback in 2026 when the partners withdrew their approval application after the drug missed on overall survival — a reminder that ADC science, however promising, doesn't always clear the bar.

The more advanced asset is sacituzumab tirumotecan (sac-TMT), a TROP2-directed ADC licensed from China's Kelun-Biotech, for which Merck has registered 15 Phase 3 trials and struck a $700 million royalty-financing deal with Blackstone. In first-line PD-L1-positive non-small-cell lung cancer, sac-TMT plus Keytruda produced a statistically significant improvement in progression-free survival over Keytruda alone — the kind of head-to-head data investors need to see repeated across indications before treating the drug as a true Keytruda successor rather than a promising add-on.

The hedge: diversifying away from oncology

Alongside the ADC bet, Merck has been buying its way into adjacent categories. The $10.8 billion acquisition of Prometheus Biosciences brought an inflammatory bowel disease candidate targeting TL1A. And Winrevair, a pulmonary arterial hypertension drug that didn't exist in Merck's portfolio three years ago, posted $525 million in first-quarter 2026 sales — up 12% sequentially — after $1.4 billion in 2025, on its way toward blockbuster status. Management has pointed to roughly $70 billion in potential commercial opportunity across the portfolio by the mid-2030s, up from a $50 billion estimate a year earlier.

What the Street thinks

Opinion is split, and the split matters more than the headline number. CEO Rob Davis has taken to calling the 2028 event "more of a hill than a cliff," pointing to the broadened pipeline as evidence Merck isn't staring into a single-product hole. BMO has called that framing increasingly credible. Berenberg disagrees: it downgraded Merck to Hold in 2026 and cut its price target to $90 from $100, arguing the company hasn't deployed Keytruda's cash flow aggressively enough and that the range of plausible post-cliff outcomes remains too wide to underwrite confidently.

That disagreement is really about sequencing, not destination. Nobody disputes Merck will lose tens of billions in Keytruda revenue to biosimilars starting in 2028. The dispute is whether Qlex, the ADC pipeline, and drugs like Winrevair arrive with enough scale and enough data behind them to fill the hole before it opens — or whether Merck spends several years with a visible growth gap while the science plays out in Phase 3 readouts. The next hard evidence arrives with sac-TMT's broader trial results and further Keytruda Qlex adoption figures, both due later this year and into 2027 — the checkpoints that will show whether "hill" or "cliff" is the more honest word.
