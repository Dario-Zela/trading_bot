Barclays cuts airline price targets as fuel costs bite

A Delta Air Lines Airbus A350 — one of the carriers whose Barclays price target was trimmed on fuel-cost pressure. — Formulanone / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Barclays cut Delta and American Airlines price targets on fuel-cost pressure from an oil spike tied to renewed Iran fighting, but spared United, citing yield strength.

Barclays trimmed price targets on two of the three biggest US airlines this week, telling clients that surging fuel costs are eating into estimates even as the carriers manage to claw some of it back through higher fares.

On September 11, the bank cut Delta Air Lines to $95 from $105, and American Airlines to $14 from $19 — a target reduction of more than a quarter for American. Both ratings held steady: Overweight on Delta, Hold on American. The moves followed a rough stretch for airline stocks generally, with weekly declines across the group as jet fuel prices climbed alongside crude.

Why fuel, and why now

Brent crude touched more than $102 a barrel on September 10, its highest intraday level since May 22, as fighting around Iran flared again and the Strait of Hormuz remained a live risk to tanker traffic. Gulf Coast jet fuel has tracked that move closely: from just over $4 a gallon in the week to September 4 to above $4.34 a few days later, a jump analysts tied directly to the renewed Iran conflict — up 71% since that war began. The International Air Transport Association has already flagged roughly $100 billion in incremental fuel costs weighing on the industry this year, enough, by its estimate, to halve global airline profits.

Barclays framed the sector call plainly: energy prices are pushing estimates lower again, despite continued yield gains. American felt it hardest. The carrier's fuel bill rose 83% year-over-year in the second quarter — more than $2.2 billion in additional cost — which is why its target took the deepest cut of the three.

The offsetting side of the ledger

That "despite continued yield gains" clause is doing real work. American's revenue rose 16.3% year-over-year in the same quarter, and the airline recovered close to half of that $2.2 billion fuel hit through pricing and mix — managed corporate revenue alone was up 26%. Government data on airfares back this up: ticket prices rose 23% year-over-year in August, evidence that carriers retain enough pricing power to pass a meaningful share of fuel inflation through to travelers rather than absorb it outright.

United is the clearer illustration of that resilience. Rather than cutting, Barclays raised United's target to $175 from $150 in its most recent call on the carrier, keeping an Overweight rating and pointing to "solid yield gains and product innovation" that leave its long-term earnings view unchanged. Delta, similarly, kept its Overweight despite the target trim — Barclays cited the carrier's "strong operational profile" even while flagging sector-wide headwinds.

The read, then, is not a uniform retreat from the sector but a repricing of who absorbs the fuel shock. American carries the most exposure and the smallest cushion; Delta and United, with stronger premium and loyalty revenue mixes, are being treated as better placed to pass costs on rather than eat them.

What to watch

The next catalyst is largely geopolitical rather than financial: any de-escalation around Iran would ease crude and jet fuel simultaneously, doing more for airline margins than an earnings beat could. Barring that, the Federal Reserve's rate decision on Wednesday will shape the discount rate applied to airline earnings just as fuel costs are squeezing the numerator. Third-quarter results next month, when analysts will see how much of the fare increases actually stuck, are the next hard data point on whether yield gains keep pace with the cost line.
