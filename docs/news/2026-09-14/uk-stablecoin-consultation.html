FCA and BoE stablecoin consultation nears close ahead of year-end rulebook

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, which shares supervision of systemic stablecoin issuers with the FCA under the emerging regime. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
BoE and FCA's stablecoin consultation closes September 30, after the Bank already dropped individual holding caps for a £40bn per-coin issuance ceiling.

The joint Bank of England and Financial Conduct Authority consultation on regulating Britain's largest stablecoin issuers closes September 30, with a finalised Code of Practice due by year-end — a deadline that matters more for what has already changed in the draft rules than for the deadline itself.

When the Bank first floated a regime for "systemic" stablecoins — those judged big enough to pose financial stability risk if they wobbled — in November 2025, it proposed capping individual holdings at £20,000 per coin and £10 million for businesses. Deputy Governor Sarah Breeden argued the caps reflected a mortgage market unusually dependent on bank deposits: if households moved savings into stablecoins at scale, lenders' funding could thin out fast.

Industry pushback was immediate and loud. Issuers and payments firms said per-holder caps would make stablecoins useless for everyday commercial use — a business collecting payments could hit the ceiling in an afternoon. By the time the Bank published its policy statement and draft Code of Practice on June 22, the caps were gone.

What replaced them

In their place sits a blunter instrument: a £40 billion issuance ceiling per stablecoin, reviewed periodically and intended to be lifted once the Bank is satisfied that credit provision to the economy isn't being squeezed. Households and businesses face no limit on what they can hold or transact — the constraint sits at the product level, not the wallet level.

The backing-asset rules also loosened. Issuers can now hold up to 70% of reserves in short-dated UK government debt, up from a proposed 60%, with the remainder parked in unremunerated Bank of England deposits to guarantee redemption on demand. It is a small percentage shift, but it changes the economics of running a systemic stablecoin meaningfully — more gilt exposure means more yield to offset compliance costs, at the price of slightly less immediately available cash.

Two clocks, one rulebook

The timeline is layered. The Bank's own consultation on the draft Code of Practice — covering firm-specific conduct, reporting and operational expectations — closes September 22. The broader joint document setting out how the Bank and FCA will divide supervisory labour, published more recently, runs a week longer to September 30. Under the split, the Bank takes prudential and financial-stability oversight — capital, safeguarding, backing assets — while the FCA keeps conduct and consumer protection, including for issuers who start out small and are later designated systemic by the Treasury.

Non-systemic issuers, including those focused purely on crypto-asset trading rather than payments, stay under FCA-only supervision via the regulator's Stablecoin Issuance Policy Statement (PS26/10). The FCA begins accepting authorisation applications from September 30, with the full mandatory regime taking effect October 25, 2027.

Why traders should care

For now, no issuer has been designated systemic — that judgment sits with HM Treasury and will follow, not precede, the finalised Code of Practice. What's set is the ceiling issuers will operate under once one is: an initial £40 billion cap that critics, including a July note from crypto-markets analyst Zennon Kapron, have framed as evidence Britain is running roughly three years behind the EU's MiCA regime while still writing what he called the world's most cautious stablecoin rulebook. Slower, more conservative rules mean less near-term competitive disruption to bank deposits — but also less likelihood a sterling stablecoin market of any scale forms before 2027.

The next marker is the Code of Practice itself, due by December 31. Its granularity on liquidity stress-testing and redemption timelines will determine whether the £40 billion ceiling is a temporary guardrail, as the Bank insists, or a de facto cap that outlasts its own review clause.
