UK rejects AI 'kill switch' as MPs and peers push for human-rights law

The Palace of Westminster, where peers debated an AI 'kill switch' amendment this month. — Adrian Pingstone / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers rejected a Lords amendment for an AI 'kill switch' the same week a cross-party committee said UK law isn't fit to handle AI's human-rights risks.

The government has a stock answer for people who worry about artificial intelligence going wrong in some catastrophic, unrecoverable way: don't worry, there's an off switch. This week, ministers said there isn't — and don't plan to build one.

The rejection came in response to an amendment tabled by the Liberal Democrat peer Lord Clement-Jones to the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, which would have given the state a "necessary emergency kill switch" — the power to direct an emergency shutdown of an AI model if it malfunctioned or was judged dangerous. The government's answer, delivered as the bill moved through the Lords earlier this month, was blunt: "Britain cannot simply turn AI off and blocking access to models in the UK would not prevent them being developed or misused elsewhere."

In one sentence: Ministers declined to mandate an emergency shutdown mechanism for advanced AI, even as a cross-party group of MPs and peers pushed the same week for a new human-rights law to govern the technology.

Why there's no off switch

The logic behind the refusal is less about complacency than about the limits of British jurisdiction. A kill switch only works on a system the UK government can physically reach — servers, APIs, models hosted or licensed within its borders. The frontier models that most worry safety campaigners are trained and run by companies in the US, and increasingly China, with UK access being just one distribution point among many. Cutting that access, officials argued, wouldn't stop the underlying system from operating, or being misused, anywhere else.

Instead, the government's position is to lean on the companies themselves. "Companies have a clear responsibility to develop their products safely and to invest in the security infrastructure this technology requires," it said, while leaving the door open a crack: officials are "exploring whether further targeted interventions may be needed," but any such measures "would need to be proportionate." That is a government keeping its options open without committing to anything that would show up as a line item for AI developers weighing whether to build or sell in Britain.

It's consistent with the approach Downing Street has taken since the 2023 Bletchley Park AI summit: voluntary commitments, a well-funded testing institute, and a reluctance to legislate ahead of the technology. Lord Clement-Jones has pushed variations of this amendment before, and been rebuffed before. What's changed is the backdrop — a summer of warnings from AI labs themselves, including Anthropic researchers cautioning that advanced systems could pose existential risks within a decade, and a Cabinet reshuffle that gave AI Safety Minister Kanishka Narayan a seat at the table under Prime Minister Andy Burnham. The tone from government has hardened rhetorically even as the substance of its regulatory offer has not.

A separate, broader case for a new law

The kill-switch amendment landed the same week as a much larger document making a related but distinct argument. The Joint Committee on Human Rights — a cross-party body of MPs and peers chaired by Labour's Alex Sobel — published a 100-page report on Monday concluding that Britain's existing legal framework simply isn't built for what AI is already doing to people's rights.

"Nowhere in the world, including the UK, has a current legislative and regulatory approach to AI that is fit for purpose," Sobel said. "It is moving with such speed and complexity that its impact is hard to accurately predict."

The committee's concerns are not about rogue superintelligence — they're about harms already occurring: facial recognition deployed without consent, algorithmic bias baked in from internet-scraped training data, and the generation and spread of sexualised images of women and girls. Its recommendations include a single, independent AI oversight body with statutory powers, a risk-tiered regulatory regime that imposes stronger duties on higher-risk systems, obligations that follow a system through its whole lifecycle — from the company that builds it to the business that deploys it — and outright bans on certain uses, such as subliminal manipulation techniques and inappropriate biometric profiling.

No draft bill has been published, and no timeline given; this is a committee report making recommendations, not legislation working its way through Parliament. The government's initial response leaned on its existing pitch — that Britain runs "the best-resourced security institute in the world" for testing frontier models — without directly engaging with the JCHR's central claim that the current patchwork of laws doesn't cover the ground.

Two asks, one gap

Taken separately, these look like two unrelated stories — a niche amendment about emergency powers, and a select committee report about human rights. Taken together, they describe a government being pressed from two directions on the same underlying question: who is responsible when an AI system causes serious harm, and does the state have any real lever to pull when it does?

On the kill switch, the answer is a considered no, made on the reasonable-sounding grounds that unilateral British action can't contain a global technology. On the human-rights law, there's no answer yet at all, just an acknowledgement that things are moving fast. What both refusals share is a government that would rather rely on voluntary industry commitments and its testing institute than write new statutory duties — the same instinct that has kept UK AI policy lighter-touch than the EU's AI Act, which is now in its first wave of compliance inspections, or the more prescriptive proposals circulating in Washington.

For companies building or deploying AI in Britain, that's a signal worth reading carefully: the regulatory bar here remains lower, for now, than in Brussels. Whether that's a competitive advantage that draws investment or a gap that eventually forces a harder legislative correction is the question both the Lords amendment and the JCHR report are, in their different ways, asking. The next marker will be whether the government issues a substantive response to the JCHR report — committees typically expect one within two months — and whether that response contains anything more concrete than a restatement of the science-led approach it has offered so far.
