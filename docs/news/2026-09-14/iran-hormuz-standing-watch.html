Iran war grinds on: Gulf talks lapse, oil holds above $100, Trump eyes post-midterm end

US Navy vessels transit the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint at the center of the stalled diplomacy and the oil-price shock. — Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class Alex R. Forster / U.S. Navy, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A first Gulf-Iran ministerial meant to ease Hormuz tensions was postponed hours before it began, as oil holds above $100 and the earlier US-Iran deal deadline sits a month expired.

The clearest sign yet of how far the Iran war has drifted from resolution arrived on Saturday, in the form of a cancellation notice. A first-ever meeting between Gulf Cooperation Council foreign ministers and Iran's top diplomat, scheduled for Monday in the Omani coastal city of Salalah, was called off — not by Washington, not by Tehran acting alone, but by Oman and Iran jointly, at the request of unnamed "regional countries."

That the meeting was even planned marked something new: it would have been the first senior-level sit-down between the six-member GCC and Iran since the United States and Israel opened the war against the Islamic Republic in February. Delegations from Iraq, Saudi Arabia and Qatar were expected. Omani Foreign Minister Sayyid Badr Albusaidi framed the postponement diplomatically, saying it served "the interests of consensus." Iranian officials described it as a joint decision to reschedule. Neither explanation obscures the substance underneath: Iran wants compulsory Gulf state contributions to any new Hormuz shipping-safety arrangement, plus lanes drawn closer to its own coast, while Oman's proposal — the one meant to be the basis for Monday's talks — was voluntary. That gap didn't close in time for the meeting it was supposed to be about.

In one sentence: A first-ever Gulf-Iran ministerial meant to calm the Strait of Hormuz was postponed hours before it was due to start, while the underlying US-Iran deal deadline lapsed a month ago and oil sits above $100.

A deadline that already expired

The Salalah cancellation is really the second missed deadline in three weeks. Back in June, Donald Trump and Iranian president Masoud Pezeshkian signed the Islamabad Memorandum — a Pakistan-brokered framework calling for an immediate halt to hostilities, reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, a phased lifting of US sanctions, and a $300 billion reconstruction plan for Iran, with 60 days set aside to hammer out final terms. That window closed on August 17 without an agreement, undone in large part by disputes over how ships should transit Hormuz. Trump ordered his envoys to stop talking to Iran the following day, and by September 2 he was dismissing the idea of a deal altogether. Fighting resumed in the strait days later.

The human cost of that breakdown showed up again on Saturday, when an Iranian commercial vessel was struck by an unidentified projectile near Hengam and Qeshm islands, killing one crew member and injuring three others. No one has claimed responsibility. It was the latest in a string of attacks that have cut daily tanker traffic through Hormuz — a passage that normally carries roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil — to a fraction of its usual volume.

Oil markets price in a war without an end date

Crude has answered accordingly. Brent settled above $101 a barrel earlier this month, its highest close since May, while WTI pushed past $96, both up more than 3% in a single session on fears that Hormuz exports would keep falling. Goldman Sachs has raised its December forecasts by $5 a barrel and warned Brent could clear $120 in 2027 if Gulf output stays roughly 4 million barrels a day below pre-war levels — a scenario that now looks less like a tail risk than a live possibility given how little diplomatic traction either the US-Iran or Gulf-Iran tracks have found.

Trump, for his part, has offered a timeline that markets can't easily price: he told reporters this month he expects the war to end "immediately after" November's midterm elections, arguing Iran is deliberately prolonging the conflict to damage him politically, and that elevated pump prices are unlikely to ease before the vote. It is a forecast built on political incentive rather than negotiating progress, and it sits awkwardly next to continued US strikes on Iranian targets and Tehran's own attacks on shipping.

What's next

Oman and Iran have said the Salalah talks will be rescheduled, not abandoned, though no date has been set. Until they happen, the practical framework for Hormuz security remains whatever ad hoc arrangement tankers and navies manage day to day — with the Islamabad Memorandum's 60-day clock already expired and Trump's midterm timeline five weeks out. The next real test is whether Oman can narrow the gap between a voluntary and a compulsory shipping-safety regime before another vessel is hit.
