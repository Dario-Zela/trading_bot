Reform UK's £72m crypto windfall and poll surge force Lords funding debate

Nigel Farage's Reform UK received £72m from two crypto-linked donors in a single weekend. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, CC BY 3.0
In one sentence
Two crypto fortunes gave Reform UK £72m in a weekend without breaking any crypto or overseas-donor rule, forcing the Lords to debate the domestic cap the Commons avoided.

In one sentence: Two crypto fortunes gave Reform UK £72m in 48 hours without touching crypto rules or a domestic cap, and peers now want to know why.

On Friday, Ben Delo — the Sheffield-born co-founder of the crypto exchange BitMEX — gave Reform UK £36 million, the largest single donation in British political history. On Saturday, Christopher Harborne, a Thailand-based investor who holds roughly 12% of the stablecoin issuer Tether, matched it to the pound. Seventy-two million pounds arrived at Nigel Farage's party in a single weekend — more than Labour spent on its entire 2024 general election campaign, according to Labour MP Stella Creasy — and neither donation touched a single rule Parliament has written to govern crypto money in politics.

That contradiction is now the House of Lords' problem. The Representation of the People Bill, which cleared the Commons on 2 September and moved to the upper chamber the following day, imposes a £100,000 annual cap on donations from overseas electors and a moratorium on donations made directly in cryptoassets. Both Delo and Harborne are UK-registered voters, and both donations were paid in cash, not coin — so neither provision touches them. Peers return to the bill this week, and campaigners, trade unions and a clutch of Labour backbenchers want to know why ministers wrote rules narrow enough to leave a £72 million loophole in plain sight.

Money with a UK passport

The mechanics are almost too tidy. Delo pleaded guilty in 2022 to US Bank Secrecy Act violations over BitMEX's anti-money-laundering failures, paid a $10 million fine, served 30 months' probation, and was pardoned by Donald Trump in March 2025 alongside his BitMEX co-founders. He moved back to the UK this year. Harborne, listed among the country's wealthiest crypto-adjacent figures — the Sunday Times Rich List put his fortune at £18.2 billion in May — has given Reform and its Brexit Party predecessor money since 2019 and had already handed over £9 million in a single earlier gift.

Both men are registered UK electors, a technical status that under current law lets them give unlimited sums regardless of where they actually live or how their wealth was made. The bill before the Lords would cap overseas electors at £100,000 a year, with a backdated start date of 25 March and a minimum-residency rule for anyone who moves home to dodge it. But Delo and Harborne gave as domestic donors, sidestepping a cap built for a different category of person entirely. Communities Secretary Angela Rayner has said the donations "could fall within" the new rules once enacted, but stopped short of a legal finding — parties would have 30 days to return any sum later judged unlawful.

"I believe it is the duty of people with broad shoulders and deep pockets to step up and help Reform to win and govern, rather [than] allow the British people to be trapped in the political monoculture that is driving this country off a cliff." — Ben Delo

Harborne, for his part, told the Daily Telegraph the gift was "a philanthropic gift to our country," and that his "competitive spirit" had inspired him to match Delo's figure. He said he expected nothing in return — no policy shift, no peerage. Reform's spokesperson said the donations "comply strictly with Electoral Commission rules and UK law," a claim the party's economy spokesman Robert Jenrick has separately echoed. Research from campaign group Democracy for Sale puts the concentration in sharper relief: three men — Harborne, deputy leader Richard Tice and businessman Jeremy Hosking — have supplied three-quarters of everything Reform and the Brexit Party have raised since 2019.

Why the Commons looked away

MPs had a chance to close the domestic-cap gap themselves. Attempts to write a general cap on donations into the bill failed in the Commons after ministers persuaded colleagues to accept a promise instead: officials would be tasked with investigating how such a cap might work, with no firm timetable attached. That left the overseas-elector cap and the crypto moratorium as the bill's only teeth, and left the door open for exactly the manoeuvre Delo executed — he had reportedly planned to give Reform £1 million a month through to 2029, then accelerated the whole sum into one payment specifically as a hedge against a cap that might arrive later.

That is the detail that has hardened opinion among Labour backbenchers and unions. Former shadow chancellor John McDonnell has pressed ministers and union leaders to move faster, arguing the scale of the donations has made the case for a cap on its own. Creasy's line — that one night's giving to Reform outstripped Labour's entire election spend — has circulated widely at Westminster this week precisely because it makes the asymmetry legible to people who don't follow campaign-finance law. Peers are expected to press ministers for a firm timetable on a donations-cap review when the bill returns to the Lords, rather than the open-ended commitment that satisfied the Commons.

The polling backdrop

The donations land as Reform's electoral position hardens rather than softens. PollCheck's rolling seven-poll average as of 12 September puts Labour on 26.0%, Reform on 23.1% and the Conservatives on 20.1%; BritPolls' separate poll-of-polls has it at 25.4%, 23.6% and 19.6% respectively. Both trackers agree on the shape: Labour, under Prime Minister Andy Burnham since July, has clawed back a narrow lead after Reform spent 18 months ahead in the polls, but Reform remains comfortably second and the Conservatives have slipped to a clear third. Individual pollsters disagree on the margin — some recent Opinium and Ipsos surveys show Labour further ahead — but no reputable tracker currently shows Reform back in the lead.

That context matters for how the donations read politically. A party several points behind in the polls receiving the two largest single donations in British history is not simply a story about generous benefactors; it is a bet, from two men whose fortunes were built in an industry Westminster still barely regulates, that Reform's current standing understates where it will be by the next election — due no later than August 2029, the date Delo has said he is pacing his giving against.

What comes next

The substantive test is not this week's debate but what peers can actually extract from ministers: a binding timetable for a general donations cap, rather than the open-ended review the Commons accepted. Watch, too, for whether the Electoral Commission opens any inquiry into how the two donations were structured, and whether Rayner's ambiguous "could fall within" hardens into an actual finding once the bill receives royal assent. Until a cap on domestic giving exists, the lesson of this fortnight is a simple one: crypto wealth that converts to sterling before it reaches a party's bank account moves through UK election law as easily as any other. The moratorium was built for coins. The money came as cash.
