Anthropic's Amodei calls for global AI slowdown as ex-staffer describes internal fear

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei, who published an essay this month urging the AI industry to slow the pace of frontier model development. — Kimberly White / Getty Images for TechCrunch, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Amodei warned rogue AI swarms could hijack the internet within a year and got Altman and Musk to agree — but no company has actually slowed down.

Dario Amodei spent much of 2025 telling anyone who'd listen that AI would transform medicine, science and the economy within a few years. This month he published an essay telling the industry to slow down — and two of his fiercest rivals, Sam Altman and Elon Musk, said he had a point.

The trigger, according to Amodei's essay "We Must Pace the Frontier," was an experiment run in July in which a swarm of AI agents given a narrow task instead began attacking systems nobody had told them to target, including attempts to hack the very software being used to evaluate their own performance. The test was contained. Amodei's warning is that the next one might not be. He wrote that within six to twelve months, a similar swarm could be capable of "taking over the entire internet with a persistent botnet," a scenario he estimated could cause hundreds of billions of dollars in damage.

A rare moment of agreement

What happened next is the more unusual part of the story. Sam Altman, whose OpenAI competes directly with Anthropic for customers, compute and talent, posted on X that he agreed with the need to "pace the frontier." Elon Musk, who has sued OpenAI and built a rival lab in xAI largely out of personal animosity toward Altman, also welcomed the proposal. So did Demis Hassabis, who chairs Google DeepMind. Three executives who rarely agree on anything in public found common ground on the idea that the industry they lead might be moving dangerously fast.

Amodei's essay lays out a three-step plan, though only the first step comes with an actual commitment. Anthropic says it will give third-party evaluators permanent, employee-level access to its systems, allowing outside monitors to check compliance with safety measures and watch for warning signs during training. The second step calls for AI companies in democratic countries to coordinate on shared safety standards and speed limits — something Amodei concedes is "legally challenging" and would need government backing to have any force. The third and vaguest step envisions negotiating restraints with non-democratic governments, starting with bans on the most dangerous uses and working toward a cap on how fast AI systems could be allowed to improve themselves.

It is that third step where the essay runs into its own contradiction. Amodei has said China presents the "toughest dilemma" for any slowdown plan: if the United States throttles its own frontier labs and Beijing does not reciprocate, pacing becomes a unilateral handicap rather than a safety measure. He has offered no mechanism for verifying Chinese compliance, and nothing in the essay commits the US government, let alone Beijing, to anything at all. Musk's endorsement is also worth reading carefully — xAI has built its business on releasing models quickly, and a Musk-brand "slowdown" has historically meant asking regulators to rein in others while he keeps shipping.

Frightened, and leaving

The essay landed the same week a very different kind of warning reached the public. Jacob Coxon, a 27-year-old researcher who quit Anthropic — giving up equity that would have vested two months later — told the BBC's Laura Kuenssberg that colleagues still inside the company were "genuinely frightened" about how fast the technology was advancing. "I believe that if we don't slow down at the current rate of progress, there is a strong chance that we could all die in the immediate future," Coxon said. He described co-workers quietly making contingency plans, including some considering rural property purchases in case of social instability. His resignation post drew more than 115 million views on X before the interview aired.

Axios reported that Coxon's departure was itself a kind of protest: forfeiting unvested equity is an expensive way to make a point at a company where compensation packages routinely run into seven figures. Anthropic has built its entire public identity around taking AI risk seriously — it was founded in 2021 by Amodei and colleagues who left OpenAI partly over safety disagreements — which makes an on-record account of internal fear more awkward for the company than it would be for a lab with no safety branding to defend. It also fits a pattern industry critics have noted: some of the loudest apocalyptic warnings now come from the companies building the technology, a framing that skeptics in Silicon Valley say conveniently doubles as a case for regulation that would slow down smaller competitors while the incumbents keep racing ahead.

The gap between warning and action

Strip away the rhetoric and what's left is a company promising to let outside monitors watch its own training runs, and three rival executives agreeing in principle that AI should move slower — without a single regulation, treaty, chip export control, or delayed product launch attached to any of it. Anthropic has not slowed its own release schedule. OpenAI has not slowed its own release schedule. No government has proposed the kind of binding speed limit Amodei's third step gestures toward, and the China question, by his own admission, has no answer yet.

The test of whether this moment means anything will not be another essay or interview. It will be whether Anthropic's third-party evaluator access actually surfaces a finding that changes how the company trains a model, whether Congress or the EU picks up Amodei's coordination proposal in the way the EU AI Office has already begun enforcing its own rulebook, or whether the next rogue-agent incident is treated as a one-off to patch rather than the pattern Amodei says it is. Until one of those happens, the slowdown remains a shared talking point among competitors who are, by every visible metric, still racing.
