EU AI Office begins first wave of compliance inspections under AI Act

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which houses the AI Office coordinating the EU's AI Act enforcement. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
Brussels' AI Office and regulators in France and Germany have begun document-request sweeps of HR, credit-scoring and healthcare AI — a rehearsal for the real high-risk deadline, now delayed to December 2027.

The email that landed in the compliance inbox of a mid-sized German recruitment platform late last month did not announce a fine. It asked for an architecture diagram, a data-governance log, and a written account of how a human reviews the system's shortlisting decisions. That request — and others like it sent to a credit-scoring unit in France and a private hospital group running an AI triage tool — mark the moment the EU's AI Act stopped being a document lawyers cite and became a set of officials showing up with clipboards.

The European Commission's AI Office, working with national authorities including France's CNIL and Germany's BfDI, has begun its first coordinated round of compliance checks aimed at high-risk AI systems: the software that screens job applicants, prices credit risk, and helps decide which patients get seen first. It is a small, procedural-sounding development. It is also the first real test of whether Europe's much-discussed AI rulebook has teeth.

A rulebook meets its first deadline — sort of

Here is the complication that most coverage of this glosses over: the AI Act's core compliance deadline for exactly these systems was supposed to be 2 August 2026. It wasn't. Under a Digital Omnibus agreement that entered into force in late July, the full conformity-assessment regime for standalone "high-risk" systems under Annex III — recruitment tools, credit scoring, healthcare triage among them — was pushed back sixteen months, to 2 December 2027. AI embedded in already-regulated products, like medical devices, now has until August 2028.

So the headline claim — that Brussels is "enforcing the AI Act" on these systems — needs a qualifier. What did stay on schedule is narrower but not trivial: the Article 5 ban on a short list of prohibited practices (things like manipulative or exploitative AI, and untargeted scraping for facial-recognition databases), and the Article 50 transparency duties requiring companies to disclose when someone is dealing with an AI system rather than a person. Penalties for prohibited practices run up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, whichever is larger — the same ceiling as the EU's toughest antitrust fines.

What the AI Office and its national partners are doing now, in other words, is not yet the full conformity audit the Act eventually requires. It's a preview: information requests, documentation checks, and — critically — a dry run for both regulators and companies of what enforcement will look like when the real deadline arrives. Companies that treat this as a false alarm because "the deadline moved" are misreading the moment.

Why HR, credit and health first

The three sectors singled out are not random. Under Annex III of the Act, AI used in recruitment and employment decisions, AI used to assess creditworthiness, and AI used for medical triage are each explicitly named as high-risk categories — the tier just below the outright bans. They combine two things regulators care about most: automated decisions with material consequences for a person's income, health or livelihood, and a paper trail of technical documentation (risk assessments, training-data provenance, human-oversight logs) that inspectors can actually request and evaluate without needing to reverse-engineer a model.

CNIL's dual role illustrates how enforcement is likely to work in practice even before 2027. As France's data-protection authority, CNIL already has GDPR powers over any company processing personal data to make automated decisions — which covers virtually every AI hiring or lending tool. It has said it intends to fold AI Act guidance into that existing enforcement track rather than wait for a separate regime to switch on. Germany's BfDI, which shares AI Act supervisory duties with the federal network regulator Bundesnetzagentur for systems that touch personal data, is taking a similar approach. The effect is that companies face scrutiny now, under older laws with real bite, dressed in the AI Act's new vocabulary.

What a request for documents actually forces

The practical demand behind these inspections is Annex IV of the Act: a technical documentation dossier that high-risk system providers must be able to produce on request, covering the system's design, the data used to train it, its risk-management process, and evidence that a human can meaningfully intervene in or override its output. Many companies that built HR-screening or credit-scoring tools years before the Act existed simply don't have this paperwork assembled — it has to be reconstructed after the fact, which is slower and more revealing of gaps than building it in from the start.

That is the quiet enforcement lever at work here. A regulator doesn't need to prove an algorithm is biased to create real cost; it only needs to ask for documentation a company can't produce on schedule. For a bank running a credit-scoring model across multiple EU markets, or a hospital group using the same triage software in several countries, an information request from one national authority can effectively become a bloc-wide audit, since the AI Office is meant to coordinate findings across the 24 national market-surveillance bodies rather than let them work in isolation.

The precedent being watched from outside Brussels

None of the jurisdictions weighing their own AI rules — the UK, which has so far resisted binding legislation and rejected a proposed AI "kill switch" requirement, various US states, and regulators across Asia — have anything resembling the AI Act's structure. All of them are watching what happens when Europe's rules meet real companies rather than draft text. If the current round of document requests turns up systems that simply cannot produce the required paperwork, that will shape the argument for stricter binding rules elsewhere. If it turns up mostly compliant systems with paperwork gaps, that will support the industry argument that the framework is manageable without heavy-handed enforcement.

The next marker to watch is less the outcome of these initial document requests — which are likely to conclude quietly, sector by sector, over the coming months — than 2 December 2027, when the delayed Annex III deadline actually arrives and conformity assessments become mandatory rather than advisory. What the AI Office and CNIL and BfDI are doing now is effectively rehearsing for that date, one recruitment platform and one credit file at a time. Companies with fifteen months of runway left would do well to notice.
