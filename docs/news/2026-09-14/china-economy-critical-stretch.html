China's economy enters 'critical' stretch as consumer spending stays weak

Shanghai's Pudong financial district, the visual shorthand for an economy caught between industrial strength and consumer weakness. — Jens Schott Knudsen / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
China's August data is expected to show factories stabilising but consumer spending growing under 1%, forcing Beijing's hand on stimulus before October's holiday lull.

China's statisticians release the August economic scorecard on Tuesday, and the number that matters most isn't the one that will make headlines. Industrial production is expected to have ticked up to roughly 4.8% year-on-year, a touch faster than July's 4.5%, according to the median forecast in a Bloomberg survey of economists. That will read, on its face, as stabilisation. The more consequential figure sits a few lines down: retail sales, the best available proxy for how Chinese households are actually spending, are forecast to have grown less than 1% from a year earlier — a fraction of the 3.6% average pace logged across 2024 and 2025.

That gap between a steadying factory floor and a stalling checkout counter is the story of China's economy heading into the final stretch of 2026, and it is why economists and officials alike are treating this week's release as more than routine housekeeping. Citigroup economists including Xiangrong Yu wrote that activity should "stay sluggish" through August, with September now cast as the real test of whether the slowdown is bottoming out or deepening.

A consumer that won't cooperate

The weakness is not new, but it is proving stubborn. Retail sales growth cooled to just 0.6% year-on-year in July — well short of the 1.8% pace economists had penciled in — as a pull-forward effect from 2025's trade-in subsidies and car-purchase tax breaks faded. Those programmes borrowed demand from the future rather than creating new appetite, and the bill is now coming due in the data. For the first seven months of 2026, total retail sales of goods and services rose just 2.6% year-on-year, with goods consumption up a meagre 1.1% even as services spending, boosted by travel and leisure, managed 5%.

The property downturn sits underneath much of this. New home prices fell 0.3% month-on-month in the latest 70-city survey and used-home prices dropped 0.58%, with only a single city recording a rise in resale prices. Property development investment is running roughly 18% below year-ago levels for the first half of the year, and forecasters expect the August reading to show a decline of more than 20%. For a household sector whose wealth is still overwhelmingly tied up in real estate, that continued erosion is doing more to shape spending decisions than any single stimulus announcement can offset.

Fixed-asset investment as a whole is expected to show a 7.1% year-on-year drop for January-through-August, steeper than the 6.7% fall recorded through July and the weakest pace since 2020. Private-sector investment has fared even worse. There are pockets of strength — high-tech manufacturing output has been growing at roughly 9.3%, and rail, ship and aircraft production at 12% — but they are concentrated in state-directed industrial policy priorities, not the broad base of consumer-facing business.

Beijing's calculus

The Politburo signalled in late July that it wanted stronger government outlays to support the economy through the rest of the year. That signal, according to Bloomberg Economics' Chang Shu and Eric Zhu, had not yet translated into actual spending by August — bureaucratic follow-through in China's fiscal system tends to lag political intent by a quarter or more. That makes September, and the data due out for it in mid-October, the more revealing test of whether Beijing is willing to move from rhetoric to renminbi.

So far, the People's Bank of China and Ministry of Finance have leaned on targeted tools rather than a broad-based blast of stimulus: interest subsidies on personal consumption loans running from September through next August, refinancing facilities aimed at services consumption and eldercare, and roughly 99.9 billion yuan in childcare subsidies for 2026, up 10.6% from the year before. Pantheon Macroeconomics has characterised the overall posture as policymakers "keeping their powder dry" — holding back firepower for a more serious downturn rather than spending it to smooth over what is, for now, a soft patch.

That restraint has a shelf life. Raymond Yeung at Australia & New Zealand Banking Group noted that September represents "an important policy window" — the last real opportunity to act before China's October national holidays, when factories and consumers alike shift into an extended lull that makes fresh stimulus harder to calibrate and slower to take effect. Some economists, including those at FXStreet's China desk, now put a real probability on a further 10-basis-point policy rate cut and a 50-basis-point reduction in banks' reserve requirement ratio in the coming weeks if the data disappoints.

What it means for the growth target

Beijing set a 2026 GDP growth target of 4.5% to 5% at the March legislative session, its lowest on record, with most private forecasters — including Goldman Sachs, which lifted its call to 4.8% — expecting the actual number to land toward the upper-middle of that band. Hitting it does not require heroics from the consumer alone; exports have held up better than feared, up 4.4% year-on-year even as shipments to the US keep shrinking, and the tech and advanced-manufacturing sectors are carrying more than their share of the load.

But a growth number propped up by factories and exporters while households retrench is a narrower, more fragile kind of expansion than one built on broad domestic demand — and it leaves China more exposed to any renewed friction in trade or a sharper property slide. That is the trade-off Tuesday's data will sharpen rather than resolve. The immediate catalysts to watch are the August retail sales print itself, any follow-through on the Politburo's July spending signal in September's fiscal data, and whether the PBOC moves on rates or reserve requirements before the October holiday window closes.
