HSBC lifts 2026 S&P 500 target to 8,100 on earnings optimism

HSBC's tower at Canary Wharf, London, headquarters of the bank whose strategists lifted their S&P 500 target to 8,100. — Christine Matthews / Geograph, CC BY-SA 2.0
In one sentence
HSBC raised its 2026 S&P 500 target to 8,100, wagering that earnings growth can outrun the Iran conflict and Fed uncertainty.

HSBC has raised its year-end 2026 target for the S&P 500 to 8,100, up from 7,650, betting that corporate earnings can keep outrunning a list of macro worries that includes the Iran conflict, a jumpy Federal Reserve and seasonal September weakness.

The call, from HSBC strategist Nicole Inui, is the bank's second upgrade to the target this year — it started 2026 at 7,500, lifted it to 7,650 mid-year, and has now added another 450 points. The new target implies roughly 5% further upside from last week's close and would put the index up close to 18% for the year, extending a rally that has already climbed 12.75% year-to-date despite two bouts of geopolitical shock.

The earnings case

HSBC's math rests almost entirely on the numerator, not the multiple. The bank estimates first-half 2026 earnings-per-share growth for the S&P 500 came in near 40%, and expects more than 25% growth in the second half — enough to put full-year 2026 earnings growth at 33%, or $360 a share. Of the 492 S&P 500 companies that had reported by early September, 86% beat analyst estimates, well above the long-run average of 67.5%.

Crucially, HSBC is not asking investors to pay more for those earnings. The bank applies a price-to-earnings multiple of 22.5 times — in line with recent historical norms — rather than assuming further multiple expansion. "Multiple expansion may be challenging even as fundamentals improve," Inui wrote, which is a more conservative framing than it might first appear: the target goes up because the E is expected to rise, not because Wall Street is willing to pay more for it. The bank points to AI-linked capital spending as the engine, flowing through into semiconductors and megacap technology names, and stays overweight technology, financials and industrials while turning more selective on consumer-facing sectors.

Betting against the headline risks

The more striking part of the call is what HSBC is choosing to wave off. Hostilities in the Middle East have rattled oil and currency markets through the year — Brent has held above $100 a barrel as the Iran conflict grinds on with Gulf ceasefire talks repeatedly lapsing — and investors have also been fretting over the possibility of a Fed rate hike rather than a cut. HSBC's response, in Inui's words, is that "these concerns are overdone." The bank expects the Fed to stay on hold rather than tighten, and forecasts the 10-year Treasury yield at 4.65% by year-end, and argues that geopolitical shocks have so far done limited aggregate damage to consumer spending.

HSBC isn't alone in the 8,000-plus camp — Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley and Citigroup all have year-end targets at or above that level — but the size of the two upgrades this year makes HSBC's the most aggressive repricing among the major houses. The bank does flag some near-term turbulence: seasonal September weakness, upcoming inflation prints, regulatory pressure on data centers and social media companies, midterm election volatility, and heavier liquidity demands from IPOs and hyperscaler AI funding rounds. None of those, in HSBC's view, are enough to break the earnings trend.

What would break the thesis

The target is a wager that fundamentals can decouple from the macro noise unsettling fixed-income and currency markets. That wager only holds as long as earnings keep delivering — a run of guidance cuts, a genuine escalation in the Gulf that pushes oil meaningfully higher, or a Fed that actually hikes rather than holds would each undercut the case in a different way. The next test arrives with the Fed's decision this Wednesday, followed by third-quarter earnings season, when the 25%-plus second-half growth HSBC is counting on either shows up in the numbers or doesn't.
