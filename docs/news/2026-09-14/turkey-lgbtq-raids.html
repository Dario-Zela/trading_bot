Turkish police detain dozens, including LGBTQ+ activists, in nationwide raids

Police detain a protester during an earlier Istanbul Pride march — a recurring flashpoint in Turkey's decade-long restriction of LGBTQ+ public life. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Turkish police detained dozens in raids on gay bars and Kaos GL activists across 12 provinces, part of a widening crackdown that rights groups call state-led discrimination.

Turkish police fanned out across twelve provinces before dawn on Sunday, raiding gay bars, activist offices and private homes in a coordinated operation that prosecutors say has now ensnared at least 63 people, with legal proceedings opened against more than 160 individuals, nine associations and thirteen businesses. The government has given the campaign a title lifted from a family-values slogan — "My Family is Safe" — and cast it as child protection. Rights groups call it something else: the criminalisation of an entire community.

The raids targeted Kaos GL, one of Turkey's oldest and most prominent LGBTQ+ organisations, whose Ankara offices were entered by police while board and audit-committee members were taken into custody. The Ankara chief prosecutor's office said 21 people were detained as part of a probe into the group, which it accuses of publishing "obscene" material accessible to children. In Istanbul, the chief prosecutor's office reported 26 detentions and said officers seized drugs, digital equipment, smuggled alcohol and a firearm during the sweep — items rights monitors say bear no relation to the LGBTQ+ advocacy that was the stated target.

A decade-long campaign, sharpening

Turkey's ruling AK Party banned Istanbul's Pride march in 2015, and every attempt to revive it since has been met with tear gas, water cannon and mass detentions — including more than 300 arrests in a single 2022 crackdown. What distinguishes this weekend's operation is its scope and its digital reach. Under a ruling from the Istanbul 7th Penal Judgeship of Peace, access to websites and social media accounts belonging to 73 associations and activists was blocked nationwide. Kaos GL's X account went dark for Turkish users days before the raids, replaced by a message that it had been "withheld in Turkey in response to a legal demand."

Justice Minister Akın Gürlek framed the crackdown as consistent with President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan's "2026–2035 Decade of Family and Population" initiative, a policy vision aimed at reversing Turkey's falling birth rate. Erdoğan himself has recently described LGBTQ+ people as a "deviant movement," language rights groups say has moved from rhetorical hostility to direct legal instrumentalisation — associations shuttered, accounts blocked, members prosecuted, in a single administrative motion.

The Turkish Human Rights Association rejected the government's framing outright. "The real name of this operation is not 'My Family is Safe,'" the group said in a statement; "it is state-led discrimination, politics of fear and the dismantling of civil society." Kaos GL, in its own account of the raid, described officers entering its Ankara premises and detaining members of its governing board — an action the group says has no basis beyond the online-obscenity allegation now under prosecution.

Reading the raids as a governance signal

For investors tracking Turkey, the weekend's raids land against a backdrop already strained by executive overreach. The March 2025 arrest of Istanbul mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu — Erdoğan's most credible rival for 2028 — triggered a roughly 15% lira plunge, forced an emergency rate hike to 46% and drained an estimated $25 billion in central bank reserves defending the currency. Fifteen opposition mayors remain imprisoned, and courts have since invalidated party conventions belonging to the main opposition CHP in what analysts term the country's "absolute nullity crisis." The pattern that emerges is not episodic but structural: a government responding to political and social pressure by widening the net of who can be prosecuted, whose organisation can be shut down, whose account can be blocked, with the judiciary functioning as an instrument of that widening rather than a check on it.

This matters commercially as much as morally. Turkey drew roughly $800 billion in cumulative foreign investment inflows over the past two decades on the strength of relatively secular, rules-based institutions relative to regional peers — a reputational asset now eroding by degrees. Rating agencies and asset managers increasingly fold civil-liberties trajectories into sovereign risk models, not out of sentiment but because a state willing to seize activists' bank accounts or freeze an NGO's social media presence by judicial fiat has demonstrated the same toolkit could, in a different dispute, be turned on a foreign-owned asset, a contract, or a currency peg. The lira's repeated crisis interventions already show a central bank spending scarce reserves to paper over confidence shocks that originate in the courts, not in the balance of payments.

What comes next

Defense lawyers for those detained say formal charges — likely to include violations of the Associations Law and obscenity statutes carrying penalties of up to several years — could be filed within the week, setting up trials that will test how far Turkish courts are willing to go in prosecuting advocacy as a criminal act. Kaos GL says it intends to challenge the account blocks and detentions through Turkey's constitutional court and, if necessary, the European Court of Human Rights, a path previous LGBTQ+ cases from Turkey have taken with mixed results. Watch also for the reaction from Brussels, where Turkey's stalled EU accession talks already cite rule-of-law backsliding; a formal condemnation from the European Parliament, expected within days given past precedent, would mark the clearest external signal yet of how this episode is being read beyond Turkey's borders.
