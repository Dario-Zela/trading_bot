BoE on track to complete £70bn gilt reduction by September

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, whose Asset Purchase Facility holds the gilts being run down under quantitative tightening. — Adrian Pingstone (Arpingstone), Wikimedia Commons, public domain
In one sentence
The BoE is on track to shrink its gilt stockpile to £488bn by month-end, with the real news — next year's QT pace — due at the 17 September MPC meeting.

The Bank of England is closing in on a self-imposed target: shrinking its stockpile of government bonds to £488 billion by the end of this month, the last stop on a year-long, deliberately unspectacular exercise in balance-sheet reduction.

The number comes from the Bank's Asset Purchase Facility (APF), the vehicle that holds gilts bought during more than a decade of quantitative easing. At its September 2025 meeting, the Monetary Policy Committee voted to run the stock down by £70 billion over the twelve months to September 2026 — a slower pace than the £100 billion taken off in the previous year, split between roughly £21 billion of active sales and the rest left to mature naturally.

As of 30 June, the APF still held £521.8 billion, meaning roughly £34 billion of reduction was left to do in the final quarter. The Bank's own market notice for that quarter — covering five auctions between July and September, together worth about £4.2 billion in short- and medium-dated gilts — flagged that "it may be necessary... to make some adjustment to the sizes of the later auctions to meet the target," the kind of hedging that suggests the runway to £488 billion is tight but intact, not that the Bank expects to miss it.

A process story, not a policy shift

None of this represents a change of course. Quantitative tightening has been under way since 2022, first through letting bonds run off as they matured and later through outright sales layered on top. The pace has consistently slowed each year — £80 billion, then £100 billion, now £70 billion — a trajectory that reflects the Bank's stated aim of avoiding disruption to the gilt market rather than any acceleration or hesitation. Gilt yields have shown no sign of unusual strain tied to the sales programme, and the Bank has continued to skew sales away from longer-dated debt, where demand has been patchier, toward the short and medium end of the curve.

That absence of drama is, in its way, the story. A programme built to unwind roughly a tenth of the government bond market's outstanding stock has proceeded on schedule, in public, without moving markets — which is exactly what a central bank wants from balance-sheet policy and exactly why it rarely makes headlines on its own.

What comes next

The more consequential number lands just after the target is reached. The MPC's annual review of QT pace is due alongside its rate decision on 17 September, when it will set the reduction programme for October 2026 to September 2027. Investors are pricing in a further slowdown to around £50 billion for that year, continuing the taper rather than reversing it. That decision, not the completion of this year's £70 billion run-down, is the one likely to move gilt yields.
