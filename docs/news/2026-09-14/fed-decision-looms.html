Fed decision looms Wednesday as markets split between hold and hike

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, whose Jackson Hole remarks that the Fed still has "work to do" on inflation pushed hike odds sharply higher. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors (public domain)
In one sentence
The Fed meets Sept 15-16 with markets near-evenly split on a hold or 25bp hike, as Iran-driven oil shocks push J.P. Morgan and Goldman to reverse their no-hike calls.

The Federal Reserve's rate-setting committee sits down Tuesday for a two-day meeting that, as recently as August, looked like a formality. Inflation was easing, the July meeting had already delivered a hold, and futures markets had priced in a quiet September. That is no longer the case. The Federal Open Market Committee meets September 15–16 with traders, economists and now two of Wall Street's biggest banks split roughly down the middle on whether the Fed holds its benchmark rate at 3.50%–3.75% or raises it a quarter point — a live possibility that did not exist a month ago.

The reason sits roughly 1,500 miles from the Eccles Building, in the waters of the Strait of Hormuz. Brent crude closed at $101.21 a barrel on September 9, up more than 9% on the week, after U.S. Central Command confirmed the destruction of several Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf of Oman. Saudi Arabia has shut its East-West pipeline as a precaution. Gulf diplomats and Iranian counterparts have been trying, and mostly failing, to agree on a temporary arrangement for keeping tankers moving through the strait. None of that is monetary policy. All of it is now the Fed's problem.

The bank calls that moved the needle

J.P. Morgan Wealth Management strategists now expect a 25 basis point hike this week, reversing a prior call of no changes for all of 2026. Goldman Sachs made the same reversal in the same week. Both cited the same trigger: consumer and producer price data released last week came in hotter than expected, undercutting the disinflation narrative that justified July's hold. Goldman's own note was candid about the mechanism — the bank said the shift was "driven less by its economic outlook and more by financial market pricing," meaning it moved because traders had already moved, not because its inflation model changed. That is itself a tell: when a bank downgrades its own conviction and hikes its forecast anyway, it is describing a market that has started pricing certainty the underlying data doesn't fully support.

The market pricing in question has swung hard. The CME FedWatch tool had the odds of a September hike at roughly 36% before Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech on August 28. After Warsh said the Fed still has "work to do" on inflation, those odds jumped above 60%, and by this week some measures put them near 87%. Not everyone reads Warsh's remarks as a policy signal — several Fed watchers noted that a hawkish speech from a chair still establishing his inflation-fighting credentials, five months into the job, is not the same as a committee vote. Warsh, confirmed by the Senate in May by a 54–45 margin, the narrowest in the position's history, has said he intends to run an independent Fed rather than one that bends to White House pressure for lower rates — a stance that makes his Jackson Hole hawkishness carry more weight than a chair with an established dovish record might.

Why oil complicates the call

Ordinarily, a Fed staring at above-target inflation would hike without much internal debate. The complication here is where the inflation is coming from. Energy-driven price spikes tied to a supply shock — tankers destroyed, a pipeline shut, a strait partially blocked — are not the same animal as demand-driven inflation from an overheating consumer. Standard central-banking doctrine treats an oil shock as something to look through, because a rate hike does nothing to un-block a shipping lane and does plenty to slow an economy that was already showing signs of cooling before the Iran conflict began.

July's meeting hinted at exactly that tension: the Fed held rates on a 9–3 vote, with three members dissenting in favor of a hike even then. That dissent is part of why the bar for a September move has "lowered," in J.P. Morgan's phrasing — markets read the split vote as evidence the committee's inflation-fighting resolve was already fraying, and each hot data print since has reinforced the reading. Hike now, on an oil shock, and the Fed risks looking like it is tightening into a war-driven supply disruption rather than a demand problem — good for near-term credibility on the 2% target, but a policy response that treats the wrong disease. Hold again, and it risks confirming the market's suspicion that this Fed will tolerate above-target inflation rather than defend its mandate, undermining the credibility a new chair needs most in his first six months.

What a surprise does to markets

The practical stakes are less about the 25 basis points themselves — a quarter point either way is a rounding error against a benchmark rate near 3.6% — and more about surprise. Rate decisions that diverge from what futures markets have priced tend to produce sharp, fast moves in equities, Treasuries and the dollar, and those moves are amplified by algorithmic and systematic strategies that react within milliseconds of the 2 p.m. statement. A market pricing an 87% chance of a hike has largely front-run the outcome; a market split closer to 50-50, which is where some prediction platforms have it, has not. Given that Brent is already up double digits on the month and equities have been trading nervously around Iran headlines, a hold that reads as complacent — or a hike that reads as panicked — both carry the ingredients for an outsized intraday swing, independent of which direction the Fed actually chooses.

J.P. Morgan has tried to draw a line for how bad the energy shock would need to get before it turns into something closer to a demand shock via recession: oil above $140 a barrel combined with a sharp equity selloff, well above the roughly $100–105 range Brent has occupied through early September. Below that line, the bank's base case is that this stays a supply-side inflation problem the Fed can address with modest tightening rather than a recessionary one requiring cuts.

What comes next

The decision lands Wednesday at 2 p.m. Eastern, followed by Warsh's press conference and an updated dot plot that will matter as much as the vote itself — a hold paired with hawkish forward guidance would land differently than a hike paired with a signal that the Fed considers this a one-and-done adjustment. Markets will also be watching the vote count: another split decision, like July's 9–3, would tell traders more about the durability of this Fed's inflation stance than the headline number does. Beyond Wednesday, the next test comes from the Gulf itself — any credible de-escalation around the Strait of Hormuz would do more to settle inflation expectations than a quarter point of tightening, and any further tanker attack would make Wednesday's decision look small by comparison.
