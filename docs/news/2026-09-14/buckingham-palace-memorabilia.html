Hidden trove of royal household memorabilia found in Buckingham Palace restoration

Buckingham Palace, where a decade-long £369m reservicing project has been rewiring and replumbing rooms untouched since the 1950s. — David Iliff / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Buckingham Palace's £369m rewiring project has turned up 1949 whist-drive tickets and 1957 football pools slips — a charming footnote with zero market relevance.

Somewhere between a rewired fuse box and a replastered ceiling, a workman at Buckingham Palace found a sixpenny ticket to a staff whist drive, still legible after seventy-seven years, embedded in a lump of building rubble. It was dated 14 April 1949 — a Thursday night, most likely, when someone below stairs shuffled a deck of cards and had no idea a construction crew would be picking through the wreckage of that same room the better part of a century later.

The ticket is one of a small haul of everyday debris turned up during the Royal Household's decade-long reservicing of the palace, a £369 million programme to replace wiring, plumbing and heating that in places had not been touched since the 1950s. Alongside it: a set of unused Littlewoods football pools slips from 1957, still blank, waiting on results that were declared and forgotten before most of today's palace staff were born; and a typed schedule from 1977 instructing Queen Elizabeth II to move through the Ballroom in a "clockwise direction" during a Silver Jubilee reception — the kind of stage direction that keeps a state occasion from turning into a scrum.

Decades in the walls

None of it was hidden on purpose. These are the kinds of things that fall down the back of a floorboard or get sealed behind a wall during some earlier repair job and simply stay there — a pair of worn black hobnail boots, an empty Silk Cut cigarette packet, a scrap of 1889 theatre listings from the Standard newspaper, all recovered in the same sweep. Taken together they sketch a rough timeline of palace staff life across more than a century, from Victorian valets to the pools-playing generation of the late 1950s to the footmen marshalling Jubilee guests in 1977.

The reservicing project itself is the real infrastructure story — 160km of electrical cable, much of it six decades old, alongside 48km of water pipes, 6,500 sockets, 5,000 light fittings and 152 lavatories are being replaced under a programme first approved by Parliament in 2016 and due to run until 2027. The finds are a byproduct of that work rather than the point of it, turned up as contractors strip out walls and floors that have not been opened since the Attlee or Callaghan governments.

A footnote, not a headline

There is no suggestion any of this will go on public display beyond the odd mention in palace communications, and the Royal Household has treated the discoveries as an incidental curiosity rather than a heritage event. That is the right way to read them. A whist-drive ticket and some unfilled pools slips say something charming about the rhythms of domestic service in mid-century Britain — the small rituals staff used to pass an evening — but they carry no institutional weight and no bearing on anything beyond the palace's own walls.

The reservicing programme itself remains on budget and broadly on schedule according to the National Audit Office's most recent review, with completion still targeted for 2027. Further finds, if any turn up, are likely to surface the same way this batch did: unannounced, in a contractor's skip, decades after anyone thought to look for them.
