Sweden's left-wing bloc edges ahead in knife-edge election

The Riksdag, Sweden's parliament building in Stockholm, where a three-seat margin will decide the next government. — Steven Lek / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Sweden's left bloc leads 176-173 in a knife-edge election with final results due Thursday, leaving markets guessing on the next government.

Sweden's voters have delivered the closest election result in a generation, and by Sunday night nobody in Stockholm — or in the currency desks watching from London and Frankfurt — could say with certainty who would govern the country by the weekend.

With 94.4 percent of ballots counted, the four-party opposition bloc led by Social Democrat Magdalena Andersson held 176 seats in the 349-seat Riksdag against 173 for Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson's right-wing coalition. That is a margin of three seats — enough for Andersson to declare that "Sweden will get a new government," but not enough for anyone else to agree with her yet. Postal ballots, including votes cast by Swedes abroad, begin being counted Wednesday, and the Election Authority does not expect a certified result until Thursday at the earliest.

A tighter race than the polls promised

The opposition had led by as much as ten points in polling earlier this year, a gap that narrowed to two or three points by election week — and then narrowed further once votes were actually counted. The Social Democrats took 28.1 percent of the vote, their worst showing in more than a century even as they remained Sweden's largest single party. The bigger story of election night, though, was on the right: the Sweden Democrats, the anti-immigration party that has propped up Kristersson's minority government since 2022 under the so-called Tidö Agreement, fell to third place with 17.6 percent, down three points from 2022 and behind Kristersson's own Moderates.

Party leader Jimmie Åkesson conceded there was no clarity on government formation, telling reporters "we will not know tonight" who would be running the country. Kristersson, for his part, was careful not to concede anything: "When we have the final result, it's a good idea to look at possible combinations." That studied vagueness is itself a signal — a right bloc that has spent four years leaning on Sweden Democrat votes in parliament without ever bringing the party into cabinet may now have even less room to maneuver if its combined seat count falls short.

What a change of government would actually change

A confirmed Andersson-led government would end eight years of centre-right rule and reverse the Tidö-era policy architecture built around migration restriction, tax cuts and market-oriented welfare reform. The left bloc — Social Democrats, Left Party, Greens, and a Centre Party that has drifted back toward cooperating with Andersson — has campaigned on reinvesting in healthcare and welfare spending, funded in part by taxes the right had cut. Energy policy is a live fault line too: Kristersson's government backed a nuclear build-out that the Greens and parts of the left bloc are far cooler on, while immigration policy — tightened dramatically after 2015 and largely consensus-tightened since — would likely see its harshest edges softened rather than reversed outright, since neither bloc is proposing to loosen the border regime much.

One thing unlikely to move regardless of who governs: the krona. The Sweden Democrats have long resisted any drift toward euro adoption, and that position tracks public opinion — only 29 percent of Swedes support joining the currency, against 52 percent opposed. The krona ticked higher on the preliminary results Sunday night, in the kind of thin, reflexive move that traders make when uncertainty resolves even slightly in one direction, but analysts don't expect it to hold without a confirmed government behind it.

Why Nordic markets are watching the clock

Sweden is a small, open, export-heavy economy — a $700 billion-plus bellwether that investors use as a proxy for broader European risk appetite, much as they watch the Netherlands or Denmark. A three-seat margin sitting on top of a not-yet-final vote count is close to the least reassuring outcome markets can get: not a decisive mandate that lets a government move quickly on tax or energy policy, but a result narrow enough that a shift of a few hundred postal ballots in a handful of districts could flip which bloc actually commands parliament. Nordic equity desks and SEK options traders are pricing that ambiguity into short-dated volatility, and it will not fully unwind until Andersson or Kristersson can point to a certified majority — and, more importantly, to a coalition that has actually agreed on a program.

That second step could take considerably longer than the vote count. Sweden's fragmented multi-party system means even a confirmed 176-173 result guarantees a government only on paper; assembling one that can pass a budget is a separate negotiation, and past Swedish coalition talks have dragged for weeks. The next hard date is Thursday, when the Election Authority is expected to certify the final result. What happens in the weeks after that — who Andersson or Kristersson can actually bring into government — will matter more to Sweden's policy direction, and to the krona, than Thursday's number itself.
