ECB flags further tightening as euro slips near one-week low

The European Central Bank's headquarters in Frankfurt, where policymakers raised the deposit rate to 2.50% and signalled further tightening ahead. — Fred Romero / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
The euro fell to a one-week low of $1.1570 despite the ECB signalling more rate hikes, as a firm dollar ahead of Wednesday's Fed decision outweighed eurozone tightening.

The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate a quarter point to 2.50% last week and signalled it isn't done. Christine Lagarde's tone at the post-meeting press conference was more hawkish than markets had priced, and traders responded exactly as textbooks would predict for bond yields — a roughly 10 basis-point backup across core euro-area government curves — and exactly opposite to what the textbook says should happen to the currency.

The euro fell to $1.1570 on Monday, its weakest level in more than a week, even as money markets moved to price three additional ECB hikes by March and a fourth by next June. Tighter policy is supposed to make a currency more attractive by lifting the return on holding it. Instead, the euro has spent the days since the ECB's move drifting lower against a dollar that has its own reasons to be firm.

Inflation up, but for the wrong reasons

The ECB's hand was forced by numbers, not a change of heart. Euro-area headline inflation hit 3.3% in August, up from 2.9% in July and the highest reading since September 2024 — the sixth straight month above the bank's 2% target. The proximate cause is energy: the war in the Middle East has kept oil prices elevated, and the ECB itself flagged gas prices as a further upside risk should winter demand collide with low storage levels.

That matters for how markets are reading the hike. A central bank raising rates because demand is overheating is a vote of confidence in the economy underneath the currency. A central bank raising rates chiefly to offset an imported energy shock is doing something closer to damage control — tightening into a supply shock rather than a demand boom. Lagarde herself acknowledged the strain, saying longer-term inflation expectations remain anchored near 2% even as "inflation expectations over shorter horizons remain at elevated levels." The ECB is not pre-committing to a rate path, but it is clearly not finished either.

The dollar side of the ledger

The euro's slide isn't only a eurozone story. The Federal Reserve delivers its own decision on Wednesday, and futures markets have priced in roughly a 91% probability of a quarter-point US hike — a rare stretch in which both central banks are tightening at once, largely for the same reason: energy-driven inflation stemming from the same Middle East conflict. With the Fed decision this close and the outcome this likely, the dollar has picked up a bid on its own hawkish repricing, leaving the euro squeezed from both sides rather than benefiting from the ECB's guidance alone.

That symmetry undercuts any simple story about eurozone weakness driving the exchange rate. But it doesn't fully explain why the euro has underperformed the hike itself. If ECB tightening were the dominant force, EUR/USD would be expected to hold ground even against a firmer dollar; instead it has given up ground on both the ECB print and the days since. That points toward a market that is more skeptical of the eurozone growth outlook than the ECB's statement — which described risks to growth as "downside" alongside "upside risks to inflation" — fully spells out.

Why the feedback loop matters

A weaker euro is not a neutral side effect for a central bank still fighting above-target inflation. Roughly half of the eurozone's energy needs are imported, priced largely in dollars; a softer euro raises the local-currency cost of that oil and gas, adding to the same inflation pressure the ECB is trying to squeeze out. In principle, that argues for even more tightening to compensate — the kind of loop that has previously forced central banks into overshooting when currency and inflation dynamics reinforce rather than offset one another.

For now, the ECB is betting that anchored long-term inflation expectations mean it won't need to chase the currency lower with additional hikes beyond what markets have already priced. That bet gets its first real test at the Fed's Wednesday decision, which will settle whether the dollar's current strength is a temporary pre-announcement effect or a more durable divergence that keeps EUR/USD pinned below $1.16 into the ECB's next meeting.
