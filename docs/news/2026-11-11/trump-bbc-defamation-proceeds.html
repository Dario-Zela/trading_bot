Judge lets Trump's $10bn defamation case against the BBC proceed

In one sentence
A US federal judge has let Trump's $10bn defamation suit over a Panorama edit of his 6 January speech continue, a procedural step that exposes the BBC to US discovery and costs.

A US federal judge has declined to stop Donald Trump's $10bn defamation suit against the BBC in its early stages. The ruling is procedural, not a verdict. It settles nothing about whether the broadcaster defamed him. It does mean a sitting president's claim against a British public broadcaster, over how a documentary edited a speech, is moving through an American court towards a trial.

The edit at the centre

The case concerns Trump: A Second Chance?, a Panorama film broadcast days before the 2024 US presidential election. Trump's lawyers say it spliced three quotes from two parts of his 6 January 2021 speech, delivered almost an hour apart, so they appeared to be one continuous passage urging supporters to "fight like hell". A passage in which he called for peaceful demonstration was left out.

The row cost the BBC dearly. Its director general and its head of news both resigned. The corporation has apologised for the edit, but it rejects the claim that it defamed Trump.

The suit was filed in the Southern District of Florida and seeks $10bn in total: $5bn for defamation and $5bn under Florida's unfair trade practices law.

What the judge decided

The judge is Roy K. Altman. As reported in February, he refused to pause discovery, the pretrial stage in which each side must hand over documents. He called the BBC's request "premature" and kept a provisional trial date of February 2027.

The BBC's position is that the case should not be heard in Florida at all. It says the court lacks jurisdiction, in part because the programme was not broadcast in the state, and that Trump has not stated a valid claim. It intends to move to dismiss on both grounds. Before that motion is decided, it asked for discovery to be postponed, arguing that it would have to produce large volumes of emails and other material on how the programme was made. Altman's answer was that the request came too early. It was not a finding that the jurisdiction or the merits arguments are wrong. I could not confirm from the sources available the date or full terms of any later order, and the grounds on which a dismissal motion might be decided remain to be tested.

The case has moved on since. A magistrate judge, Enjoliqué Lett, has reportedly ruled on the scope of discovery. The BBC may examine how Trump's supporters understood his speech, but the trial will not re-examine the causes or legality of the January 6 riot. Reputational and economic damages are said to be live issues, and Trump's team says the order is narrower than reported and is still to be formalised. Those details come largely from US media accounts relying on the parties' own characterisations, and they may change on appeal to Altman.

Why it matters beyond the BBC

US defamation law is generally friendlier to publishers than English law. A public figure such as a president must show "actual malice": that the publisher knew the statement was false or recklessly disregarded the truth. A documentary edit is an unusual vehicle for such a claim, because the words were Trump's own and the dispute is over what the splice implied. The court's eventual treatment of that point will be watched by other broadcasters, many of whom compress long speeches into short clips every day.

The practical stakes arrive well before any verdict. If the case survives the BBC's dismissal motion, the corporation faces US discovery and legal costs, paid from a licence-fee-funded budget. A sitting president would also have shown that American courts can be a route to pressure foreign media. Press-freedom groups are likely to watch for any ruling suggesting that a foreign outlet can be hauled into a US court over a programme not shown there.

What to watch

The next marker is the ruling on the BBC's motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction and failure to state a claim. If it is denied, the February 2027 trial date comes into view, and so does the scope of discovery. If it is granted, the Florida case ends there, although Trump could appeal.
