Bank of England: warnings of 'higher for longer' after the hike to 4%

In one sentence
The BoE lifted Bank Rate to 4% with a 9-0 vote for a hike, projects CPI near 5% by mid-2027, and several members see more tightening, but the Bank has set no pre-set path.

The Bank of England raised Bank Rate to 4% on 5 November, its first increase since 2023, and the Monetary Policy Committee has since been read as signalling that borrowing costs will stay high. The 'higher for longer' label is a gloss on that decision, not the Bank's own phrase. The minutes support part of it and cut against part of it.

What the Bank did

Bank Rate went from 3.75% to 4%. All nine members voted to raise: eight for 0.25 percentage points and one, Catherine Mann, for 0.5 points, to 4.25%. Markets had expected a 7-2 split, so the vote was a little more hawkish than priced, though the move itself was anticipated.

The driver is energy. UK CPI inflation rose to 3.5% in September. The Bank projects it will reach around 4½% in the first quarter of 2027 and around 5% by the middle of next year, before falling back. It attributes the rise to higher global energy prices linked to the conflict in the Middle East. It also says it has seen limited evidence so far that these costs are spreading into wider prices and wages.

Where 'higher for longer' holds, and where it doesn't

The minutes do not commit the Bank to a path. The summary says: "Monetary policy is not on a pre-set path," and that the Committee "will adjust Bank Rate as necessary to ensure CPI inflation returns to the 2% target in the medium term."

Individual members differ on what comes next:

  • Megan Greene expects that "additional hikes will likely be required going forward," depending on pay, pricing and slack.
  • Clare Lombardelli sees a case for a further increase in the near term if inflation pressures do not ease.
  • Huw Pill expects more tightening is likely needed, including in the near term.
  • Andrew Bailey sees "the argument for a further increase" if news does not soften the inflation risks, and otherwise favours a conditional approach.
  • Alan Taylor is the counterweight. He calls the move a risk-management insurance hike and says it is not the start of a hiking cycle, with the next move possibly up, down or sideways.

The evidence therefore backs a narrower claim than the headline. Rates are higher, several members lean towards more, and the inflation projection peaks at around 5% only in mid-2027. A prolonged plateau is a reasonable inference from that, but it is an inference. The Bank has offered no forecast of how long 4% or higher will last. Mann's own projection, 4.9% by mid-2027, assumes a significant fall in energy prices, so the path depends heavily on a variable the Bank does not control.

Why gilts and mortgages are watching

The concern is mechanical. Mortgage pricing and gilt yields follow expected Bank Rate, and an inflation peak that arrives in 2027 pushes back the point at which cuts can resume. Given the timing of the projections, markets have little reason to price cuts before inflation turns. That is a reading of the numbers, not a statement from the Bank, and the Guardian Politics piece behind this item could not be checked here.

What to watch

The next decisive data are the monthly CPI releases, for evidence on whether energy costs are feeding into services prices and pay, which Greene and Lombardelli name as their tests. The next MPC decision, expected in December, will show whether the near-term hawks have gained ground or whether Taylor's single-hike reading prevails.
