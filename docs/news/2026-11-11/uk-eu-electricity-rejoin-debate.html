UK open to paying into EU electricity market as Scottish Labour leader backs rejoining

In one sentence
Britain's openness to discussing a payment for access to the EU electricity market is a soft signal, not an agreed deal, as Scottish Labour's leader backs rejoining the EU.

The British government has said it is willing to talk about paying into the European Union as the price of joining its electricity market. The remark falls well short of a decision. A minister has agreed to discuss the idea, and no sum, mechanism or timetable has been agreed. It does test a question the post-Brexit reset has so far avoided: whether it can move beyond trade rules into the pricing of energy, and what Britain would pay for that.

What was said

The Guardian reported on 10 November that Britain would discuss contributing towards infrastructure in poorer EU regions in return for access to the EU's internal electricity market. Hamish Falconer, the minister leading the EU talks, is reported to have said the UK has not accepted the principle of tying electricity trade to the EU's cohesion fund. He said the two sides would take the matter up after this month's summit.

That wording matters. Openness to a discussion is a softer position than acceptance of a payment, and ministers have so far presented the electricity track in terms of cost savings. They have argued that a deal would cut the cost of trading power across the Channel. The EU has been blunter. Diplomats have told Reuters that member states want a British contribution to cohesion funding in exchange for market access, and that the size of the payment is still being debated. Reported figures conflict: one outlet cites an entry fee of up to €6bn, another annual payments of about £1bn. Neither has been confirmed, and both should be read as negotiating-room estimates.

Five European leaders have said publicly that the UK would have to contribute to a cohesion fund to join the market. UK officials have rejected a formal link between electricity reforms and that fund.

Why electricity

The talks have a paper trail. The UK-EU summit of May 2025 committed both sides to explore closer energy cooperation, including British participation in EU electricity arrangements. The Commission later concluded exploratory discussions with London, and in March 2026 EU governments authorised the opening of formal talks. A UK-EU joint statement describes cooperation on British participation in the internal electricity market, with details to follow.

The economic logic is that Britain trades power with the continent through interconnectors but, outside the EU's market coupling system, does so less efficiently. Joining would tie UK wholesale prices more closely to continental ones. That could lower costs when the continent has surplus supply and import them when it does not. For the Treasury, a payment into EU funds would become a budget line, and a politically visible one. Brexit supporters campaigned on ending British contributions, so any payment, however it is labelled, invites comparison with the pre-2016 arrangements.

A louder argument in Labour

The story landed alongside a separate one. Michael Marra, who has led Scottish Labour since September, has called for the UK to rejoin the EU, describing it as a long-term objective. In an LBC article and in comments reported by STV, he argued it is in the interests of Scotland and the whole UK. He has said he does not expect it quickly, and he declined to say whether rejoining would require another referendum. He has also said he and the party would back including it in the next UK manifesto.

Relations with the EU are reserved to Westminster, so Marra's call changes the framing of the argument and nothing else. Scottish Liberal Democrat leader Alex Cole-Hamilton welcomed the move and said the UK Labour government's position was "as clear as mud". Prime Minister Andy Burnham is reported to want a much closer relationship with the EU and has raised the possibility of eventual membership. That makes the gap between Marra and the UK party narrower than it once was, though the government's current wording on the single market and customs union should be checked before it is treated as fixed.

The two stories fit together. A senior Labour figure calling for rejoining makes a payment for partial integration look moderate by comparison. Opponents will portray it as the first instalment of something larger.

What to watch

The next marker is the UK-EU summit this month, after which Falconer has said the cohesion question will be discussed. The questions to answer are whether the EU will accept a fee described as a payment for market access rather than a cohesion contribution, how large it would be, and whether Britain secures price-linked benefits in return. Until figures appear, the minister's remark is best read as a signal that the government will not walk away from the table over the principle of paying. It is not evidence that it has agreed to pay.
