Nvidia hires a major US law firm as the FBI probes Huang's alleged link to a suspected Chinese spy, and Reuters reports H200s reached a PLA-linked university

In one sentence
Nvidia's board is investigating CEO Jensen Huang over reported links to an alleged Chinese intelligence asset; an FBI probe and a PLA-linked H200 sale remain unconfirmed.

Nvidia's board has opened an inquiry into its own chief executive. The cause is reporting that Jensen Huang had an intimate relationship with a business executive whom US intelligence is said to have judged to be gathering information for Beijing. Nvidia makes the AI chips that Washington most wants to keep out of Chinese military hands. That makes a personal allegation a national-security question for the company.

The brief behind this piece adds two further claims: that President Trump has said the FBI is investigating, and that Nvidia has retained a major US law firm. Neither could be confirmed in the sources checked for this article. Nor could the Reuters report that Nvidia H200 chips reached a university linked to the People's Liberation Army. The claims come from BBC and Reuters reporting relayed through summaries. They are set out below as reported, not as established.

What is on the record

Nvidia's board announced the inquiry on Sunday, 8 November. Huang supports it and is cooperating, and he remains CEO while it runs. According to the New York Times, as relayed by Fox, Huang told the board he had been intimate with a business executive. The paper reported that US intelligence concluded she was passing information directly to Beijing.

The executive is Alice Huang, a top figure at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data-centre company. Reporting identifies her as a Chinese citizen. The coverage reviewed does not say whether she is related to Jensen Huang. Megaspeed has bought about $4.8bn of Nvidia chips over two years, which makes it one of Nvidia's largest customers. The New York Times has also reported that US officials are examining Megaspeed's China ties.

The board said it has "no reason to believe" that national security or Nvidia's secrets were compromised. It said the inquiry "will examine all available information" and that US national and economic security are its top priorities. In a message to staff, reported by the Times, Huang said he had been unfaithful to his wife and was "deeply sorry". China's foreign ministry called the allegations unsubstantiated and described them as smears.

The FBI and the law firm

The triage brief says Trump has publicly said the FBI is investigating, and that Nvidia has hired an outside US law firm. The BBC is cited as the source. Searches of the available coverage found no FBI statement, no White House transcript and no Nvidia statement naming either. The firm's name is not known. If Trump did say it, that is the step that matters. An FBI inquiry would sit alongside, and could override, the board's own review, and it would bring Nvidia's leadership into the export-control debate.

It also bears on how much weight a board review can carry. A company investigating its own chief executive is not independent. Outside counsel is the usual way boards try to supply independence, but that only works if the firm reports to directors and not to management.

Congress and the chips

The parallel pressure is already documented. Representatives Brian Mast, John Moolenaar and Rick Crawford have invited Huang to a joint briefing on 18 November, the day after Nvidia's quarterly results. They want answers on his contacts with Alice Huang and on how Nvidia stops foreign espionage. It is not known whether he will attend. Separately, in March, US lawmakers asked whether his remarks on chip smuggling had misled regulators.

The Reuters report belongs here. As relayed in the brief, it says a PLA-linked university bought H200 chips despite being on a US blacklist. The H200 is a restricted chip. I could not locate that specific story. Reuters did report in March that PLA-linked universities, including Beihang and Harbin Institute of Technology, bought Super Micro servers containing restricted Nvidia A100 chips. Those buyers are on the export blacklist. A purchase of H200s by such a buyer would be a more serious case, because it would involve a newer chip that Washington has handled more cautiously. Until the report is checked, it should be treated as unverified.

If it holds, the two stories connect. The first is about who has Huang's ear. The second is about where the chips end up. Together they pose one question: can the company that sells the world's most sought-after AI hardware show that it knows where it goes?

What to watch

Three dates and documents will settle most of this. Nvidia's results on 17 November may bring management comment on the inquiry. The congressional briefing on 18 November will show whether Huang appears. The third is an on-record statement from the FBI, the White House or Nvidia on the Trump claim and the law firm. Until one arrives, the confirmed position is an internal board inquiry, a congressional request and a company that says nothing was compromised.
