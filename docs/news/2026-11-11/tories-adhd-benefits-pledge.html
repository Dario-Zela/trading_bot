Tories pledge to cut benefits for children with 'milder' ADHD

In one sentence
The Conservatives would end DLA cash payments for children with milder ADHD, claiming £2.8bn a year in savings, while Labour calls the plan a 'charter for homelessness and human misery'.

The Conservatives have said they would stop paying cash disability support to children with what they call milder ADHD, replacing it with other forms of help. Labour has called the plan a "charter for homelessness and human misery". The proposal was announced on 10 November 2026 as part of a wider welfare package. It would link a child's access to cash support to how severe their condition is judged to be, not to the diagnosis alone.

What is proposed

The benefit at issue is Disability Living Allowance (DLA) for children. According to Big Issue's account of the announcement, the party would replace DLA cash payments for children with milder ADHD, behavioural disorders and learning difficulties with non-cash support. Only the 30% of children with the highest needs would keep the cash. Children with physical disabilities would be protected. Press reports of the announcement say the change would apply to new claims and reassessments.

The Conservatives put the saving at £2.8bn a year, with £1bn of that reinvested in mental health services and family hubs. The measure sits inside a larger plan. Overall, Kemi Badenoch has pledged to cut annual welfare spending by £36.1bn if she becomes prime minister. The figures below come from the party's own announcement as reported, and none has been independently costed.

Measure Claimed saving
Children's DLA changes £2.8bn a year
All disability-benefit changes (including DLA) £13.7bn
Reinstating the two-child limit £3.1bn a year

The numbers behind it

The policy responds to a steep rise in claims. Big Issue reports that the number of children on DLA rose from 521,476 in 2020 to around 914,000 in 2026. Rising claims do not by themselves show over-diagnosis, and the reports reviewed here do not say how "milder" would be defined or by whom. Part of the increase may reflect better recognition of conditions in children. That is a clinical judgement, and the coverage does not settle it.

Helen Whately, the shadow work and pensions secretary, defended the approach. As reported, she argued that the current system risks putting children on a "conveyor belt" towards a "lifetime on benefits". Badenoch argued that telling a young person their ADHD means they are defective could be more harmful than the condition itself.

Reaction

An unnamed Labour spokesperson described the plans as a "charter for homelessness and human misery". The spokesperson warned that they would push families into hardship and cost the state more in emergency support. The spokesperson also said further reforms would follow the Milburn and Timms review. The government has itself launched a review into whether some mental health and neurodivergent conditions are being over-diagnosed, so Labour's objection is to this proposal's scope and not to scrutiny of the system.

Sara Ogilvie, policy director at the Child Poverty Action Group, said the proposals would "take the UK back to the dark ages, causing the biggest overnight increase in child poverty since records began". That is an advocacy group's projection, and the reports do not set out the modelling behind it.

What to watch

The plan is an opposition pledge, not law, so nothing changes for families now. The questions that would decide its effect are practical ones. These are the clinical or administrative test for "milder", what the non-cash support would consist of, and whether schools and NHS services could deliver it. The Conservatives have not yet said, in the coverage reviewed, who would apply that test. The Milburn and Timms review is the next point at which Labour's own position on children's and mental-health-related benefits may become clearer.
