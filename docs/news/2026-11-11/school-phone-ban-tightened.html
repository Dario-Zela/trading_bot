Phones to be banned from pupils' bags and pockets in English schools

In one sentence
England is moving from voluntary school phone guidance towards a stricter rule covering pupils' bags and pockets, though whether it will be statutory is not yet confirmed.

England's schools are being pushed towards a stricter line on mobile phones. Under the plan reported by the BBC, pupils would no longer be allowed to carry phones in their bags or pockets during the school day, a step beyond the guidance that has applied since early 2024. The full wording, start date and enforcement arrangements were not available when this article was written, and the detail below is limited accordingly.

From guidance to rule

The current position dates from February 2024, when the Department for Education (DfE) issued guidance advising schools in England to prohibit phone use for the whole school day, breaks and lunch included. That guidance was not statutory. It told headteachers what the government expected, but it did not oblige them to do so, and it left the method to each school.

That latitude produced a patchwork. Some schools collect phones at the gate or lock them in pouches. Others ask pupils to keep devices out of sight, which in practice means in a bag or a pocket. The new approach appears aimed at that second category. A phone that is switched off but still on the pupil's person is, on this reading, still a phone in school.

The distinction matters because a rule about use is difficult to police. A teacher must catch the device in the act. A rule about possession is easier to apply: either the phone is in the bag or it is not.

What is not yet clear

The central question is whether the change will be statutory. If it is written into law or binding regulations, phone-free policy would become something schools must follow rather than something they are advised to follow, and it would be open to inspection and challenge. If it is strengthened guidance, it would be tougher in tone but would leave heads with the same discretion as before.

Several other points remain unconfirmed:

  • the date from which schools would be expected to comply;
  • how compliance would be checked, including any role for Ofsted, the schools inspectorate;
  • how exceptions would work for pupils who need a phone for medical reasons, such as monitoring diabetes, or for those with caring responsibilities;
  • what schools should do with phones that are handed in, and who is liable if one is lost or damaged.

The last two have been the practical sticking points wherever strict bans have been tried. Storage lockers and pouches cost money, and parents often object to being unable to reach a child in an emergency.

Why it matters beyond England

Phone restrictions in schools have spread quickly across countries in recent years, with national and regional governments tending to move from advice to compulsion once voluntary approaches prove uneven. England is following that pattern. A legally enforceable rule covering bags and pockets would be among the more demanding versions, and other education systems weighing their own policies may look to how it works in practice.

The economic stakes are small. This is a domestic education measure with little direct market relevance. The political stakes are larger, since the policy is popular with many parents and teachers but raises questions about enforcement that fall on school leaders.

What to watch

The test is in the text. The DfE's announcement, and any accompanying consultation or draft legislation, should show whether the rule is statutory, when it starts and who polices it. Reaction from head teachers' unions to the enforcement and cost questions will show how workable the plan is.
