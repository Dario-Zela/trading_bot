US soldier arrested in UK on suspicion of spying for Russia; three Iran-backed suspects accused of plots

In one sentence
A US soldier at a NATO base in Gloucestershire has been arrested on suspicion of Russia-linked espionage, weeks after two men were charged over an alleged Iran-directed plot at RAF Fairford.

Two counter-intelligence cases, one Russian and one Iranian, have put American personnel and British military sites on the same page this autumn. On Tuesday, counter-terrorism police arrested a 41-year-old US Army soldier near Gloucester on suspicion of offences related to Russia. Two weeks earlier, two men were charged over an alleged Iran-directed plot at RAF Fairford, the Gloucestershire base US bombers had been using. Neither case has come to trial, and nobody has been convicted of anything.

The headline's reference to "three suspects" in Iran-linked plots needs qualifying. The reporting reviewed for this article shows two men charged over Fairford. A third man, a 25-year-old dual British-Iranian national, was re-arrested on 20 October on suspicion of assisting Iran's intelligence service and released without charge. He remains on police bail. The charges against him are not established.

The soldier at Imjin Barracks

The Metropolitan Police said the man was arrested on Tuesday near Gloucester as part of a Counter Terrorism Policing investigation. He is held at a London police station. He has not been named, and his name is expected to emerge only if he is formally charged. Police searched two addresses in Gloucestershire. A source told ABC News he was stationed at Imjin Barracks, home of NATO's Allied Rapid Reaction Corps headquarters, where a small US intelligence contingent helps process some of the alliance's intelligence.

He was arrested on suspicion of offences under Sections 1 and 3 of the National Security Act 2023, the law that covers espionage and foreign interference. British authorities said: "The country to which the allegations relate is Russia." Police said the case is not linked to other Counter Terrorism Policing investigations.

The US side has been unusually open about its involvement. Brig. Gen. Sean Stinchon, who leads US Army Counterintelligence Command, called him a "senior soldier" and described the arrest as "a sobering reminder that insider threats are real and persistent." He thanked UK and FBI colleagues for their teamwork. FBI Director Kash Patel said the case "involves allegations of espionage, which is of the utmost concern to the United States and our allies." A source told ABC that he may also face action from the US Army. The specific charges remain unclear.

Fairford: from vans to charges

The Iranian case has a longer paper trail. In the early hours of 27 September, armed police and army bomb-disposal teams converged on a rural area near RAF Fairford after reports of suspicious vans heading towards the base. Five British men were arrested outside it. US bombers, which had used Fairford for strikes on Iran, were withdrawn on security grounds shortly afterwards.

On 27 October, prosecutors charged two of those held, both 24, under the National Security Act:

  • Mohmed Abdulkarim, a British national, is charged with conduct likely to materially assist a foreign intelligence service between 31 August and 28 September. Police say the country is believed to be Iran.
  • Nouman Essghir, a dual British-Iranian national, is charged with entering a prohibited place for a purpose prejudicial to the UK on 22 August.

Six other people remain on police bail. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said the activity was likely coordinated in Iran, and the government's position has been that the Revolutionary Guard was almost certainly behind it. Iran's ambassador has denied involvement. Assistant Commissioner Laurence Taylor, head of National Counter Terrorism Policing, said: "We have seen a range of incidents in recent months that we believe have been directed by the Iranian state."

A pattern, or two files on one desk?

The two cases differ in almost every operational respect. One involves an alleged insider with access to alliance intelligence, apparently working for Moscow. The other involves outsiders, mostly young British men, accused of reconnaissance and possible hostile action against a base for Tehran. The Met has said the soldier's case is unconnected to its other investigations, and nothing published ties the two together.

What they share is a target set. Both involve American military activity on British soil, one at a NATO headquarters and one at an airfield used in an active conflict. Both are being handled under the National Security Act 2023, which gives police and prosecutors broader tools than the old Official Secrets regime. The US Army's public thanks to MI5, Defence Counter Intelligence and the FBI suggest that allied cooperation, rather than a single agency, produced the arrest.

That is an inference from the pattern of official statements, not a finding. Hostile services have long taken an interest in US bases in Britain. What is new is how many such cases are reaching the public in a short space of time, and that charges rather than leaks now define them.

What to watch

The first marker is whether the soldier is charged and named, which should show which sections of the Act are in play and whether the US pursues its own case. For Fairford, the next steps are the committal of Abdulkarim and Essghir and a decision on whether the third man and the six on bail are charged. A charging decision in either case will test whether the Russian and Iranian threads stay separate or start to overlap.
