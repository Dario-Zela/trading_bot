Labour MPs fear Burnham's 10-year plan is not radical enough; top civil servant calls Whitehall 'tame'

In one sentence
Labour backbenchers reportedly fear Burnham's 10-year plan is not radical enough while the cabinet secretary calls Whitehall 'tame', though both reports are unconfirmed beyond headlines.

Andy Burnham's government has promised a 10-year plan to remake how Britain is run. Two reports now suggest it faces doubt from two directions at once. Labour backbenchers fear it will not be radical enough, and the country's most senior civil servant has reportedly said the machinery meant to deliver it is too timid.

Both accounts come from Guardian Politics headlines. The full texts were not available when this piece was written, so the detail below rests on the headlines and on earlier reporting of the plan. No MP is named here, and nothing is quoted beyond the headline wording.

The backbench worry

The headline says Labour MPs fear the plan will not go far enough. That fits a pattern that predates this report. The New Statesman has reported that Burnham faces the same soft-left pressure that dogged his predecessor, from MPs who hoped a new prime minister would be as radical in office as he once promised to be. Separately, about 80 Labour MPs have urged him to "turn rhetoric into reality" and keep the promises he made on the road to No 10, according to one account.

The worry has also been fed by what is known of the plan itself. One report said it was expected to be a speech plus a slimmed-down document, after suggestions that it might be less ambitious than first thought. Burnham has acknowledged that publication may slip from December into the new year. The New Statesman reported that some inside government fear the exercise could become another case of "kicking the can".

The headline gives no vote, letter or on-record quote. Unease of this kind is common in any governing party. It matters here because the plan is the prime minister's main statement of what the government is for, and a wide gap between that statement and his own backbenchers' hopes would narrow his room for manoeuvre.

'Tame' Whitehall

The second thread is the civil service. The Guardian headline has the cabinet secretary, the head of the civil service, calling Whitehall too "tame, reactive and risk-averse". The wording should be checked against the full article. The speaker's position is the point: this is an insider's verdict, not an opposition attack.

It also echoes the prime minister's own framing. Burnham has cast the state as part of the problem. He has promised the "biggest rebalancing of power our country has seen" and has said Whitehall must accept that growth cannot be ordered from the top down. He has also said, in the Civil Service World report, that departments will be compelled to help local authorities with staff and resources. Departments that are cautious by habit would make that harder to deliver.

Two doubts, one problem

The two criticisms sound different but point the same way. Backbenchers doubt the ambition of the plan. The head of the civil service doubts the capacity of the institution that must carry it out. Between them they leave the government short of both a bold enough design and a bold enough delivery system.

Outside Labour the attack is simpler. Reform UK has dismissed the plan as "a load of waffle" and argued that Britain cannot wait 10 years. A long horizon gives a government cover to be patient, but it also invites the charge that nothing will change soon.

What to watch

The first marker is the publication date, and whether the plan arrives before Christmas or slips into the new year. The second is its content: whether it contains dated, costed commitments that satisfy the 80-odd MPs pressing for action, or a statement of direction that leaves them unconvinced. The third is whether the cabinet secretary's remarks are followed by changes to how departments are run, or stay a candid aside. Until the Guardian text and named sources are confirmed, how far this unease reaches inside the party remains unproven.
