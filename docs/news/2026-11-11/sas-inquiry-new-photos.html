New photographs are 'essential evidence' of possible SAS war crimes, inquiry told

In one sentence
The SAS inquiry released photos of 17 people killed in two 2011 Helmand raids, which counsel says may show people shot while asleep; they were never sent for forensic testing.

The statutory inquiry into alleged unlawful killings by British special forces in Afghanistan has published, for the first time, photographs taken after two SAS night raids in Helmand province in 2011. Its lead barrister told the hearing they may be evidence of possible war crimes. The photographs move the inquiry from competing accounts of what happened inside Afghan homes towards physical evidence that can be tested.

What the photographs show

The images were released on Tuesday. According to the BBC's report, they show multiple people lying in beds with head wounds across the two operations. One person lies under a floral blanket. Others have their heads on pillows, with blood pooling around them. Blood spatter low on one wall is described as consistent with someone being shot while lying down.

Oliver Glasgow KC, counsel to the inquiry, said many of the dead "look like they sustained head shots" and that one body appears to have its head on a pillow. Further analysis, he said, might confirm whether people were "shot when asleep." The inquiry is putting the photographs through expert forensic analysis. The BBC report does not name the experts.

Seventeen people were killed across the two operations. The youngest victims were 15 and 14 years old, one in each raid. The SAS was working alongside Afghan special forces on night raids against the Taliban.

Two accounts of the same raids

The official special forces account is that insurgents fired on troops as they entered the compounds, and that the SAS returned fire. The photographs sit uneasily with that.

The BBC's Panorama programme examined four raids on homes that killed 26 people. Weapons experts consulted for that investigation said the evidence suggested multiple rounds were fired from above, which would indicate the victims were lying down or sitting against a wall rather than caught in a firefight. Relatives told the programme their family members had been shot cleanly in the head or chest. Panorama's 2022 investigation led to the inquiry being set up that year. It examines allegations that the SAS murdered unarmed civilians and detainees on night raids between 2010 and 2013, and planted weapons to cover the killings up. Counsel's remarks are allegations and questions for expert analysis. They are not findings.

The question of why they were not tested

The inquiry also heard that these images are not entirely new to the state. Lt Col John Harvey, who led the military police murder investigation, agreed the photographs "didn't look right" and said his team regarded them as potentially vital evidence. Investigators repeatedly asked for them to be submitted for further testing. That never happened.

An anonymous investigator told the BBC that low bullet marks on the walls seemed to contradict the special forces' version of events. The inquiry also heard that investigators were still in Afghanistan in July 2019, looking for eyewitnesses, when senior officers were preparing to close the murder investigation and inform ministers. A member of the Afghan partner unit told military police he had personally seen a British soldier plant a rifle on an unarmed person after they had been killed.

The Ministry of Defence has said two independent investigations have examined the conduct of British forces in Afghanistan and that neither found sufficient evidence to prosecute. The inquiry's task is partly to test that conclusion, and the untested photographs bear directly on it. The sequence the hearing described is that investigators saw the images as important, asked for expert work, and did not get it.

What comes next

The inquiry is already under strain from its volume of material. In September the Ministry of Defence disclosed about 97 million further documents, which the chair, Lord Justice Haddon-Cave, has treated as a cause of further delay and cost. He has also said the inquiry holds some 10,000 photographs from the raids that appear not to have formed part of the military police investigation. The forensic results on the 2011 images will show whether the sleeping-victim hypothesis survives expert scrutiny. If it does, the question of why the evidence went untested will move to the centre of the inquiry's examination of the investigation itself.
