Bowman expects GSIB surcharge and Basel III rules within weeks

In one sentence
The Fed's Michelle Bowman reportedly expects final GSIB surcharge and Basel III rules within weeks, but the impact on UK banks' US units is unquantified.

The Federal Reserve's long-running rewrite of bank capital rules is close to the finish line, according to its top bank supervisor. Michelle Bowman, the Fed's Vice Chair for Supervision, expects the surcharge on the largest US banks and the US version of the Basel III capital standards to be finalised "within weeks", Benzinga reported. That is a forward-looking timetable from a single source, not a published rule, and no final text has yet appeared.

What is being finalised

Two rules are in play. The first is the GSIB surcharge, an extra layer of capital charged to the largest US-based banks deemed globally systemically important. The second is the final phase of Basel III, often called the "endgame", which resets how banks calculate risk-weighted assets and so how much capital they must hold against them.

Both were proposed in March. In a speech on 12 March, Bowman said the Basel proposal would modestly raise requirements for the largest banks, while changes to the surcharge would modestly lower them. She described the package as a recalibration of post-crisis rules. Reporting on the proposals put the combined effect on common equity requirements for the biggest firms at a decline of roughly 5%, once October 2025 changes to the stress capital buffer are counted. One proposed change to the surcharge would calculate it in steps of 10 basis points rather than 50.

The word "weeks" has history here. In March, Bowman said the Fed would "propose rules" in the "coming weeks", and the agencies duly published proposals on 19 March. The November claim is about final rules, a later and more consequential stage. It rests on a secondary report, and a Fed or Federal Register notice would settle it.

The foreign-bank question

The angle for British readers is the US operations of UK lenders such as Barclays and HSBC. The surcharge itself applies to US-headquartered institutions, so it bears on foreign banks only indirectly, through competition with the American giants. Basel III is the more direct channel: foreign banks above certain size thresholds hold their US assets in a US intermediate holding company, which faces its own capital rules.

Whether those holding companies fall under the stricter categories of the new regime, and by how much their capital requirements would move, is not stated in the source and could not be confirmed from available material. Neither bank's exposure has been quantified. A lighter overall package would, on the March proposals' direction, mean less additional capital rather than more, but the final calibration may differ from the proposal.

What to watch

The test is the final text. Comment letters on the March proposals included requests from industry groups to remove what they called "gold-plating" in the Basel rules and to extend the comment period, so the final calibration could shift. The next markers are a Fed board meeting notice or a Federal Register publication, and then the phase-in dates, which determine when any extra capital at foreign lenders' US units would bite.
