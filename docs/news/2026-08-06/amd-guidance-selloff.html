AMD beats on revenue and profit but guidance jitters wipe 9% off shares

AMD chief executive Lisa Su, who told investors Helios AI-server demand is "tracking ahead" of forecasts even as margins held flat. — Gene Wang / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
AMD beat Q2 revenue and EPS estimates and raised Q3 guidance, but flat margins and tripled capex sent shares down 8.8% anyway.

AMD walked into its second-quarter earnings report with almost every number moving in the right direction — and walked out having lost roughly $35 billion of market value in a single after-hours session. The chipmaker beat Wall Street's revenue and profit targets by a comfortable margin. Investors sold the stock anyway, sending shares down nearly 9% as the numbers behind the beat — thinning cash flow, flat margins, capital spending that nearly tripled — took over the conversation.

Shares had closed at $518.58 on August 4, the day of the report, capping a run that had left the stock up more than 130% for the year and trading at over 40 times forward earnings. By the next session AMD was changing hands near $472.94. For a stock priced for near-perfection, a good quarter wasn't the same thing as a perfect one.

The beat, in numbers

AMD reported revenue of $11.54 billion for the quarter, up 50% year-on-year and above the $11.28 billion analysts had modelled. Adjusted earnings came in at $1.66 a share against a consensus of $1.62. The headline driver was data centre: revenue there more than doubled to a record $6.72 billion, comfortably ahead of the roughly $6.5 billion Wall Street expected, and now accounts for 58 cents of every dollar AMD brings in. Data-centre operating income reached $2.1 billion.

The rest of the portfolio was more mixed. Client revenue — the Ryzen processors that go into PCs — rose 23% to $3.1 billion on what chief executive Lisa Su described as resilient demand, even as elevated prices have, in her words, "weighed on" the broader consumer market. Gaming revenue fell 31% to $779 million as semi-custom console chip sales cooled. Embedded, the smallest segment, grew 19% to $977 million. None of that would normally trouble a stock built on the AI data-centre story — and it didn't. What troubled investors was what showed up further down the income statement.

Where the beat came undone

Adjusted gross margin held at 56%, roughly two points better than a year earlier but flat against the prior quarter — and AMD guided to the same 56% for the third quarter, offering no line of sight to the margin expansion that bulls had priced in as the AI ramp matured. Capital expenditure told a starker story: $808 million in the quarter, nearly triple what AMD spent a year earlier and roughly double the first quarter's outlay, largely tied to standing up production for its Helios AI server racks. Free cash flow came in at $1.6 billion, a respectable 14% of revenue, but that was down 39% from the prior quarter as the capex bill landed.

The guidance itself was, on its face, strong: AMD pointed to roughly $13 billion in third-quarter revenue, plus or minus $300 million, which sits above the roughly $12.5 billion analysts had modelled and would mark 41% year-on-year growth. Chief financial officer Jean Hu told investors the company expects "data centre sales to accelerate in the second half of 2026, driving stronger overall revenue growth and continued earnings expansion." Su said customer pull for Helios was "very strong and tracking ahead of our initial forecasts," with shipments starting this quarter and ramping "much more substantially" in the fourth, and pointed to Anthropic's commitment to deploy up to two gigawatts of AMD's MI450-series chips as validation of the roadmap. AMD claims the MI450 platform delivers 15% higher throughput at equivalent rack power and 30% better tokens-per-dollar economics than rival hardware — a direct shot at Nvidia's dominant position.

None of that was enough. A raised outlook that merely matches an AI-fevered market's baseline expectations, paired with margins that refuse to move and a cash-flow line under visible strain from the infrastructure build-out, was read as evidence that the AI ramp costs more than it currently pays back.

A market recalibrating what a beat is worth

The reaction says as much about where semiconductor investors have positioned themselves as it does about AMD. A quarter that would have been celebrated eighteen months ago — record data-centre revenue, a raised forward guide, a name-brand AI customer signing on for gigawatts of compute — instead triggered an 8.8% repricing, because the bar has shifted from "is the AI story real" to "is the AI story self-funding." AMD's move landed the same week the Dow notched a fifth straight record close even as chip weakness dragged on the broader tape, underscoring that the scrutiny is concentrated on the AI infrastructure names specifically, not on equities generally. It also arrived days after SpaceX disclosed an exclusive Nvidia GPU commitment for its own compute build-out, a reminder that AMD is fighting for AI infrastructure share against a competitor that still sets the pricing and margin benchmark the market measures everyone else against.

The next test lands in AMD's third-quarter report, due in early November, when the market will be watching two things specifically: whether the roughly $13 billion revenue guide actually gets hit, and whether gross margin finally moves off 56% as Helios shipments — due to ramp "substantially" in the fourth quarter, by Su's own account — start converting from capital outlay into recognised revenue. Until margins move, a beat-and-raise quarter may keep buying AMD scrutiny rather than a rally.
