SpaceX's debut earnings: revenue up 92%, a capex shock, and an exclusive Nvidia bet that cuts out AMD

A SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket delivers a Starlink payload to orbit. — Johnnyboy1890 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
SpaceX beat on revenue with $7.8bn, up 92%, but an $18.4bn AI capex bill and an exclusive Nvidia chip pledge that cut out AMD sent shares down over 8%.

SpaceX has spent 24 years turning aerospace skeptics into believers by doing the physically improbable: landing boosters standing up, launching more mass to orbit in a year than the rest of the planet combined. On Tuesday, in its first earnings report since June's record-breaking IPO, the company delivered a number just as improbable for a business built on rockets and satellites — 92% revenue growth — and Wall Street still knocked more than a tenth off the share price.

The reason sits in a single line item. SpaceX spent $18.4bn on capital expenditure in the second quarter, nearly $16bn of it on AI computing infrastructure, according to the earnings release. Analysts had penciled in less than half that. Shares, which had briefly traded up more than 9% on the revenue beat, reversed hard once the capex figure and the earnings call sank in, falling as much as 12.9% before closing down roughly 8% after hours.

The numbers that split the room

SpaceX's headline figures were, on their own terms, hard to argue with. Revenue came in at $7.81bn against a Wall Street consensus near $6.9bn, according to FactSet estimates cited by CNBC. Starlink remains the anchor of the business: connectivity revenue rose 66% to $4.3bn as subscribers doubled to 12 million, and it is the only segment currently turning a profit, with $1.7bn in operating income. The newer AI unit — which bundles cloud compute, xAI and the X platform — grew revenue 247% to $2.56bn. The net loss narrowed to $541m from $1bn a year earlier, and adjusted EBITDA rose 191% to $3.5bn.

None of that stopped the sell-off. Capex jumped from $10.1bn in the first quarter to $18.4bn in the second, and the AI compute slice alone more than doubled, from $7.7bn to $15.8bn. CFO Bret Johnsen tried to reframe the number as discipline rather than excess, telling analysts the company is getting "less than a one-year payback" on AI compute capital and is on pace for $100bn in annualised recurring revenue by year-end. Elon Musk, never one to undersell, told the call SpaceX would now reach $1trn in annual revenue by 2030 — a year earlier than his previous forecast — and likened the company's advantage in data centres to "the New York Yankees going in and playing a Little League team."

Investors were less swayed by the metaphor than by the mismatch between spending and proof of return. An S&P Global analyst summed up the market's read plainly: the stock fell because AI capex "was more than double what was expected." Sell-side reaction has since split accordingly — Bank of America reiterated a Buy with a $235 target, while at least one bearish analyst has set a target near $62, implying the stock is worth roughly half where it now trades. The average target across 31 analysts sits at $233, per compiled estimates, well above the post-earnings price — a spread wide enough to signal that nobody has a confident model yet for what SpaceX's orbital AI bet is actually worth.

Nvidia's moat, AMD's exit

The capex story has a specific address: an orbital computing project called Starmind. On the same call, Musk said SpaceX would build its AI infrastructure "exclusively" on Nvidia hardware, formalising a partnership under which Nvidia is co-developing the compute payload for the Starmind AI1 satellite. Each unit is designed to carry the equivalent of a full Vera Rubin NVL72 rack — 36 Vera CPUs and 72 Rubin GPUs — into low Earth orbit, with the first satellite slated for launch next year and Musk describing an eventual constellation that could run into the hundreds of thousands of satellites, linked by lasers into a distributed supercomputer in space.

The exclusivity is the news. As recently as May, Musk had indicated SpaceX and Tesla would keep buying chips from both Nvidia and AMD. That optionality is now gone, at least for the orbital AI programme, and the market repriced both stocks within the hour: Nvidia rose roughly 4% to $221.33, while AMD — which had just reported the best quarterly results in its 56-year history, with data-centre revenue more than doubling — fell as much as 9%. Bank of America told clients to "ignore the noise," arguing Starmind's near-term chip volumes are too small to matter to AMD's earnings. The market, at least on the day, disagreed: a single customer's loyalty pledge outweighed a record quarter, underscoring how much of the AI hardware trade now runs on who a marquee buyer chooses rather than on backlogs already booked.

Retail holds its nerve

The most counterintuitive thread in the story is what SpaceX's newly public shareholders did next. SpaceX allocated up to 30% of its June IPO — priced at $135 and the largest in US history — to retail investors through Schwab, Fidelity, Robinhood, SoFi and E*Trade, far above the low-single-digit share retail typically gets. With the stock down sharply after earnings, and a lock-up expiration on Wednesday freeing the first 20% of roughly 911.5 million insider-held shares, the conditions were in place for retail to head for the exits.

They did the opposite. Vanda Research data show retail investors bought a net $22.7m of SpaceX shares in the first hour of trading after the post-earnings drop — the third-highest first-hour total of the 37 sessions since the IPO — and the firm noted SpaceX has not recorded a single day of net retail selling since it began trading on 12 June. "This tells us that retail dip-buying has not stepped back after the earnings sell-off — they've become even more opportunistic," Vanda wrote. That conviction is now about to be tested from a different angle: with insiders newly free to sell into a stock already down roughly 43% from its post-IPO peak, the float is expanding just as the buyer base proves it is willing to absorb the supply.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the quarter reads less like a single verdict on SpaceX than three separate bets colliding at once — Starlink's cash generation funding an AI compute build-out large enough to make a chipmaker's earnings call collateral damage, while retail investors, freshly liquid and undeterred by an 8% air-pocket, keep buying into a stock whose Wall Street price targets now span from $62 to $235. The next test arrives quickly: how the market absorbs the lock-up's insider supply through the rest of August, and whether Johnsen's promised sub-one-year payback on AI capex shows up in the numbers by the time SpaceX reports its third quarter.
