HSBC profit jumps 23% but a modest $1bn buyback leaves investors cold

HSBC's global headquarters at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London. — Gordon Joly / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
HSBC's pretax profit jumped 23% to $19.5bn, but a $1bn buyback — half what analysts expected — left the shares essentially flat.

HSBC made more money in the first half of this year than analysts expected, by a healthy margin. The market shrugged.

Pretax profit for the six months to June rose 23% to $19.5bn, up from $15.8bn a year earlier and comfortably ahead of the $18.9bn analysts had pencilled in. Revenue climbed 11% to $37.7bn. Net interest income was up 8% to $18.2bn, fee income nearly 10% to $7.3bn, and the wealth management division — the bank's preferred growth story since it began retreating from lower-return corners of its global network — grew 20% to $5.5bn. On its own, that is about as clean a beat as a bank can post.

The reaction was a shrug because of what came with it: a share buyback of up to $1bn, the bank's first since it paused repurchases last October to fund taking Hong Kong's Hang Seng Bank private. Investors had budgeted for something closer to $2bn — Jefferies and UBS had both pencilled in that figure, and Citi's consensus estimate ran to roughly $2.2bn. Getting half of that, even alongside a profit beat, read to the market less as generosity than as restraint.

A buyback that undershot

HSBC's Hong Kong-listed shares fell as much as 2.5% to HK$164 immediately after the announcement, before paring the loss to around 1%. The stock is still up 36% this year, and the drop looks minor against that run — but the direction, on a day the bank beat forecasts on every headline number, told its own story. In New York, HSBC's shares were essentially flat, slipping a few cents to $107.46 near a 52-week high, which is itself a kind of verdict: a stock already priced for good news doesn't move much further on news that turns out to be merely good.

Jefferies analyst Joseph Dickerson called the payout "a modestly lower buyback than we expected" that "may underwhelm." Citi went further, asking whether HSBC is settling into a pattern of smaller, more frequent buybacks rather than resuming the roughly $2bn-a-quarter pace it ran before the Hang Seng deal — a question the bank's chief financial officer, Pam Kaur, addressed only obliquely. She said loan growth this quarter ran at twice its usual rate, eating into the capital that would otherwise have gone to shareholders. HSBC also lifted its second interim dividend to $0.10 a share and raised its 2026 cost-cutting target to $2bn from $1.5bn, trimming headcount by 2,559 to 206,161 — both moves framed internally as discipline, neither of which moved the share price much either.

The read for the sector

The episode is a reminder of how far the bar has shifted for what counts as a win in bank earnings season. Profit growth, margin expansion, a dividend increase — HSBC delivered all three and still finished the day roughly where it started. What investors wanted was evidence that capital freed up by strong lending and wealth income would come back to them faster than it is, and $1bn against a $2bn expectation read as a bank hedging its bets rather than committing to them.

The next test comes at HSBC's third-quarter results, when the current buyback is due to complete and the bank will have to signal whether Tuesday's number was a one-off dented by loan demand or the new normal for capital returns.
