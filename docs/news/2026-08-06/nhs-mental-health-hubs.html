NHS to open 159 walk-in mental health hubs in banks and libraries

A high-street bank branch of the kind slated for conversion into a walk-in mental health hub under the £343m programme. — Roy Hughes / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
England will convert 159 sites — including banks and libraries — into walk-in mental health centres, backed by £343m in the biggest such redesign in a generation.

The NHS is about to try something it has never done at this scale: putting mental health care where the foot traffic already is. From this autumn, walk-in centres will start opening inside libraries, high-street bank branches and other everyday buildings across England, part of a £343m package the government is calling the biggest redesign of mental health services in a generation.

The numbers break down into two types of site. There will be 100 new community mental health centres, offering same-day, no-referral support for people who might otherwise wait months to be seen — 2.36 million people in England currently have an open mental health referral, up from 2.10 million a year ago, according to NHS figures cited alongside the announcement. Alongside those, 59 new mental health emergency departments will handle crisis cases who are medically fit but need specialist psychiatric care rather than a general A&E bed, taking the national total of such units from 23 to 82. Of the 159 sites combined, 57 are newbuilds; the rest — 102 of them — are conversions of existing premises, which is where the banks and libraries come in.

Borrowing the high street's foot traffic

The logic is straightforward enough: banks and libraries are, for now, still where people go. Branch networks have shrunk for a decade, but the buildings that remain sit on the streets people already walk down for other reasons, without the clinical signage that can put someone off crossing the threshold of a hospital. Prime Minister Andy Burnham framed it as removing the barrier of asking for help at all: "Nobody should be left to struggle with their mental health alone. But every day, thousands of people are waiting months for help." Health Secretary Yvette Cooper's line was blunter about the current failure mode — that too many people "only get support when reaching crisis point, after struggling far too long."

Charities gave it a cautious welcome rather than an unqualified one. Rethink Mental Illness called it "a vital step"; Mind's Tom Pollard described it as "a promising step," the kind of phrasing that acknowledges scale without pretending the capacity problem is solved. The first sites open this autumn, with further facilities following from March 2027 — a rollout the government has folded into a wider Mental Health Strategy due alongside an independent review of mental health, ADHD and autism services later this year.

What it tests

For a market watching healthcare and fintech from the sidelines, the interesting bit isn't the clinical model — it's the property one. Co-locating public health services inside commercial retail space is a template the private sector has flirted with (in-store pharmacy clinics, supermarket GP surgeries) without ever proving it scales. If 102 conversions land on schedule and stick, it becomes a reference case for anyone thinking about health-adjacent uses for shrinking bank-branch footprints. If they don't, the next data point to watch is simply whether the autumn openings happen on time.
