EU's AI Act transparency and deepfake-labelling rules become enforceable

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which enforces the AI Act — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
EU AI Act transparency rules took effect August 2, forcing AI disclosure and deepfake labelling with fines up to €15m or 3% of global turnover — UK firms included.

Starting this month, if a company operating in the EU wants to use a chatbot, generate a synthetic image, or clone a voice, it has to say so — or risk a fine that scales with its global revenue, not just its EU business. The transparency obligations in the EU's AI Act became legally enforceable on 2 August, marking the moment Europe's flagship AI law stopped being a document firms were merely preparing for and became one they can be fined for breaking.

The headline number is €15 million, or 3% of a company's worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. For a large platform or bank, that percentage figure matters more than the flat one — 3% of global revenue dwarfs €15 million once a firm passes roughly €500 million in turnover. EU institutions that fall foul of the same rules face a comparatively modest cap of €750,000.

What Article 50 actually requires

The obligations sit in Article 50 of the AI Act, and they split into four distinct duties. Providers of interactive AI systems — chatbots, voice assistants — must design them so a person knows they're talking to a machine, unless that's already obvious from context. Providers of tools that generate synthetic audio, image, video or text must mark the output in a machine-readable format, so it can be automatically detected as AI-made. Deployers using emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems must tell the people exposed to them. And deployers who publish deepfakes, or AI-generated text on matters of public interest, have to label the content as artificially generated or manipulated.

The European Commission has been explicit about what counts: a fully AI-generated video of a politician saying something they never said, AI-composed music passed off as human work, an AI-written news summary presented without disclosure. The Commission adopted formal guidelines on the obligations on 20 July, giving companies just under two weeks' notice before the rules took legal effect — tight, even by Brussels standards.

There is one carve-out. The machine-readable marking duty for generative tools already on the market before 2 August gets a grace period, running until 2 December 2026. The disclosure duties for chatbots, deepfakes and biometric systems, by contrast, apply immediately, with no transition window.

The rules don't stop at the Channel

What makes this consequential well beyond the EU's own borders is reach: the obligations apply to any firm serving EU customers, regardless of where it is incorporated. A London-based company running a customer-service chatbot for EU users, or including synthetic content in an EU marketing campaign, is caught in exactly the same way a Berlin-based one is. Brexit did not create an exemption. More than 180 organisations — spanning tech platforms, media companies and AI developers — have signed the Commission's voluntary Code of Practice on AI transparency, though the underlying legal obligations bind everyone whether they sign or not.

Why trading platforms and bot operators are watching closely

The transparency duties land in a financial-services sector that is already navigating a parallel, tougher track under the AI Act: systems used for credit scoring, fraud detection and automated decisions that affect access to financial services are classified as "high-risk," which brings its own obligations around risk management, human oversight, documentation and continuous monitoring — separate from, and in addition to, the disclosure rules that took effect this month. Trading algorithms that touch EU customers or EU market data fall into this same overlapping territory, sitting alongside existing MiFID II obligations rather than replacing them.

For a bot operator or algorithmic trading desk, the practical upshot is less about slapping an "AI-generated" label on a chart and more about the compliance posture underneath: can the firm show, on demand, when and how an AI system is influencing a decision that reaches a client? Regulators now have a concrete legal hook — and a nine-figure penalty scale — to ask that question formally rather than rhetorically.

What's next

The next hard deadline is 2 December 2026, when the grace period for machine-readable content marking expires and every generative tool already on the market has to comply, not just new ones. Before then, expect the AI Office and national regulators to test the disclosure rules with early enforcement cases — likely against chatbot deployments or deepfake content rather than the more technically demanding marking requirement. How aggressively Brussels moves in those first cases will tell firms, UK ones included, how much runway they actually have.
