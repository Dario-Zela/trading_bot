Hamas agrees to disarmament roadmap, but Israel stays silent as Gaza ceasefire wavers

Destruction in Rafah, Gaza Strip, after Israeli forces withdrew and the ceasefire took hold. — Ashraf Amra / UNRWA, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0 IGO)
In one sentence
Hamas accepted a US-brokered disarmament roadmap, but Netanyahu says Israel never agreed to it, and strikes have continued regardless.

The choreography looked familiar. A US-brokered framework, a triumphant announcement from the White House, a militant group's grudging assent, and then — silence from the one government whose signature actually matters. Nine days after President Trump declared a "historic agreement" on Hamas's disarmament, Israel still has not said yes. Strikes have continued. And the roadmap that was supposed to open the ceasefire's next chapter is instead exposing how far apart the two sides remain.

A roadmap with one signature missing

On July 30, Trump announced what he called a breakthrough: a 15-point roadmap, drafted through the Board of Peace and mediated by Egypt, Qatar, Turkey and Washington, laying out how Hamas would decommission its heavy weapons, production sites and tunnel network in staged phases, handing them to Palestinian technocratic control, while Israeli forces pulled back in parallel stages and an international stabilisation force partnered with a new Palestinian police contingent. Senior Hamas official Basem Naim posted on X that the group had "agreed to the roadmap." It was the furthest any Gaza-related disarmament proposal had travelled since the ceasefire took hold in October 2025.

What followed was not endorsement but escalating public irritation from Jerusalem. By August 4, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu had gone further than his office's earlier expressions of "serious concerns," stating flatly that Israel had not agreed to the American draft at all. "The White House sent us a draft," he said. "We did not agree to it. It is not our draft. We sent our comments." In a video posted to Facebook, he added: "I am standing firm on our security interests — we will not withdraw from our current lines until Hamas is completely disarmed."

That single sentence undercuts the roadmap's core sequencing. The plan envisions Israeli forces pulling back to the so-called Yellow Line in stages tied to verified Hamas disarmament milestones — a gradual, reciprocal unwind. Netanyahu's position — full disarmament first, withdrawal only after — is the same maximalist demand Israel held before the roadmap was drafted, suggesting the negotiating gap that the document was meant to close remains essentially where it started.

Strikes continue, the toll climbs

Hamas has mirrored that intransigence from its own end. Negotiator Ghazi Hamad told Al Jazeera the group would "not take any steps regarding disarmament before Israel withdraws from the Gaza Strip," describing the roadmap as a "comprehensive package" — an end to Israel's offensive, full troop withdrawal, deployment of the national committee and international forces, and reconstruction — rather than a straightforward weapons handover. Each side, in other words, has conditioned its own compliance on the other's moving first.

Meanwhile the shooting never stopped. Israeli strikes killed at least 26 Palestinians over the weekend following the July 30 announcement, including a strike that killed 19 people in a single day. Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's envoy, said "two days of strikes across Gaza have killed civilians and destroyed medical supplies that people depend on" — a reference that included the destruction of a medicine warehouse at Al-Aqsa Hospital, which Gaza's health minister, Munir al-Bursh, said turned "medicine and illness itself into a tool of war." July was the deadliest month in Gaza since the ceasefire began, with 152 deaths; by August 3 the cumulative toll since October had reached roughly 1,250 killed and more than 4,100 wounded, according to figures cited by mediators.

At the UN Security Council, briefers have described a ceasefire that is technically holding but practically fraying. Officials have warned that Gaza risks a "permanent" state of limbo if the transition plan stalls, and that delays in implementing the Council-backed framework only deepen the suffering they were meant to end. Nearly a million people across the strip still need urgent shelter assistance; roughly 46% of essential medicines and two-thirds of medical consumables are out of stock, according to the World Health Organization. Funding shortfalls have forced aid agencies to scale back services even as the caseload grows.

What a framework without a signatory means

Ceasefire frameworks that collapse tend to fail in a specific way: not through dramatic renunciation, but through quiet non-endorsement while facts on the ground keep moving. That is the pattern taking shape here. Hamas's agreement to the roadmap was, on paper, the harder political lift — a militant group publicly committing to hand over its arsenal. But without Israeli sign-off, that commitment has no counterpart obligation to activate it, and the strikes that have continued since the announcement suggest Israel is not treating the roadmap as operative in the meantime.

The unresolved sequencing question — who moves first, disarmament or withdrawal — is not a technicality. It is the same dispute that stalled the ceasefire's second phase for months before the Board of Peace roadmap was drafted, and Netanyahu's explicit rejection of the American text suggests it has not actually been resolved, only re-papered. For a region where every prior truce since October 2023 has been renegotiated, extended or partially violated within weeks of signature, a plan that one of its two principal parties says it never agreed to is a fragile basis for the next phase.

What happens next likely turns on whether Washington pushes Netanyahu toward a revised text he will publicly back, or lets the current ambiguity persist while strikes continue. The Security Council is due to receive further briefings on the transition plan's implementation in the coming weeks; the toll figures and shelter statistics briefers cite there are, for now, the clearest available gauge of whether the roadmap is doing anything at all on the ground.
