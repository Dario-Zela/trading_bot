US tightens the screws on China with polysilicon price floors and forced-labour tariffs

Polycrystalline silicon wafer, the raw material at the center of Washington's new price-floor mechanism. — Georg Slickers / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
The US is pairing a 15% polysilicon tariff and price floor with a new 12.5% forced-labour tariff on China, while Beijing cuts its own tech and healthcare duties.

The Trump administration is preparing to do something it has mostly avoided in its tariff campaign against China: tell the market what price to charge. As soon as this month, the Commerce Department is expected to impose a 15% national-security tariff on polysilicon derivatives alongside a minimum import price covering polysilicon, wafers, solar cells and finished panels — a hybrid mechanism that goes beyond taxing Chinese imports and starts setting a floor under what they can legally sell for in the United States. It lands in the same week that the U.S. Trade Representative's office begins enforcing a separate 12.5% tariff on Chinese goods tied to forced-labour import rules, part of a sweeping action against 60 economies. Taken together, the two moves mark a shift from tariff-as-protection to tariff-as-market-engineering — and China, for its part, is responding not by de-escalating but by cutting its own duties on the technology and healthcare imports the same U.S. sectors depend on.

A price floor, not just a tariff

The polysilicon action stems from a year-long Section 232 national-security investigation — the same authority Trump has used to tariff steel, autos and semiconductors — into a material most consumers have never heard of but that sits at the base of two supply chains Washington cares about deeply: solar panels and chips. Polysilicon is refined to different purity grades for each. China now controls something close to 90% of global polysilicon capacity, according to industry researcher Bernreuter Research, up from roughly 30% just over a decade ago, when Michigan-based Hemlock Semiconductor was still the world's largest producer. Beijing's state subsidies pushed prices down far enough that Western capacity — Hemlock, Germany's Wacker, South Korea's OCI — struggled to compete on cost alone.

A tariff addresses that only partially, because Chinese producers can still undercut a tariffed price if their underlying cost is low enough. A price floor closes that gap by setting a minimum import price beneath which polysilicon and its derivatives cannot legally be sold into the U.S. market, regardless of what it costs to produce. Commerce is reportedly pairing this with a carve-out: importers who invest in domestic wafer and cell manufacturing can offset some of the tariff cost, an incentive structure aimed at pulling solar and chip-grade production back onshore rather than merely taxing what comes in. Markets read the signal quickly — shares in Corning, which owns a stake in Hemlock, jumped as the reports circulated, and South Korea's OCI and Hanwha Solutions saw similar lifts. Commerce and the White House did not respond to requests for comment on the plan's final terms, which are expected to be formalized this month.

The forced-labour tariff nobody negotiated away

Running in parallel, and easy to conflate with the polysilicon action but legally distinct, is a new tranche of Section 301 tariffs that took effect July 24. Jamieson Greer's USTR concluded that 60 trading partners — accounting for roughly 99.4% of U.S. imports — have failed to adequately prohibit imports made with forced labour, and imposed an additional 10% tariff on economies with at least some ban in place and 12.5% on those with none. China falls into the higher tier. Unlike prior Section 301 actions, which typically targeted one country's specific practices, this one applies broadly across nearly the entire U.S. import base without requiring proof that an individual shipment was tied to forced labour — a scope the Tax Foundation estimates could add on the order of $600 billion in tariff revenue, effectively a tax on U.S. importers and consumers, over the coming decade.

For China specifically, forced-labour enforcement functions as a tariff that survives even a trade truce, since it is framed as a human-rights and enforcement issue rather than a negotiable trade grievance. That makes it harder for Beijing to bargain away in the way it might push back on a straightforward Section 232 or 301 tariff tied to market conditions.

Beijing cuts the other way

What makes the timing notable is that China's own 2026 tariff schedule, which took effect January 1, moves in the opposite direction. The State Council Tariff Commission lowered duties on hundreds of products, with particular reductions in high-tech components and healthcare goods — diagnostic reagents, medical-device bearings, artificial blood vessels — the same categories most exposed to U.S. protectionism. Rather than retaliating tariff-for-tariff, Beijing appears to be signalling that it will keep its own market open to imports it needs while relying on scale, subsidy and, according to U.S. trade researchers, transshipment through Southeast Asian intermediaries to blunt the effect of American duties on its exports. China posted a record trade surplus of roughly $1.1 trillion over the past year, a figure Washington's tariff campaign was explicitly meant to shrink.

What it adds up to

The throughline across both actions is a White House willing to manage prices and enforcement thresholds directly rather than rely on tariffs alone to reshape trade flows — a bet that market-setting tools succeed where taxation has so far failed to dent China's share of solar and chip-adjacent supply chains. The cost lands on U.S. solar installers and electronics makers first, in the form of higher input prices for panels and semiconductor-grade materials; consumer electronics and clean-energy project economics follow with a lag. Watch for Commerce's formal polysilicon determination this month, which will set the actual floor price and could draw a WTO challenge, and for whether China treats the forced-labour tariffs as grounds to retaliate against U.S. agricultural or tech exports rather than simply absorb them.
