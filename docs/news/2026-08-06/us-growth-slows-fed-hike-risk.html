STAGFLATION · By M. Halloway · 2026-08-06 · 5 min read

US growth slows to 1.5% as stagflation fears sharpen the Fed's rate debate

The Marriner S. Eccles Building, headquarters of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Q2 GDP growth slowed to 1.5% as the GDP price index jumped 6.2%, and Fed Governor Cook now says she's ready to back a rate hike.

The economy is still growing. That is about the only unambiguous sentence available after Wednesday's data. The Bureau of Economic Analysis's advance estimate put second-quarter GDP growth at an annualised 1.5%, down from 2.1% in the first quarter — a deceleration, not a contraction, but one arriving alongside a price gauge that moved the wrong way. For a Federal Reserve already split over which mandate deserves priority, the report removed the easy answer.

A softer number, a harder price gauge

Consumer spending, investment and exports carried the quarter; government spending was a drag, and a rise in imports — which subtract from the GDP calculation — did the rest of the damage. On its own, 1.5% would read as an unremarkable soft patch, in line with an economy that grew at just 0.5% in the final quarter of last year before rebounding.

What changes the reading is the price side of the same report. The GDP price index rose at an annualised 6.2% in the second quarter, and the price index for gross domestic purchases — the broader gauge of what households and businesses actually paid — jumped to 5.7%, up sharply from 3.6% in the first quarter. The headline PCE price index accelerated to 5.1% from 4.6%. Core PCE, stripped of food and energy, actually eased to 3.4% from 4.4%, so the inflation picture inside the report is not uniformly worse — but the broad, tariff-exposed price gauges that matter most to the Fed's headline mandate moved in the direction policymakers least wanted to see, even as growth slowed. That combination — decelerating output, accelerating headline prices — is the textbook definition of a stagflationary quarter, and it landed two days before Fed officials were due back at the podium.

The timing compounds the problem. Markets had already been unsettled: the Dow fell roughly 1,100 points on 28 July, its worst single day since April 2025, after the Fed held rates steady and traders concluded the central bank was falling behind on inflation. The S&P 500 slid 1.52% the following session to 7,316.15. CME FedWatch pricing now puts the odds of at least one more rate hike by year-end at 86% — a startling reversal from where futures sat at the start of 2026, when multiple cuts were the consensus trade.

Cook tips her hand

Into that backdrop stepped Fed Governor Lisa Cook, a member of the Board since 2022 and, until recently, a reliable voice for weighing labour-market risk alongside inflation risk. Speaking in Anchorage on 5 August, Cook said plainly that the calculus has shifted.

"If I do not see signs of continued disinflation soon, I am prepared to act," Cook said, adding that she considers "the risks to the inflation side of the dual mandate higher than the risks to the employment side at this point."

She went further, warning that "with five years of above-target inflation, the risk grows that higher inflation may become entrenched in price- and wage-setting behavior," and that the Fed does "not have that luxury" of waiting for more evidence before acting. Cook voted with the majority last month to hold rates steady; her remarks suggest that vote may not repeat itself if the September data don't cooperate. That a governor generally read as centrist — not a Fed hawk by reputation — is now openly discussing a hike is, on its own, a data point about where the committee's internal weather has moved.

Warsh's Fed, recalibrated

Cook's shift cannot be read apart from who now runs the meetings. Kevin Warsh was sworn in as the Fed's 17th chair on 22 May, after a bruising 54-45 Senate confirmation — the most divided vote for a Fed chair on record — and his appointment was broadly understood in markets as a hawkish tilt after the Powell era. Warsh, who served as a Fed governor from 2006 to 2011 and was an early and persistent critic of ultra-loose policy during that period, has kept his public statements since taking the gavel notably guarded. But a chair's preferences tend to show up in the committee's centre of gravity before they show up in his own quotes, and Cook's Anchorage remarks read as evidence of exactly that migration. When a governor who would once have anchored the dovish flank is publicly rehearsing the case for tightening, the debate at the table has moved.

Equities without a safety net

The practical consequence for markets is the loss of an assumption that has underwritten valuations for most of the past two years: that if growth wobbles, the Fed cuts, and multiples get a floor under them. Stagflation breaks that trade. Slower growth normally argues for lower rates; sticky headline inflation argues for higher ones. A central bank that leans toward the latter — as Cook's comments and Warsh's installation both suggest — leaves equities to be priced on earnings and discount rates alone, with no policy put underneath them.

That tension is already visible beneath the surface of an index that, taken as a whole, has kept setting records. It is a market where a slowing economy, a hawkish-leaning Fed and still-elevated headline inflation are all true simultaneously, and where the next data print carries more weight than usual precisely because the Fed has signalled it is watching closely rather than running on autopilot.

What to watch

Two dates now matter more than the calendar would suggest. The Fed's Jackson Hole symposium, running 27–29 August in Wyoming, gives Warsh his first extended public platform since Cook's remarks to either confirm or temper the hawkish drift markets are pricing in. Then comes the September 15–16 FOMC meeting, where the committee will have a full round of August inflation and employment data in hand — and where Cook's conditional threat to "act" gets tested against the numbers rather than a speech. Between now and then, every inflation release will be read for whether the entrenchment Cook warned about is starting to show up, or whether the second quarter's price jump proves to be noise in an otherwise slowing economy.
