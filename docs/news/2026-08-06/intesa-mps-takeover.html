Intesa Sanpaolo's $35bn Monte dei Paschi takeover nears the finish line

Palazzo Salimbeni in Siena, headquarters of Monte dei Paschi di Siena, the world's oldest surviving bank and the target of Intesa Sanpaolo's takeover bid. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Intesa Sanpaolo's €30.6bn hostile bid for Monte dei Paschi cleared a key antitrust remedy in July and heads toward a December close pending ECB and Italian regulatory sign-off.

Intesa Sanpaolo's €30.6 billion ($35bn) bid for Monte dei Paschi di Siena is entering its final stretch, with the two sides still trading barbs over price even as the regulatory machinery behind the deal grinds toward approval. If it closes as planned in December, the deal will fuse Italy's largest bank with its oldest — one founded in 1472 — into a group with roughly €1.7 trillion in customer assets, second in the eurozone by market value only to Santander.

Intesa launched the unsolicited offer on 8 June, proposing 16 new Intesa shares plus €1 in cash for every 10 MPS shares — a price of roughly €10.09 per share that implied a 12.5% premium at announcement. MPS's board has pushed back, arguing the offer undervalues the bank and pointing to lingering uncertainty over antitrust remedies and merger synergies. Intesa chief executive Carlo Messina has not moved on price. Late last month he invoked Mario Draghi's 2012 euro-crisis pledge, telling reporters Intesa would do "whatever it takes" to close the deal, which he has cast as a low-risk move that pulls forward the bank's strategic targets by three years.

Clearing the antitrust hurdle

The deal's structure was built around its biggest obstacle from the outset. Because Intesa already dominates several Italian regional markets — a legacy of its 2020 acquisition of UBI Banca — it pre-agreed to offload a chunk of MPS to the insurer Unipol before the tender offer even opened. Unipol will pay €3 billion to €3.5 billion for a carved-out bank comprising roughly 635 MPS branches and the Monte dei Paschi brand itself, assets it intends to fold into its existing lender BPER. On 30 July, Unipol shareholders cleared the way for their side of the bargain, approving a capital increase of up to €2.5 billion to fund the purchase, pending sign-off from insurance regulator IVASS. It is the kind of remedy Unipol has performed before — it played an identical role in shrinking Intesa's UBI overlap five years ago — and its approval removes one of the last self-inflicted contingencies in the deal.

What's left before December

Intesa has called an extraordinary shareholder meeting for 10 September to authorise issuing up to 5.7 billion new shares to fund the transaction. From there, the timeline compresses: sign-off is still required from the European Central Bank, the Bank of Italy, IVASS, and Italy's antitrust authority, alongside a Golden Power screening that has already snarled a rival Italian bank tie-up this year when UniCredit walked away from its bid for Banco BPM over unmet government conditions. Rome has since signalled it will not deploy Golden Power as aggressively against the Intesa-MPS transaction, easing what had been the most politically charged variable in the process.

Assuming the remaining approvals land on schedule, the tender offer would formally close before year-end, folding MPS — and, by extension, the Mediobanca and Generali stakes MPS acquired in its own hostile takeover last year — into Intesa's balance sheet. For a sector that spent much of the past decade shrinking under the weight of bad loans, a €35 billion transaction of this size is a marker of how far Italian banking's fortunes have turned, and a preview of the consolidation pressure now building across the rest of the continent. The next checkpoint is Intesa's 10 September shareholder vote; after that, the deal's fate rests with Frankfurt and Rome.
