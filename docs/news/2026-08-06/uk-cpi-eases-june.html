UK inflation cools to 2.6%, lowest since March 2025

The Office for National Statistics' headquarters at Drummond Gate, London, which published the June inflation figures on July 22. — Londoneye / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK CPI cooled to 2.6% in June on cheaper fuel and food, but core and services inflation held flat — leaving August 19's July print as the real signal.

UK inflation slowed to 2.6% in the year to June, the Office for National Statistics said, its lowest reading since March 2025 and a touch below the 2.7% economists had pencilled in. It was the second straight monthly fall, down from 2.8% in May, and the clearest sign yet that the surge in prices triggered by 2025's energy and food shocks is losing momentum.

The relief was concentrated in one place: the fuel pump. Diesel fell 10.7p a litre between May and June to 176.4p, while petrol eased 2.1p to 155.3p — the first monthly petrol decline since late February. Transport inflation as a whole dropped to 5.7% from 6.8%. Food prices added to the disinflation, with the annual rate down to 1.7% from 2.2%, its lowest since August 2024, as chocolate, confectionery and cooking oils pulled back.

A narrower, shallower cool-down

Strip out the volatile components and the picture is less flattering. Core CPI, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, held at 2.6% — unchanged from May. Services inflation, the measure the Bank of England watches most closely for evidence of embedded price pressure in wages and rents, stayed at 3.6%. CPIH, the ONS's preferred headline measure that includes owner-occupiers' housing costs, fell to 2.8% from 3.0%.

In other words: the fall was real, but it was fuel- and food-led rather than broad-based. Wholesale energy and commodity costs have been normalising for months, and June's print is largely that filtering through to the forecourt and the supermarket till. The stickier, services-driven core of inflation — the part tied to domestic wage growth and demand — barely moved.

A print that is already out of date

Here is the awkward arithmetic for anyone trading off this release: June ended more than five weeks before the ONS published the figures on July 22, and by the time this data reached desks the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee had already met and moved on. At its July 30 decision, the MPC voted 6-3 to hold Bank Rate at 3.75% — with the hawkish minority widening from two members to three, as Megan Greene, Huw Pill and Catherine Mann all pushed for a quarter-point hike rather than a cut.

Their argument was not that June's number was wrong, but that it doesn't settle the question. Greene has pointed to inflation running above the Bank's 2% target for roughly five years running, and to fresh supply risks — a second potential chokepoint in the Red Sea, tightness in AI-related hardware — that could reignite cost pressure just as the fuel-driven relief fades. The Bank's own Monetary Policy Report reportedly sees headline inflation climbing back above 3% later this year, which is the real reason a softer June print didn't trigger a rate-cut rally in sterling: currency and rates markets price the deviation from expectations and the forward path, not a single month's headline number.

What actually matters next

That forward path gets its next real data point on August 19, when the ONS publishes July's inflation figures. Unlike June's release — filed against a backdrop the MPC has already priced and moved past — the July number lands with no scheduled MPC meeting between now and it, giving markets a cleaner read on whether disinflation is broadening beyond fuel and food or whether the hawks' second-round-effects worry is showing up in services and wages.

The things to watch inside that release: whether services inflation finally cracks below 3.6%, whether food prices keep falling now that the sugar-and-confectionery effect has largely worked through, and whether motor fuel — after two months of relief — starts reversing as global crude and shipping-route risk (Hormuz, the Red Sea) feed back into pump prices. Any one of those breaking the wrong way hands the MPC's three dissenters more ammunition ahead of the Bank's next scheduled decision, and confirms the case that June's 2.6% was a floor, not a trend.
