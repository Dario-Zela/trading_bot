Shell beats Q2 estimates on higher oil prices, confirms Aphrodite gas exit

Wael Sawan, now Shell's chief executive, pictured in 2010 during his earlier posting as Vice President Commercial for Qatar Shell. — Chatham House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's profit doubled on higher oil prices even as it confirmed exiting a non-operated Cyprus gas stake to focus capital on assets it controls.

Shell posted its strongest quarterly profit in four years on Thursday, and used the same week to confirm it is walking away from a gas field it has held an interest in for more than a decade — a pairing that says as much about the company's priorities as either headline alone.

Adjusted earnings for the second quarter came in at $9.84bn, more than double the $4.26bn Shell reported a year earlier and comfortably ahead of the roughly $8.8bn-$8.9bn analysts had pencilled in, according to an LSEG-compiled consensus. Earnings per share of $3.52 beat estimates by $0.29. Revenue reached $94.7bn against a consensus near $86.2bn. Brent crude averaged $104 a barrel in the quarter, up 28% from $81 in the first three months of the year, as the Middle East conflict tightened supply and lifted prices across crude, gas and refined products.

Cash flow from operations came to $21.4bn, and Shell launched another $3bn share buyback — its nineteenth consecutive quarter of buybacks at that level or higher, in line with a policy of returning 40-50% of operating cash flow through the cycle. Not every line moved in Shell's favour: integrated gas output fell 31% quarter-on-quarter after QatarEnergy shut in production following a missile strike on Shell's Pearl gas-to-liquids plant, with full repairs not expected until the first quarter of 2027. Record refining utilisation and record upstream output in Brazil covered the gap.

Why walk away from a producing gas stake now

Set against that backdrop, Shell's decision to sell its 35% non-operated interest in the Aphrodite gas field, off Cyprus, reads less like retreat and more like triage. The buyer is Hungary's MOL Group, paying up to $720m for BG Cyprus, the Shell subsidiary that has held the stake since Shell's 2016 acquisition of BG Group. Chevron's Cypriot unit operates the block with an equal 35%, and Israel's NewMed Energy holds the remaining 30%. The deal is expected to close in early 2027, pending regulatory approval.

"Our decision to exit is driven by disciplined capital allocation and portfolio choices, as we focus on opportunities that strengthen our integrated LNG value chain," said Cederic Cremers, Shell's Integrated Gas president.

The field itself has been a slow burn: discovered in 2011, it still has no final investment decision, with one not expected before 2027 and first gas not projected until 2031 — two decades after discovery. Shell's exit is not a rejection of gas broadly; the company is simultaneously closing its acquisition of Canada's ARC Resources. Rather, it is a statement about which gas assets are worth Shell's capital: ones it operates and can slot directly into its own LNG supply chain, not minority stakes in projects still years from producing a molecule.

That distinction is the one worth watching as other majors weigh their own portfolios. A company posting its best quarter since the 2022 energy shock, with no shortage of cash to deploy, chose to sell rather than hold a non-operated stake in a field with a 2031 start date. The next marker is Pearl's return to service in Qatar, expected in the first quarter of 2027 — roughly the same window in which the Aphrodite sale is due to close.
