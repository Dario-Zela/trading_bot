EA delists from Nasdaq in record $55bn Saudi-led buyout

Electronic Arts' Redwood City, California headquarters, now under majority Saudi PIF ownership after the $55bn take-private. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
EA delisted from Nasdaq after a record $55bn buyout left Saudi Arabia's PIF holding 93.4%, alongside Silver Lake and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners.

Electronic Arts stopped trading on the Nasdaq this week, closing out a nine-month process that ends with the maker of Madden, FIFA and The Sims answering to a boardroom dominated by Riyadh rather than Wall Street. The $55 billion take-private — the largest leveraged buyout ever recorded — formally closed on August 4, with Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund holding 93.4% of the company, private equity firm Silver Lake 5.5%, and Affinity Partners, run by Jared Kushner, the remaining 1.1%.

Shareholders received $210 in cash per share, a 25% premium to EA's last unaffected close of $168.32 before the deal was announced on September 29, 2025. The consortium beat the previous record — the 2007 buyout of Texas utility TXU, worth just over $48 billion including debt — by a wide margin, and did it without the credit-bubble excess that defined that earlier era of mega-deals. On an inflation-adjusted basis, 1989's RJR Nabisco buyout, immortalized in Barbarians at the Gate, still ranks larger. But among deals struck since 2010, EA is the only one to break into the top five, and by a considerable distance.

How the numbers work

PIF supplied the bulk of a roughly $36 billion equity contribution. JPMorgan Chase, which also advised on the deal, arranged more than $20 billion in debt financing — pushing EA's balance-sheet debt from about $2.2 billion to close to $20 billion and adding an estimated $1.8 billion a year in interest expense. The company has told investors it will fund roughly $700 million in cost cuts, including $170 million tagged as "organisational efficiencies" — the kind of language that, in a studio system already trimmed by hundreds of job losses over the past year, has developers bracing for another round of layoffs. No cuts have been announced yet, but the debt load leaves the new owners with limited room to avoid them.

The deal required sign-off from regulators on both sides of the Atlantic. The European Union cleared it on July 23; the last outstanding hurdle, a review by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States, was cleared by July 30. That CFIUS review drew scrutiny in Washington precisely because of who was involved: Kushner, son-in-law to and former senior adviser for President Trump, holds a stake through a firm whose funding has come overwhelmingly from PIF itself. Democratic lawmakers flagged the arrangement as a conflict of interest and questioned whether a foreign government now controls a platform that collects behavioral, communications and payment data on hundreds of millions of players — the exact category of concern CFIUS was built to police. With Republicans holding both chambers, no formal probe has materialized.

A bigger bet than one studio

EA is not an isolated purchase. It is the largest piece yet in a buildout PIF has run through Savvy Games Group, its dedicated gaming arm, which now has roughly $38 billion earmarked for the sector. Savvy already controls Scopely, the top mobile publisher in the US, and ESL FACEIT Group, the leading esports operator, and has been consolidating stakes in Nintendo and Bandai Namco along with a reported $6 billion approach for Chinese developer Moonton. Savvy finished 2024 as the world's eighth-largest games publisher by net revenue. Owning EA outright — rather than holding a passive stake, as PIF has done in Activision Blizzard and Take-Two — moves the kingdom from investor to operator of one of the industry's largest back catalogues, with Sims, FIFA-descendant football titles and a sports-simulation business that reaches deep into markets PIF is separately courting through LIV Golf and Newcastle United, both chaired by PIF governor Yasir Al-Rumayyan.

The mechanical fallout

EA's removal from the S&P 500 and Nasdaq-100 forces a rebalancing that has nothing to do with anyone's view of the company. Index funds tracking those benchmarks are mandated to hold the stocks in them at prescribed weights; when a constituent disappears, funds must sell out entirely and redistribute the proceeds across the index's replacement and remaining members, typically timed to the announcement's effective date rather than any judgment about value. EA carried a market weight large enough — it ranked among the more heavily traded gaming names on the Nasdaq — that passive vehicles and the algorithmic strategies that shadow index composition had non-trivial rebalancing to do this week, alongside active gaming-sector funds needing to mark a major holding to zero and redeploy. S&P Dow Jones Indices and Nasdaq have not yet named EA's index replacements; that typically follows within days of a delisting rather than months.

What happens next runs on two clocks. The financial one is straightforward: EA's new owners now carry roughly $1.8 billion a year in interest payments against a company whose free cash flow will need to cover both that debt service and the promised cost cuts, with the first full private-ownership earnings picture likely to emerge whenever the consortium next reports to its bondholders. The political one is murkier — whether Congress presses further on the Kushner-PIF-CFIUS overlap, and whether other large, data-rich US technology and entertainment assets become the next test case for how much scrutiny a Gulf-backed buyer with a well-placed minority partner actually faces.
