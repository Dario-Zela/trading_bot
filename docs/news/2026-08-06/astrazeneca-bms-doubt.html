AstraZeneca shares whipsaw as $400bn Bristol Myers merger talk is cast into doubt

AstraZeneca's Cambridge, UK headquarters and R&D centre, at the centre of a week of merger speculation. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca shares swung as much as 9% on reports of $400bn merger talks with Bristol Myers Squibb, then partly reversed after a source told Reuters no such talks exist.

AstraZeneca's stock spent the week doing what merger rumours do best: moving first and asking questions later. Shares in the Anglo-Swedish drugmaker fell as much as 9% in London on Monday — their worst one-day drop in years — after the Financial Times and Reuters reported the company had held preliminary talks with Bristol Myers Squibb about a combination that would create the world's fourth-largest pharmaceutical company by market value, worth close to $400 billion combined. By Wednesday, a Reuters source was denying the talks had ever happened at all, and AstraZeneca's stock rebounded to claw back most of what it lost.

The reports, which first surfaced over the weekend, described months of discussions between the two companies. Bristol Myers shares moved the other way on the initial news, rising as much as 6% in US trading — a fairly standard pattern for the smaller party in a takeover rumour, where investors price in a premium. AstraZeneca, as the larger and more richly valued of the two at roughly $256 billion against Bristol Myers' $130 billion, absorbed the bulk of the market's discomfort.

A deal that never quite made sense

What set this episode apart from the usual pop-and-fade of merger chatter was how quickly the market's own analysts turned on the premise. Jefferies called itself "a bit perplexed," noting that "if there is one company that doesn't need financial engineering, it's AZ." Markus Manns, a portfolio manager at Union Investment, said a tie-up "does not make strategic or financial sense" and risked disrupting "a well-run company with a full pipeline." Sean Conroy at Shore Capital was more measured, observing that investors are "not often fans of mega mergers of this ilk," though he conceded the logic of using Bristol Myers' cash flows to soften AstraZeneca's own patent cliff after 2030.

That cliff is real: both companies face a combined roughly $50 billion of exclusivity losses on key drugs by the end of the decade, and Bristol Myers alone stands to lose about $30 billion of sales to expiring patents before AstraZeneca's own losses even begin. Jeremy Levin, Bristol Myers' former head of M&A and later chief executive of Teva, argued the deal would camouflage that cliff rather than fix it, warning that three to four years of integration turmoil was a poor trade at a moment when Chinese biotech rivals and GLP-1 drugmakers are both closing in. The overlap between AstraZeneca's Imfinzi and Bristol Myers' Opdivo — competing checkpoint-inhibitor cancer therapies the two companies were still litigating against each other as recently as 2023 — added a further antitrust complication that neither side seemed eager to court.

On Wednesday, a senior source told Reuters flatly that none of it was true: "There is no deal between AstraZeneca and BMS. There never was a deal to be done, and there are no discussions between the companies." Bristol Myers shares fell back roughly 3.6% on the denial while AstraZeneca's London stock rose about 4.5%, though neither company has gone on the record to confirm or deny anything directly.

For a market that spent three days repricing two large-cap drugmakers on unconfirmed reporting, the whipsaw is itself the story: no filing, no confirmed talks, and a flat denial from someone close to the matter, yet nearly $20 billion moved in and out of AstraZeneca's market cap along the way. Whether the FT and Reuters' original sourcing or Wednesday's denial holds up will likely depend on whether either company is forced to comment on the record — the next thing worth watching is whether AstraZeneca addresses the speculation directly when it next reports earnings.
