China cracks down on AI deepfake disinformation after typhoon disasters

Streets in Shenzhen during Typhoon Noul, July 25, 2026 — the storm that triggered a wave of fabricated AI disaster footage. — A.Classical-Futurist / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Fake AI-generated flood and rescue videos disrupted emergency response during China's typhoon season, prompting Beijing to threaten criminal charges for synthetic disaster content.

When Typhoon Noul tore into Huizhou on the Guangdong coast in the early hours of July 26, it was, by Chinese state media's own reckoning, the strongest storm to make landfall in the country this year. More than 801,000 people were evacuated. Eleven rivers breached warning levels. Hong Kong's airport cancelled some 350 flights. It was, in other words, already a serious disaster before anyone had to guess at how bad it was.

They had to guess anyway. Within hours of Noul's landfall, social platforms were carrying videos of bodies floating in floodwater, of stadiums supposedly packed with corpses, of towns underwater that had stayed dry. A widely shared claim of an imminent regional power outage triggered a run on emergency supplies. In one case, after snakes genuinely did escape a breeding farm in Hengzhou, Guangxi, during flooding in early July, AI-generated images recast the incident as crocodiles being loosed into a nearby river. None of it was true. Almost all of it looked convincing enough to matter.

In one sentence: Fake AI videos of floods and rescue failures spread during China's typhoons and disrupted emergency response, pushing Beijing toward criminal penalties for synthetic disaster content.

A season built for fabrication

Noul was China's twelfth typhoon of the year, arriving on top of a flood season that had already killed dozens in unrelated disasters and put Hunan on a third-level emergency footing. That combination — real chaos, urgent public need for accurate information, and a news cycle moving faster than official channels can confirm anything — is exactly the environment synthetic media exploits best. Huang Zhihua, a deputy chief for online security with Zhejiang police, put it bluntly to reporters: creators use disaster news to gain followers, because sensational content performs, and so they fabricate it deliberately. Chen Bin, a professor tracking the phenomenon, made the more structural point — that generative AI has collapsed the skill required to produce convincing fake video, audio and text down to a prompt.

The fakes were not uniformly random noise. Chinese authorities, in cases released under the Ministry of Public Security's Cybersecurity Bureau, described a pattern: repackaged flood footage from other regions and other years, presented as live; fabricated casualty and damage figures; false claims of dam failures, levee breaches or chemical leaks; posts impersonating government agencies using their logos and names. A 45-year-old man in Chengde, Hebei, was given an administrative penalty for an AI video falsely claiming ground collapse in Kuancheng county — police said it caused local panic and disrupted flood prevention work. A 53-year-old woman in Zhanjiang, Guangdong, faced the same for a false claim about bodies in a stadium, which police said misled large numbers of users and interfered with relief operations. These are not abstract harms. Rescue coordination during a typhoon depends on people trusting the difference between what's happening and what isn't; fake footage that looks like the former erodes exactly that.

Beijing's response

On July 23, the Cyberspace Administration of China, working jointly with the Ministry of Emergency Management, launched a nationwide campaign against disaster misinformation, folded into the broader Clean Net 2026 enforcement push. The public security bureau disclosed 20 cases built around AI-generated fake video, repurposed footage and fabricated claims designed to attract traffic — with penalties ranging from administrative fines up to prison terms of as much as seven years for the most serious offences. It sits inside a larger apparatus: the CAC's Qinglang campaign series removed more than 960,000 pieces of content and penalized over 3,700 accounts in its 2025 iteration, and mandatory labelling rules for AI-generated content took effect in September 2025.

That scale of enforcement is notable given China already runs one of the most heavily policed internet environments anywhere. The Great Firewall, real-name registration and an army of content moderators have not stopped fabricated disaster footage from spreading fast enough to interfere with actual rescue operations. Xu Xiaoke, a professor studying the problem, described detection and generation technology as locked in “a constant cat-and-mouse game” — an admission, from within a state apparatus not known for admitting limits, that authentication cannot keep pace with fabrication using current tools.

The wider stakes

What happened in Guangdong is a preview available to any country with widespread smartphone access and a generative-AI tool three taps away. Emergency response has always depended on a shared, fast-forming picture of what is actually happening — which roads are out, which buildings collapsed, where the water is rising. AI-generated content doesn't need to fool everyone to do damage; it only needs to be indistinguishable enough, for long enough, to pull resources or attention toward the wrong place, or to make officials look like they're withholding real information. Officials in China are now telling residents to wait for statements from accredited agencies before acting on what they see online — sound advice, but also a tacit admission that the platforms themselves cannot be trusted to sort fact from fabrication in real time.

Criminal penalties address the people caught making these videos after the fact; they don't solve the authentication problem during the event itself, when it matters most. That gap — between disaster-speed disinformation and investigation-speed enforcement — is the part worth watching. China's crackdown is a test case for what comes next: whether governments and platforms elsewhere invest in real-time content verification before their own next disaster, or wait to react the way Beijing just did, case by case, after the damage from a rumor is already done. The next major typhoon to hit China's coast, expected before the season ends in October, will be the first real test of whether the new enforcement changes what spreads in the first six hours.
