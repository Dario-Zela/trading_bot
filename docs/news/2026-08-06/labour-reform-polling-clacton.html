Labour overtakes Reform in the polls as Farage fights to hold Clacton amid funding scandal

In one sentence
Labour narrowly leads Reform UK in polling for the first time since March 2025, but Farage looks set to easily retain Clacton on August 13 despite a funding scandal.

For the first time in more than a year, more Britons say they would vote Labour than Reform UK. It is not much of a lead — 27% to 25% in the latest JL Partners survey, and a narrower 25.9% to 24.0% in PollCheck's seven-poll rolling average — but after eighteen months in which Nigel Farage's party looked like the default alternative to a faltering Labour government, the reversal has a name: the 'Burnham Bounce'.

The name is doing more work than the evidence yet supports. Andy Burnham became prime minister on 20 July, Britain's seventh in a decade, after Keir Starmer resigned amid sinking personal approval and a party in open revolt over his direction. Since then Reform's polling average has slid from a peak of 33% last autumn to 24% now, and Labour has edged past it. Whether that is Burnham's doing, or simply the novelty of a new face after two years of Starmer fatigue, is the question pollsters cannot yet answer — and the one that will determine whether this is a turning point or a blip.

What actually moved the numbers

Burnham has spent his first fortnight in office trying to make the shift look deliberate rather than incidental. He has cut VAT on domestic electricity from 5% to zero for six months from October, worth an estimated £25 to a typical household on the price cap, and restored a £2 cap on bus fares outside London from January. The package costs the Treasury roughly £850 million, which Downing Street says will be funded by scrapping Starmer's digital ID scheme rather than new borrowing — a distinction Burnham has been careful to draw as he insists he will stick to Labour's fiscal rules. He has framed the whole effort under a single label, telling his new ministers they are now a "cost of living government" whose job is convincing voters that "help is coming".

It is a plausible story: cost-of-living relief announced within days of taking office, followed by a polling lead within a fortnight. But pollster Peter Kellner, writing before the JL Partners numbers landed, cautioned that "changing party leader was a necessary but far from sufficient condition for reviving Labour's fortunes" — personal approval for a new leader typically rises regardless of policy, while the party's underlying brand problem usually does not move with it. He called an early bounce "neither here nor there" until it survives a few months of scrutiny. Two competing MRP models produced wildly different outcomes this summer — one showing Farage close to a Commons majority, the other Burnham comfortably ahead — which is less a contradiction than a reminder of how thin the current lead actually is.

Farage's test case, in miniature

If the national polls are ambiguous, Clacton offers a sharper read on how durable Reform's brand damage might be. Farage resigned his seat on 7 July, triggering an August 13 by-election he is contesting again, after parliamentary scrutiny intensified around a reported £5 million payment from the cryptocurrency investor Christopher Harborne — since referred to the National Crime Agency under anti-money-laundering protocols — alongside undeclared gifts allegedly linked to George Cottrell, a Farage associate convicted of wire fraud in the US, and questions over his constituency property declarations. Farage denies wrongdoing and has cast the re-election as a chance to let voters, not investigators, settle the matter.

The contest has drawn a record 34 candidates, breaking a mark set in 2008, after Labour, the Conservatives, the Liberal Democrats and the Greens all declined to field candidates — leaving the novelty campaigner Count Binface, whose platform amounts to little more than "I'm not Nigel Farage", as the nominal main challenger. A Survation poll taken August 2–3 put Farage at 73% on an expected low turnout, with Binface a distant second on 20%. Clacton looks, on those numbers, less like a referendum on the funding allegations than a formality — but the scale of the candidate field, and the fact that every mainstream party judged it not worth contesting, says something about how little national opposition to Farage has coalesced around any single alternative.

Signal, noise, and what markets should watch

The two threads point in slightly different directions. Nationally, Reform's slide and Labour's tentative recovery suggest the electoral arithmetic that has unsettled gilt investors and driven speculation about a snap 2027 election — the scenario Farage himself has invoked, warning that "Labour chaos" could force Keir Starmer's successor to the polls early — may be less certain than it looked a month ago. Locally, in the seat that put Farage back in Parliament, none of that has dented his standing at all. A one-point national swing and a 73% by-election vote share are not in tension; they simply measure different things. The former is about whether disaffected 2024 Conservative and Labour voters are drifting back toward the governing party under a new leader with a cost-of-living pitch; the latter is about a safe seat where Farage's personal brand has so far proven scandal-resistant.

The more useful test than either poll is time. Kellner's benchmark — whether a bounce survives beyond the initial press cycle — will be answerable only once JL Partners, PollCheck and others have several more weeks of data past the VAT cut's implementation and past Clacton's result on August 13. Until then, the honest reading is that Labour has narrowed a gap it was losing badly, on the strength of a leadership change and a cost-of-living package whose funding — a scrapped digital ID scheme rather than new taxes — will itself face scrutiny as the October VAT cut approaches and the bill for the bus fare cap comes due in the new year.
