CLIMATE · UK HEATWAVE · By S. Whitfield · 2026-08-06 · 6 min read

UK heads for its deadliest heat summer and one of its worst harvests on record

A UK reservoir at reduced capacity during drought conditions — reservoir storage nationally has fallen well below the long-term average this summer. — Peter Bond / Geograph, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
UKHSA estimates 2,877 heat deaths in May-June alone as hospitals, harvests and insurers all buckle under Britain's worst heat summer on record.

Britain spent the early summer of 2026 breaking records it did not want to break. Kew Gardens logged 35.1°C on 26 May, the hottest May day ever recorded in the UK. A month later, Lingwood in Norfolk hit 37.7°C. In between and after, the country's health agency, water companies and hospitals were left counting a cost that is proving far larger — and far more structural — than the seasonal inconvenience of a hot summer.

The UK Health Security Agency now estimates that 2,877 people died as a result of heat exposure across just two spells of hot weather in May and June — 753 during a four-day May episode, and 2,124 during an eight-day June heatwave that triggered only the second red heat-health alert England has ever issued. That figure is already closing in on the 2,985 heat deaths recorded across the whole of 2022, previously the worst year on record, and it does not yet include July, when a third heatwave arrived on top of the driest spell across parts of southern England since 1836.

"These figures highlight the significant danger that very hot weather can pose to people's health," said Dr Ross Thompson of UKHSA, noting that older people and those with existing health conditions carried the bulk of the risk. Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper described the toll as an "increasingly serious impact on the health of thousands," layered on top of what she called mounting "pressures facing our NHS."

A hospital that got too hot to operate in

The clearest illustration of how unprepared British infrastructure is for this kind of heat came not from a care home or a Tube platform but from an acute hospital. On 24 June, at the height of the June heatwave, Queen Alexandra Hospital in Portsmouth declared a critical incident after several chiller units serving its critical systems failed. The loss of cooling knocked out operating theatres, cardiac catheter laboratories and diagnostic scanning facilities; some planned surgery and appointments were postponed, and patients travelling to the site were warned that the building itself was dangerously hot. Engineers eventually restored the cooling and the incident was stood down, but Portsmouth was not an isolated case — similar critical incidents were declared at other NHS trusts during the same heatwave.

The episode is a small but pointed case study in adaptation lag. British hospitals, like British housing stock and British rail infrastructure, were built and equipped for a cooler, more temperate climate than the one now arriving. Chillers, cabling and building fabric rated for occasional warm spells are being asked to perform through repeated, sustained extremes, and in Portsmouth's case the mechanical plant simply gave out — at precisely the moment demand on emergency and critical care was highest. Analysts at Verdant, an economics consultancy tracking heat-related output losses, estimate the June heatwave alone cost the UK economy at least £2.4bn in lost productivity as roughly 1.25 million workers were unable to work through the worst days; they project cumulative output losses from heat could reach £25bn by the end of the decade without a serious adaptation programme.

The fields are failing too

While hospitals strained, farmland was drying out. Analysis from the Energy and Climate Intelligence Unit suggests the exceptionally dry spring and repeated summer heatwaves have cut as much as 2.5 million tonnes off the UK's cereal and oilseed harvest compared with earlier forecasts, costing arable farmers up to £390 million in lost revenue. With just over half the wheat crop harvested and yields running well below the ten-year average — 6.8 tonnes per hectare against a norm closer to 8 — the 2026 harvest is on course to total around 19.5 million tonnes, which would make it the worst UK cereal and oilseed harvest since detailed records began in 1984, worse even than 2020, itself a poor year.

"Farming is now the sector most exposed to climate change and the risks and damage that it brings," said Tom Lancaster of ECIU. Cambridgeshire farmer Martin Lines put it more bluntly, calling the harvest "a real kick in the teeth" after two already-poor years. Four of the five worst UK harvests since 1984 have now occurred within this decade — not a run of bad luck so much as a shift in baseline conditions that arable farming has not caught up with. The National Farmers' Union has separately warned that prolonged heat and drought could translate into shortages of specific food products through the autumn, layering a fresh source of price pressure onto a food supply chain still recovering from earlier inflation shocks.

What the markets are missing

The throughline across hospitals, harvests and headcounts is that the costs of heat in Britain are arriving faster than the institutions meant to absorb them can adapt. Property insurers, who paid out a record £307 million in domestic subsidence claims in 2025 after the hottest summer on record, are now watching a third consecutive surge year: subsidence claims already total £153 million for the first half of 2026, with the average settled claim reaching £17,820 in the first quarter — the highest on record and up 9% year on year. Three subsidence surge years within eight years, industry analysts note, is no longer an episodic pattern that can be underwritten as a one-off; it looks structural.

That is the broader point sitting underneath the death toll, the hospital chillers and the shrivelled wheat: a developed economy with mature institutions, a functioning health service and a temperate reputation is nonetheless proving vulnerable to heat in ways its infrastructure, insurance models and harvest forecasts did not anticipate. The costs are showing up simultaneously in excess mortality, in NHS operational failures, in farm incomes and in insurance loss ratios — different ledgers, same underlying driver. If adaptation spending continues to lag the pace of warming, as it has through three heatwaves in a single summer, those costs look set to compound rather than plateau.

UKHSA's full-year heat mortality figures, together with July's excess death data, are due in the autumn and will show whether 2026 has overtaken 2022 as the deadliest year on record. AHDB's final harvest figures, expected once combining wraps up in September, will confirm whether the 1984 low has actually been breached. Both numbers will matter well beyond the farm gate and the mortuary register — they are the clearest signal yet of how much climate adaptation Britain still has to pay for, and how quickly the bill is coming due.
