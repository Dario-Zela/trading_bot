Piper Sandler warns Hormuz disruption risk is underpriced in Shell's valuation

Satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz, the 21-mile-wide chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of global crude oil shipments normally pass. — NASA / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Piper Sandler's extended Hormuz closure forecast exposes Shell's 'value stock' label as a geopolitical bet in disguise, with risk of rapid reversal if oil prices normalise.

When hedge funds describe Shell plc as a cheap stock, the conversation usually runs to low price-to-earnings multiples, robust free cash flow, and a balance sheet that has held up through a turbulent energy cycle. What that framing tends to omit is what is actually driving those earnings — and how exposed the whole thesis is to a strip of water 21 miles wide at its narrowest point.

On May 27, Piper Sandler circulated a note that complicates the value thesis directly. The firm expects the Strait of Hormuz to remain largely closed for several more months, and it expressed "very little confidence" that commercial traffic will recover to even half of pre-war levels within the coming month. Oil shortages will intensify, Piper Sandler argues, and West Texas Intermediate crude — which surged toward $120 a barrel when the Iran conflict ignited in late February — could push beyond that level again this summer.

Buy now, risk embedded

The firm carries a Buy rating on Shell (NYSE: SHEL) with a price target of $106, roughly 26 percent above the stock's recent trading range around $84. That upside is coherent with a high-oil-price environment — Shell posted $6.92 billion in Q1 2026 adjusted earnings, its strongest quarterly result in two years, beating analyst estimates of $6.36 billion. On conventional metrics the stock looks inexpensive. The problem is that those metrics quietly embed a geopolitical premium that nobody in the "low cost stocks" conversation is naming explicitly.

The Strait handles roughly a fifth of global crude oil shipments and a comparable share of LNG exports. Since Iran declared the waterway closed on March 4, tankers bound for Asian and European markets have been rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope — adding weeks to delivery schedules and driving freight costs sharply higher. Shell and its peers have benefited from the resulting price floor, but that floor is crisis-derived, not structural. Piper Sandler's energy and macro teams noted that "the situation in the Middle East remains far from resolved" — a direct pushback against the Trump administration's periodic claims that an Iran deal is imminent. Those claims have repeatedly failed to produce results; Islamabad talks between Washington and Tehran collapsed in April.

The asymmetry is the underpriced element. A sudden diplomatic breakthrough — or a successful U.S. Navy escort campaign reopening commercial lanes — could deflate the oil price faster than current positioning assumes. Shell's earnings are real, but they are crisis-era earnings. A partial normalisation in supply would reprice the entire energy complex, and a stock that looks cheap at $84 against $107 oil looks considerably less so if Brent drifts back toward $80.

Markets have spent months treating elevated crude as the new baseline. Piper Sandler is asking, with some precision, how many more months that baseline holds. The next data points to watch: Q2 results from Shell, due in late July, and the next round of U.S.-Iran diplomatic contacts, whose outcome will do more to set Shell's earnings trajectory than any broker model.
