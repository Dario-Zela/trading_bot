National Grid challenges FERC's competitive bidding rules over returns threat

500kV three-phase transmission lines — the high-voltage infrastructure at the centre of FERC's competitive bidding dispute — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
National Grid's FERC protest over competitive transmission bidding, paired with a push for higher New England returns, signals growing friction over the economics of grid investment.

National Grid has filed a protest with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission over the agency's proposed competitive bidding framework for U.S. transmission projects — a move that, read alongside a parallel push for higher regulated returns, points to a sharpening fight between major infrastructure operators and their regulator over the economics of grid investment.

The filing, which surfaced in early June, targets aspects of how FERC awards future transmission contracts. The core concern, according to analysis of the protest, is that the rules as structured could compress the return profile on transmission assets — making large-scale capital deployment harder to justify against the company's cost of capital.

What the rules say — and why operators object

The immediate backdrop is FERC Order 1000, the 2011 mandate that required competitive solicitation for regional transmission projects, effectively ending the automatic "right of first refusal" that incumbent utilities had long held. The rule was designed to lower costs through competition; independent analysis suggests it has succeeded on that measure, with competitive solicitations cutting project costs by roughly 21% in the Southwest Power Pool and 38% in the Midcontinent ISO.

But a coalition of transmission owners — including International Transmission Co., Ameren, Entergy, and Xcel Energy — filed a separate complaint with FERC in April, arguing that competitive processes add 16 to 20 months to project timelines. The so-called Grid Acceleration Coalition proposed either exempting high-priority projects from competitive bidding or suspending the solicitation requirement for five years. MISO, the grid operator, pushed back, telling federal regulators that utilities were overstating the delays; the Electricity Transmission Competition Coalition, representing more than 93 organisations across all 50 states, countered that limiting participation would concentrate risk among a smaller pool of incumbents and slow the pace of buildout.

National Grid's protest is directed at the same structural question: whether the competitive framework adequately prices the capital risk that transmission owners bear.

Returns under pressure on two fronts

The timing of the protest is pointed. In late April, National Grid joined other New England transmission owners in filing a request under Section 205 of the Federal Power Act, asking FERC to raise the base return on equity for New England transmission assets to 11.39% — up from the 9.57% FERC had set in a March order. State governors from Massachusetts to Rhode Island promptly urged the commission to reject the increase; Governor Maura Healey noted that transmission costs are already "one of the fastest growing components of our electricity bills."

The two filings are technically separate proceedings, but they carry the same message: a company with a £70 billion, five-year investment plan — including £17 billion committed to New York's regulated network and £12 billion to New England — needs the regulatory framework to deliver adequate returns. National Grid's shares trade at roughly £11.99, about 13% below analyst consensus of £13.77, suggesting some market scepticism about whether that framework will deliver.

The broader stakes are not abstract. Transmission is the critical bottleneck in the energy transition: renewable generation capacity is expanding faster than the wires to carry it. If the return environment deters large operators from committing capital, the gap widens.

FERC's response to the Grid Acceleration Coalition's April petition — whether it grants any suspension of solicitation requirements — will be the clearer signal of which way regulatory sentiment runs. The commission's position on the New England ROE filing, due by mid-year, will set the other half of the picture.
