Waitrose and Morrisons pledge to keep brown eggs as Sainsbury's net zero move backfires

Brown eggs have been the dominant variety on British supermarket shelves since the 1980s, when consumer perception linked shell colour with welfare — a belief unsupported by nutrition science. — Wikimedia Commons / Meddygarnet (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Sainsbury's switch to white eggs on climate grounds has handed Waitrose and Morrisons a ready-made customer-choice narrative at no cost.

Sainsbury's announced last week that it is switching its own-brand egg range from brown to white-shelled varieties, citing research showing that white eggs carry a 12.7 per cent lower carbon footprint than brown alternatives. The reasoning is straightforward enough: hens that produce white eggs are typically smaller, eat less feed, and are less prone to feather-pecking — a welfare benefit Sainsbury's folded into the same announcement. The move is framed as a contribution to the retailer's target of net zero across its own operations by 2035, extending to its supply chain by 2050.

Within days, two of its main rivals had moved to fill the gap. Waitrose and Morrisons both confirmed they would continue stocking brown eggs, reframing the decision as a matter of customer choice rather than environmental necessity. "We can achieve high welfare and reduce our environmental impact at the same time," a Waitrose spokesperson said — a pointed implicit rebuke to Sainsbury's logic. Morrisons, which had already introduced a white-egg own-brand range under the Chuckle label in 2024, said it would continue offering both colours side by side.

Consumer perception versus carbon arithmetic

Brown eggs have dominated British supermarket shelves since the 1980s, when consumers came to associate them with free-range or higher-welfare production. In fact, shell colour is determined entirely by the breed of the hen and has no bearing on nutritional content. In the United States, white eggs account for around 75 per cent of consumption; in Britain, the proportions run the other way.

The pointed irony in Sainsbury's situation is that Waitrose shares the identical 2035 net zero target — yet has declined to draw the same operational conclusion. That makes it difficult to argue the switch is a climate necessity rather than a supply-chain or cost-management decision given sustainability framing. Sainsbury's spokesperson stated that white eggs "result in lower emissions and better welfare outcomes for the hens that lay them", but the carbon differential — 12.7 per cent — is a modest number on which to stake a conspicuous product change.

The supply-side challenge is real and not short-term. According to the British Free Range Egg Producers Association, white-egg-laying hens currently account for around 15 per cent of the UK national flock. Scaling that up to serve a grocer with roughly 1,500 stores will take years and require sustained commitment from farmers who, for decades, have been incentivised by consumer demand for brown-laying breeds.

The competitive risk for Sainsbury's now runs in both directions. If brown-egg loyalists migrate to Waitrose or Morrisons, rivals will have extracted a market-share gain at zero strategic cost. If white eggs prove acceptable to shoppers and the carbon savings hold up under independent scrutiny, Sainsbury's eventually wins reputational credit — but the payoff is likely to arrive slowly and quietly, while the backlash is immediate and loud.

For those tracking ESG execution risk in consumer-facing sectors, the episode illustrates a recurring pattern: aggressive first-mover pledges can hand competitors a low-cost differentiation play until consumer preferences close the gap. Sainsbury's own-brand egg sales in the second half of 2026 will be the first hard evidence of whether that gap is as wide as rivals are betting.
