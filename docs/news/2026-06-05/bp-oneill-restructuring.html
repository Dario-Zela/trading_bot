BP splits into upstream and downstream under new CEO Meg O'Neill

BP's global headquarters at 1 St James's Square, Westminster, London — the address from which five chief executives have operated since 2020. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
BP's new CEO Meg O'Neill dismantles Bernard Looney's three-unit green-pivot structure, reverting to upstream and downstream divisions while targeting $20bn in asset sales by 2027.

Meg O'Neill took the reins at BP on April 1, 2026 — the first woman to lead one of the five major oil companies, recruited from the helm of Australian producer Woodside Energy — and gave the organisation a fortnight before announcing its most significant structural overhaul in six years. In a companywide call on April 14, she told staff that BP would dissolve its current three-unit arrangement into two clearly defined divisions: an upstream arm covering exploration and production, and a downstream arm encompassing refining, fuel distribution and retail energy. The trading business, run by deputy chief executive Carol Howle, would remain separate.

Reversing Looney's Playbook

In 2020, then-CEO Bernard Looney broke BP into three units as part of an ambitious pivot toward renewable energy — gas and low-carbon, oil production and operations, and customers and products. The strategy won applause from climate campaigners and near-immediate punishment from investors; BP's share price fell sharply in the wake of the announcement. By 2023, Looney's successors were already quietly dialling back the 2030 production-cut target from 40% to 25%. O'Neill's April decision completes that reversal, returning the company to the upstream-downstream configuration it held before 2020.

The move was not improvised. Elliott Management, the activist hedge fund that built a stake of just over 5% in BP, had been calling loudly for precisely this reorganisation for roughly a year before O'Neill arrived. She told staff the restructuring would create a "simpler, stronger, more valuable bp," and cited rebuilding trust with employees — eroded after years of leadership churn — as an explicit priority. BP has had five chief executives since 2020.

The specific division heads have not yet been named, and the company set no implementation timeline. Emma Delaney, who ran the customers and products unit, has already departed, heading to OMV as its chief executive in September. The technology, legal, gas, low-carbon and human resources functions will be absorbed into the two new arms.

The Divestment Agenda

Structural simplification is one half of O'Neill's brief; asset disposal is the other. The combined target is $20 billion in divestments by the end of 2027 — a pace the company is already running at. Before she even arrived, BP had struck a deal in December 2025 to sell a 65% stake in its Castrol lubricants business to US private equity firm Stonepeak for $6 billion, valuing the entire division at $10.1 billion. BP retains a 35% stake under a two-year lock-in.

In March 2026, BP agreed to sell the Gelsenkirchen refinery — which processes about 12 million tonnes of crude per year and employs roughly 1,800 people — to the Klesch Group, with completion targeted for the second half of this year. That deal lifted BP's 2027 structural cost-reduction target to $6.5 to $7.5 billion, including approximately $1 billion in annual operating savings embedded in Gelsenkirchen's cost base — equivalent to around 30% of the company's 2023 baseline.

Separately, BP has explored a full exit from the UK North Sea. Negotiations with independent rival Ithaca Energy progressed to late stages before reportedly stalling; the business could fetch approximately £2 billion ($2.7 billion) in a full sale. BP's effective tax rate on UK offshore production — elevated to 78% by the windfall levy — makes retention increasingly difficult to justify on capital-return grounds.

The balance-sheet target is net debt in the $14 billion to $18 billion range by 2027. The dividend, pledged to grow at least 4% per share per year, paid 8.32 cents per ordinary share in the first quarter. With the stock trading around $42 on the New York Stock Exchange — well below the 52-week high of $48.27 — the yield remains among the highest of the oil majors, which partly explains why income-focused investors have not yet moved for the exits.

Governance in Flux

The restructuring announcement landed against a backdrop of unusual boardroom turbulence. On May 26, BP's board ousted Albert Manifold — who had only become chair in October 2025, succeeding Helge Lund after less than eight months — over what it described as "serious concerns related to important governance standards, oversight and conduct." The board appointed Ian Tyler, a former chief of Balfour Beatty who joined BP's board last year, as interim chair. Manifold, previously CEO of Irish building materials group CRH, disputed the characterisation publicly, saying he would "not allow a false narrative to go unchallenged." A formal search for a permanent chair is underway.

The episode adds procedural noise to an already complicated turnaround. O'Neill is simultaneously rebuilding internal trust, projecting external stability, prosecuting a $20 billion divestment programme, redesigning the corporate structure, and overseeing a management reshuffle — all with an activist investor watching from a 5% position and the structural detail of who runs which division still unanswered.

What to Watch

For shareholders, the critical question is sequencing. The cost-savings targets are concrete — $6.5 to $7.5 billion by 2027 against a 2023 baseline — but the new divisional structure has no announced implementation date and no named division chiefs. Markets will be looking for both at the next strategy update.

The North Sea remains a live variable. If BP completes a sale to Ithaca Energy or another buyer, it would be the most symbolically significant exit from a founding asset in the company's history. How that transaction is framed — disciplined portfolio management or retreat — will shape the narrative around O'Neill's tenure.

The broader ESG tension runs underneath all of it. Reversing the green pivot has kept income-focused shareholders from selling, but it has complicated BP's relationship with ESG-conscious institutional funds that had priced in a faster transition. O'Neill has not yet articulated new decarbonisation milestones. Until she does, that cohort remains a latent source of pressure. The permanent chair appointment — expected before year-end — will be the next governance datapoint investors use to judge whether the boardroom instability is genuinely resolved.
