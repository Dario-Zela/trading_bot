Trump pegs Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac at $1tn but offers no privatisation roadmap

Fannie Mae's Colonial Revival headquarters at 3900 Wisconsin Avenue NW, Washington D.C., designed by Leon Chatelain Jr. in 1956. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump valued Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac at $1 trillion but named no privatisation mechanism, leaving both GSEs in conservatorship with no new policy pathway.

President Donald Trump stated on Thursday that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac are "probably worth" around $1 trillion, sending shares of both government-sponsored enterprises higher — FNMA gained roughly 4.94% on the day, FMCC about 4.39% — even though the remark offered no new mechanism for extracting that value.

The two mortgage giants have been in federal conservatorship since September 2008, when the Treasury injected a combined $187.5 billion to prevent their collapse during the housing crisis. Since then, successive administrations have floated the idea of releasing them, and Trump has consistently been the most vocal advocate. Thursday's $1 trillion figure is a higher end of the range than most independent analysts have put forward: earlier this year, estimates for a combined valuation ran from roughly $500 billion to $700 billion, though FHFA Director Bill Pulte said in May that "these businesses could be worth trillions one day."

A valuation without a vehicle

The problem with Trump's number is not the arithmetic — it is the missing architecture. Full privatisation of the GSEs would require Congressional legislation, a new capital framework, and resolution of the Treasury's senior preferred stock position accumulated over seventeen years of conservatorship. None of that was mentioned, and no bill is currently moving on Capitol Hill to achieve it.

An alternative route — floating a minority stake via IPO while keeping the GSEs in conservatorship — has been on the table since early 2026. The Trump administration signalled an IPO could begin as early as the second quarter of this year. That window appears to have slipped. In May, Pulte himself narrowed the ambition: "We're talking about an IPO … but we could do that and keep them in conservatorship," he told interviewers — a materially different proposition from the full privatisation that Treasury hawks and some mortgage investors have urged.

The timing is further complicated by Pulte's own recent move. On June 2 Trump appointed him acting Director of National Intelligence, a posting he is meant to hold alongside his FHFA chairmanship. Whether a figure now nominally running the intelligence community retains bandwidth to drive a complex capital-markets transaction is a question Wall Street has begun to ask quietly.

Market context

The GSEs together guarantee roughly 70% of US home loans. Any structural change to their ownership or government backing would reverberate through the $12 trillion agency mortgage-backed securities market — affecting the spread between Treasury yields and the 30-year fixed mortgage rate that tens of millions of Americans pay each month. KBW analysts have said the privatisation window is narrowing ahead of the November midterms, after which the legislative calendar becomes harder to clear.

Trump's $1 trillion tag is not technically implausible for two companies that between them earned $3.7 billion in first-quarter net income (Fannie alone) and have built combined net worth north of $100 billion. But a valuation is not a policy. Until the administration produces either draft legislation or a credible IPO prospectus timeline, Thursday's statement is best read as a familiar talking point restated — an opening bid that has yet to find a counterparty in Congress.
