Trump invokes Defence Production Act to rescue 13 coal plants, pledges $700m for new capacity

A coal-fired power plant in operation — the fuel's share of U.S. electricity has fallen from roughly 50 percent to 15 percent over two decades. — Rennett Stowe / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Trump invokes the Defense Production Act to direct $425m to 13 coal plants and commits $200m to build America's first new coal-fired plants since 2013.

President Donald Trump invoked the Defense Production Act on Thursday to direct $700 million in federal funds toward the coal industry — $425 million to shore up 13 struggling coal-fired plants, $200 million in Energy Department grants to build two new facilities and restart a third, and $75 million for a long-stalled export terminal in Oakland, California.

The announcement, made from the Oval Office with Interior Secretary Doug Burgum, Energy Secretary Chris Wright, and EPA Administrator Lee Zeldin in attendance, marks one of the more aggressive uses of the 1950 Cold War statute, which grants the president authority to direct domestic industries for national security purposes. Trump had first invoked it for the energy sector in April 2025; Thursday's moves name specific facilities and attach dollar amounts.

"Today, we're taking historic action to bring down the price of energy and the cost of living for all Americans with the power of clean, beautiful coal," Trump said.

Where the money goes

The $425 million in DPA funds targets existing coal plants across West Virginia, Kentucky, North Carolina, Tennessee, Arizona, Arkansas, Oklahoma, North Dakota, and Wisconsin — plants that have faced closure pressure as natural gas and renewables have undercut their economics. A separate $75 million goes to the proposed West Gateway Terminal export facility in Oakland, a project that has faced years of local regulatory opposition.

The $200 million in Energy Department grants carries the more headline-grabbing ambition: underwriting a 1.6-gigawatt coal plant at the West Virginia Energy Campus near Mount Storm, a new plant in Alaska, and the restart of the AES Warrior Run facility in Allegany County, Maryland. The West Virginia and Alaska plants would be the first new coal-fired capacity built in the United States since 2013.

Coal companies pledged an additional $1.7 billion in private construction support alongside the federal commitment, and the administration projects around 14,000 jobs across coal mining, construction, rail, and maritime sectors.

The structural headwind

The policy rationale leans heavily on AI-driven electricity demand. National Mining Association CEO Rich Nolan argued the move shields consumers from volatile energy prices as data-centre load growth strains the grid. Energy Secretary Wright framed coal alongside gas and nuclear as the irreplaceable dispatchable backbone that renewables cannot replace on their own.

The numbers tell a different story about coal's trajectory. It accounted for roughly 8 percent of U.S. primary energy consumption in 2024, down from around 50 percent of the electricity mix a generation ago; it now sits near 15 percent. Wind and solar hit a record 17 percent of U.S. generation in 2025. New coal plants built today face operating lives measured in decades against an energy landscape that is repricing toward lower marginal-cost sources.

Reaction split along predictable lines. West Virginia Coal Association President Chris Hamilton called it "the best news this industry's received since President Carter was president." The NRDC's Kit Kennedy dismissed it as "propping up coal billionaires with taxpayer money."

The DPA mechanism gives the administration a tool that sidesteps the normal congressional appropriations process, but it does not insulate new plants from power-market economics once built. Whether utilities and grid operators actually sign long-term offtake contracts for new coal capacity — at costs that independent analysts already put above gas and renewables — is the question that will determine whether Thursday's pledge translates into operational megawatts. The Energy Department's grant terms, expected to be detailed in coming weeks, will be the first real test of that.
