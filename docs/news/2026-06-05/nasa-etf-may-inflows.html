Space economy ETFs drew $2.1bn in May as investors chase the SpaceX halo

In one sentence
Tema's NASA ETF drew $2.11bn in May inflows—third among thematic funds—but the SpaceX "halo" rests on a 6% SPV stake, leaving 93% of the portfolio to justify the enthusiasm.

Investors poured $2.11 billion into the Tema Space Innovators ETF in May, making the fund — trading under the ticker NASA on NYSE Arca — the third-biggest winner among thematic funds last month and pushing its assets under management to roughly $2.7 billion by month-end. The fund launched on March 31 with $1 million in seed capital; it crossed $1 billion in 37 trading days, a pace outrun in the thematic category only once before.

The money is flowing on a single story: SpaceX. Tema's fund offers one of the few routes for retail investors to gain indirect exposure to the private space company, via a special purpose vehicle that represented about 6.4% of portfolio assets as of early June. SpaceX itself — targeting an IPO priced around June 11 at a $1.75 trillion valuation, which would make it the largest public offering in history — remains inaccessible through conventional share purchases. The fund's SPV stake held 1.35 million common-share equivalents valued at $171 million, implying a SpaceX market capitalisation of approximately $1.51 trillion.

The remaining 93 percent of the portfolio is publicly listed space-economy companies: satellite operators, launch providers, defence electronics firms and adjacent names. Whether that slice attracted $2.11 billion on its own merits, or whether investors are effectively paying a premium to queue for pre-IPO exposure, is not answered by the flow data alone.

The flow picture

NASA was not May's hottest thematic bet. That title went to the Roundhill Memory ETF — a DRAM-focused product — at $7.95 billion in net inflows, followed by the iShares International Country Rotation Active ETF at $3.06 billion. NASA came third, just ahead of the iShares Expanded Tech-Software Sector ETF at $1.88 billion and the iShares Semiconductor ETF at $1.72 billion. The gap between DRAM and NASA is not trivial: memory attracted nearly four times as much capital last month.

Total U.S.-listed ETF inflows hit $199.4 billion in May, so NASA's $2.11 billion is roughly one percent of a historically broad intake. The context is suggestive: May coincided with public confirmation that SpaceX had confidentially filed with the SEC and intended to begin its investor roadshow on June 8. Timing is circumstantial, not causal, but the correlation is hard to discount.

Tema chief executive Maurits Pot has described the space economy as "still in early innings" — a phrase that captures both the thesis and the uncertainty. Commercial space does encompass strategically critical infrastructure: communications constellations, navigation systems, launch services. A legitimate fundamental case exists. What the inflow data cannot tell you is how much of May's $2.11 billion was made on that case, and how much was made on the assumption that a $1.75 trillion IPO would lift everything attached to it.

Investors will get a partial answer when SpaceX debutes on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, expected June 12. If the stock holds its issue price and the non-SpaceX names in the portfolio trade in sympathy, the argument for durable flows becomes credible. If the IPO generates selling pressure and the portfolio's publicly listed names drift, May may prove to have been a momentum peak. The roadshow opens June 8.
