Burnham and Makerfield: Reform UK tests Labour's heartland

Andy Burnham, newly elected MP for Makerfield, is now eligible under Labour party rules to mount a leadership challenge against Keir Starmer — UK Government / Open Government Licence
In one sentence
Reform holds 34.5% in a Labour stronghold even against Burnham's star power, signalling constrained policy space for a government now fighting on two fronts.

The result was not in doubt. Makerfield — a Greater Manchester constituency that has returned Labour MPs without interruption since its creation in 1983 — was always going to elect Andy Burnham. What the count, finished in the small hours of Friday in Wigan's civic hall, actually revealed was something else: a Conservative near-erasure, a Reform floor that refused to shift, and a political arithmetic that has just delivered Labour's most credible internal challenger a seat in the House of Commons.

Burnham, until Thursday the mayor of Greater Manchester, took 54.8% and a majority of 9,231. Reform UK's Robert Kenyon — a local plumber elected to Wigan Council in the May elections, whose campaign was dogged by resurfaced posts containing Covid conspiracies and sexualised comments about female rugby players — still polled 34.5%. That is two and a half points above Reform's 31.8% share in Makerfield at the 2024 general election, despite Burnham's formidable personal popularity. The Conservatives managed 2.2%.

One number worth filing: Restore Britain, the breakaway movement led by expelled Reform MP Rupert Lowe, pulled 6.8%. The combined non-Labour, non-Conservative vote was north of 41%. That Reform held its 2024 baseline rather than absorbing the splinter group is arguably the evening's most telling signal.

The numbers that matter beyond Wigan

A by-election engineered by the unusual step of a sitting Labour MP vacating his own safe seat to clear the path for his mayor is a thin base for sweeping conclusions. Labour won comfortably. But the conditions that produced this result carry information for anyone tracking UK political risk.

Reform's 34-35% in Labour heartland territory is no longer polling: it is demonstrated voting behaviour. May's local elections — in which Reform gained 1,257 council seats while Labour lost 1,121 — showed the party could convert poll ratings into ballot results at the local level. Makerfield suggests it can hold that floor in Westminster contests too, even against the most formidable opponent Labour could put forward. A government perpetually managing a 35% insurgency in its own industrial north does not have the political bandwidth for ambitious regulatory change or significant fiscal flexibility.

The second squeeze is internal. Burnham enters Parliament now eligible, under Labour party rules, to challenge Keir Starmer for the leadership. Ninety-seven Labour MPs had publicly called for Starmer to announce a departure timetable before Thursday's vote. Gilts are untroubled this morning — the result was well flagged and the seat was never at risk. The catalyst to watch is whether that backbench figure shifts in the coming days, and what a Burnham-led Labour — whose instincts run toward public investment and industrial policy rather than fiscal restraint — would signal on the policy questions traders are actually following.
