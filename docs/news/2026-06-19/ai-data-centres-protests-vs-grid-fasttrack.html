AI data centres pull in opposite directions: conservative protesters mobilise as regulators fast-track grid access

Aerial view of a large-scale US data centre campus — the type of facility drawing opposition over land use, water consumption, and grid strain — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Tea Party veterans and green groups converge against AI data centres on the same day FERC ordered faster grid access — political headwinds meet regulatory acceleration.

On Thursday, federal energy regulators voted unanimously to expedite the connections that allow AI data centres to draw industrial-scale power from the national grid. The same day, a conservative grassroots network announced a nationwide series of rallies aimed at stopping those data centres from being built in the first place.

The juxtaposition is more than coincidence. It maps the emerging fault lines around one of the most capital-intensive infrastructure buildouts in a generation — and signals that the political terrain is shifting in ways the sector may not have fully priced in.

A coalition from unexpected corners

The protest campaign is the work of Humans First, a conservative organisation whose chair, Amy Kremer, is best known as one of the original architects of the Tea Party movement in 2009. Kremer is framing the July 18 "Nationwide Day of Protest" in explicitly populist terms: Washington politicians, she said, are "doing the bidding of big tech and big AI billionaires" at the expense of communities that have to live next to the facilities. Rallies are confirmed for at least 13 locations across Georgia, California, Texas, Florida and Virginia.

What makes the mobilisation politically notable is its ideological breadth. On the specific objections — land consumption, water draw, grid strain — Humans First has arrived at positions long advanced by environmental groups. The convergence is not a formal coalition so much as a collision of independent grievances that happen to land on identical targets.

The grievances are concrete. A hyperscale data centre — the 100-megawatt-plus class now standard in AI infrastructure — typically occupies 10 or more acres and can withdraw between one million and five million gallons of water per day, equivalent to the daily consumption of a small city. Google's facility in Council Bluffs, Iowa, withdrew an average of 3.9 million gallons daily. US data centres consumed an estimated 17 billion gallons of water in 2023; industry projections put the figure between 38 and 73 billion gallons annually by 2028 as AI workloads scale.

Electricity is the third pressure point. Data centres currently account for roughly 5 percent of US electricity demand. Federal and industry projections indicate that figure could triple by 2035 as training and inference workloads for large AI models expand. With more than 4,000 data centres already operating in the US and 3,000 more planned or under construction, the demand curve is not theoretical.

The pushback has already produced tangible results. More than 70 data centre applications were rejected or restricted in the first four months of 2026. Virginia — the country's largest data centre market — now hosts 42 activist groups engaged in opposition campaigns, including the Data Center Reform Coalition, which coordinates homeowner associations, environmental advocates and conservation groups.

FERC moves the other way

While Kremer's network was gearing up for the streets, regulators in Washington were moving in the opposite direction. On June 18, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission voted five-to-zero to issue tailored orders to the six regional grid operators managing roughly two-thirds of the US transmission system: PJM Interconnection, MISO, Southwest Power Pool, the California ISO, ISO New England and the New York ISO — operators collectively serving around 200 million Americans.

The orders require each operator to submit reliability reports within 30 days detailing how they will ensure adequate generation capacity for large loads, and to justify or reform interconnection tariffs within 60 days. Loads above 20 megawatts — covering virtually all AI-scale proposals — are explicitly in scope. Data centres that require transmission infrastructure upgrades will bear the full cost, a provision aimed at insulating residential ratepayers.

The action is the culmination of months of White House pressure. Energy Secretary Chris Wright directed FERC in October 2025 to prioritise interconnection reform, framing faster timelines as a competitive necessity relative to China. FERC Chair Laura Swett called Thursday's vote "historic," adding that "Americans across the country are concerned about affordability, and so are we" — a line calibrated to address the same cost anxieties the protest coalition has tapped into, from the other side.

The pre-existing interconnection process for large-scale users typically ran to five years or more from application to commercial operation. The new 30- and 60-day compliance windows do not compress that timeline directly, but they strip away procedural latitude that grid operators have used to defer or slow-walk decisions. States retain control of retail electric rates and renewable energy standards — a safeguard commissioners were careful to reaffirm — though utilities and state commissions have privately worried that federal pressure will erode their authority to manage where and how demand connects to their networks.

What ties the threads together

The administration's posture is premised on speed — on the view that infrastructure constraints are the primary risk to US AI competitiveness, and that regulatory friction is a problem to be cleared rather than a signal to be heeded. The emerging protest coalition is premised on the opposite: that communities absorbing the physical costs of data centres — the aquifer draws, the grid upgrades, the industrial noise — have a legitimate claim on the process that current approval frameworks largely ignore.

What changes the political calculus is the cross-party composition of the opposition. Conservative resistance to large-scale industrial siting is not new; localism does not require an ideological home. But an organised national campaign, with Tea Party infrastructure behind it and a firm protest date already set across 13 locations in five states, represents something more durable than localised friction. If it holds together, it creates complications for legislators in Republican districts where data centre proposals are concentrated — and complicates the administration's narrative that AI infrastructure is an unambiguous national priority.

The next concrete markers arrive quickly: whether the six grid operators file tariff reforms before August deadlines, and whether the July 18 rallies produce the turnout that forces congressional attention. For data centre operators and their utility partners, both calendars now matter.
