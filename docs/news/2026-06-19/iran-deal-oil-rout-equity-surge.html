IRAN-HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-06-19 · 6 min read

Brent toward $75 and equities surge as Hormuz reopens; US cash dark for Juneteenth

The Strait of Hormuz separating Iran from the Musandam Peninsula, the 34-kilometre chokepoint through which roughly 20 million barrels of crude and products transited daily before the February closure. — NASA/MODIS — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Iranian barrels flooded the Strait of Hormuz under a 60-day truce, sending Brent toward $75 and equities surging globally — but the deal's hard expiry date means geopolitical risk is deferred, not gone.

The numbers tell the story cleanly. On the night of June 17–18, 12.5 million barrels of crude moved through the Strait of Hormuz without a single shot fired — the highest single-session figure since the conflict began in February. Vice President JD Vance cited the figure at a White House press briefing Thursday morning, noting that "the Iranians, for the second night in a row, did not shoot at any ships in the Strait of Hormuz" and adding: "They are honoring their end of the commitment." By early European trading, Brent had fallen to $78.66 a barrel, its weakest since early March. WTI slipped to $75.81. Markets were pricing not just the barrels that moved overnight, but the barrels that will keep moving if the deal holds.

The catalyst was the formal conclusion of a 14-point memorandum of understanding between Washington and Tehran, digitally signed Wednesday and set for an in-person ceremony Thursday in Geneva. The core of the agreement: Iran commits to 60 days of free commercial passage through the strait with no tolls or interference; the United States lifts its naval blockade on Iranian vessels; negotiations on Iran's nuclear programme, the scope of sanctions relief, and a US-backed $300 billion reconstruction commitment run on the same 60-day clock. None of those larger questions resolves inside that window. The MoU explicitly brackets them.

12.5 Million Barrels and a New Baseline

Before the war that began in February, roughly 20 million barrels a day transited the Strait of Hormuz — about a fifth of global oil demand. Thursday's overnight figure of 12.5 million represents a partial but meaningful restoration. The shipping backlog built up over four months of closure will not clear in a week; industry executives and shipping analysts have warned that full normalisation could take weeks and, for some vessel categories, months. Mine clearance in contested Gulf waters adds further uncertainty. But for a market that had priced supply disruption at historically extreme levels — Brent briefly touched $108 in late April as earlier talks unravelled — even a partial reopening registers as a regime change.

The immediate effect ran well beyond crude. Heating oil futures fell sharply. The forward curve for Brent flattened as the backwardation that had rewarded traders for holding physical barrels began to compress. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Iraq — whose own export routes had been constrained by the closure — are now expected to begin restoring millions of barrels of halted output, layering additional supply onto a market already absorbing returning Iranian volumes.

Goldman Sachs moved quickly to reprice. The bank cut its Q4 2026 Brent forecast to $80 a barrel from $90, and trimmed its 2027 average to $75 from $80, citing an accelerated timeline for Persian Gulf export normalisation. Goldman now assumes pre-war flow levels return by end-July, a month earlier than its prior model. That single-month shift in supply assumption reduces its fair-value estimate by around $10 a barrel for the fourth quarter and $5 for 2027. The bank flagged two-sided risks: on the downside, resumed hostilities or tanker strikes could keep shippers risk-averse even after the political deal holds; on the upside, if the broader nuclear talks collapse and Iran moves to close the strait again, Brent has a credible path back toward $125.

Risk-On Trades, Though Only Half the World Is Open

Equity markets outside the United States took the ceasefire and ran. Nasdaq 100 futures surged more than 2% on the news. European indices opened firmer across the board, with travel and leisure leading by a margin that recalls April's partial-ceasefire session, when pan-European travel stocks gained more than 7% in a single day and Lufthansa and easyJet each added around 10%.

In London the FTSE 100 opened higher, but the composition told the real story. Airlines, consumer staples, and industrials rose. BP and Shell fell — both carry significant Gulf exposure and higher crude had been padding their earnings assumptions, so the price reversal stings in both directions. Defence names retreated sharply: BAE Systems dropped 5% and Babcock International fell 2.2% as analysts began stripping out a geopolitical risk premium that had quietly embedded itself in order-book projections over the past decade of heightened Gulf tension.

US equity cash markets are dark. NYSE and Nasdaq observe Juneteenth on June 19 and do not reopen until Monday, June 22, at 9:30 a.m. Eastern. Futures continued to trade, and the risk-on signal was unambiguous. But US traders will walk into Monday having absorbed roughly 24 hours of European and Asian repricing without being able to respond in size. The gap-open direction is not in question — the signal points squarely risk-on — but the magnitude of conviction, and how much of Thursday's move survives the first hour of serious US cash trading, remains the outstanding variable.

The Day-61 Problem

There is a structural peculiarity in trading a truce with a known termination date. The 60-day window that started Thursday sets the clock on negotiations that were never going to conclude in 60 days: Iran's nuclear programme, the schedule for lifting both primary and secondary US sanctions, the unfreezing of Iranian assets, and the architecture of a $300 billion reconstruction fund backed by Washington, Gulf states, and European partners. The MoU creates a negotiating runway, not a resolution.

That means traders must price not just what oil is worth today, but what it is worth on day 61. If the broader talks stall — whether because Iran's Supreme Leader withholds final ratification, Israel exercises its reserved right to self-defence, or the US Senate delays legislation required to lift sanctions — Brent has a credible path back toward prior highs near $125 a barrel. The ceasefire does not dissolve the geopolitical risk premium embedded in crude markets since February; it suspends it, with the meter running.

In that reading, Thursday's price action is two trades compressed into one session. The first — sell crude, buy equities — is a direct response to supply returning to market. The second will play out more slowly, in the volatility surface of Brent options contracts expiring in mid-August, where traders will reveal how seriously they are pricing the scenario in which day 61 arrives without a permanent deal. That structure, and how it shifts as the negotiating weeks count down, is the signal to track when US cash markets reopen Monday morning.
