Rio Tinto restarts Oyu Tolgoi copper exports after community protest halt

Oyu Tolgoi's surface infrastructure in Mongolia's South Gobi Desert, where trucks haul copper concentrate to the Chinese border roughly 80 kilometres south. — Brücke-Osteuropa / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A one-day blockade by Mongolia's Radical Reform Movement halted copper shipments from Rio Tinto's Oyu Tolgoi before exports resumed Thursday, leaving the deeper revenue dispute unresolved.

Copper concentrate trucks rolled again out of Mongolia's Oyu Tolgoi mine on Thursday, a day after protesters from the Radical Reform Movement blocked the key haulage road to the Chinese border, triggering a brief but closely watched supply disruption at one of the world's largest copper deposits.

The blockade began at 9 a.m. local time on June 17, when demonstrators erected a tyre barrier bearing a "Stop Rio Tinto" banner on the road linking the South Gobi operation to the border crossing roughly 80 kilometres to the south. By the following morning, Oyu Tolgoi LLC announced via social media that "concentrate exports had returned to normal." The company offered no explanation for how the situation resolved, and Rio Tinto did not respond to media requests for comment.

The Radical Reform Movement's demands sit within a long-running debate: that Mongolia, despite hosting one of the most consequential copper discoveries of the past two decades, captures too little of the wealth flowing out of it. Its more vocal members have called for the expulsion of foreign investors altogether. The mine contributes roughly 9% of Mongolia's total tax revenues, and Oyu Tolgoi LLC estimated that a week-long blockade would cost the government $13.3 million — a figure that somewhat undercuts the protesters' leverage, given that the state is also a 34% shareholder in the project.

Prime Minister Uchral Nyam-Osor directed officials to "enforce the law and hold accountable those who unlawfully obstruct or interfere with business operations" — putting the government and the street demonstrators on opposite sides of the barricade, even if both nominally argue for Mongolian economic benefit.

The bigger fight

The street protest is the visible layer of a deeper dispute. Since March, Mongolia's government has been pressing Rio Tinto to renegotiate terms toward something closer to a 60% profit share, up from its current 34% stake held through state-owned Erdenes Mongol. A separate lawsuit alleges roughly $450 million in tax underpayments covering the 2021 and 2022 fiscal years, a case now before the courts.

For copper traders, the one-day blockade is a footnote. The structural trajectory of the mine is not. Oyu Tolgoi produced 345,000 tonnes of copper in concentrate in 2025 — a 61% year-on-year jump as the underground expansion came online — and is forecast to reach 500,000 tonnes annually by 2028, eventually ranking among the four largest copper producers on earth by output. Rio Tinto is targeting 800,000–870,000 tonnes of group-wide copper production in 2026, with Mongolia an ever-larger share of that base.

That concentration of supply in a single politically active jurisdiction is the market's real consideration. A one-day blockade moves little metal. A protracted renegotiation of the underlying investment agreement — or an adverse ruling in the tax case — is a different calculation altogether. The next concrete marker is the Mongolian government's ongoing legal proceeding, with the contract renegotiation push likely to intensify as national elections, expected in 2027, approach.
