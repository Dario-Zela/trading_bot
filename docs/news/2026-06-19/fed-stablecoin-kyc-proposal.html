Fed proposes mandatory KYC rules for payment stablecoin issuers

The Marriner S. Eccles Building, headquarters of the Federal Reserve Board of Governors, Washington DC — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The Fed and four agencies proposed bank-style KYC for payment stablecoin issuers under the GENIUS Act, with a 60-day comment period opening June 22.

The Federal Reserve and four other federal agencies on Wednesday opened a formal comment period on rules that would require payment stablecoin issuers to run bank-style customer identification programmes — a significant step in bringing crypto settlement infrastructure under the same anti-money laundering framework that governs conventional finance.

The joint proposal, issued June 18 by the Fed's Board of Governors alongside the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the FDIC, and the National Credit Union Administration, implements provisions of the GENIUS Act — the legislation enacted in July 2025 that created the first federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins. The 60-day public comment window opens when the rule is published in the Federal Register, currently scheduled for June 22.

What the rule requires

Under the proposal, "permitted payment stablecoin issuers" — the formal category created by the GENIUS Act — would have to collect customers' names, dates of birth, physical addresses, and government identification numbers before accounts open. P.O. boxes and virtual addresses are prohibited. Records must be retained for five years after account closure, and issuers must cross-reference customer data against government-maintained terrorism and sanctions lists.

Importantly, the rule applies only to direct customer relationships — the primary market. Agencies explicitly declined to extend the obligation to secondary market participants, noting that a "global" KYC requirement covering every wallet touching an issuer's smart contracts "would be nearly impossible for PPSIs to implement." Documentary verification (passports, driving licences) and non-documentary approaches (consumer database cross-checks, digital identity tools) are both permitted; no specific technical standard is mandated.

The vote was not unanimous. Five of the Fed's governors approved the proposed rulemaking. Chair Kevin Warsh — confirmed to the post in May 2026 — abstained without issuing a statement; a Fed spokesperson declined to comment on his reasoning. Governor Michael Barr voted in favour but attached a concern:

"I remain concerned, however, that the GENIUS Act regulatory framework does not do enough so far to address the risks of illicit finance"

— pointing precisely at secondary-market activity, which the proposal chose to leave out.

Who absorbs the cost

Established issuers are well positioned. Circle and Paxos have operated KYC programmes for years, and the proposed framework mirrors the customer identification standards already applied to banks and credit unions supervised by the Fed. Smaller issuers and new entrants face a steeper climb; the compliance overhead concentrates market share toward incumbents with built-out compliance infrastructure.

For algorithmic traders, the practical near-term effect depends on where they sit in the structure. Secondary-market access — through exchanges and decentralised protocols — falls outside the rule's immediate scope. But a market that consolidates around fewer, federally regulated issuers is a market with fewer liquidity alternatives if any single issuer imposes access restrictions or suffers an outage. The real test for bot-driven strategies will arrive if regulators move to close the secondary-market gap that Barr's statement explicitly flags.

The comment period closes approximately 60 days after June 22. A preliminary notice period drew roughly 450 responses from banks, exchanges, and trade groups — a sign of how closely the industry has been watching. Final rules follow comment review; the stablecoin regulatory calendar is filling up fast.
