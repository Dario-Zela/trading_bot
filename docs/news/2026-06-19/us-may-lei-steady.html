US May LEI +0.1%: economy growing but lacking conviction

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — the focal point of monetary policy debate as the LEI signals slower growth ahead. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
The Conference Board's May LEI edged up 0.1% to 99.3, driven entirely by financial components, leaving the Fed caught between supporting slowing growth and holding the line on inflation.

The Conference Board's Leading Economic Index rose 0.1% in May to 99.3 (2016=100), matching consensus forecasts and extending a narrow two-month winning streak. The headline number is technically positive — but the composition of the gain says more than the magnitude. The improvement was driven entirely by financial components: stock prices and the interest rate spread. The real-economy gauges remained soft.

"The Leading Index for the US increased slightly in May, fueled entirely by positive contributions from financial components," said Justyna Zabinska-La Monica, Senior Manager of Business Cycle Indicators at the Conference Board. On the non-financial side, only the ISM New Orders Index showed meaningful strength. Consumer expectations remained the single largest drag on the index — a warning light that financial markets alone cannot extinguish.

A cycle in neutral

Two months of narrow gains do not reverse a deteriorating trend. The LEI is down 0.3% over the six months through May, though that is a marked improvement from the 1.3% contraction recorded in the prior six-month window. Both the six-month and twelve-month growth rates remain negative, pointing to slower expansion rather than an imminent stall.

The Conference Board now projects US GDP growth of 1.8% in 2026, down from 2.1% last year. The underlying squeeze is visible in the consumer data: energy and gas costs have outpaced income growth, and household spending is shifting away from consumer goods toward business investment concentrated in AI infrastructure. That is not a demand-led acceleration; it is a reshuffling within a decelerating economy.

The Fed's uncomfortable position

For the Federal Reserve, a near-flat LEI arriving in June narrows the policy runway. The index has historically preceded major policy pivots, and the current reading neither forces the Fed's hand nor gives it cover to hold indefinitely. Keeping rates where they are risks amplifying the deceleration already visible in consumer expectations. Cutting rates, meanwhile, would ease financial conditions at a moment when the oil price collapse triggered by the US-Iran accord has already done some of that work — loosening conditions the Fed has not explicitly chosen to loosen.

The job market, per the Conference Board's assessment, is expected to stay healthy even as growth moderates. That complicates the easing argument: a Fed that moves early risks importing inflation through a weaker dollar precisely as commodity prices are reshaping the broader macro backdrop.

The July LEI release, alongside June employment data and the Fed's next policy meeting, will determine whether May's reading was the floor of a soft patch or the ceiling of a brief reprieve. For now, the verdict is plain: an economy growing slowly and without conviction is the kind of economy that makes central banking hardest.
