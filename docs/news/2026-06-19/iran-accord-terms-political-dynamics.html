Inside the US-Iran accord: sanctions lifted, blockade ending, 60 days to make it permanent

The Strait of Hormuz as captured by NASA's Terra satellite in December 2020. The chokepoint has been effectively closed to commercial shipping since early March 2026, strangling roughly 25% of global seaborne oil trade. — NASA / Terra MODIS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A 14-point US-Iran MOU halts 110 days of war and opens a 60-day window to negotiate nuclear disarmament, sanctions relief, and Lebanon stability — with the Strait of Hormuz blockade lifting within 30 days.

The fourteen-point memorandum of understanding that Washington and Tehran initialled this week does not end anything permanently — not yet. It pauses a 110-day war, removes a naval blockade that has strangled roughly a quarter of the world's seaborne oil and gas trade, and opens a 60-day negotiating window inside which two governments with a decades-long history of mutual distrust must settle nuclear disarmament, sanctions removal, and the future of Lebanon. What happens in that window will determine whether this accord becomes a durable settlement or a pause in conflict.

The fighting that brought both sides to this point began on February 28, when US and Israeli forces struck Iranian territory. Iran's response came within days: by early March, its forces had effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, the 34-mile-wide channel through which approximately 25 percent of the world's seaborne oil and 20 percent of its liquefied natural gas normally flows. Insurance on vessels attempting the transit became unavailable or prohibitively expensive. The strait, in practical terms, shut. Brent crude defied the worst expectations — partly because clandestine tanker flows continued to move Iranian barrels in volume — but stockpiles have drawn down steadily, and the reopening of the waterway is the immediate economic fact underpinning the agreement.

What the accord commits each side to

The fourteen points fall into three broad baskets: security, sanctions, and nuclear material.

On security, both parties commit to an immediate halt to military operations on all fronts. The United States undertakes to lift its naval blockade of Iranian ports within 30 days. The accord's scope explicitly extends to Lebanon, requiring stability provisions that touch the Israel-Hezbollah front — a clause Iran and Israel are already interpreting differently, and one that the Centre for Strategic and International Studies has identified as carrying "the greatest potential to derail the deal."

On sanctions, Washington has agreed in principle to terminate existing Iran sanctions and allow oil export waivers that would enable Iranian crude to re-enter global markets, contingent on implementation of the final deal. Iran's frozen sovereign assets are to be released under procedures still to be negotiated. There is a jurisdictional complication: a significant portion of those frozen funds sits not in American financial institutions but in China and Iraq, outside direct US reach, which will complicate any asset-release timeline agreed in the 60-day window.

On nuclear material, leaked MOU text indicates that Iran's existing stockpile of highly enriched uranium will be diluted in-country rather than exported. Diluted material remaining inside Iran is harder for outside parties to verify than material shipped abroad — a distinction that will feature prominently in the technical talks. The fate of Iran's enrichment capacity, and the ceiling on future enrichment activity, remains the central subject of negotiations now beginning.

Both parties intend to seek a binding UN Security Council resolution endorsing any final agreement — an unusual mechanism designed to insulate the deal from domestic political reversal on either side. That architecture introduces its own failure mode: Russia and China hold permanent vetoes, and both have commercial and strategic reasons to shape, delay, or block any resolution that does not suit their interests.

Tehran frames victory; Washington demands verification

Iran's Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei — who took office on March 8, weeks after his father's death — issued a written statement on June 18 authorizing the MOU, noting that he held reservations "in principle." The statement's political framing was pointed: the agreement came about, Khamenei wrote, because an American president had used "all kinds of leverage out of desperation." The language is calibrated for a domestic audience that has endured 110 days of war and needs the deal presented as a position of strength, not concession. That Tehran's leadership is confident enough to sell the terms publicly is itself a signal: without that confidence, the 60-day negotiations would be structurally weaker before they begin.

Both sides are already offering diverging accounts of what the MOU actually says. Iranian officials have emphasised ceasefire provisions on the Lebanese front that Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu publicly disputes, and Tehran has advanced a broader reading of asset-release timelines than American officials acknowledge. These interpretive gaps are not incidental; they reflect the domestic political constraints each capital is managing as it prepares for the harder conversations ahead.

Vice President Vance held a White House briefing on June 18 in which his sharpest language was directed not at Tehran but at Jerusalem. Israeli cabinet members who had publicly criticised the deal were told to "wake up and smell the reality" of their country's international isolation. Vance reminded them that Donald Trump remained "the only head of state in the entire world who is sympathetic to the nation of Israel at this moment," and warned against attacking "the only powerful ally left in the entire world." The rebuke signalled that the administration regards the accord as settled US policy, not a text open to revision under allied pressure.

On sanctions relief, Vance resisted the framing that Washington had given away leverage. Iran, he said, would receive the agreed rewards only after demonstrating compliance with terms still to be negotiated. "Words don't matter, ladies and gentlemen, we're about verification," Vance said. The administration has asserted it will retain visibility into how Iran sends and receives money under the framework — a claimed capability that, if credible, would limit Tehran's ability to route sanctioned flows through third-party jurisdictions.

The 60-day clock and what it needs to produce

Technical negotiations were due to begin over the weekend of June 21-22. The agenda is formidable: the ceiling on Iran's enrichment capacity, a verification protocol for uranium dilution, legal and logistical procedures for frozen-asset release, sequencing of sanctions removal, and Lebanon ceasefire terms that Washington, Tehran, and Jerusalem read differently.

The commodity market variable with the clearest near-term trigger is oil. Iranian barrels returning to global supply would represent a material increase in volume at a moment when the 60-day window provides no guarantee of a final deal. Markets will price the probability of negotiation failure or compliance gaps — either of which would pause or reverse sanctions relief — against the baseline that the blockade ends and supply normalises. Brent's trajectory over the next fortnight will be as informative as any diplomatic statement.

The first concrete indicator of whether the interim agreement is functioning is the 30-day blockade-removal deadline. If the US Navy stands down on schedule, it becomes the most credible early signal that both parties are honouring the MOU's terms. If that milestone slips, the 60-day window will close under considerably heavier pressure — and the gap between the two sides' interpretations of what was agreed will widen accordingly.
