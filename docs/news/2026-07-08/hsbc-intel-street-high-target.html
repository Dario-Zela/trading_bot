HSBC issues Street-high $200 price target on Intel

Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan, who has staked the company's turnaround on server CPUs and a foundry business built to manufacture chips for outside customers. — U.S. Department of Commerce, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC doubled its Intel price target to $200 — the Street's highest — betting server-chip growth and a newly-valued foundry business outrun a shaky consensus.

Intel shares have had a rough few weeks even as one of the loudest voices on Wall Street decided the stock has room to almost double. HSBC analyst Frank Lee raised his price target on Intel to $200, up from $100, and kept his Buy rating — the highest target on the Street by a wide margin, and roughly double where the average analyst has the stock pegged.

The timing is almost comic. Intel stock slid about 21 percent over the week bracketing the note, from around $140 on June 30 to near $110, after Bank of America warned that AI-chip valuations broadly were getting stretched. HSBC's call went the other way: rather than trimming exposure to a sector wobble, Lee argued Intel's turnaround case — built on selling more server processors and, more speculatively, renting out its factories to other chipmakers — is being underpriced even after a punishing year of stock swings.

Two bets bundled into one

The case rests on two separate businesses that Intel has spent the past two years trying to fix at the same time.

The first is straightforward: server chips. HSBC now expects Intel's server CPU shipments to grow 25 percent in 2026, up from a prior estimate of 20 percent, and 30 percent in 2027. That feeds into a data-center and AI revenue forecast of $24.1 billion for 2026 — about 4 percent above what the rest of Wall Street expects — rising to roughly $33 billion in 2027, some 20 percent above consensus. The underlying logic is that as capacity is freed up and Intel's newest manufacturing process, known as 18A, ramps up, Intel can claw back server share it lost to AMD over the past several years.

The second bet is newer and harder to underwrite: Intel Foundry, the division built to manufacture chips for other companies, the way Taiwan's TSMC does. HSBC's note marks the first time the firm has folded foundry into its sum-of-the-parts valuation of Intel, a methodological shift as much as a bullish one. Lee's reasoning is that capacity bottlenecks across the industry — everyone wanting cutting-edge manufacturing slots and not enough of them existing outside Taiwan — have made Intel's spare capacity more valuable, and that outside customers are starting to show up to use it. Apple has already signed on for some capacity; HSBC points to active discussions with Google and Nvidia as design commitments are expected to start materializing in the second half of 2026.

Intel chief executive Lip-Bu Tan, who took over in March 2025 with a mandate to cut costs and decide whether the foundry business survives in its current form, has framed the pitch in similar terms publicly: that the next wave of AI infrastructure will depend not just on chip designs but on who controls wafer manufacturing capacity and advanced packaging — areas where Intel, unusually among Western chipmakers, still owns its own factories.

The skeptics have a point too

None of this is uncontested, and it's worth being blunt about why. Intel's 18A process — the manufacturing node central to both the server CPU story and the foundry pitch — is not expected to reach profitable yields until late 2026 or 2027 at the earliest, which pushes back the timeline for the 50 percent gross margins Intel has told investors to expect from a mature foundry business. That is a multi-year execution gap between the thesis and the cash flow that would prove it.

The broader analyst community reflects that uncertainty more than it reflects HSBC's conviction. Of roughly three dozen analysts covering the stock, the consensus rating sits at Hold — nine Buys, twenty Holds, two Sells — with an average price target closer to $100, essentially where the stock traded before its latest run. Intel now changes hands at more than 70 times forward earnings, a multiple that assumes a lot of the AI-and-foundry turnaround already happens on schedule. The spread between the Street's lowest target, in the $20s, and HSBC's $200 is unusually wide for a company this size, and it says less about Intel specifically than about how divided investors are on whether legacy chipmakers can actually win a seat at the AI table.

What would settle the argument

The near-term test arrives quickly. Intel reports second-quarter earnings on July 23, and investors will be listening for two things: whether server CPU shipment trends actually support HSBC's upgraded growth numbers, and whether management offers anything concrete on foundry yields or new external customers signing up for 18A capacity. Until then, the $200 target and the $100 consensus are both essentially forecasts about the same set of numbers Intel hasn't yet had to show its work on — which is another way of saying that for now, the size of the bet says more about the bettor's patience than about Intel's fundamentals.
