Kering pulls Gucci beauty back from Coty early, hands it to L'Oréal

Kering CEO Luca de Meo, who has moved to unwind his predecessor's in-house beauty ambitions and hand Gucci's fragrance business to L'Oréal. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Coty will hand the Gucci Beauty license back to Kering a year early for $400 million, clearing the way for L'Oréal's 50-year licensing deal to start in mid-2027.

Kering has decided it does not want to wait until 2028 to find out whether L'Oréal can do more with Gucci's perfume counter than Coty has managed. On July 7, the three companies said Coty will hand the Gucci Beauty license back to its parent a year early, for roughly $400 million, clearing the runway for L'Oréal — the world's largest cosmetics group — to take over under a 50-year exclusive deal starting in mid-2027.

The mechanics are straightforward. Coty gets $250 million in cash this year and up to $150 million more by September 2027, part of it contingent on transition terms, in exchange for redeeming a license that was contractually due to run through June 30, 2028. L'Oréal is picking up roughly 70% of that redemption bill itself, paid to Kering, as the price of moving its own clock forward by a year. Kering CEO Luca de Meo called it an agreement that "creates value for Gucci, L'Oréal and Coty alike" — the sort of line that papers over what was, until recently, an open legal fight.

From lawsuit to settlement

That fight is worth dwelling on, because it explains why "ahead of schedule" undersells what happened. When Kering and L'Oréal first announced their beauty tie-up in October 2025 — a €4 billion agreement in which L'Oréal bought the fragrance house Creed outright and took 50-year licenses on Bottega Veneta and Balenciaga, with Gucci earmarked to follow once Coty's contract lapsed — a Coty subsidiary responded by suing Kering and Gucci in a UK commercial court. Its argument, filed that November: discussing Gucci's post-2028 future with a third party while Coty still held the license undermined the rights it was paying for. Kering called the claims "unfounded" and said it would defend itself vigorously. Then-CEO Sue Nabi said only that Coty would "defend its rights until the last day of the contract.Shin"

Nabi did not see the dispute through. She exited at the end of 2025, replaced on an interim basis by Markus Strobel, a three-decade Procter & Gamble veteran, as Coty pushed through a Citi-led strategic review of its debt and portfolio. This week's settlement — dressed up as a mutual-value agreement rather than a resolution to litigation — reads as the practical end of that case. Coty gets cash it badly needs; Kering and L'Oréal get their license a year sooner than the lawsuit might otherwise have allowed.

Why Coty is the one selling

The $400 million lands at a useful moment for Coty, whose shares have lost more than 80% of their value since the start of 2024 on weak mass-market demand and a leadership shuffle. The Gucci license had been one of the more valuable pieces of the portfolio Coty built after buying Procter & Gamble's beauty brands in 2016 — brand revenue reportedly grew more than 60% since 2019, and the business has generated on the order of $550-600 million in annual retail sales. Losing it early is a real earnings hit: TD Securities has estimated the eventual license expiration could cut Coty's annual earnings per share by anywhere from 4% to 19%, depending on how much of the gap gets replaced.

Coty shares ticked up modestly on the announcement, a sign investors valued the cash — earmarked for debt paydown alongside proceeds from Coty's recent sale of its remaining Wella stake to KKR — more than they valued keeping the license itself. That is a telling reaction for a company whose stock has been in near-continuous decline: the market's verdict is that certainty and a balance-sheet repair beat a fight over a shrinking claim to Gucci's fragrance counter.

Kering's bigger unwind

For Kering, the Gucci settlement is one piece of a larger retreat from an ambition that predates Luca de Meo's arrival as CEO in 2025. His predecessor, François-Henri Pinault, had set up an in-house beauty division, Kering Beauté, to diversify the group beyond apparel and leather goods. It never got there: the division was still lossmaking through the first half of 2025, even as Gucci's core fashion revenue fell 26% over the same period and Kering's net debt swelled to roughly €9.5 billion. De Meo's answer was to sell the whole beauty ambition to a specialist. Kering shares jumped roughly 5% on the original October announcement, and the strategic logic looks consistent now: hand fragrance and cosmetics to the company built to run them at scale, and put the proceeds and management attention back into fixing Gucci's actual runway business.

What happens next depends on regulatory sign-off for the L'Oréal license, expected to clear in time for the mid-2027 handover, and on whether L'Oréal's distribution muscle can arrest — or at least stabilize — a Gucci beauty business that has been treated all year as a asset in transition rather than a growth story. Coty, for its part, has eighteen months left as Gucci's operator and a fresh $400 million to show for giving up the last one early.
