ExxonMobil deepens Nigeria deepwater bet alongside Shell

The Bonga FPSO vessel, central to Shell's Nigerian deepwater operations, during fitting-out at Wallsend, UK, in 2003. — Vin Mullen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
ExxonMobil is committing fresh billions to Nigerian deepwater oil alongside Shell, betting African crude demand outlasts Western climate pledges.

ExxonMobil is putting fresh money behind Nigeria's deepwater oil fields at a moment when much of the Western energy industry is talking about winding fossil fuels down, not building them up. The U.S. major is advancing plans for as much as $24 billion in offshore projects — a wager that runs directly against the grain of climate-transition narratives dominating boardrooms in London and New York.

The centrepiece is Owowo, a deepwater project estimated to cost $7 billion to $8 billion, where ExxonMobil holds a 27% stake and operatorship. Partners include Chevron Nigeria (27%), TotalEnergies (18%), Nexen Petroleum Deepwater Nigeria (18%) and the state-owned Nigerian National Petroleum Company (10%). Hunter Farris, senior vice president of ExxonMobil's deepwater upstream business, told Nigerian regulators in April that a final investment decision could come as early as 2027. Owowo alone is expected to add 100,000 barrels of oil per day and 100 million cubic feet of gas daily once developed.

Alongside it, ExxonMobil has secured a 17-year extension to its production-sharing contract on the Erha field, pushing the deal out to 2042, and is pouring money into life-extension work on the Erha floating production vessel. A parallel infill campaign at Erha and the nearby Usan field is projected to unlock another 60,000 bpd.

Shell's parallel bet

ExxonMobil is not moving alone. Shell has already taken a final investment decision on Bonga North, a $5 billion-plus project expected to produce 110,000 bpd by the end of the decade, tied back to its existing Bonga floating platform. It is also pushing toward a decision on Bonga South West, a prospect Nigerian officials value at up to $20 billion, after Abuja approved targeted fiscal incentives in January to unstick a project that had stalled for years. Shell chief executive Wael Sawan has said a final call on that field is likely by 2027. Partners on Bonga South West include ExxonMobil, TotalEnergies, Eni and NNPC — meaning the two American and Anglo-Dutch majors are, in places, co-investors as well as rivals.

Why Nigeria, why now

The shift follows years in which Western majors were doing the opposite in Nigeria — selling off onshore and shallow-water fields plagued by pipeline theft and community unrest to local firms such as Seplat and Renaissance. Exxon's onshore assets went to Seplat; Shell's went to Renaissance; Eni's to Oando. That divestment freed the majors to concentrate on deepwater blocks, where oil theft is harder and fields are large enough to justify multibillion-dollar single investments.

Abuja has also rewritten the rules to keep that capital coming. The 2021 Petroleum Industry Act gave investors a more predictable fiscal regime after a decade of legislative limbo, and President Bola Tinubu's government followed it with an executive order streamlining approvals and a cost-efficiency incentive order offering tax credits of up to 20% for upstream projects. Nigerian officials say the reforms have already helped attract more than $8 billion in new final investment decisions and $10 billion in capital inflows since 2023, with 28 new field development plans worth $18.2 billion signed in 2025 alone. Rig counts have climbed from eight in 2021 to 69 by late last year. Nigeria also became the first OPEC member to join the International Energy Agency, giving it access to the IEA's demand and market data — a symbolically pointed move for a producer betting its economy on oil holding up.

The bigger picture

The scale of the commitments — Exxon's potential $24 billion, Shell's roughly $20 billion at Bonga South West alone — sits awkwardly next to the emissions pledges both companies have made. But it reflects a calculation that has become more common among oil majors this year: that African and Asian demand growth will outpace the retreat in Europe and North America for long enough to justify decades-long field developments, and that deepwater Nigeria, insulated from the onshore security problems that drove the majors out a few years ago, offers rare scale at a moment when new large discoveries are increasingly hard to find.

The next marker to watch is Exxon's Owowo final investment decision, expected in early 2027, alongside Shell's parallel call on Bonga South West. A new upstream licensing round that Nigeria has flagged for the third quarter of this year will show whether the reforms are pulling in additional bidders beyond the incumbents already committed.
