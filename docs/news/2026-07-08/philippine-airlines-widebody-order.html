Philippine Airlines eyes 20-jet split order between Boeing and Airbus

A Philippine Airlines Airbus A350-900 arrives at Toronto Pearson International Airport. — B. Bukowski / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Philippine Airlines is weighing a roughly even 10-Boeing-787/10-Airbus-A350 order, possibly unveiled at Farnborough starting July 20.

Philippine Airlines is moving toward one of the more evenly balanced widebody orders the industry has seen in years — a split contract for roughly 20 aircraft that would hand Boeing and Airbus about 10 jets apiece, according to people familiar with the discussions cited by Bloomberg. The carrier is weighing 10 Boeing 787 Dreamliners against 10 Airbus A350s, with an announcement possible at the Farnborough International Airshow, which runs July 20-24 in Hampshire, England.

Nothing is signed. PAL chief operating officer Carlu Fernandez has said the airline has yet to finalize its plans, and aircraft orders of this size routinely shift in mix and count until the ink is down. But the shape of the deal, if it holds, would end a long dry spell for Boeing at the Philippine flag carrier — its first direct order from PAL since 2007 — while extending Airbus's run as the airline's default widebody supplier.

Why now

The trigger is fleet age, not ambition for its own sake. PAL's 11 Airbus A330-300s and 10 Boeing 777-300ERs both average roughly 11.7 years old, putting them within striking distance of the point where airlines typically start locking in replacements to avoid a maintenance and leasing squeeze. President Richard Nuttall told the IATA annual general meeting in June that the priority is "concentrating more on increasing the size of our aircraft and operating more intercontinental flights" — a nod to capacity limits at Manila's Ninoy Aquino International Airport, which is also why PAL has already ruled out the larger Boeing 777X.

The airline isn't starting from zero on the Airbus side. PAL already flies two A350-900s and has nine A350-1000s on order, five of which are due for delivery this year and the rest in 2027, alongside purchase rights for three more. A split order would effectively double down on that family rather than pivot away from it — while also reopening a Boeing relationship the airline shelved nearly two decades ago in favor of Airbus narrowbodies and, more recently, A350 widebodies.

What an even split signals

A 10-and-10 order is a notably different signal than a clean sweep for one manufacturer. It suggests PAL sees distinct roles for each jet rather than a single best answer — plausible given the 787 and A350 sit at different sizes and range points, and airlines increasingly like the flexibility of running mixed widebody fleets rather than concentrating spares, training and maintenance risk on one type. For Boeing and Airbus, both chasing order books in a region where Asia-Pacific carriers are steadily replacing pandemic-era fleets, a split deal reads as neither a loss nor an outright win — but as confirmation that competition for mid-tier Asian carriers remains genuinely contested rather than settled in either manufacturer's favor.

The financial stakes are real even at PAL's scale. The carrier has been managing elevated fuel costs by trimming capacity roughly 15% and deferring about $100 million in capital spending, alongside refurbishing existing widebodies at an estimated $15 million per aircraft. Committing to 20 new jets — likely a multibillion-dollar list-price commitment before the customary discounts — marks a shift from deferral back toward expansion.

What happens next is straightforward: Farnborough opens July 20, and any formal signing or letter of intent would land during the show's trade days. Absent an announcement there, the working assumption in the market should be that the mix, or the timeline, has moved again.
