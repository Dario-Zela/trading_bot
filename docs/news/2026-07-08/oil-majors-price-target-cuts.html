Analysts trim targets across Exxon, BP and TotalEnergies

An ExxonMobil filling station in Hiawassee, Georgia. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
TD Cowen and JPMorgan trimmed targets on Exxon, TotalEnergies and BP for a crude dip that had mostly reversed by the time a Hormuz tanker attack hit.

The math looked simple enough: three oil majors, three trimmed price targets, all inside a single week. TD Cowen took $17 off its Exxon Mobil target and $4 off TotalEnergies. JPMorgan took £50 off BP. Read as isolated notes, each looks like a minor recalibration. Read together, they tell a sharper story — analysts were marking down the energy sector for a crude-price dip that had already started reversing by the time the ink dried.

The cuts, itemized

TD Cowen analyst Jason Gabelman lowered Exxon Mobil's (NYSE: XOM) price target to $155 from $172 on July 2, a 9.9% reduction, while keeping the stock rated Buy. The new target still implies roughly 13% upside from where shares were trading in the high $130s. TD Cowen made the same move on TotalEnergies (NYSE: TTE), cutting its target to $102 from $106 — a smaller trim, and one that still leaves TotalEnergies as the firm's self-described "Top Pick" among integrated oil companies, with projected production growth near 3% annually through 2030 and free cash flow the firm expects to reach $20 billion this year.

Gabelman's stated reason for both cuts was the same: a "rapid correction" in crude prices and energy equities following the U.S.–Iran memorandum of understanding signed in mid-June, which had briefly cooled the war premium built into oil markets. TD Cowen described the pullback as creating "pockets of opportunity" and said it now prefers Shell, Chevron and TotalEnergies over Exxon heading into second-quarter earnings — a preference-ranking footnote that matters more than the headline number, since it puts Exxon at the back of TD Cowen's own energy queue even as the firm keeps its Buy rating intact.

Across the Atlantic, JPMorgan cut its BP (LON: BP) target to 550 pence from 600 pence on July 3, holding a Neutral rating. That is a smaller percentage cut than TD Cowen's Exxon move, and JPMorgan's own number still implies more than 14% upside. Wall Street as a whole is considerably more bullish: the average analyst target on BP implies 34.5% upside, among the widest consensus-to-price gaps in the sector, alongside TotalEnergies' 32.15% gap. JPMorgan's timing, ahead of BP's August 5 second-quarter results, pointed to seasonal maintenance and Middle East-linked supply disruption weighing on the quarter's upstream output — a caveat that would look prescient, then almost immediately dated.

The dividend counter-argument

What makes the Exxon cut the most interesting of the three is the company it keeps on other lists. Exxon carries a 3.01% dividend yield backed by 43 consecutive years of per-share dividend growth, and Insider Monkey's survey of hedge fund holdings places it among the 14 best blue-chip dividend stocks favored by institutional money. That is not a contradiction of TD Cowen's call — a lower price target and a reliable payout are different axes of the same stock — but it does illustrate a split in how capital treats Exxon right now. Analysts trimming near-term upside on commodity-price grounds are one signal; income-focused funds anchoring positions to a four-decade dividend streak are another. Both can be correct at once, which is exactly why the stock keeps showing up on opposing types of lists in the same week.

Oil didn't stay down

The irony is that the crude correction TD Cowen cited as its rationale had largely unwound before most investors read the note. Brent and WTI slid through mid-to-late June on the strength of the U.S.-Iran memorandum, but that calm broke on July 7, when projectiles struck a crude tanker and an LNG carrier near the Strait of Hormuz. Washington responded by revoking a sanctions waiver that had allowed Iranian oil sales to continue through August 21, moving the wind-down date up to July 17. WTI jumped more than 5% to above $72 a barrel; Brent rose 5.3% to above $75. Exxon shares themselves moved up 3.25% that day, tracking the reversal.

None of the three banks has yet revised targets back upward in response, and a single week of tanker-attack volatility is not grounds to reverse a model built on quarterly cash-flow assumptions. But it underscores the fragility of pinning integrated-oil price targets to a single geopolitical data point in a region that has now produced two opposing shocks — de-escalation, then attack — inside a month.

What ties it together

Set side by side, the three notes describe less a bearish turn on energy and more a sector still being priced off a moving geopolitical baseline, with dividend-oriented capital providing a floor that near-term price targets don't fully capture. BP reports second-quarter results on August 5, the first hard test of JPMorgan's maintenance and supply-disruption thesis. Exxon and TotalEnergies typically report in the final week of July, which will show whether TD Cowen's preference for Shell, Chevron and TotalEnergies over Exxon survives a quarter that opened with oil prices moving the opposite direction from what the price-target cuts assumed.
