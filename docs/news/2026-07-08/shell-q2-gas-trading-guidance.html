Shell shares rise as strong gas trading offsets a flat oil desk

Shell's headquarters, the Shell Centre, on London's South Bank. — Nvk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's gas desk turned Qatar's outages into a trading windfall; its oil desk merely matched an already strong quarter — a subtler signal on demand.

Shell's shares climbed as much as 2.7% on July 7, touching 3,002.74p in London, after the company told investors its trading desks had turned in another strong quarter — but the two halves of that story point in different directions. Gas trading swung sharply higher despite a war-battered Qatar operation. Oil trading merely held its ground, matching a first quarter that was already unusually strong.

The update was a pre-results trading statement, the kind of guidance note oil majors issue ahead of full earnings to manage expectations. Shell's complete second-quarter figures are due July 30. What the market got on Monday was enough to move the stock: confirmation that the trading engine which has propped up Shell's earnings all year was still running hot in the gas market, even as its physical Qatari assets sat idle.

The Qatar disruption

The reason integrated gas production is expected to fall so sharply — to a range of 610,000 to 650,000 barrels of oil equivalent a day, down from 909,000 boe/d in the first quarter — traces back to March, when an attack on Ras Laffan Industrial City knocked out one of the two processing trains at Shell's Pearl gas-to-liquids plant. Pearl normally produces around 140,000 boe/d; Shell has said full repair of the damaged train could take roughly a year. QatarEnergy separately declared force majeure on LNG production on March 4, a disruption Shell estimates will cost its 30% stake in QatarEnergy LNG about 2.4 million tonnes of output annually.

That is the kind of hit that would normally show up cleanly in a quarter's numbers. Instead, Shell told investors its Integrated Gas Trading & Optimisation result would be "significantly higher" than the first quarter — enough that it nudged the top end of its LNG liquefaction guidance up, to 7.4–7.8 million tonnes from a prior 6.8–7.4 million tonnes. The mechanism is familiar to anyone who has watched trading desks perform during supply shocks: volatile, dislocated prices across regional gas markets create arbitrage opportunities that a scaled trading book can exploit, even while the underlying production asset is offline. The same Middle East conflict that idled Pearl — the broader confrontation between Israel, the United States and Iran that has periodically closed shipping lanes near the Strait of Hormuz — is what generated the price swings Shell's traders profited from.

A flat oil desk

The oil side of the ledger tells a quieter story. Shell said trading and optimisation results in its Chemicals & Products division — which houses the bulk of its oil trading and supply business — would come in "in line with" the first quarter. On its face that sounds like stability. But the first quarter was itself an outsized result, lifted by the same Iran-driven volatility that pushed Shell's adjusted earnings to $6.9 billion and beat consensus estimates. Matching that bar, rather than clearing it, means the desk found no fresh edge in oil markets even as chemicals and refining margins moved firmly in Shell's favour: the indicative refining margin is tracking near $20 a barrel, up from $17 in the first quarter, and the indicative chemicals margin has jumped to roughly $240 a tonne from $139.

Upstream and downstream volumes, meanwhile, were essentially unchanged — upstream production guided at 1.75–1.85 million boe/d against 1.843 million in the first quarter, marketing sales at 2.55–2.65 million barrels a day against 2.627 million. Nothing in the physical oil business moved much. If oil traders aren't finding new margin even with refining spreads widening, it raises a fair question for a trading-focused audience: is that a sign crude demand isn't giving traders much to work with, even as gas markets remain unsettled enough to keep generating profit?

What the market is pricing

Investors, for now, appear to be reading the update as a net positive — the gas trading upside dominated the reaction, and the stock's rise suggests the market is more interested in the offset than the demand signal buried inside it. Some analysts see room for the shares to run further: a valuation model from Simply Wall St pegs fair value at roughly £35.51 against Monday's £30.11 close, a 15% gap it attributes largely to Shell's LNG optimisation business and disciplined capital spending, with the stock trading at a price-to-earnings ratio of about 12.2 times. That thesis leans heavily on the same trading arbitrage that just bailed out a battered Qatar operation — a dependency worth watching if gas-market volatility fades before Pearl's second train is repaired.

The next real test comes July 30, when Shell publishes full second-quarter results and, likely, more detail on how the oil trading desk actually performed rather than simply matched. Two things are worth tracking between now and then: whether Qatari output shows any sign of a faster-than-guided recovery, and whether crude trading margins pick up as the Iran-related risk premium in oil prices settles — or stays flat, which would say more about underlying demand than any single quarter's guidance note.
