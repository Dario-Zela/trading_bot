Jefferies joins Barclays in backing a major gold miner

Gold bullion bars — the metal's price has slid from January highs near $5,600 to around $4,143 an ounce, dragging mining stocks down with it. — Stevebidmead / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Jefferies and Barclays both back Agnico Eagle even as gold's slide below $4,000 drags down miners broadly, betting the sell-off mispriced a low-cost producer.

Two Wall Street banks looked at the same beaten-down gold miner this week and reached the same conclusion: the market has overreacted.

On Monday, Jefferies upgraded Agnico Eagle Mines (NYSE: AEM) to Buy from Hold, lifting its price target to $200 from $187. Barclays, which had initiated coverage on the stock in May at Overweight with a $213 target, trimmed that target slightly to $210 the same day — but kept its bullish rating intact. Both banks are, in effect, telling clients the same thing: this is a good business caught in a bad tape.

The tape has been bad. Spot gold broke below $4,000 an ounce in late June for the first time since November 2025, a sharp comedown from highs near $5,600 in January. By Monday it was changing hands around $4,143. Agnico Eagle's shares have fallen with it — down roughly 3% on the week alone, and off double digits from their highs — even though little has changed about the company's underlying operations.

The case for a mispriced miner

That's precisely the point Jefferies analyst Fahad Tariq made in his note. "Recent share price weakness [is] creating an attractive entry point for the senior gold producer," he wrote, pointing to what he called "one of the sector's strongest combinations of asset quality, jurisdictional exposure, operational consistency, balance sheet strength, and visible production growth after 2029."

The numbers back up the framing. Agnico Eagle's all-in sustaining cost — the standard measure of what it actually costs to pull an ounce of gold out of the ground and keep the mine running — sits at roughly $1,456, according to Jefferies, versus an $1,800 average for its peer group. Barclays analyst Richard Garchitorena has made a similar point since initiating coverage, noting that more than 85% of the company's production comes from Canada and Finland — about as low-risk a jurisdictional footprint as gold mining offers — and crediting management's record of turning acquisitions (Kirkland Lake, Yamana, O3 Mining) into shareholder returns rather than write-downs. Jefferies projects production growing roughly 17% between 2026 and 2031.

Panic doesn't do due diligence

None of this is new information, which is the point. Gold's retreat from January's highs has been driven largely by macro forces that have nothing to do with any individual miner's operations: a Federal Reserve seen as leaning hawkish into late 2026, position-unwinding after a historic rally, and ETF outflows as momentum traders head for the exits. Mining equities, which tend to amplify gold's moves in both directions, have taken it on the chin regardless of which companies are running lean operations and which aren't.

That's the dynamic dual bank backing is meant to signal — not a new catalyst at Agnico Eagle itself, but a market correction that swept up a low-cost, geopolitically low-risk producer alongside far shakier operators. When Barclays trims a price target by $3 while reiterating Overweight, and Jefferies raises its target by $13 while upgrading two full notches, the message is that the sell-off overshot the fundamentals, not that anything changed at the company.

What's next

Agnico Eagle's next full quarterly results, due in early August, will be the next real test of whether the cost and production story analysts are citing holds up against a lower gold price. Until then, the more immediate signal to watch is whether other banks follow Jefferies and Barclays in treating the gold-sector sell-off as indiscriminate — or whether more downgrades arrive to suggest the market got the pricing right the first time.
