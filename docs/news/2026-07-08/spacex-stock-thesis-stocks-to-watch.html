BEYOND THE TAPE · SPACEX · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-07-08 · 4 min read

SpaceX chatter dominates a mixed stocks-to-watch session

SpaceX's IPO and Nasdaq-100 debut have made its stock one of the market's most-watched — and most volatile — new names. — NASA / Tony Gray and Kevin O'Connell (public domain)
In one sentence
Strategist Jay Woods says SpaceX could double long-term but isn't a short-term trade — a call underscored by a session where even Samsung's record quarter got sold.

There is a particular tone market strategists reach for when they want to be bullish and cautious in the same breath, and Jay Woods hit it dead-on this week: buy the stock, he told Benzinga, and then "put it away, don't look at it."

The stock is SpaceX, and the advice arrives at an odd moment for it. Elon Musk's rocket and satellite company completed the largest initial public offering in U.S. history on June 12, pricing at $135 a share and briefly touching $225.64 four days later before sliding to a post-IPO low of $147.11. On July 7, it entered the Nasdaq-100 — the first company ever to do so under a newly created "fast-track" rule that lets mega-IPOs skip the usual waiting period for index inclusion — and promptly fell more than 6% anyway. It is the kind of session that makes long-term theses easier to state than to act on.

The pitch: buy it, then forget it

Woods, chief market strategist at Freedom Capital Markets and a 25-year veteran of the NYSE floor, is not shy about the valuation problem. "I think the valuation and the pre-market valuations, they scare me," he told Benzinga — a notable admission for someone recommending the stock at all. SpaceX priced its IPO around a $1.75 trillion valuation and traded above $2 trillion in its early days, backed by $18.67 billion in 2025 revenue and a $4.94 billion net loss driven largely by investment in Starship and satellite infrastructure. Starlink, the satellite internet arm, supplies an estimated 58% of group revenue and is, in Woods's framing, "going to be the biggest grower over time."

His resolution of that tension is to separate the trade from the holding. "This stock will eventually double from where it is," he said, "but I don't know the time frame as to when that will happen, and I think there are better opportunities right now for a trader than to be in space." The logic, as he laid it out, is less about SpaceX's fundamentals over the next quarter and more about not betting against Musk over a longer arc: "If you believe in Elon Musk, you don't bet against him." It is a thesis built for someone who can tolerate a wide entry-price band and total silence for a few years — not for anyone trying to time the Nasdaq-100 flows.

A session that would not cooperate

Woods's caution about near-term positioning got an unplanned assist from the rest of Tuesday's tape. Samsung Electronics reported preliminary operating profit of roughly 89.4 trillion won — about $58.4 billion — for the second quarter, an increase of nearly 1,810% from a year earlier and its third consecutive record quarter, driven by AI-server memory and high-bandwidth chips. Investors shrugged, and Samsung shares fell more than 6% on the day. Two very different companies, on the same morning, delivered good news — a historic index entry for one, a historic earnings beat for the other — and both were sold.

The rest of the day's watch list reinforced the mood: Fiserv rose more than 2% on a Wall Street Journal report that it had approached major banks about selling its debit-processing infrastructure business, while Rivian dropped 14% after announcing a 75-million-share capital raise. None of it points to a single macro story so much as a market parsing each company's news on its own, increasingly unforgiving terms — rewarding a possible divestiture, punishing a dilutive raise, and treating even a fast-tracked index inclusion or a record chip quarter as an excuse to sell into strength.

What the caution is actually about

That is the context Woods's remarks sit inside, whether he intended it or not. He is not arguing SpaceX is overvalued in some abstract sense; he is arguing that a stock less than a month removed from IPO, freshly added to a major index, and trading through the kind of volatility that took it from $135 to $225 and back below $150, is not a place to be making short-term bets — regardless of the long-term merit underneath it. Woods himself has said SpaceX likely needs a couple of quarters of results as a public company before the hype from its S-1 filing gives way to something an investor can actually underwrite.

That first test comes soon enough. SpaceX's inaugural quarterly report as a public company will be the first hard data point against which Woods's doubling thesis can be measured, and Samsung publishes fuller second-quarter detail on July 30 — a chance to see whether Tuesday's sell-the-record reaction was noise or the start of a broader re-rating of AI-linked hardware names. Until then, the stocks-to-watch list keeps filling with reminders that a good headline and a rising share price are, this earnings season, two different things.
