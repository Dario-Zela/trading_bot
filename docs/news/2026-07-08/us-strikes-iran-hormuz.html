U.S. strikes Iran after Hormuz tanker attacks, risking oil and shipping flows

U.S. Navy vessels transit the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint at the center of the renewed U.S.-Iran conflict. — U.S. Navy / Mass Communication Specialist 3rd Class Alex R. Forster, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
U.S. forces struck 80+ targets in Iran after tanker attacks in the Strait of Hormuz shattered a June ceasefire, jolting oil and shipping markets.

U.S. forces struck more than 80 targets inside Iran on Tuesday, Central Command said, in retaliation for Iranian attacks on three commercial vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz — the world's most important oil chokepoint and, once again, its most dangerous stretch of water.

"U.S. Central Command forces have begun launching a series of powerful strikes against Iran to impose heavy costs for targeting and attacking commercial shipping crewed by innocent civilians in an international waterway," CENTCOM wrote on X. It called Iran's actions "unwarranted, dangerous, and a clear violation of the ceasefire."

Three ships, then retaliation

The immediate trigger came in two waves. On Monday, projectiles struck the Qatari-owned LNG carrier Al Rekayat and the Saudi-flagged supertanker Wedyan as they moved through the strait; the Al Rekayat took a fire to its engine room serious enough that salvage crews feared an explosion. Less than a day later, Iran's Revolutionary Guard Corps hit a third vessel. By Tuesday, Washington had answered with the heaviest air campaign since the war's initial phase in February.

Iran's Press TV, the state broadcaster, reported at least seven explosions around Sirik, a port town on the Hormuz coast, and on nearby Qeshm Island, saying projectiles struck a commercial pier and a fishing jetty at the village of Ziarat, with several people wounded by shrapnel. Civilian fishing boats near Bandar Abbas were also reportedly hit. None of this has independent confirmation beyond Iranian state media, and Tehran has not detailed casualties or damage to port operations — but the location tracks with CENTCOM's stated targets near the strait, and it fits the broader pattern of a campaign aimed at the infrastructure Iran has used to threaten shipping.

A ceasefire built to survive this, and didn't

What makes Tuesday's exchange different from a one-off flare-up is what it breaks. The current war between Iran and the U.S.-Israel coalition dates to February 28, when Washington and Jerusalem launched Operation Epic Fury against Iranian military and nuclear sites; Iran answered by closing the strait outright, mining approaches and boarding merchant ships. Over the following weeks at least 14 seafarers and a port worker were killed, roughly 2,000 vessels carrying 20,000 mariners were stranded, and Brent crude spiked past $100 a barrel for the first time in four years, eventually touching $126.

A first ceasefire in April collapsed within days. A second, more durable arrangement — the Islamabad Memorandum, signed remotely by Donald Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian on June 17 — reopened the strait toll-free for 60 days, lifted the U.S. Navy's blockade of Iranian ports, and waived (without eliminating) sanctions on Iran's oil exports in exchange for a pledge not to pursue nuclear weapons. It left the hardest questions — Iran's uranium stockpile, its ballistic-missile program, its regional militias — for follow-on talks. Three weeks later, that framework is the casualty. The U.S. Treasury has already reimposed the sanctions the memorandum had waived, unwinding the oil-export relief that briefly restored Tehran's access to world crude markets.

Oil and shipping repriced in hours

Markets moved before the strikes were even confirmed. Brent settled 3% higher at $74.16 a barrel on the tanker attacks alone, with West Texas Intermediate up 2.8% to $70.44 — a comparatively contained move next to March's triple-digit spike, but a clear signal that traders no longer treat the ceasefire as a floor under prices. War-risk insurance, which had eased as the Islamabad Memorandum held, was already climbing again before Tuesday: premiums for a strait transit had risen from roughly 0.125% of a vessel's insured value to between 0.2% and 0.4%, adding on the order of a quarter of a million dollars to the cost of moving a very large crude carrier through the waterway. Several major maritime insurers have suspended or repriced war-risk coverage for the Persian Gulf altogether.

The strait carries about a fifth of the world's seaborne oil and roughly the same share of global LNG shipments — Qatar alone sends the bulk of its gas exports through it, which is why an LNG carrier was among the first ships hit. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who has previously downplayed the disruption risk, saying "the only thing prohibiting transit in the straits right now is Iran shooting at shipping," is due in Israel on Wednesday, underscoring how quickly the crisis has pulled Washington's attention back to the region after weeks of relative quiet.

What to watch

The question now is whether Tuesday's strikes are a bounded reprisal, calibrated to the three tanker attacks, or the reopening of the wider war that the Islamabad Memorandum was meant to end. Iran's response — whether it renews its full closure of the strait, as it did in February, or absorbs the strikes and looks to salvage the ceasefire framework in future talks — will matter more to oil markets than Tuesday's raid itself. Traders will be watching Brent's open Wednesday, the scale and location of any Iranian retaliation, and whether Gulf insurers move further before the next data point: whatever CENTCOM or Tehran says next about the state of the truce.
