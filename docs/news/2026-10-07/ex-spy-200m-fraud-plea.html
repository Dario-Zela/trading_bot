Ex-US spy who stashed gold bars at home admits to $200m scam

In one sentence
Former CIA official David Rush pleaded guilty to wire fraud over nearly $200m in invented classified programmes, and will forfeit at least $194m including 298 gold bars.

A former CIA official has admitted inventing classified programmes to siphon nearly $200 million out of the US government, then spending the money on Florida mansions and gold bars. David J. Rush pleaded guilty on 6 October to a single count of wire fraud in federal court in Virginia.

The gold is what made the case famous. When the FBI searched Rush's home in Ashburn, Virginia, in May, agents found roughly 300 one-kilogram bars worth more than $40 million. They also found about $2 million in cash and some 35 luxury watches. Prosecutors say the bullion was only part of the haul.

A secret with no one to check it

According to the plea papers, Rush, a senior-grade CIA employee, "created false classified authorities, directed and approved the associated expenditures, caused contractors and subcontractors to carry out the transactions, and thereafter exercised possession or control over the acquired assets."

There were two strands. In one, he invented a classified programme to route $145 million through a front company, which bought Florida properties in Palm Beach and Hobe Sound that were meant to be resold at a profit. In the other, prosecutors say he "fabricated a separate purported sensitive government activity to justify the acquisition of gold bars."

The scheme's cover was secrecy itself. A request labelled classified invites fewer questions, and few people are cleared to ask them. The design needed no hacking, only authority and a plausible code name.

Not the résumé it seemed

The earlier salary case, which prompted the first charges, painted a similar picture. Prosecutors alleged Rush had claimed 744 hours of military leave after leaving the Navy in 2015. They also said he had inflated his pay by falsely claiming degrees from Clemson University and Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. His lawyer initially described the gold as "a sensational tidbit" unrelated to that charge. That position did not survive the plea.

The lesson for security-minded employers is uncomfortable. Rush held a clearance, which means he had been vetted. Vetting, though, is a snapshot of a person at a point in time. It does not audit what a trusted officer does with spending authority years later, and it clearly did not catch the credentials at issue.

What he gives up

Under the plea agreement Rush will forfeit at least $194 million. That includes the 298 gold bars, four Florida homes, two BMWs and the watches. He faces up to 20 years in prison. A judge ruled in June that he should stay in custody, finding him a serious flight risk. The CIA referred the matter to the FBI. NBC News also reported that he admitted exposing a secret intelligence source to a foreign official, a detail not in the other accounts reviewed here.

The case has little bearing on markets. It is a criminal-justice story with a good set-dressing: bullion, a classified budget and a clearance that opened doors. The next date to watch is sentencing, which has not yet been reported. Whether the CIA or Congress then looks at how a programme that cost $145 million went unquestioned is a separate matter, and one that no official has yet signalled.
