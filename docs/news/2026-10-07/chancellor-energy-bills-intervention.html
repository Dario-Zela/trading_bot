Chancellor plans major intervention to ease energy bills for poorer households

In one sentence
Reeves is preparing income-targeted help with energy bills as the price cap rises 4% and a bigger January increase looms, with the cost and design still unpublished.

The Chancellor is preparing a significant energy-bill measure aimed at lower-income households. It would be the government's response to a winter in which gas prices, and the Ofgem price cap that follows them, are rising again. The Guardian reports the plan as in preparation, not announced. No design, cost or start date has been confirmed. The framing, though, is settled: support is to be targeted, not universal.

What is known

The outline matches what Rachel Reeves has said since the spring, when oil prices rose above $100 a barrel during the Iran conflict. She ruled out across-the-board support and said help would be based on household income. Press reports at the time described a limited package over the summer and a larger one in the autumn. The autumn is now here.

Her stated reason is the 2022 experience. In her words: "When Russia invaded Ukraine, the richest, the best-off third of households got more than a third of the support. That makes no sense at all." The Treasury's current objection to a blanket subsidy is therefore about distribution as well as cost. A flat per-household payment sends a large share of its money to people who do not need it.

The mechanism is not known. Reeves's remarks pointed towards some form of social tariff or income-linked rebate. A benefits-only test would be cheap and easy to run, but it would miss working households on low pay. A wider income test needs data the Department for Work and Pensions and HMRC can combine, which takes time to build. That trade-off will probably decide how fast the money can reach people.

The bills behind it

Ofgem raised the cap by 4% from 1 October. The typical dual-fuel bill rose from £1,663 to £1,723 a year, an increase of £60. The regulator pointed to wholesale gas prices pushed up by the Middle East conflict. A government decision to remove VAT from domestic electricity bills has kept electricity costs broadly stable, which softened that increase.

The larger worry is January. Ofgem sets that cap in late November. Forecasters currently disagree on the size of the rise: Cornwall Insight's latest projection is £1,872 a year, 8.6% above October levels, while an earlier forecast reported a rise as high as 14%. Both are estimates, not announced figures. Even the lower figure would add roughly £150 a year to a typical bill during the coldest months, when households use the most energy. The Joseph Rowntree Foundation has been pressing the case that lower-income households have the least cushion against such a rise.

The politics are awkward for Reeves. Her record on household energy support is mixed. She cut winter fuel payments for pensioners who do not receive means-tested benefits, citing a £22bn hole in the public finances, and she faced criticism for it. A targeted package gives her a way to be seen to act without reopening the fiscal choices she has already defended.

The fiscal constraint

The constraint behind the word "targeted" is the gilt market. A universal scheme on the 2022 model would add to borrowing at a time when energy-driven inflation already complicates the Bank of England's task. A narrower scheme costs less, and the cost can be capped in advance. A cap makes it easier to fit within the existing fiscal rules, though the Treasury has published no costing.

The inflation effect also depends on design. A bill rebate paid directly to suppliers can lower measured prices. A cash transfer leaves the price cap unchanged and so leaves headline inflation at its higher level, although it supports spending power. Which route is chosen will affect rate expectations, and the scale will matter to gilt investors.

The market-relevant questions are narrow ones:

  • Scale: whether the package costs hundreds of millions of pounds or several billion.
  • Funding: whether it is paid for by extra borrowing or by offsetting measures in the Budget.
  • Inflation: whether it works through bills, which would lower the measured figure, or through cash transfers, which would not.

Spending on discretionary goods is the other channel. Lower-income households spend almost all of any extra income, so a targeted transfer supports retail and consumer demand more per pound than a universal one. Retail forecasters will need the detail before changing their numbers.

What to watch

Three dates will settle most of this. Ofgem's January price-cap announcement, due in late November, will set how large the problem is. The Budget will show how the measure is funded and whether it comes with offsetting tax or spending decisions. Gilt yields on the day of each announcement will show whether markets accept the "targeted" label as fiscal discipline. Until the Treasury publishes the scheme's eligibility rules and cost, the Guardian's report describes an intention and nothing more.
