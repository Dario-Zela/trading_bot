Fed's Bowman outlines five-region bank supervision structure

In one sentence
The Fed's Michelle Bowman announced a five-region bank supervision structure led by accountable regional heads, with leaders to be hired and the system live in early 2027.

The Federal Reserve is rebuilding the way it supervises US banks. Michelle Bowman, the Fed's vice chair for supervision, said on 6 October that oversight will be reorganised into five geographic regions, each run by a single regional leader. Supervision is currently split between the Board in Washington and the 12 Reserve Banks. The new system is due to start early next year.

Benzinga's account, which the triage notes were drawn from, gave her first name as Michael. The Fed's own speech page and Reuters give it as Michelle.

What changes

The Board in Washington writes the rules, and the 12 Reserve Banks do most of the examining. Under the new design, each of the five regions will have a leader "accountable for all supervisory activity in region," according to the text of Bowman's speech. The regions will follow state lines rather than Reserve Bank district boundaries. That is meant to ease coordination with state regulators, since many banks hold state charters. Examiners will stay in their existing Reserve Bank locations, so the physical footprint is unchanged.

Bowman said she expects to begin interviewing for the regional leader roles early next year, and the posts will be advertised.

The accountability argument

Bowman's case is about who answers for supervisory decisions. She said the current arrangement "disincentivized a critical link between responsibility and accountability." She also criticised the internal committees that grew up around the old model, saying they "became a source for plausible deniability and a disincentive for examiners to take prompt and decisive action." Her speech said those committees run to dozens.

The backdrop is Silicon Valley Bank. An independent review of its 2023 collapse found Fed examiners were slow to respond to problems they had identified. Bowman said the realignment is meant to "implement a culture of accountability and clear decisionmaking authority."

What remains unclear

The speech, as summarised, does not say how the largest banks will be handled. It does not say whether the biggest firms will sit inside the regional structure or be supervised separately. Nor does it say how the regional leaders will report to Bowman, or whether they will carry the authority now held by Reserve Bank presidents. Reserve Bank presidents sit on the Federal Open Market Committee, so the change touches the Fed's internal balance of power as well as its examination routine. Those answers will determine whether the change alters how banks are examined or only who signs off.

The same speech flagged a second item. The Fed will consider updating the asset thresholds that trigger stricter oversight, and is exploring a mechanism to adjust them every five years for inflation and economic growth. TD Cowen analyst Jaret Seiberg said this could stop banks being pulled into enhanced supervision simply because they grow with the economy. Together the two changes point toward faster supervisory decisions and fewer banks under the heaviest rules. Neither alters a capital or liquidity requirement directly, so any market effect would come through how examiners behave rather than through the rulebook.

What to watch

The first marker is the job postings and the naming of the five regional leaders, expected early in 2027. The second is any proposal on the asset thresholds. The treatment of the biggest banks inside the new structure is the detail that would show how much changes in practice.
