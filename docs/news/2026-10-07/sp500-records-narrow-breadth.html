S&P 500 and Nasdaq close at records on power-stock rally, but only 3% of constituents are at highs

In one sentence
US indices closed at records on 6 October, led by utilities after Google's 890MW Constellation nuclear deal, though breadth remains thin with only about 3% of S&P 500 stocks at highs.

The S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite both closed at records on Tuesday, and the sector at the front of the line was not software or semiconductors. It was utilities. The index gains were modest, 0.58% and 0.45%. The rotation underneath them is the story, and so is how few stocks the records are resting on.

The S&P 500 rose to 7,818.93, its first close above 7,800 and its first record since 13 August. The Nasdaq Composite finished at 27,599.79. The Dow Jones Industrial Average added 253.38 points, or 0.49%, to 51,521.28. Ten of the S&P's 11 sectors rose, with utilities leading and healthcare the lone decliner. Chipmakers helped as well: Marvell gained 5.8%, Broadcom 3.7% and AMD nearly 3%. Treasury yields eased from multi-year highs and oil slipped, which gave the session a friendly backdrop.

A nuclear deal, and a sector that moved

The catalyst for the power trade came the same day. Google announced a long-term agreement with Constellation Energy to add 890 megawatts of new nuclear capacity to the PJM grid, the mid-Atlantic and Midwest market, over the next 20 years. The additions are uprates, meaning capacity gained by upgrading existing units at plants in Illinois, Pennsylvania and New Jersey. The first is expected online in 2028. Constellation said it would commit more than $4.3bn in new investment and that the work would support about 7,200 construction jobs.

The package goes further. The companies also signed a 15-year supply agreement for an additional 2,700 megawatts within PJM, and Constellation chose Google Cloud and Gemini Enterprise under an expanded five-year technology alliance. CNBC's headline put the combined scale at 3.6 gigawatts.

The structure matters more than the headline figure. Earlier hyperscaler nuclear deals centred on restarting a dormant reactor. Microsoft's arrangement over Three Mile Island Unit 1 is the best known, and Google's own 25-year agreement with NextEra over Iowa's Duane Arnold plant followed the same pattern. This one pays for incremental output from plants that are already running. That is faster and cheaper than new construction, and it suggests the buyers' binding constraint is megawatts delivered on a known date, not the brand of reactor.

On the sector move, the verifiable fact is the ranking: utilities finished first among the 11 S&P sectors. The size of the gain and of Constellation's own move were not available in the sources reviewed, so none is given here.

Records on a narrow base

The breadth data complicate the celebratory reading. Benzinga reported that only about 3% of S&P 500 constituents were at record highs. That figure was not independently confirmed here, but it is consistent with other breadth measures. As of 25 September, with the index 0.8% below its high, only 26% of members were above their 50-day average and 54% were below their 200-day. The ratio of the cap-weighted S&P 500 to its equal-weighted version is reported to be at its highest since August 2003, and the median constituent sits roughly 11% below its 52-week high.

Concentration is the other side of that picture. Nvidia, Apple and Microsoft together account for roughly 21% of the index's market capitalisation, according to the triage data and consistent with earlier published estimates. When three stocks carry a fifth of the weight, an index record can be set while most of its members go nowhere.

Breadth divergence of this kind is often read as a warning, but the track record is mixed. Narrow leadership persisted for long stretches in 1999 and 2000 before breaking, and it also persisted through other periods that ended without a crash. It is a statement about fragility, not a timetable.

What the two facts say together

The two threads look contradictory: a power-led rotation suggests leadership is broadening, while the 3% figure says it is not. The reconciliation is that both are happening at different scales. Within a market dominated by AI-linked mega-caps, money is moving down the supply chain, from the companies that write the models to the companies that supply electrons to them. But the supply chain is a small slice of 500 stocks. Tuesday's tape is better read as the AI trade extending its reach than as the market broadening.

That extension carries a real-economy price. Power-hungry data centres compete with households for grid capacity, a tension visible in Finland's order halting work on two Google data centres and in Westminster's current preoccupation with household energy bills. A deal that secures Google's megawatts does nothing for the retail customer, and PJM's capacity costs are ultimately paid by someone.

What to watch

The next test arrives with the third-quarter earnings season now beginning, where power producers and hyperscalers will both be asked to show that contracted megawatts turn into revenue and capex into returns. Before that, the next Fed communications will show whether yields, which eased on Tuesday, stay low enough to support valuations at these levels. If breadth stays at 3% while the index keeps climbing, the concentration risk grows with every new record.
