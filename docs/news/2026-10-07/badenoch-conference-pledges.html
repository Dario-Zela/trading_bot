Badenoch's conference pitch: £2.3bn for youth hiring, £10bn for air defence, and no deal with Reform

In one sentence
Badenoch proposed a £2.3bn employer NICs cut for younger staff and £10bn for air defence, while ruling out any Reform deal, but costings and message discipline remain open questions.

Kemi Badenoch arrived at the Conservative conference season with three messages. She would cut the cost of hiring young workers, spend heavily on a new air-defence system, and refuse any arrangement with Reform UK, even at the price of another election. Each is aimed at a different audience. Together they show a party trying to present itself as a government-in-waiting while its polling position leaves that claim untested.

The hiring pitch

The economic centrepiece is a proposed £2.3bn cut to employer national insurance contributions (NICs) for younger workers. Employer NICs are the payroll tax businesses pay on top of wages. They became a political sore point after the rise announced in Labour's first Budget, which employers have since cited as a drag on hiring. A cut aimed at younger staff would lower the cost of the hire that firms currently see as the riskiest and least productive on day one.

The exact age threshold was not confirmed in the material available. Earlier Conservative proposals of this kind have typically targeted workers under 30, and that is the likeliest design. The party presents the plan as growth-focused and as a direct contrast with the Labour government's fiscal stance.

The figure is modest next to the size of the public finances, which is part of the point. A targeted subsidy is cheaper than a broad cut and easier to defend against the charge of unfunded promises. It also puts the opposition in a debate that has moved towards the cost to business of investing and employing in Britain. Neither the Conservatives nor the Treasury has yet published detailed costings that would let outside analysts test the £2.3bn. Until they do, the number is a statement of intent rather than a forecast.

For employers and investors, the practical reading is about direction. Badenoch's opposition is placing its main pro-business lever on labour costs, not on corporation tax or capital allowances. If the party returned to office, that choice would shape hiring incentives across retail, hospitality and other sectors with many young staff. Opposition pledges are not policy, and this one has no date attached.

The £10bn shield, and a message problem

The second announcement was a £10bn commitment to a UK anti-missile air-defence system. The Conservative press release described it as a "British Iron Dome", a reference to Israel's interceptor network. Badenoch then publicly denied the system was an Iron Dome. The party's own paperwork and its leader's words pointed in different directions on the same day.

The contradiction matters less for the substance than for what it says about discipline. Defence pledges depend on credibility, and a comparison the leader disowns invites the question of what is actually being promised. Air defence is also expensive and technically demanding. A £10bn headline invites scrutiny of what it would cover, how long it would take to build and where the money would come from. Together with the £2.3bn hiring cut, it adds up to a spending-heavy offer from a party that built its case on fiscal restraint.

No deal with Reform

The third message concerned the party's own survival. In remarks reported by BBC Politics on 7 October, ahead of conference, Badenoch said she would rather face another election than do a deal with Reform UK. She framed this as part of a "battle of ideas".

Reform has risen in the polls and could become a kingmaker if the Conservatives fall short of a majority at the next election. Ruling out that route is a bet that the party can win outright, or at least that voters will punish a party that looks ready to trade its principles for office. The risk is the mirror image. If she underperforms, the arithmetic could leave no workable government, and a second election would follow. That is the scenario she has just said she would accept.

The defection of Robert Jenrick adds a farcical footnote. A satirical tapestry mocking him as a "turncoat" has circulated, and Conservative leaders say they are "delighted" by it. The humour is a way to downplay the loss. It also shows that the argument about the party's direction is still being fought in public.

What to watch

The test is whether the offers survive scrutiny. The costings behind the £2.3bn NICs cut and the £10bn air-defence plan, and the age threshold for the hiring incentive, will show how much of this is policy and how much is positioning. The next fixed point is the rest of conference week, where Badenoch's closing speech will show whether the three messages hold together or whether the Iron Dome confusion becomes the story. The longer-term question is the one she has set herself: whether the Conservatives can win without Reform, and what happens if they cannot.
