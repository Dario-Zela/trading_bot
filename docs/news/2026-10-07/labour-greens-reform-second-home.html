Labour rules out working with Greens; Reform MP buys £800,000 second home

In one sentence
Labour has ruled out working with the Greens after a Zionism conference motion, while a Reform MP who raised second-home concerns has bought one for £800,000.

Labour has said it will not work with the Green Party. The announcement follows a conference motion on Zionism, and it comes as a Green member faces scrutiny over alleged links to a jailed al-Qaeda supporter. Separately, a Reform UK MP who raised concerns about second-home ownership has bought a second home for £800,000.

Neither story is likely to move a gilt yield or a sterling cross. Both are what the political calendar produces in conference season: parties sorting out who they will stand beside and who they will stand apart from.

Labour closes the door

The immediate cause was a resolution at a recent Green conference describing Zionism as racism. Labour figures responded by ruling out cooperation with the Greens. The reports describe this as an ideological break rather than a tactical one.

The scrutiny of the Green member has added to the pressure. Reporting says the member has alleged links to a jailed al-Qaeda supporter. The detail available so far is thin. It is an allegation about one individual, not a finding about the party, and it should be read that way. It nonetheless gives Labour a second argument for keeping its distance: the policy dispute, plus the question of who is attached to the Greens.

The rift also has a practical side. Labour and the Greens compete for much of the same left-of-centre vote, and a public refusal to cooperate costs Labour little. The Greens are not a governing party, and nothing here alters the arithmetic of the Commons. There is no coalition to wobble and no policy lever to pull.

A second home, awkwardly timed

The Reform story is smaller but more human. The MP concerned had publicly raised concerns about second-home ownership, then bought one for £800,000. The gap between the stated position and the purchase is the story. Rivals will use it as evidence that Reform's rhetoric runs ahead of its discipline.

The purchase is not unlawful, and MPs with constituencies far from Westminster have long had to weigh where to live. The difficulty is that the MP's own earlier remarks set the standard they are now being measured against. A party that campaigns against the perks of office tends to be held to a stricter test than one that does not.

What ties them together

The two stories share one point: conference season rewards clear lines, and clear lines invite scrutiny. Labour has drawn one against the Greens. Reform has found that a position taken in public can be tested against private spending.

The next checks are practical. Watch whether Labour's line holds when local-election pacts and by-election tactics come up, and whether the Reform MP gives any fuller account of the purchase. Until then, the market relevance stays close to zero, and the interest is in the politics.
