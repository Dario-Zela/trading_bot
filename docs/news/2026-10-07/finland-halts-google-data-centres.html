Finland orders halt to work on two Google data centres

In one sentence
Finland's regulator has ordered Google to halt site work at two data-centre projects over missing environmental assessments, delaying part of its €13bn investment.

Finland has told Google to stop clearing land for two of its planned data centres. The reason is not a shortage of power or water, which is what many in the industry fear will slow the AI build-out. It is paperwork: the work went ahead before the environmental assessments the law requires had been completed.

On 6 October the Finnish Licensing and Supervision Authority (LVV) ordered Tuike Finland Oy, the project company acting for Google, to suspend preparatory work at sites in Muhos and Kajaani, in northern Finland. The order covers anything that significantly changes the land: tree felling, stripping topsoil, building site roads and altering drainage ditches. Suspension must be in place by 23 October.

What was done, and what was missing

The scale is what makes this more than a technicality. Reports put the clearance at more than 300 hectares of forest across the two sites, roughly 420 football pitches. Trees have been removed, topsoil taken off, site roads and storage areas built and ditches reworked.

Under Finnish rules, projects of this kind need an environmental impact assessment before work that significantly alters the site. The regulator's position is that the work began without one. The assessments are expected to finish later in 2026.

The company has until 14 October to provide explanations, according to reports of the order, or face enforcement proceedings.

Google has not contested the order. Sondre Ronander, its head of communications for Finland and Norway, said the company is "examining the observations by the Finnish authorities carefully and will act in line with the instructions by Finnish authorities." In a separate statement the company went further: "We understand the concerns and have fallen short of our own high standards in this instance." It has also pointed to a plan to plant trees across 130 hectares at Muhos as part of its biodiversity work.

The climate and environment minister, Sari Multala, stressed that every company operating in Finland, domestic or foreign, must follow Finnish law, and that responsibility extends down through subcontracting chains. She has said the projects will likely be delayed.

A big bet in a friendly country

The sites are part of a €13bn (about $15bn) investment Google announced on 9 September, its largest single commitment in Europe. It covers new data centres in several Finnish municipalities and an expansion of the existing campus at Hamina, and is meant to serve demand for Search, Maps and AI services over the next two years.

Finland has long been a favourite for this kind of build. The climate is cold, which cuts cooling costs. The grid has a lot of low-carbon generation. Land is plentiful and politicians have mostly welcomed the investment. Google has operated in Hamina for years.

That is why the order matters. It is not a hostile jurisdiction blocking a project. It is a welcoming one insisting that its own procedures be followed, and the consequence is still a stop-work order on a flagship site.

What it means for the capex story

The interpretation needs care. Nothing in the order says Finland is rationing power or water for data centres, and nothing suggests the projects are cancelled. The evidence points to a process failure by the developer, which Google has effectively admitted, rather than a policy shift against AI infrastructure.

But the practical effect is what investors in the cloud and AI spending cycle watch for: delay. Hyperscalers have guided to very large capital budgets on the assumption that sites move from approval to construction to revenue on a predictable schedule. A site that has to pause for an assessment, however routine, pushes capacity back by months. Land clearance is the earliest and cheapest stage of a build, so the order interrupts the start of the project, not the middle.

A single delayed campus is unlikely to move Google's overall spending plans, and the company has not said it will. The wider signal is about habits. Developers racing to secure capacity have an incentive to start site work early and sort the permitting afterwards. This case shows a regulator in a friendly country will stop that, and publicly. Others elsewhere in Europe, where land, grid connections and water are already contested, may feel emboldened to do the same.

What to watch

Three dates and documents will show how serious this is. The first is 14 October, when Tuike Finland's explanation to the regulator is due. The second is 23 October, the deadline for suspension. The third is the environmental assessments themselves, expected before the end of the year. If they are completed without major changes to the plans, this will look like a few months' slip. If they require a smaller footprint or different sites, it becomes a more meaningful constraint, and a test of how much of the spending already committed can be built where it was planned.
