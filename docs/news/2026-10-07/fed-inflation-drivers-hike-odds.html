Fed weighs AI, tariffs and energy in inflation outlook as October hike odds fall to 21.6%

In one sentence
Markets price a 21.6% chance of an October Fed hike as officials cite AI, tariffs and energy, a supply-side framing that favours a hold but leaves the hawks unconvinced.

The Federal Reserve is trying to explain an inflation problem that no longer fits one story. Officials have spent recent weeks naming three separate pressures — the build-out of artificial-intelligence infrastructure, a renewed round of tariffs, and energy prices lifted by the US-Iran conflict. Futures markets, meanwhile, put the chance of a hike at the October meeting at 21.6%, according to Benzinga's reporting of Axios. That is low enough to leave a hold as the base case, and high enough that nobody is calling the matter closed.

A wider diagnosis

The conventional account of inflation is about demand: too much spending chasing too few goods, corrected by dearer money. The three factors now in circulation at the Fed are different. Energy is a commodity-price shock. Tariffs are a tax on imported goods. AI investment is a surge in orders for chips, power equipment and construction.

The first two are plainly supply-side. The third is harder to classify. It adds to demand for scarce inputs, yet officials have also argued over whether the technology will eventually push prices down.

The most recent hard data fit the mixed picture. CFO Dive reported that in the July CPI release, core inflation was 2.5% year on year, down from 2.6% in June. Headline CPI was 3.4%, down from 3.5%. Energy costs were up 24.7% over the twelve months. The gap between a 2.5% core reading and a 3.4% headline is mostly energy, which is why a supply-side reading has traction.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly has framed the concern as one of interaction. She has asked whether the three shocks will have a "compounding effect", with energy costs feeding through into the prices of goods already made dearer by tariffs. Governor Michael Barr has gone further, citing high energy prices and the surge in AI-related investment as factors that have knocked the Fed off course in its inflation fight. Barr's late-September remarks were reported as leaning towards more tightening, not less, though the full text could not be checked for this article.

What the 21.6% says

The probability is a market price, not a Fed signal. Several things are worth separating.

The committee has not been united. At its August decision it held the federal funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%, with three policymakers dissenting in favour of a quarter-point rise, according to CFO Dive. A committee with three hawkish dissents is not one that has stopped worrying about inflation.

A 21.6% hike probability is also not a cut. The triage framing, that officials have largely discounted recession risk and see inflation as self-correcting, is an inference from the choice of diagnosis, and the evidence for it is indirect. If officials believe energy and tariffs will fade on their own, they have a reason to hold rather than hike. That is a long way from planning cuts. Some reports have also put the odds of any cut this year at only about 21%, and one aggregator snippet describing a September hike conflicts with the August hold, so the rate path should be read with caution until the next statement.

It is also unclear how the odds have moved. The triage notes said they had fallen from roughly 50% at the start of the year. That figure could not be confirmed, and it is not relied on here.

Why algorithmic desks care

Rate expectations feed almost every systematic strategy. Bond yields are set off the expected policy path, equity multiples depend on the discount rate, and FX carry trades depend on the gap between policy rates. A fall in hike odds from one meeting to the next changes the inputs to all three.

A supply-side reading carries its own risk. If energy and tariffs are the drivers, the Fed cannot do much about them with interest rates, and the inflation could persist for as long as the shocks do. A central bank that holds because it cannot fix the cause is not the same as one that holds because inflation is falling.

The equity market is already testing how far the AI part of the story reaches. Records in the S&P 500 and Nasdaq have been led by power-related stocks, with very few constituents at highs, a pattern covered in the paper's market report.

What to watch

The October 27–28 FOMC meeting is the decision point, and the September CPI release before it is the data that could move the 21.6%. Two things to read in the statement and the dissents are whether the three hawks from August stay at three, and whether the language on energy and tariffs is described as temporary or as something that may spread into core prices. The first sign of a cut path would be a drop in the dissent count and a softer energy line in the statement.
