Cambridge must answer key questions over Jason Arday's death, senior academics say

In one sentence
More than 40 senior Cambridge academics say the university's review of Jason Arday's death must identify who decided what, and that serious findings must carry consequences.

More than 40 senior Cambridge academics have written to the university's leadership. They say its review into the death of Professor Jason Arday may not answer the questions that matter most. Their letter, reported on 6 October, sets out what they want the inquiry to establish. It also asks what should follow if it finds fault.

Arday, 41, was appointed professor of sociology of education at Cambridge in 2023. He was widely described as the youngest Black professor in the university's history. He resigned on 5 August after questions were raised about alleged plagiarism in his work and about his qualifications. He was found dead on 14 August. His family, in a statement issued through Simon & Schuster UK, said he had been "subjected to a campaign of sustained abuse" since taking the post.

What the letter asks

The signatories include professors Cahir O'Kane (genetics), Caroline Bassett (digital humanities), Clair Wills (English literature) and Clément Mouhot (mathematical sciences). The letter went to the Chancellor, Chris Smith, and to the university's governing council. According to Brit Brief's account, it says it is "not yet clear" how the review will address Cambridge's own part in events.

The central demand is specific: the investigation "must establish who authorised and directed the university's public promotion of Professor Arday". The academics also want decisions and named offices traced through his appointment and his treatment. And they say that "serious findings must have consequences for the individuals and offices responsible".

That last line is the pointed one. Reviews of this kind often end in recommendations about process. The signatories want accountability for people.

The review itself

Cambridge announced the review on 4 September. Nazir Afzal OBE, a former Chief Crown Prosecutor for North West England, chairs it. It runs in two parts.

  • Part one examines the welfare protections and support available to Arday before his death. It formally launched on 14 September and is due to report within three months of the panel being established.
  • Part two looks wider. It covers the recruitment of senior academic staff, mentoring and support, how allegations of research misconduct were handled, and the help available to academics facing "extraordinary" public and media scrutiny. It has six months.

The academics are not the first to object. Varsity reported earlier criticism from the Students' Union, which said the announcement "came as a surprise" and was made without substantial student involvement. Student groups made similar complaints about appointments to the panel. Professor Oliver Linton, chair of the Faculty of Economics, argued that the review had been "narrowed substantially" towards support provision alone.

Why it reaches beyond one case

The dispute is about scope as much as sympathy. A welfare review asks whether an individual was looked after. A governance review asks who decided to promote him, who checked the claims, and who answered when scrutiny arrived. The academics' letter pushes for the second kind. Both are legitimate questions, and the order in which they are answered shapes what the university can say publicly.

Cambridge has not, in the coverage reviewed, published a detailed reply to the letter. The first marker is part one's report, due around mid-December if the three-month clock runs from mid-September. Whether the terms of reference are widened before then will show how seriously the university takes the signatories.
