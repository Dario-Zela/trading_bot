Military training sites to be investigated after rape and abuse claims at Army college

In one sentence
A review of UK military training sites follows reports of rape and abuse at the Army Foundation College in Harrogate, which trains 16- and 17-year-old recruits.

The British Army's college for teenage recruits is at the centre of a formal review of how the armed forces look after the youngest people in their care. Reports of rape and sexual abuse at the Army Foundation College in Harrogate, North Yorkshire, have led to an investigation that extends to other military training sites. The full terms of the review were not available at the time of writing.

The college is not an officer academy, as some early descriptions suggested. It is the Army's only initial training unit for people who enlist at 16 or 17½, and it runs two courses: a 49-week course for future infantry and combat soldiers and a 23-week course for those heading into technical and support trades. Its students are children in law, and the Army is their legal guardian in practice for the length of the course. That is why the allegations carry the weight they do.

What has been alleged

The renewed scrutiny follows accounts from four women who attended the college over the past five years. They describe a culture of misogyny in which female recruits were routinely demeaned and sexual abuse was normalised. One says she was raped for hours by several fellow junior soldiers when she was 17.

The figures reported alongside those accounts point beyond a handful of cases. Press coverage cites 176 complaints of inappropriate sexual behaviour recorded between 2018 and June 2025. In the year to June, 16 sexual offence reports were linked to the college, of which 32 allegations involved permanent staff and 144 involved junior soldiers. Two Army instructors have been convicted of sexual offences in separate cases in recent years. Earlier reporting counted nine rapes reported at the college in a single year.

Those numbers measure complaints, not proven offences, and they say nothing about how many went unreported. Still, the split between staff and fellow trainees matters. It suggests two distinct failures: supervision of instructors, and control of peer behaviour in a barracks full of teenagers.

Why a wider review

Ministers have faced calls for an independent inquiry. Liberal Democrat defence spokesperson James MacCleary wrote to the Defence Secretary asking for a full, independent examination of the culture and safeguarding practices at Harrogate. A review covering several training establishments, rather than one college, implies officials accept that the problem may lie in the system rather than in a single site.

The question for any such review is independence. The armed forces have commissioned reviews of their own conduct before, and critics have often argued that internal inquiries struggle to command trust among those who made complaints. The Ministry of Defence is also deciding the future of the Harrogate site as its private finance contract nears expiry, and the Army has weighed adding capacity for under-18 recruits. Expanding a pipeline while its safeguarding is under investigation would be awkward.

What to watch

Three things will show how serious the review is: whether it is led by someone outside the services, whether its findings are published in full, and whether former recruits are formally invited to give evidence. Publication of the terms of reference should settle the first of these.
