Meloni files to trademark her voice against AI misuse

In one sentence
Italian PM Giorgia Meloni has applied to the EU to trademark a four-second recording of her voice, an untested tactic against deepfakes that would not stop them on its own.

Italy's prime minister has applied to register her own voice as a trademark. The aim is to give her a legal lever against AI-generated fakes. Whether the lever reaches anything is far from clear.

Giorgia Meloni's application, dated 5 October, is on the website of the European Union Intellectual Property Office (EUIPO). It is a sound mark: a four-second audio clip of her saying, twice, in Italian, "I am Giorgia Meloni." The application is still under examination, and nothing has been granted.

Why a trademark

Trademark law was built to stop one company passing off its goods as another's. Sound marks exist, and the best-known examples are jingles and brand sonic logos. Using the regime for a politician's speaking voice is an improvisation. No court has established that a voice mark can be used against deepfakes, and the triage facts for this piece are explicit that there is no precedent.

The weak points are easy to name:

  • Commercial use. Trademark claims generally turn on use in trade. A fake clip posted to sway voters, or a scam call using a cloned voice, may not be that.
  • What is registered. The mark is one specific recording. A synthetic voice that sounds like Meloni is not obviously the same thing as a registered four-second clip.
  • Jurisdiction. An EU registration does nothing about a fake posted from outside the bloc.

Reporting on the filing makes the same point: even if approved, the mark would not by itself prevent her voice being used in deepfakes. At most it could add legal obstacles for people trying to replicate it.

A target for years

Meloni has personal reasons to try anything. Her face and voice have been cloned before. In 2024 she sued two men, a father and son, over deepfake pornographic videos of her and sought €100,000 in damages. She called the act "a form of violence."

More recently she shared a fabricated image of herself in underwear to warn about the technology. "I can defend myself. Many others cannot," she wrote, urging people to "verify before believing, and believe before sharing."

The law already moved, elsewhere

Italy has not left the field empty. Law No. 132, in force since 10 October 2025, makes it a crime to distribute AI-generated or altered images, video or voices without consent where they can mislead about authenticity and cause unjust harm. A prime minister who can point to that statute and still reaches for trademark is telling you something. Either the criminal route is too slow or too narrow for her purposes, or she wants a civil tool she can use directly.

That reading is an inference, not something her office has said. The filing's logic is plausible but untested.

What to watch

The EUIPO examination is the first checkpoint. Examiners will decide whether a spoken name counts as a distinctive sound mark at all. If it is accepted, the real test comes only when she tries to enforce it against a specific fake. One prime minister's registration will not change how deepfakes are policed. If it fails, it will be a clear sign that dedicated rules, not borrowed commercial ones, are what's missing.
