Disney lawyer accuses FCC of seeking to 'censor, control' ABC's coverage

In one sentence
Disney's lawyer told a federal judge the FCC's unprecedented early review of ABC's eight station licences is retaliation for editorial choices, as the court weighs an injunction.

Disney told a federal judge on Tuesday that the Trump administration is using the Federal Communications Commission to punish ABC for what it chooses to put on air. The company asked the court to stop the regulator's early review of the licences for its eight ABC-owned television stations.

The argument came in Washington before US District Judge Loren AliKhan. Beth Wilkinson, Disney's lawyer, said the company is already behaving differently. "We are watching everything that we do," she said, "because every time we do something — even not broadcasting this presidential speech — brings out the anger in the administration and the retaliation from the agency." She said Disney is "chilled" and "making different editorial decisions."

What the FCC did

The dispute rests on a procedural step that is rarely taken. FCC Chair Brendan Carr ordered early renewal reviews of the eight stations' licences. Reports on the hearing describe it as the first such move in more than 50 years. Broadcast licences normally come up for renewal on a fixed cycle, so pulling the review forward is a change in practice, not a routine administrative act.

Disney's suit, filed in August, alleges a "retaliatory campaign" in which the administration encouraged the FCC to use its regulatory power against editorial content President Trump dislikes. The company points to two episodes. One is ABC's decision not to carry a live broadcast of Trump's July 16 speech reviving his attacks on US election security. The other is an FCC investigation into the network's diversity practices, which preceded the renewal reviews.

The judge's question

Judge AliKhan pressed Disney on selectivity. "I imagine we have a lot of discovery disputes with a lot of your licensees," she said. "So what is it that made this so different?" The question goes to the core of the case. Disney has to show that the early review was triggered by its coverage and not by ordinary regulatory friction.

The government's position, as reported, is that Disney keeps its licences throughout the process, so no harm has yet occurred. Disney's reply is that the harm is the pressure itself. Licence revocations are rare, but a pending review gives a regulator leverage over a licence-holder's editorial calls, which is the chilling effect Wilkinson described. Wilkinson compared ABC's position to waiting under a guillotine.

Why investors care

The stations are a small part of Disney's business, which is dominated by parks, streaming and cable networks. The exposure is regulatory and political. A broadcaster that believes its licences depend on avoiding offence faces a different cost-benefit calculation on news judgement. The same regulator also oversees merger approvals, which gives it influence over Disney's wider deal-making. Reports on the hearing did not cite any move in Disney shares, and the case does not turn on the stations' earnings.

The outcome will also serve as a test for other licence-holders. If the court finds the First Amendment claim plausible, it limits how freely a regulator can bring reviews forward. If it declines to intervene, the FCC's discretion over timing stands, and broadcasters are likely to price in the risk.

What to watch

The next step is Judge AliKhan's ruling on Disney's request for an injunction against the review. No decision date was reported. The timetable of the FCC's review itself will determine how long the stations stay in limbo if the request is denied.
