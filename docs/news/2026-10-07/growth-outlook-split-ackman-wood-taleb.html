Ackman fears the 1970s, Wood sees a boom, and Taleb disputes Bessent on the data

In one sentence
Ackman warns of 1970s-style stagflation, Wood forecasts high single-digit GDP growth, and Taleb has challenged Bessent over the reliability of US data.

Two well-known investors and one risk theorist are describing very different American economies this week, and one of them is arguing with the Treasury Secretary about whether the numbers can be trusted. The reporting so far, mostly from Benzinga, is thin on specifics. It gives positions and not the evidence behind them.

Stagflation against a boom

Bill Ackman, the founder of Pershing Square, has warned of a return to 1970s-style conditions. That means stagnant growth alongside persistent inflation, the combination that defined the decade and forced the Federal Reserve into its long, painful tightening under Paul Volcker. Ackman has used the comparison before, arguing that the Fed erred in the 1970s by easing before inflation was beaten.

Cathie Wood of Ark Investment Management is on the other side. She is forecasting US GDP growth that stays in the high single digits. The reporting does not say whether she means real or nominal growth, over what horizon, or on what assumptions. The distinction matters. Real growth at that pace would be far above anything the US has posted in decades, while a nominal figure of that size, which includes inflation, would be compatible with a much less exuberant economy. It would even fit a stagflationary one. Wood's long-standing thesis is that AI-driven productivity gains will lift output and pull prices down, which is the reverse of Ackman's worry.

The two positions imply different portfolios. One favours macro hedges and real assets, the other growth exposure. Triage notes suggest allocators may be shifting accordingly, but no flow data accompanies the claim, so it remains an inference.

The dispute over the data

A second thread concerns the figures themselves. Nassim Taleb, author of The Black Swan, has publicly disagreed with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent over how far US economic data can be trusted. Which indicators are in dispute has not been reported. Taleb has long argued that headline statistics understate tail risk and fragility. Bessent has spent the year presenting a strong-economy case, including a February forecast of robust 2026 growth that also acknowledged public unease about the economy.

The exchange matters less for who is right than for what it says about the inputs. If a Treasury Secretary and a prominent sceptic cannot agree that the data are reliable, then Ackman's and Wood's forecasts are being built on contested foundations.

What would settle it

None of this arrives with a catalyst: no earnings miss, policy change or data release prompted it. Without one, it is closer to commentary than to news. It is still worth noting that the disagreement runs through the same question, which is whether growth is real and whether prices are under control. The next readings on inflation and GDP, and the Fed's response to them, are the nearest tests. Fed policymakers are already weighing AI, tariffs and energy in their inflation outlook, and market pricing for an October hike has fallen to 21.6%. That suggests traders are, for now, giving Ackman's scenario less weight than his warning implies.
