CFTC and prediction markets win Illinois court ruling on sports event contracts

In one sentence
A Chicago federal judge found Kalshi's sports contracts are likely swaps under federal law, giving prediction markets and the CFTC a rare win amid a deepening split among courts.

A federal judge in Chicago has handed prediction-market operators, and the regulator that backs them, their first significant courtroom win in months. On 2 October, Judge Martha Pacold of the US District Court for the Northern District of Illinois granted a preliminary injunction, in part, to Kalshi, Coinbase and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission. It bars Illinois from enforcing certain gaming laws against sports event contracts while the case proceeds.

The ruling is preliminary. It nonetheless matters because it runs against the recent run of decisions, and because it deepens a conflict among courts that increasingly looks headed for the Supreme Court.

What the court found

The dispute turns on one classification question: is a contract that pays out depending on a game's result a "swap" under the Commodity Exchange Act, or a bet under state gambling law? If it is a swap, the CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction and state licensing rules are likely preempted. If it is a bet, states can regulate it.

Pacold concluded that Kalshi's core sports contracts are likely swaps. She wrote: "Many of the financial instruments at issue are likely swaps as defined by the Commodity Exchange Act—they just happen to be swaps that people find entertaining."

She also found that the plaintiffs would suffer irreparable harm without relief. She held that several Illinois laws likely conflict with federal law. Those laws govern which contracts may be sold, where they may be sold and to whom. The CFTC joined Kalshi and Coinbase as a plaintiff, so the agency is itself asserting its authority against a state.

The win is not total. The judge indicated that Illinois's new tiered transaction tax on prediction-market operators, reported as 1.75% rising to 3.5% after five million wagers, might survive. She drew a line between taxing the money operators make and regulating the market itself. The injunction was not extended to the tax, so the revenue question remains open.

A losing streak interrupted

The decision cuts against the recent trend. Federal appellate panels in the Sixth and Ninth Circuits have held in recent months that sports event contracts are bets rather than swaps. They ruled that the contracts could not escape state rules while the litigation plays out. In July, a federal court in the Eastern District of Wisconsin refused the CFTC a similar preliminary injunction.

That last point creates an awkward situation. Wisconsin and Illinois both sit within the Seventh Circuit, and two district courts there have now reached opposite preliminary conclusions on essentially the same question. Bloomberg Law, in its analysis of the ruling, said the decision deepens the urgency for the high court to step in. The reasoning is simple enough: with appellate courts and district courts pointing in different directions, a single contract could be lawful in one state and unlicensed gambling in the next.

What it means for the market

For operators, the ruling restores a federal-preemption argument that had been losing ground. For the CFTC, it supports the claim that event derivatives fall under its statute. That claim matters beyond sports. If the swap characterisation holds, a regulated exchange could in principle list event contracts on a wide range of outcomes under federal oversight alone. If it fails, each state becomes a separate licensing regime.

The limits are plain. A preliminary injunction rests on a finding of likely success, not a final judgment. Illinois can appeal to the Seventh Circuit, and the contrary Wisconsin ruling gives it a ready counterpoint. The split with the Sixth and Ninth Circuits is also unresolved. One district court has not overturned two appellate panels.

What to watch

The next step is whether Illinois appeals to the Seventh Circuit, which would have to reconcile its two district courts. Beyond that, the likelier catalyst is a petition to the Supreme Court. The justices would then have to decide whether a sports contract is a swap or a wager. Until then, operators face different rules depending on the state.
