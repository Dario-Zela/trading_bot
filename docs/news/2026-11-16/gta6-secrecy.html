Even GTA's original creators haven't played GTA 6

In one sentence
Rockstar's secrecy around GTA VI, launching 19 November, extends even to the franchise's founders, and leaves investors in Take-Two with little to check beyond preorders and guidance.

Grand Theft Auto VI goes on sale on 19 November, three days from now. Players who have queued for years will be among the first to see it. The people who started the series are not.

Rockstar Games has run one of the tightest launch campaigns in the industry. The studio has released two trailers and a small number of screenshots, and little else. Even the people closest to the franchise's history are watching from outside.

Dan Houser, the Rockstar co-founder who wrote the modern GTA games, has said he has not watched the GTA VI reveal and plans to keep it that way. "I will try and avoid it because I always try and avoid stuff that's going to distract or disrupt me creatively," he said, as reported by Games.gg. He has also said he wrote none of the new game: the world, he told PC Gamer, has "probably had enough GTA from me".

Houser is a Rockstar founder, though, not one of the original 1997 team. That game was made in Dundee by DMA Design, the studio David Jones founded in 1987, and the lineage matters to the story. Jones and the early developers have said little publicly about GTA VI, and nothing found in reporting for this piece shows whether any of them has seen more than the public trailers. The headline's claim is best read as the broad point: the secrecy extends far beyond the gaming press.

A studio that grew from a handful of people

DMA Design became Rockstar North. By 2018 it employed around 650 people, a long way from the small Scottish team that shipped the first game. The relationship has not always been warm. Jones has said Rockstar filed copyright claims against videos he posted showing prototype builds of the original game, a reminder that the company guards even its oldest material.

The delay history reinforces the pattern. Rockstar first targeted May 2026, then announced in November 2025 that the game would slip to 19 November 2026. Take-Two has since said the date holds.

Where the money is

The market link is more direct than the anecdote. Take-Two, Rockstar's publisher and parent, has built its financial year around this launch. Its most recent guidance, as reported, put fiscal 2027 net bookings at $8 billion to $8.2 billion, with adjusted earnings of $5.75 to $6 a share. That was below some analyst estimates, which ranged from about $8.5 billion to $9.1 billion depending on the source. Chief executive Strauss Zelnick has said preorders are running well ahead of expectations. The game is priced at $80.

Secrecy cuts both ways for investors. It sustains the anticipation that preorders feed on, and it leaves almost nothing for outsiders to check. Nobody beyond Rockstar can say how polished the game is, how large the day-one audience will be, or whether the guidance is cautious or merely hopeful. Shares have moved sharply on date announcements, which suggests the market treats each scrap of news as significant.

What to watch

The first reviews and launch-week sales figures will test the guidance. Take-Two's next quarterly results, where the company is likely to update its full-year forecast, should show how much of the early demand has turned into bookings.
