Italy fines Meta €805m over access to users' photos

In one sentence
Italy's data protection authority fined Meta €805m over a Facebook Android feature that accessed users' photos by default, without specific consent; Meta says device permissions suffice.

Italy's data protection authority has fined Meta €805m (about $887m) over a Facebook feature that reached into the photo libraries of Android users' phones. Several outlets report that the regulator, the Garante, announced the penalty on 14 November. The Garante says Meta did not obtain specific consent from the people affected.

The finding itself concerns a gallery-suggestion tool. Introduced in 2023, it offers photos and videos stored on a phone as candidates for posting while a user browses the Facebook app. According to reports of the decision, the Garante found the feature was switched on by default. It says the app accessed photos, videos and related metadata without explicit consent and without clear disclosure of what was happening. Italy's data protection regulator weighed the scale: about 20 million Android users in Italy were affected.

The size of the penalty

The fine works out at roughly 0.47 percent of Meta's global turnover, according to the reports. That is well below the GDPR ceiling of 4 percent. It is still described as by far the largest penalty the Italian authority has issued. For comparison, Ireland's regulator, which leads on Meta's EU data protection because the company's European headquarters is in Dublin, imposed the record €1.2bn GDPR fine in 2023. The Italian case is notable because a national authority acted on its own.

The probe began in July 2025 after a complaint from a consumer group in Bolzano.

What Meta must change

Beyond the money, the order carries deadlines. Meta has 60 days to disable the feature by default and to ask for separate, feature-specific consent. It must report on its initiatives within 30 days and on its measures within 90 days.

Meta's defence is that the feature only works once users have granted permission on their device, and that they can change that permission at any time in their settings. The dispute, in other words, is over whether a phone-level permission to read photos amounts to informed consent for a particular use inside an app. The Garante has said it does not. This is the part that could travel beyond Italy: any app that offers to suggest or upload from a camera roll relies on the same operating-system prompt.

Caveats

Some details rest on secondary reporting. The reports reviewed here include Bloomberg, Al Jazeera, The Hacker News and ABC News. The Garante's own published decision has not been checked, so the legal basis and the turnover percentage are as reported. The Garante has also been under a cloud: financial police raided its Rome headquarters in January 2026 as part of a corruption investigation. Nothing found suggests that affects this decision.

What to watch

The first marker is the 30-day report to the Garante, due in mid-December. Meta can also appeal in the Italian courts, and it has not said whether it will. If the decision stands, other EU regulators may look at the same feature in their own markets.
