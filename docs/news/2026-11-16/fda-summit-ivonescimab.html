FDA declines to approve Summit's China-developed lung cancer drug

In one sentence
The FDA has reportedly declined to approve Summit's ivonescimab; the agency's reasons are unverified, and so is whether China-run trial data played any part.

The US Food and Drug Administration has declined to approve ivonescimab, the lung cancer drug that Summit Therapeutics licensed from China's Akeso, according to a report in Investor's Business Daily. That report is the only source for the decision. The text of the FDA's letter, the agency's reasons and any statement from Summit could not be checked, and the points below that depend on them are marked as unconfirmed.

The decision was due on or around 14 November. The FDA had set that date as the goal for acting on Summit's application, according to BioSpace. Whether the refusal took the form of a complete response letter, the usual vehicle for a rejection that leaves room for resubmission, is not confirmed.

What was filed

Ivonescimab is a bispecific antibody. A single molecule blocks two targets: PD-1, the immune checkpoint that drugs such as Keytruda act on, and VEGF, a signal that tumours use to grow blood vessels. Akeso developed it in China, and Summit holds the rights outside China and a few other territories. Those licensing terms are background knowledge and were not re-checked here.

The application the FDA accepted covered a narrow population. It sought approval of ivonescimab plus chemotherapy for locally advanced or metastatic non-squamous non-small cell lung cancer with an EGFR mutation, in patients whose disease had progressed after treatment with a third-generation EGFR inhibitor (Barchart, Summit release). The supporting trial, HARMONi, reported a progression-free survival hazard ratio of 0.58 (95% CI 0.45–0.73) by investigator assessment, as presented at the World Conference on Lung Cancer in 2025 (OncoDaily).

The overall-survival problem

The more consequential number was on survival. Before the decision, the FDA had told Summit that "a statistically significant overall survival benefit is necessary to support marketing authorization," as Fierce Pharma reported. Ivonescimab plus chemotherapy cut the risk of death by 21%, a result that narrowly missed statistical significance. Summit filed anyway and later gave the agency updated survival data. Fierce Pharma described the outcome as highly uncertain, and noted that the FDA has approved at least two agents in previously treated EGFR-mutant disease without a statistically significant survival benefit.

This context bears on interpretation. A rejection that rests on missing statistical significance in overall survival is a different story from one that rests on where the trial was run. The pre-decision record points toward the first explanation. It does not rule out the second.

The China-data question

The angle that gives the story wider reach is whether the FDA discounted evidence generated mostly in China. HARMONi enrolled patients in several regions. The proportion from the US and other Western countries, and any FDA comment on regional applicability, were not verified. Without the action letter, any claim that this rejection sets a precedent for China-originated assets is a hypothesis, not a finding.

The commercial stakes are plain enough. Western drugmakers have in recent years paid substantial sums to license Chinese-developed oncology candidates, and those prices assume that the US regulator will accept data generated largely in China. One refusal on survival grounds would leave that assumption intact. A refusal citing population relevance would not.

What to watch

Three documents decide the matter: the FDA's letter, Summit's own statement on its next steps, and any filing describing a path to resubmission. The first will say whether the agency's concern was statistical, regional or manufacturing-related. The second will show whether Summit can resolve it with more survival follow-up or needs a new trial. Until those appear, the reasoning behind the decision remains unknown.
