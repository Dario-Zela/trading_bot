Gucci cuts prices while Louis Vuitton holds the line

In one sentence
Gucci has cut selected prices while Louis Vuitton holds its flagships, but third-quarter figures show neither strategy has yet restored growth at Kering or LVMH.

Gucci has cut prices on selected items, and Louis Vuitton has not followed. That is the shorthand for a divergence between Europe's two largest luxury groups, Kering and LVMH. Their latest quarterly figures show that neither strategy is yet paying off.

The split should be read with care. The Wall Street Journal's account is the only one behind the headline, and the discounts are not uniform. One report puts the cut on Gucci's medium Mercato bag at roughly 20–25%. That figure comes from a single outlet and has not been checked against Gucci's own price lists.

Kering concedes, LVMH trims at the edges

Kering's chief executive, Luca de Meo, said on the July earnings call that the group had in some categories "gone too far on pricing". He also said the volume effect of doing so was "exponential" rather than linear. Gucci has since lowered selected prices and is pricing new collections more competitively, while keeping marketing at about 9% of revenue. De Meo has indicated that much of the adjustment will come through new, cheaper products rather than list-price cuts.

LVMH has been slower to retreat from the post-Covid price surge. In the first half it applied low-single-digit price increases in fashion and leather goods, with volume and mix roughly flat. Louis Vuitton's answer to weaker demand is to add entry-level items, such as the Multi Pochette, while leaving its flagship bags alone and expanding a high-priced range that, per management, carries waiting lists.

What the numbers say

The third-quarter results do not show an obvious winner.

  • Kering: Group revenue was about €3.35bn, down 1% on a comparable basis. Gucci brought in €1.29bn, down 6% comparable. Outside Gucci, comparable retail sales in the fashion and leather goods division rose 2%, helped by Saint Laurent, Bottega Veneta and Brioni. Kering shares fell 12% on 23 October, the day of the results.
  • LVMH: Group revenue was about €18.5bn, up 1% organically. Fashion and leather goods, the profit engine that includes Louis Vuitton, fell about 5% organically, worse than the roughly 2% fall analysts had expected. Management blamed China. The US, Europe and Japan grew.

Gucci's decline had narrowed to 2% in the second quarter from 8% in the first, so the third-quarter fall of 6% is a step back. Louis Vuitton's parent, meanwhile, had only just returned to 1% growth in fashion and leather in the second quarter, after seven quarters of decline, before slipping again. Holding prices has not protected LVMH's volumes, and cutting them has not yet restored Gucci's.

Read-across for the sector

The pricing question matters because margins, not sales, drive how these stocks are valued. A brand that holds prices through a demand dip keeps its gross margin but risks losing the aspirational customer. A brand that cuts gains volume at the cost of that margin and, arguably, some of its scarcity value. Bernstein's Luca Solca said he was "not convinced yet that Gucci is rebuilding desirability". He describes the target group as "luxury orphans", shoppers priced out in the boom years.

Hermès sits outside the debate. It has no plans for a Gucci-style correction, and its first-half revenue rose 6% at constant exchange rates, with a 41.1% operating margin. Its chief executive, Axel Dumas, said "our clients have turned up once again". On that evidence, pricing power at the very top survives while the middle tier is under strain.

The data do not yet show whether Gucci's cuts widen the gap with Louis Vuitton or merely reflect a weaker starting point. The next test is the fourth-quarter and full-year results, due in the new year. They will show whether Gucci's volumes respond to lower prices, and whether LVMH's fashion and leather division can stabilise without conceding any.
