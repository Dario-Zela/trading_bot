Jet fuel bill puts European airlines' margins under pressure

In one sentence
An unverified £12bn jet fuel figure is circulating, but published hedging data suggest IAG, easyJet and Ryanair are well covered near term, with cover thinning into 2027.

A figure of £12bn is circulating as the cost that higher jet fuel could add to European airlines' bills. It is attributed to the Telegraph, and it has not been checked. The article itself could not be located, so its time frame, its definition of "European airlines" and its method are all unknown. The number is treated here as a claim rather than a fact.

The more useful question is how much of any price rise reaches the income statements of IAG, easyJet and Ryanair. The answer depends on hedging: the forward purchases that fix a share of fuel costs months or years ahead.

What the hedge books show

Published hedging data suggest the three carriers are better protected than a headline cost figure implies, at least for now. S&P Global reported in July that European airlines hedge roughly 70% to 87% of their fuel costs. It said those programmes absorbed much of the price volatility that followed this year's conflict-related supply disruption.

The carrier-level figures, as reported by S&P and others, vary:

  • easyJet: about 84% hedged for the first half of its financial year, 62% for the second half and 43% for the first half of 2027.
  • Ryanair: roughly 80% of next year's fuel requirement locked in. Michael O'Leary has reportedly said he is prepared to wait until "September, October" before hedging next summer.
  • IAG: reported hedging gains of €769m in the first half. One report says the group expects to recover about 60% of higher fuel costs through pricing and cost actions, and that it has paused new hedging contracts since the conflict began. That comes from a single source and should be read with caution.

These figures come from different dates and outlets, and the hedge ratios change with each results statement. They are indicative, not a current position.

Where the exposure sits

The pattern in the data is that cover is deep near term and thins into 2027. A carrier 80% hedged against a price spike pays only a fraction of the increase on that fuel, but the unhedged fraction, and any fuel bought after existing contracts expire, is priced at the market. If prices stay elevated, the squeeze arrives in later periods rather than the current one.

The second line of defence is fares. Ryanair has reportedly flagged European fare increases of 10% to 20% over the next twelve months. That is a reported remark, not a forecast from this desk. Whether demand tolerates such rises will matter as much to margins as the fuel price does.

A gross £12bn sector-wide figure also says little about margins on its own. It would need to be set against hedge gains, fuel surcharges and fare increases before it tells a reader anything about profit.

What to watch

The next data points are the carriers' own statements. Each trading update and results release will restate hedge ratios and the share of fuel cost passed on in fares. Until the Telegraph's figure can be matched to a stated period and method, and checked against those disclosures, it remains an unconfirmed headline.
