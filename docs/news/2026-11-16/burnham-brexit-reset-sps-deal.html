BREXIT RESET · By C. Tanaka · 2026-11-16 · 4 min read

Burnham says Brexit 'has not worked' and pledges to sign an EU reset, as a food-rules deal draws sovereignty fire

In one sentence
Prime Minister Andy Burnham is reported to have said Brexit 'has not worked' and to be ready to sign an EU reset, with a food-rules deal drawing sovereignty attacks; unverified.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said Brexit "has not worked" and that Britain lost more than it gained from leaving the European Union. He has pledged to sign a new reset agreement with Brussels at a UK-EU summit reportedly due on 20 November. If the reports hold, a sitting British prime minister would be saying in public what previous governments of both main parties avoided saying: that the 2016 decision has failed on its own terms.

A caveat applies throughout. The account rests on candidate reports citing the BBC, the Guardian and City AM. None of those originals has been checked against this article, and neither has the summit date. The quotations and the timetable below are attributed accordingly.

What Burnham is reported to have said

The reported words are blunt. Brexit "has not worked", and the UK "lost more than it gained". Burnham is said to have promised to sign a new deal with the EU ahead of, or at, the summit.

The phrase matters because of the position it replaces. Since the 2020 departure, ministers have generally described the EU relationship as a question of making the settlement work, not of judging whether it was right. They have also said they would not reopen it. A prime minister who calls the outcome a failure moves from managing the settlement to openly revising it.

What the text does not say is how far he intends to go. A "reset" is a trade-and-regulation exercise, not a reversal. Nothing in the material reviewed suggests rejoining the single market or the customs union, and none of it should be read into the reported remarks.

The food deal at the centre

The most concrete piece of the reset already has a name. At their summit on 19 May 2025, UK and EU leaders agreed to negotiate a sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement. SPS rules govern food safety and animal and plant health, and they are the checks that slow farm and food exports at the border.

As described in the reporting, the arrangement would have the UK align with EU SPS rules. In exchange, Britain would get a "voice" in how those rules are shaped, but no formal vote. The trade-off is plain. Britain takes rules it did not write, which makes agri-food trade smoother. It gets consultation rights, not decision rights.

That gap is what the deal's critics are using. The Conservatives and Reform UK have attacked the arrangement on sovereignty grounds, according to BBC Politics coverage cited in the briefing. The specific wording of their criticisms has not been verified here. The thrust is that dynamic alignment hands Brussels control over British food standards while Westminster keeps only the right to be heard.

Why the argument is hard to settle

Both sides have a point, which is why the dispute is durable. Supporters of the deal can say that alignment on food rules is a bounded concession in a narrow field. Producers and exporters, who have borne the border costs, get something tangible. Critics can answer that the precedent is the real cost. Once the UK accepts a rule-taking model in one sector, the pressure to extend it to others is built into the logic of the arrangement.

Burnham's reported remarks sharpen that argument. If the prime minister says Brexit has failed, opponents will say the SPS deal is the first step of a re-entry by instalments. Supporters will say it is simply a pragmatic repair. The frank language gives opponents more ammunition than a technocratic framing would have.

The politics also cut across Labour's own history. Labour has so far presented its EU approach as pragmatic and limited. A government that now describes the original decision as a failure may find that its more cautious earlier language sits awkwardly beside it.

What to watch

The first test is whether the summit date and the quotes survive checking against the original BBC and Guardian reports. The second is the text of whatever is signed. The detail that will matter most is how the "voice" is defined: whether it is a formal consultation mechanism with real timelines or something looser. The same applies to how disputes are resolved, and to which other sectors the reset is meant to cover.

Markets and businesses will read the same document for something more practical, namely how much regulatory divergence remains and how much border friction is removed. The sovereignty argument will run alongside it. The Conservatives and Reform have already set out their line, and the signing, if it happens on the reported date, will give them a specific text to attack.
