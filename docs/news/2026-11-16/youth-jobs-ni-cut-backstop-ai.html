Youth jobs: Milburn's NI cut and Burnham's 'benefits backstop' meet a survey of AI-driven redundancies

In one sentence
Milburn's proposed employer NI cut for under-25s, Burnham's floated 'benefits backstop' and an AI job-cuts survey all hinge on unconfirmed costings and methodology.

Britain's youth jobs problem has produced three separate responses in a short span. A review led by Alan Milburn has proposed cutting employer National Insurance for under-25s. Ministers around Andy Burnham have set out an early-intervention package and floated a 'benefits backstop'. A survey reported by The Telegraph suggests that about a quarter of UK employers plan job cuts because of artificial intelligence. Together they frame a question that none of them settles: whether the state can pay for young people's jobs, and whether employers will create them.

Several details were not available at the time of writing. They include the size of the proposed National Insurance cut, its cost and its start date, as well as any legislative timetable for the Burnham package. Where the sources do not supply a number, this article does not offer one.

The headline proposal: a cheaper young worker

The Milburn review's central idea is simple. Employers pay National Insurance contributions on the wages of staff they hire. Cut that charge for workers under 25 and, the argument runs, a young hire becomes cheaper relative to an older one, and more of them get taken on.

The appeal is political as much as economic. A payroll-tax cut is easy to explain, and it works through the employer's own hiring decision rather than through a new programme. The proposal is also described as a jobs measure aimed squarely at the young, a group that has fared badly as entry-level vacancies have thinned.

The weakness is the one that attends every such scheme. A payroll subsidy is paid on all young workers an employer takes on, including those the employer would have hired anyway. Economists call this deadweight: public money spent on hires that needed no encouragement. The more of a cut's cost that goes to existing hiring, the less it buys per pound. Evidence from earlier wage-subsidy and payroll-tax schemes in other countries is mixed, and the review's own assessment of that evidence needs reading in full before the proposal can be judged.

The cost is the real test. A cut would move expense from employers to the public purse. Whether the Treasury can accommodate that depends on a costing and a size that are not yet confirmed, and on a fiscal position in which any new giveaway must be paid for elsewhere. Until the government responds formally, this is a recommendation, not a policy.

The government's package: intent before law

The government's own approach sits alongside the review rather than inside it. Ministers have set out a youth employment package that includes early intervention for young people at risk of long-term unemployment, the idea being to reach those drifting out of education or work before a gap on the CV hardens into a career-long penalty.

The more striking element is the 'benefits backstop'. One minister has backed a legal right to job support for young people. That is a different kind of commitment from a tax cut. A tax cut nudges employers. A legal entitlement binds the state, which would then owe a young person support whether or not an employer is hiring.

It is important to be clear about status. The backstop has been floated by a minister and the package announced as a pledge. Nothing in the material available shows a costed budget, a bill or a start date. The headline outruns the reporting: this is announced intent, not enacted law.

The two ideas also pull in different directions on cost. The National Insurance cut is a revenue loss the Treasury would absorb. A legal backstop would be a spending commitment with no obvious ceiling if youth unemployment rose. Taken together they widen the potential bill, and the government has yet to say how it would reconcile them.

The AI variable

The third thread complicates both. The Telegraph reports a survey in which around a quarter of UK employers say they plan job cuts because of AI. If accurate, that would suggest a shift from hiring freezes, which quietly shut young people out of entry-level roles, towards outright redundancies.

The figure should be handled with care. The report gives no survey name, sample size or fieldwork dates in the material available, and it does not say whether the survey measured youth hiring specifically. A stated intention to cut jobs is also not the same as jobs lost; firms often report plans that are later scaled back or never carried out. The survey is best read as a signal of employer sentiment, not a measurement of redundancies.

Even so, it bears directly on the review's logic. A cut in employer National Insurance works by lowering the price of labour. If employers are weighing AI against headcount, the price of a young worker may not be the binding constraint. A firm that believes software can do an entry-level task will not necessarily be tempted back by a smaller payroll charge. The proposal's effect could therefore be weaker in exactly the sectors where young people most often start.

What to watch

The next data points are concrete. The first is the government's formal response to the Milburn review, which should show whether it accepts the National Insurance cut and, crucially, what the Treasury says it would cost. The second is whether the 'benefits backstop' acquires draft legislation or a funding line, or remains a ministerial aspiration. The third is the methodology behind the AI survey, which will determine how much weight its quarter-of-employers figure can bear.

If the cut is adopted, it will test an old proposition: that tax incentives can reduce youth unemployment at an acceptable price. If it is not, the backstop and early intervention will carry the weight alone, and the question will be whether announced intent can become funded policy.
