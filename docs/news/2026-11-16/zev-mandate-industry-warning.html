Industry warns that weakening UK EV targets would hurt carmakers

In one sentence
Industry groups are split over the UK ZEV mandate: carmakers want it eased, while others warn that loosening it would damage investment and jobs.

Britain's zero-emission vehicle mandate has become a dispute over who pays for the switch to electric cars. The carmakers are one side of it. Another group of industry voices, including charging firms and suppliers, warns that loosening the rules would itself cost investment and jobs. Ministers are being pressed from both directions.

The mandate, in force since 1 January 2024, sets a rising share of new car sales that must be zero-emission: 22% in 2024, 80% by 2030 and 100% by 2035. Manufacturers that miss the target buy credits from rivals or pay fines. In 2025 the government eased the terms. It cut the per-car penalty from £15,000 to £12,000 and added flexibilities such as borrowing and carry-over credits. The pattern is of rules bending under industry pressure, and the bending has not stopped the lobbying.

The carmakers' case

The Society of Motor Manufacturers and Traders (SMMT) has pressed for an urgent review. Its line, as reported in trade coverage, is that the mandate should be "not abandoned, but amended to reflect reality". It argues that manufacturers have spent more than £12bn on discounts and incentives to move electric cars, money that is not going into plants, models or staff. It also says electric demand still trails the mandate's trajectory, and it ties reform to wider asks on trade with the EU and on energy costs.

The argument rests on the 2030 and 2035 cliffs. A target that climbs steeply towards 80% leaves less room each year to absorb a shortfall in buyer demand. The industry says the gap is made up by cutting prices on electric models, which squeezes margins.

The other side of the ledger

The counter-argument is that uncertainty is the real cost. Charging operators and battery and component suppliers have invested on the assumption that the targets will hold. The Climate Change Committee has urged ministers not to weaken the mandate further. One industry analysis, from the trade body Beama, put the cost to the economy of a weaker target at at least £2.9bn, though the scenario it compared and its method have not been checked here.

This is where the cost-sharing question bites. Loosening the mandate would let manufacturers sell more petrol and hybrid cars for longer. The cost of that choice would fall elsewhere: on suppliers and charging firms that built capacity for an electric market, and on the climate targets the mandate exists to meet. Keeping the targets leaves the burden with carmakers, who say they are discounting electric cars at a loss, and potentially with the workers in UK plants if investment goes abroad.

What to watch

The government has previously said it would not weaken the mandate, while looking at flexibilities within it. That distinction matters. A further easing of fines, credit rules or hybrid allowances would be a smaller step than cutting the 2030 target, but it would send much the same signal about how far ministers will bend when climate rules are framed as a jobs issue. The signals to look for are any formal review of the mandate, and the monthly SMMT registration figures, which show how far electric sales sit below the required share.

The specific figures in this piece come from industry statements and trade reporting. The original Guardian report could not be read in full, so the identity of the group behind the latest warning is not confirmed here.
