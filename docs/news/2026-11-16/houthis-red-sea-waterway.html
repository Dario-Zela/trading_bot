Yemeni forces retreat, leaving Houthis in control of a key Red Sea waterway

In one sentence
Reported Houthi control of a Red Sea waterway is unconfirmed; whether it moves freight or energy prices depends on renewed attacks on shipping, which have not been reported.

A Red Sea waterway, reported by the BBC to have passed into Houthi hands after Yemeni government forces fell back, is the latest turn in a fight that has run along Yemen's western coast since September. The Bay of the story is the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the narrow gate between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden. The BBC's 16 November report, as summarised for this edition, does not name the waterway in the material available to The Bot Tribune. This article could not read the BBC piece itself, so the identification of the strait, the date of the retreat and the extent of Houthi control are unconfirmed. What follows sets out what independent reporting shows, and what would have to be true for markets to care.

What is known about the front

The coastal campaign has swung in both directions. In September, Reuters and other outlets reported that the Houthis, the Iran-aligned movement that holds Sanaa, took the Red Sea port of Mocha after government troops pulled out overnight. Mocha lies less than 50 miles from Bab el-Mandeb. Fighting then pushed government-aligned units south toward Dhubab, the stretch between Mocha and the strait, and the Houthis moved into the Hanish Islands after government naval units withdrew, according to Euronews and CGTN.

The government answered. On 5 October, Saudi-backed Yemeni forces said they had driven the Houthis out of key areas around the strait after a counteroffensive supported by Saudi air strikes. Information minister Moammar al-Eryani told Reuters that forces had "effectively secured control" of the strait. Reuters noted that this was a claim, and that the Houthis could still hit ships with drones and missiles from other positions.

No search for this article found independent confirmation of either side's hold on the strait after early October, nor anything dated November. A retreat that hands the strait's coast back to the Houthis would reverse the October claim. It would also fit a pattern in which territory around Mocha and Dhubab has changed hands more than once in a few weeks.

Why the geography matters

Bab el-Mandeb is about 18 miles wide at its narrowest and is split by Perim Island into two channels. Ships heading between Asia and the Suez Canal pass through it, carrying a large share of the container traffic and the oil and fuel cargoes bound for Europe. When the Houthis attacked merchant ships from late 2023, many carriers rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope, adding roughly ten days to Asia–Europe voyages and lifting freight and insurance costs.

Control of the coast is not the same as control of the sea. The Houthis' earlier campaign was waged with drones, missiles and small craft launched from territory they already held, including the Hodeidah coast well to the north. Taking a stretch of shoreline closer to the strait shortens the distance and improves surveillance and targeting. It does not by itself close a shipping lane, and a movement that already had the means to threaten traffic gains an incremental, not a categorical, advantage.

Priced in, or not?

The angle that matters to readers of the markets pages is whether this changes shipping risk enough to move freight and energy prices. The honest answer is that the evidence is not yet there. The freight and energy consequences are inference, not reported fact, and nothing in the BBC summary or in the independent coverage found here says attacks on ships have resumed or that carriers have changed routes this week.

Three things would change that. The first is a renewed Houthi attack on a merchant vessel in or near the strait. The second is a public decision by major carriers or war-risk underwriters to widen exclusion zones or lift premiums. The third is a visible move of tanker traffic away from the route. Absent those signals, a front-line shift that was already in motion since September is more likely to be treated as a local development than a new shock. Many shipping lines never returned fully to the Suez route in any case, so the marginal effect on freight capacity may be smaller than in 2023.

European airlines, whose jet fuel costs are already squeezing margins, are among those with an indirect stake in any renewed disruption to Gulf-to-Europe fuel flows; that story is covered elsewhere in this edition.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the BBC's account is corroborated by a second outlet and by Yemeni government and Saudi statements, which have so far favoured the opposite narrative. After that, the signals are maritime: advisories from the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre and the US-led naval task force, any reported launches toward shipping, and war-risk premium quotes for Red Sea transits. A confirmed attack would turn a territorial story into a trade one. Until then, the question of pricing remains open rather than answered.
