Fed's Schmid says inflation is moving the wrong way as markets open the week on hike risk

In one sentence
Kansas City Fed president Jeffrey Schmid says inflation is uncomfortably high and moving the wrong way, reinforcing hike pricing that began with Warsh's hawkish Jackson Hole speech.

A Federal Reserve policymaker has told markets that inflation is heading the wrong way, and that his task has become harder since Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech. Jeffrey Schmid, president of the Kansas City Fed, described price growth as "uncomfortably" above the 2% target. He said the central bank has more work to do, according to Benzinga's account of his remarks. The comments landed as markets opened the week already leaning towards a rate rise rather than a cut.

What Schmid said

Benzinga's summary gives three points. Inflation is uncomfortably above target. It is moving in the wrong direction. And the policy job has become tougher since Warsh spoke at Jackson Hole. The full text of the remarks was not available at the time of writing, so the wording beyond "uncomfortably" is paraphrase rather than quotation.

The message is consistent with Schmid's recent record. In July he said inflation was too high and had stayed above target for too long. In August he argued that tighter policy was needed to bring it back to 2%. He has also said the current policy rate, 3.50% to 3.75%, does not look restrictive. He has called inflation "stubborn" and "sticky", and has pointed to AI-related investment as one more source of price pressure.

That consistency matters for how the remarks should be read. This is not a conversion. It is a known hawk restating his case, with a new reference point in the chair's own speech.

The Warsh backdrop

Warsh's Jackson Hole address, his first speech as chair since May, was received as hawkish. According to AJ Bell's market report from 28 August, he said policymakers need confidence that inflation is slowing, or the Fed has "work to do". He called the 2% goal firm and fixed. He judged the economy to have strengthened, and described the price-stability numbers as "more concerning".

Markets moved at once. AJ Bell reported that futures priced about 53 basis points of tightening over twelve months, up from 45 beforehand. Gold fell on the hawkish tone. Treasury yields stood at 4.30% for the two-year, 4.68% for the ten-year and 5.18% for the 30-year. Pantheon's Samuel Tombs said a single 25bp hike by December could not be ruled out, though he still expected easing next year.

Those figures are from late August. The brief for this piece also drew on an AJ Bell roundup headlined on inflation and US rates, but the page the research located carries the 28 August date, not 16 November. It is therefore evidence of how the hawkish repricing began, not of where levels stand this week. Current pricing should be checked against live futures before it is cited.

One voice or a shift?

The test of whether this matters is whether Schmid is speaking for a wider group. Two points argue for caution. Kansas City is not among the presidents who vote on the FOMC this year under the rotation, so his view shapes the debate rather than the vote. And Warsh himself has avoided firm forward guidance. Reporting on his July testimony said he held back from signalling support for increases.

Two points argue the other way. Schmid's remarks tie his case directly to the chair's Jackson Hole language, which suggests the leadership's tone and a hawkish regional view are now pointing in the same direction. And the market response in August shows that investors were willing to add hike pricing on far less than a formal commitment.

The unresolved question is whether the committee's voters share the concern. A single hawkish president, however consistent, does not make a majority. Several voters echoing the "moving the wrong way" language would.

What to watch

The next catalysts are the inflation data and the Fed's own communications. Another firm price reading would lend weight to Schmid's argument, and a soft one would undercut it. The minutes of the latest FOMC meeting and the next round of speeches from voting presidents will show whether the hawkish view is spreading. Until one of those arrives, hike pricing rests on one chair's tone and a regional president's repetition of it.
