Union urges Burnham not to give £2bn military contract to US defence giant

In one sentence
Unite is urging ministers to give the Skynet 6 satellite contract, reported at £2bn–£2.4bn, to Airbus rather than Lockheed Martin, but no award decision has been confirmed.

A trade union is lobbying the government to give a multibillion-pound military satellite contract to a European manufacturer with British factories, not to a US defence group. The decision has been pending for months. As far as the available reporting shows, it has not been made.

The union is Unite. It wants the Skynet 6 wideband satellite system, a programme reported as worth about £2.4bn and so a little above the £2bn in the Telegraph's framing, to go to Airbus rather than Lockheed Martin. Its general secretary, Sharon Graham, has described an award to the American company as a "massive mistake". She has framed the choice as a test of Andy Burnham's pledge to back British industry.

What is being bought

Skynet 6 is the next stage of the UK's military satellite communications. The wideband system would put two geostationary satellites into orbit to carry the Ministry of Defence's most sensitive data. Airbus and its predecessor companies have built the UK's military satellites for decades, including the Skynet 5 generation, with work at Stevenage and Portsmouth. Unite says up to 600 jobs could be lost if the contract goes elsewhere.

Lockheed Martin's counter-offer is built around jobs and local presence. It has promised about 500 direct posts in north-east England, including an assembly facility in Durham, and a further 1,500 in the supply chain. It has also pledged to keep UK-developed intellectual property and has a research partnership with Northumbria University. Critics say the American firm would in practice lean on US intellectual property rather than UK suppliers. Neither company has commented publicly on the competition.

A decision still open

The reporting around the contract points to an unresolved argument inside government. Press accounts have said officials favoured the American bid, and that ministers were reluctant to settle the question while spending pressures remained. They have also suggested that the case for Airbus is partly political, since a US award could be read as a signal of where Britain's defence procurement is heading. The Ministry of Defence has said no final decisions have been made. Airbus has declined to comment while the procurement is live.

A few details in the coverage do not line up. The value is variously given as £2bn and £2.4bn, and Mr Burnham is described in some reports as Defence Secretary and in others as Prime Minister. The sole source cited for the triage headline is the Telegraph, and no procurement document confirms a timetable.

The read-across

For investors, the link to UK-listed defence stocks is thin. Airbus is listed in Paris, Lockheed in New York, and neither is a UK stock. The more meaningful signal is about procurement policy. A decision for Airbus would suggest that domestic jobs and sovereign control of intellectual property weigh heavily in large UK contracts. A decision for Lockheed would suggest that capability, cost and the US relationship still count for more. Either way, the outcome would be read by smaller British suppliers hoping for work on whichever prime contractor wins.

The next catalyst is an award announcement, which has so far been reported only as a possibility. Until then, the union's lobbying is a statement of position and not evidence of where the government is leaning.
