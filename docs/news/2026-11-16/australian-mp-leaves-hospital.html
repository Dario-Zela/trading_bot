Australian MP leaves hospital after alleged car attack

In one sentence
Independent MP Helen Haines has left hospital, thanking supporters, after what police allege was a deliberate car attack in Wangaratta; the motive is still unknown.

An Australian member of parliament has left hospital weeks after what police allege was a deliberate car attack, and has said she is "full of gratitude" to those who have supported her. The report of her release, carried by BBC World, gives few details beyond that. The surrounding facts are better documented, and they make this more than a routine discharge notice.

The MP is Helen Haines, the independent member for the rural Victorian seat of Indi. The Tribune could not independently confirm the discharge date or the full text of her statement. The hospital stopped issuing daily condition updates on 6 November, when she was described as stable.

What happened

On Monday 19 October, Haines was struck by a car outside her electoral office in Wangaratta, in north-east Victoria. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said she had been deliberately run over twice. A local barber, Ricky Jefferies, reportedly ran out and restrained the driver, believing at first that he had witnessed a crash.

Her injuries were severe. Doctors amputated her left leg and could not save the right. Her husband, Phil Haines, told reporters: "The good news in all this is Helen is alive, and she's stable." She was treated at the Alfred Hospital in Melbourne. Her condition was reported as critical but stable, then serious but stable, then simply stable.

The case against the suspect

Brendan Nugent, 43, has been charged with:

  • dangerous driving causing serious injury;
  • intentionally causing injury;
  • causing harm to a public official;
  • cultivation and possession of cannabis, according to court filings.

He made a brief court appearance on 21 October. His lawyer said he has a rare neurological condition, idiopathic transverse myelitis, and was recovering from a concussion. He has yet to enter a plea and is due back in court in February. Police have not publicly established a motive.

Federal Police Commissioner Krissy Barrett said authorities held no intelligence that Haines was under threat, and none that would have pointed to an attack of this kind that afternoon. That matters for the wider debate. Nothing yet published suggests the attack followed a known campaign against her.

A single incident, or a pattern?

Attacks on politicians are rare in Australia, though concern has grown over online abuse and threats against elected officials. On the evidence available, this case does not show a trend. The motive is unknown, the defence has raised a medical condition, and police have said nothing to link the alleged attack to Haines's politics. Reading it as part of a wider rise in political violence would go beyond what has been established.

It still carries weight for public life. Haines is a rural independent who has held Indi since 2019, retaining it in 2022 and 2025. She was attacked at her constituency office, the kind of open-door local setting where MPs meet voters with little or no security. Whatever the motive turns out to be, the case is likely to renew questions about how such offices are protected.

What to watch

The next fixed point is Nugent's return to court in February, when a plea and possibly more detail on the prosecution case may emerge. Any police finding on motive would decide whether this is read as a personal crime or a political one. Haines's own plans are unclear: she has not said when, or whether, she will return to parliament.
