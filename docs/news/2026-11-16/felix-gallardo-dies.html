Mexican drug lord Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo dies aged 80

In one sentence
Félix Gallardo, founder of the Guadalajara cartel, died at 80, but the regional networks born from his arrest-era split still run Mexico's trafficking map.

Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo, the man who built Mexico's first modern drug-trafficking federation and then watched it break apart, has died at 80. Jalisco's security ministry said he died on Sunday 15 November in a private hospital in Guadalajara, with multiple organ failure given as the cause, according to Mexican press reports. He was still in prison, 37 years into his sentence.

He is remembered less for how he died than for what he left behind. The territorial carve-up that followed his arrest still shapes the map of Mexican trafficking, and his death does not alter that map.

The founder

Born in Sinaloa in 1946, Félix Gallardo began as a state police officer before moving into the drug trade. In the 1980s he co-founded the Guadalajara cartel with Rafael Caro Quintero and Ernesto Fonseca Carrillo. Mexican media gave him the nickname "El Jefe de Jefes" — the boss of bosses — because he coordinated the country's trafficking operators under one roof rather than leaving them to compete. That arrangement gave him an unusual degree of control over supply routes and the officials who protected them.

The arrangement ended with a murder. In 1985 the US Drug Enforcement Administration agent Enrique "Kiki" Camarena was kidnapped and killed in Guadalajara. Washington's response was relentless, and Félix Gallardo was arrested in 1989. He was convicted in 1990 over Camarena's killing and sentenced to 40 years. He was later also held responsible for the killing of the pilot Alfredo Zavala Avelar, and press reports say he was serving two sentences totalling 77 years for drug offences and those homicides.

The split that outlasted him

The more consequential act came from prison, or just before it. After his arrest, the cartel's territories were divided among its former lieutenants and associates. The pieces became the Sinaloa, Juárez and Tijuana organisations, which went on to dominate Mexican trafficking for decades.

That history matters because it shows how the structure worked. A single boss had been the point of coordination. Once he was removed, the network did not dissolve; it separated into regional franchises with their own leaders, routes and rivalries. Later fractures within those groups have produced the more numerous and more violent factions of today. The pattern of a founder removed and a structure persisting recurs through the story of Mexican organised crime.

By the time of his death Félix Gallardo had long been a figure of the past rather than of current power. In 2022 a judge granted him house arrest on health grounds, but prosecutors challenged the ruling and he stayed in the Puente Grande prison complex. Reports say he was taken to hospital in the days before he died. His death therefore changes nothing in the present balance among the cartels. It is a historical marker, not a shift in power.

What to watch

The immediate questions are small ones: where he will be buried, which Mexican reports have not yet said, and whether his family or the authorities say more about his final days. The larger point is that the organisations descended from his split have outlived him, as they outlived his arrest.
