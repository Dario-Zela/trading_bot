Tories pledge to halt screen-based school exams and cut coursework

In one sentence
The Conservatives, in opposition, have pledged to move school exams off screens and cut coursework, but costings, timetable and affected exam boards have not been set out.

The Conservatives have pledged that, if returned to government, they would move school exams in England back off screens and reduce the share of grades that comes from coursework. The announcement, reported by BBC Politics, is an opposition promise. It reverses a direction of travel rather than changing any current rule. No policy has been altered, and the detail so far is thin.

The source material does not say what the pledge would cost, when it would take effect, or which exam boards and qualifications it would cover. Those gaps matter, because the practical meaning of "scrapping" screen-based exams depends on how many exist to be scrapped.

Where on-screen exams actually stand

School exams in England remain overwhelmingly pen and paper. The regulator, Ofqual, consulted in late 2025 on how it would regulate on-screen assessment in GCSEs, AS and A levels. Its proposals kept paper central. Coverage of the consultation indicated that the most heavily entered subjects would stay on paper for now, and that wider digital assessment was unlikely before the 2030s.

The pledge is therefore aimed less at a system already in place than at a direction of travel. Exam boards and ministers have explored digital delivery as a way to modernise marking and reduce logistical burdens. Supporters of paper argue that it is the more reliable and equitable format. One UnHerd article from July made the case that moving exams online disadvantages working-class pupils, and a ConservativeHome piece in January argued that AI has made unsupervised assessment unreliable. Neither is party policy, but both show the argument the pledge draws on.

Why coursework is in the frame

The coursework half of the pledge follows a similar logic. Coursework completed outside supervised conditions is harder to verify as a pupil's own work now that generative AI tools can produce plausible essays on demand. Cutting its weight, and returning more of the final grade to timed, invigilated papers, is a way of restoring confidence in results. The trade-off is familiar. Coursework suits pupils who do better away from exam-hall pressure, and critics of exam-heavy systems say that a narrower reliance on terminal papers rewards memory and speed over sustained work.

The announcement does not specify which subjects or qualifications would be affected, or by how much coursework would fall. Without those figures it is hard to say whether this is a modest rebalancing or a substantial redesign.

What to watch

The test will be whether the pledge acquires detail: a stated cap on non-exam assessment, a timetable, and some account of how it would sit with Ofqual's independent role in setting the rules. Ofqual's own decision on regulating on-screen assessment, and any response from the government and exam boards, will show how far the Conservatives are departing from the current course. Until then, it is a marker of intent in what could become a live education dispute ahead of the next election.
