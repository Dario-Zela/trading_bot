Home Office accused of trying to cancel online talk by Andreas Malm

In one sentence
The Guardian reports the Home Office tried to stop an online talk by climate author Andreas Malm; details, grounds and any official response are not yet established.

A free-speech dispute has opened in Britain over a report that the Home Office tried to stop an online talk by Andreas Malm, the Swedish climate writer and academic. The only account so far comes from the Guardian's politics coverage. It says the department sought to cancel the event. The material available to The Bot Tribune gives no date for the talk, no name for the host and no response from the Home Office. The allegation should be read as an allegation.

Who Malm is

Malm teaches human ecology at Lund University in Sweden. He is best known for Fossil Capital, a history of how steam power came to dominate British industry, and for How to Blow Up a Pipeline (2020). The second book argues that the climate movement should consider property destruction aimed at fossil-fuel infrastructure. It was adapted into a 2022 film of the same name.

The book has made him a divisive figure. Supporters describe him as a serious scholar asking whether decades of peaceful campaigning have failed. Critics say he normalises sabotage. He has said he is arguing for attacks on property rather than on people, and the argument sits within a long tradition of debate about civil disobedience.

What is alleged, and what is not known

The claim is that officials tried to prevent an online event at which Malm was due to speak. Several basic questions remain open:

  • Mechanism. It is unclear whether the department made a formal request or an informal approach, and to whom. That could mean a platform, a host institution or the organisers.
  • Grounds. No legal or policy basis has been reported. Ministers have in recent years leaned on counter-extremism and public-order powers when dealing with protest groups. Whether any such rationale was cited here is unknown.
  • Outcome. It is not stated whether the talk went ahead, was moved or was cancelled.
  • Response. No Home Office statement has been seen.

Until those points are filled in, the story rests on one report, and its weight depends on the detail behind it.

Why it matters if confirmed

The case would sit within a wider argument about how far the state may go in discouraging speech that is lawful but unwelcome. Britain protects freedom of expression under Article 10 of the European Convention on Human Rights, as incorporated by the Human Rights Act. Government pressure on a private event, as opposed to a ban backed by law, is the kind of grey area that tends to draw challenge. It matters more when the speaker is a foreign academic addressing an online audience, because the usual tools of immigration control do not obviously apply.

It would also add to an existing tension over climate protest. Direct-action groups have faced tougher policing and sentencing in the UK in recent years, and ministers have described some tactics as a threat to public order. A department intervening over a talk, rather than a protest, would move that argument from conduct to ideas.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether the Home Office confirms or denies the report and, if it confirms it, what power it says it relied on. Organisers of the event and any platform involved may also say what they were asked to do. Opposition MPs and free-speech groups are likely to press for answers, and a formal request for the correspondence would show quickly whether this was a policy decision or an informal approach.
