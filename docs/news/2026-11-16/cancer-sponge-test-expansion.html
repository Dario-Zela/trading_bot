Cancer 'sponge' tests could be offered to more people with bad indigestion

In one sentence
The NHS is looking at offering the swallowable sponge test to more people with persistent indigestion, though evidence on lives saved is still awaited.

The NHS is looking at offering a swallowable 'sponge' test to more people with persistent indigestion or heartburn. The aim is to find oesophageal cancer, and the condition that can precede it, without sending every patient for an endoscopy first. The BBC reports that wider use could speed diagnosis. The report's specific rollout numbers and timeline could not be independently confirmed for this article, so they are not repeated here.

How the test works

The device is a pill-sized capsule attached to a string. The patient swallows it with water. Once it reaches the stomach, the capsule dissolves and releases a small mesh sponge. The clinician then pulls the string to bring the sponge back up. As it travels through the oesophagus it picks up cells from the lining, and these are sent to a laboratory for analysis.[^1]

The lab looks for changes associated with Barrett's oesophagus, a condition in which the lining of the gullet is altered by long-term acid reflux. Barrett's can precede oesophageal cancer in a minority of cases. The attraction of the test is that it can be done in a few minutes, without sedation and without the equipment and staffing a conventional endoscopy needs.

What the evidence shows

The test, originally developed in Cambridge under the name Cytosponge, has been used in NHS England since 2021. NHS England said in February 2024 that around 8,500 patients had been tested at about 30 hospitals. It reported that roughly eight in ten were able to avoid a further endoscopy, and that the approach was cost-saving, at around £400 per patient compared with endoscopy alone.[^2] These figures come from NHS England's own announcement, not from an independent audit, and 'discharged without further testing' is not the same as a measured effect on cancer outcomes.

Scotland began its own rollout in October 2020 in NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde and NHS Lanarkshire.[^3]

More recently, pilots have moved into the community. In July 2025 it was reported that a dozen pharmacies in the East Midlands and London would offer the capsule sponge test (marketed by Cyted as EndoSign) to people with long-running heartburn, with around 1,500 people expected to be reached from early 2026 and an evaluation to follow.[^4] I could not find a published result of that evaluation or a confirmed decision on national adoption, so how far any expansion has actually been approved remains unclear from the sources checked.

What is still unproven

The key open question is whether earlier detection translates into fewer deaths. Cancer Research UK and the NIHR are funding a large trial, aiming to recruit 120,000 people, to test whether offering the sponge to people with reflux lowers oesophageal cancer mortality.[^5] Until it reports, the case for wider use rests on shorter waits, lower costs and freeing endoscopy capacity, rather than on proven survival gains.

There are practical caveats too. A positive sponge result still leads to an endoscopy, so the test sorts patients rather than replacing the procedure outright. And extending it from people on a Barrett's surveillance pathway to a wider population with indigestion will mean more tests, more lab work and more follow-up, which health-service capacity will have to absorb.

What to watch

The markers to follow are the evaluation of the pharmacy pilots, any formal NHS England guidance or funding commitment on broader use, and the eventual results of the mortality trial. Oesophageal cancer is often diagnosed late and has poor survival, which is why even a modest gain in how early patients enter the diagnostic pathway would matter.
