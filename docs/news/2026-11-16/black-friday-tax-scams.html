Black Friday and tax return scams: how to spot them

In one sentence
With Black Friday on 27 November, consumer advice repeats familiar warnings on fake shops, delivery texts and tax-refund phishing; no new loss data or scam types are reported.

Every autumn the same email arrives, dressed a little differently. A parcel could not be delivered. A tax refund is waiting. A games console is on sale at a price that no retailer could sustain. With Black Friday falling on Friday 27 November, the day after US Thanksgiving, the season when shoppers are most distracted and most willing to click is nearly here.

This is a reminder rather than a scoop. The BBC's latest consumer-advice item, which prompted this note, gives no new loss figures, victim counts or novel scam methods, and none are claimed here. Nothing in it points to an effect on markets. What it offers is a seasonal checklist, and the checklist is worth repeating because the tricks hardly change.

The shop that is too good to be true

The classic Black Friday fraud is the fake storefront: a slick website, often built in an afternoon, advertising branded goods at deep discounts. The buyer pays by card or bank transfer and receives nothing, or a counterfeit. Social-media adverts and sponsored search results carry many of them, because a convincing logo costs almost nothing to copy.

The usual defences are unglamorous:

  • Check the web address closely. Lookalike domains with an extra word or a swapped letter are common.
  • Be wary of any price far below every other retailer's. Real sales trim margins; they do not abolish them.
  • Pay by credit card where possible. In the UK, Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act makes the card issuer jointly liable for purchases between £100 and £30,000, a protection that bank transfers lack.
  • Avoid sellers who insist on transfers, gift cards or cryptocurrency.

Delivery texts and the tax refund

Black Friday also fills inboxes with parcels, which makes fake delivery notices plausible. A text claiming a package is held and asking for a small redelivery fee is designed to harvest card details, not to move a box. The safer route is to go to the retailer's or courier's own app or website rather than follow a link.

Tax-return scams ride a different calendar but the same psychology. In the UK, the Self Assessment deadline on 31 January draws a steady flow of messages impersonating HMRC, offering a refund or threatening penalties and asking for bank details. Tax authorities in both Britain and the US say they do not demand payment or personal data by text or unsolicited email, and that genuine refunds are not arranged through links in messages. Urgency is the tell: a deadline of hours, a threat of arrest, a refund that must be claimed now.

What to do if caught out

The advice is consistent across regulators and banks. Contact the card issuer or bank at once, using the number on the card rather than one in the message. Change passwords on any account that shared the compromised details. Report the attempt: in the UK, suspicious emails go to the National Cyber Security Centre and texts can be forwarded to 7726, while Action Fraud takes reports of losses. In the US, the Federal Trade Commission runs ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

The next fixed dates are plain enough. Black Friday itself arrives on 27 November, with Cyber Monday following on 30 November, and the Self Assessment deadline lands at the end of January. Fraud reports tend to be tallied afterwards, and any figures showing whether the problem is growing will come from those releases, not from the advice that precedes them.
