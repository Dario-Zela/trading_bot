Wealthy Americans haven't saved enough for retirement either

In one sentence
An HSBC survey of affluent Americans finds they have saved on average just 52% of the $2.6m they say they need to retire comfortably.

A retirement shortfall is usually pictured as a problem of the low-paid: people on thin wages, with patchy pension coverage and little left over at the end of the month. A newly released survey from HSBC suggests the gap reaches a good deal further up the income ladder. Americans who count as affluent say they have so far put aside roughly half of what they think they will need.

The research, conducted by Ipsos and reported by Fortune on 3 November, defines affluent as having at least $100,000 in investable assets. Those respondents put the sum needed for a comfortable retirement at $2.6m, up 26% on a year earlier. On average they have saved 52% of it. The sample size and the survey's formal name were not given in the coverage, and the full methodology has not been checked here.

A target that keeps moving

The arithmetic is partly about the goalposts. A 26% rise in the stated target in twelve months means that even steady saving can leave someone further from the line than before. The figures vary sharply by age:

  • Baby boomers: 74% of their goal
  • Gen X: 55%
  • Gen Z: 45%
  • Millennials: 42%

No generation is fully on track, and the cohorts furthest from retirement are the ones with the most ground to cover. That is a smaller worry than it sounds for Gen Z, who have decades of contributions ahead. For millennials, who are mid-career and often carrying housing and childcare costs, it is harder to wave away.

"A comfortable retirement is becoming more expensive, even for those who have substantial wealth," said Racquel Oden, HSBC's US head of international wealth and premier banking.

Longer lives, longer lists

The survey suggests the money is being asked to do more than it once did. Some 43% of respondents named funding retirement as their top goal, but 37% also cited supporting children and ageing parents, and 39% personal or professional reinvention. Longevity is changing the plan: 59% said living longer has altered their legacy intentions, and 31% are putting more towards their own future costs even if that shrinks what heirs receive. Almost half (48%) said their main aim is for children or grandchildren to become fully financially independent.

Health care sits underneath much of this. Fidelity's July estimate is that a 65-year-old retiring this year can expect to spend an average of $185,500 on health care, up 7.5% year on year, a figure that does not care much about the size of the portfolio around it.

Other surveys point the same way, though they are not directly comparable. BlackRock found respondents saying they need about $2.1m, with 62% holding less than $150,000. Catherine Collinson, chief executive of the Transamerica Institute, described the combination of pressures as "a perfect storm".

What it means for the usual advice

Self-reported targets are not the same as actuarial need, and a survey of people with $100,000 or more in assets is a wide net; it takes in many who would not think of themselves as wealthy. Respondents may also name a number that reflects anxiety as much as arithmetic. Similar findings go back more than a decade, when Natixis found that many millionaires did not know how much they needed.

Still, the pattern challenges a comfortable assumption. If households with real assets and decent incomes also feel short, then "save more" is incomplete advice. The harder questions are what retirement is expected to pay for, how long it must last, and how much of the bill falls on children and parents at the same time. The next data point to watch is whether the stated target keeps climbing when HSBC repeats the research next year.
