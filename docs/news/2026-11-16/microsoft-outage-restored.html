Microsoft says services fully restored after day-long global outage

File photo of a sign on the Microsoft campus in Redmond, Washington. — Reuters
In one sentence
Microsoft says services are fully back after an outage of more than a day across 74 countries, blamed on a flawed network upgrade, not an attack, and it has revived concerns about cloud concentration.

Microsoft said on Saturday that its services were fully restored after an outage that began on Thursday and ran for more than a day. Outlook, Teams and Copilot were among the products hit, and reports put the affected users in 74 countries. The company has given a cause, and it is an internal one. The question the episode leaves behind is how much ordinary life now runs through a handful of cloud providers.

What happened

The trouble began at about 16:00 GMT on Thursday 12 November, according to Al Jazeera's account. It spread across Microsoft 365, the company's bundle of email, chat and office software, and its Copilot AI assistant. Microsoft restored service gradually rather than all at once. On Saturday it said it had "restored service to the remaining impacted customers and regions".

Microsoft's explanation is technical and fairly modest. It said an earlier network-capacity upgrade introduced a latent fault, which then surfaced after a later change to its systems. It also said there was no sign of a cyberattack and no involvement of AI tools or agents. It apologised, saying it was "deeply sorry for the disruption this incident has caused our customers and partners".

One caveat on the numbers. Coverage disagrees on duration: some outlets say "more than 24 hours", while one headline says two days. The 74-country figure comes from press reports, and Microsoft's own post-incident report, if it publishes one, would be the place to check it. Neither the BBC report cited in triage nor Microsoft's status history was available to confirm the finer details.

Who felt it

The disruption reached well beyond office email. Reports from the outage describe:

  • Taiwan's foreign banks falling back on fax.
  • Euronext asking traders to phone in trades.
  • Norway's parliament losing email.
  • Between 80 and 90 Danish municipalities affected.
  • Rail operators in Germany, the UK and Canada struggling with timetables and tickets.
  • The NHS moving to paper records.
  • A trial in Manchester halted after the judge sent jurors home because of delays.

The governments of Japan, Singapore and Australia also reported operational effects. None of these institutions chose to depend on a single vendor's network upgrade. They depended on it because email, calendars and file storage sit on the same platform, and that platform is the default almost everywhere.

The concentration question

The case for worry is simple arithmetic. When one provider's fault can stall courts, hospitals and trading desks in different countries at once, the failure is no longer local. Spreading workloads across regions inside one vendor does not help when the fault is in the vendor's own change process, as it appears to have been here.

Walter Pasquarelli of the University of Cambridge called it "first and foremost a communication risk". That framing is useful. Much of the damage came from organisations being unable to tell staff and customers what was going on, because the tools they would use to do so were the ones that had failed.

There are limits to what this proves. One outage, even a long one, does not show that the cloud is less reliable than the self-run servers it replaced; those failed too, usually in less public ways. What it does show is that fallback plans such as fax, phone trading and paper records worked only because some institutions still remembered how to use them. Whether others have a tested plan, and not just a contract with a service-level promise, is the practical test.

What to watch

The next marker is Microsoft's formal post-incident review, which should say how a capacity upgrade went unnoticed long enough to cause a day of disruption. Regulators in Europe, where financial and public-sector customers face operational-resilience rules, are likely to ask for it. Customers, for their part, will be looking for whether the review changes how Microsoft rolls out network changes, rather than only how it apologises for them.
