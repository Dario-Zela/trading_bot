Special needs overhaul in England opens new routes to extra support

In one sentence
England's SEND overhaul widens routes to support, but who bears the multibillion-pound cost, councils or the Treasury, will not be settled until the 2027 Spending Review.

England's system for supporting children with special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) is being rebuilt, and the part of the story that matters most to town halls and the Treasury is the one the announcements say least about: who pays, and when.

The Guardian reports that reforms to SEND provision have been set out, opening new routes by which children can get extra help. No cost figure accompanied that report. Any estimate of the bill for councils or central government therefore rests on earlier published material, not on new numbers.

The system under strain

SEND support in England runs mostly through education, health and care plans (EHCPs), legal documents that oblige a local authority to secure specified help for a child. Demand for them has risen steadily, and the cost has fallen on councils' high-needs budgets. The Office for Budget Responsibility has forecast that the gap between council SEND funding and spending would reach about £6bn by 2028-29. Many councils have kept the resulting deficits off their main accounts under an accounting arrangement that ministers have extended, which is a temporary fix, not a settlement.

What the reforms propose

The government's Schools White Paper, "Every Child Achieving and Thriving", published on 23 February 2026, set out the direction. It was backed by a package of about £4bn over three years, including a £1.6bn inclusive mainstream fund paid directly into school budgets. The idea is that more children get help earlier, inside ordinary schools, without needing a statutory plan. The paper also proposed limiting what councils pay independent special schools to a "reasonable price". Consultation ran until 18 May, with draft legislation to follow.

The fiscal logic is that wider, earlier support should reduce reliance on costly specialist placements. That is a bet. If new routes to support widen eligibility faster than placements fall, total spending rises before any saving appears.

Who carries the cost

The government has said that from 2028-29 it will absorb SEND costs within departmental budgets, meaning the liability would shift from council reserves to the centre. How much money accompanies that shift is to be settled at the next Spending Review, expected in 2027. Critics, including commentators who called the white paper a blank cheque, argue that the Treasury has not said how it will be paid for and that the £4bn is unlikely to close the whole gap. Unions said the funding was not enough. Sources differ on how much of the £4bn is genuinely new.

For families, the practical test is whether the new routes deliver support in schools sooner than the old plan process did. For councils, it is whether the deficit is cleared or merely relabelled.

What to watch

Two things will settle the question: the text of the draft legislation, which will show who qualifies for what, and the 2027 Spending Review, which will show how much the Treasury commits for 2028-29 onwards. Until then the headline reforms are clearer than their price.
