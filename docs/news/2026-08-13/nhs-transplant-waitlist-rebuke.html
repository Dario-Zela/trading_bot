NHS Blood and Transplant chief rebukes leadership over organ waiting lists

A kidney removed from a living donor for transplant — kidneys make up the large majority of the UK's record transplant waiting list. — Rmarlin / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
NHSBT's transplant leadership publicly criticised the NHS over unfair waits, as consent for donation fell from 69% to 57% in five years and South Asian patients wait 20-60% longer for a kidney.

The most senior transplant clinicians at NHS Blood and Transplant have gone public this month with an unusually blunt message for the rest of the health service: the system for deciding who gets an organ, and how quickly, is failing patients unfairly — and NHS leadership has not fixed it.

The criticism lands alongside NHS Blood and Transplant's own annual figures, published August 5th, which are hard to read as anything but a system under strain. A record 8,296 patients, including 241 children, were on the UK's active transplant waiting list as of March 31st 2026. Family consent for donation — the single biggest bottleneck in the system — has slid from 69% in 2020/21 to just 57% last year, a near fifth of its base rate lost in five years, despite England, Scotland and Wales all having moved to opt-out consent laws over that period.[^1]

Kidneys make up the bulk of the strain. Kidney Research UK's response to the same figures counted 7,203 people waiting for a kidney — 264 more than the year before — with 328 dying while on the list. Average wait time rose from 503 days to 547, even as the number of kidney transplants performed fell by 27 on the previous year.[^2]

Where the unfairness bites hardest

The disparity the NHSBT chief singled out is not evenly spread. Patients of South Asian heritage wait an average of 648 days for a kidney — 20% longer than the national average — and for South Asian children the gap widens to 709 days against 450 for other children, a 60% premium.[^3] The supply side explains much of it: only 26 deceased donors of South Asian heritage came forward across the UK in the year to March 2026, against 554 patients of the same heritage on the waiting list. Family consent among South Asian heritage households runs at 67%, versus 85% for all other ethnic groups combined — even when the deceased had already placed their name on the NHS Organ Donor Register.[^4] Blood and tissue types cluster by ancestry, so a shortage of South Asian donors does not merely delay transplants for South Asian patients — it makes many of them structurally harder to match at all.

Blame, but not yet a fix

What NHSBT's leadership has not offered, in the reporting so far, is a costed plan to close the gap — only a call for the rest of the NHS to review allocation pathways, tackle workforce and operating-theatre capacity shortages, and take "coordinated" action on equity of access. That is a familiar pattern in NHS disputes: a national body identifies a systemic failure sitting largely outside its own control — hospital trusts schedule the transplant surgery, not NHSBT — and calls on leadership elsewhere to act. NHSBT's own lever is softer: a campaign, launched the same week, to add a million new names to the 29-million-strong Organ Donor Register by the start of 2027, on the logic that a registered decision makes it easier for grieving families to say yes.[^5]

Whether that campaign moves the consent needle will be visible in next year's activity report. Until then, the more immediate marker to watch is whether NHS England's leadership responds substantively to the criticism, or whether — as with previous NHSBT warnings on donor shortages — it becomes a recurring feature of each August's transplant statistics rather than a trigger for change.
