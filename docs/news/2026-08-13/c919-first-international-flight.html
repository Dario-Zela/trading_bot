China's C919 jet completes first international commercial flight

A Comac C919 takes off — the jet flew its first scheduled international route, Beijing to Ulaanbaatar, on August 12, 2026. — Windmemories / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China's C919 flew its first scheduled international route on August 12, but Western certification delays and shaky production keep it far from threatening Boeing and Airbus outside China.

The flight itself was unremarkable: a two-hour hop from Beijing to Ulaanbaatar, carrying passengers who would have made the same journey on a Boeing 737 MAX the day before. What made Air China flight CA723 news on August 12 was the aircraft in front — a Comac C919, China's homegrown narrow-body jet, making its first scheduled international commercial flight after three years of flying only domestic routes.

The C919 took off from Beijing Capital International Airport at 3pm local time and landed in Mongolia's capital two hours later, replacing a 737 MAX 8 on a route Air China now plans to fly daily. Comac says the flight shows the jet has cleared bilateral aviation-safety checks between Chinese and Mongolian regulators, formally qualifying it for scheduled international passenger service for the first time. As of August 10, the aircraft had flown 57 domestic routes linking 25 Chinese cities and carried more than 7.5 million passengers since entering service in May 2023.

A market Boeing and Airbus have owned for decades

The C919 matters because of what it's aimed at, not what it did on Wednesday. It's a 158-to-192-seat single-aisle jet built to compete directly with the Boeing 737 MAX and Airbus A320neo — the two families that dominate the busiest and most profitable segment in commercial aviation, short-to-medium-haul routes flown by the world's biggest low-cost and legacy carriers alike. No manufacturer has broken that duopoly in the modern jet era. Every Chinese, Russian, Brazilian or Canadian narrow-body project of the past thirty years has either stalled, stayed regional, or been absorbed into one of the two giants' supply chains.

Comac has spent close to two decades and, by most estimates, tens of billions of dollars trying to be the exception. The company says it holds order commitments for roughly 1,000 C919s. Almost all of them come from Chinese state-owned airlines and leasing companies, placed at the direction of Beijing rather than won in open competition — Air China, China Eastern and China Southern together make up the entire in-service fleet. No foreign carrier has ordered one. Malaysia Airlines, seen as a plausible early foreign customer given its geographic and political proximity to China, has said publicly it won't place an order until the jet is certified by either European or American regulators.

That certification is the real barrier, and it isn't close. The C919 holds a type certificate from China's own aviation regulator, but not from the European Union Aviation Safety Agency or America's FAA — without one of which it cannot legally fly commercial routes into most of the world's aviation markets. EASA has had inspection teams conducting validation test flights in Shanghai, and the agency's director said in 2025 that full certification could take three to six more years, landing anywhere between 2028 and 2031. Until then, the C919's international ambitions are effectively limited to countries willing to accept China's own safety sign-off — a group that, for now, extends to Mongolia and not much further.

Production is the other constraint

Even inside China, Comac has struggled to build the jets fast enough to work through its own backlog. The company delivered only 15 C919s in 2025, against an internal target of 75, and reporting from early 2026 put first-quarter deliveries at just three aircraft. Analysts point to a familiar culprit: the CFM LEAP-1C engine, built by the US-French joint venture CFM International, has been arriving late and in short supply, with shortages of components like turbine blades rippling back through the supply chain. Export licence disruptions tied to US-China trade friction added a further, and more geopolitically fraught, source of delay in 2025.

That dependence on a Western engine supplier is one of the odder features of a jet marketed as a symbol of Chinese industrial self-sufficiency. It also means Comac's production rate is hostage to the same kind of supply-chain fragility that has dogged Boeing, whose own 737 MAX programme is only now climbing back toward a planned 47 jets a month in 2026 after years of certification setbacks, a paused-delivery episode over wiring damage, and a rebuilding supplier base. Boeing's problems are real, but they are the problems of a manufacturer producing dozens of aircraft a month; Comac's are the problems of one still trying to reach single-digit monthly output reliably.

What the milestone actually proves

Wednesday's flight confirms something narrow and specific: that a Chinese-built narrow-body jet can now be flown across a border on a scheduled route, cleared by regulators other than Beijing's own. It does not confirm that Comac can build these aircraft at the pace, cost or reliability needed to take share from Boeing and Airbus outside China, and it does nothing to change the certification calendar in Brussels or Washington that keeps the C919 out of Europe and the United States for years yet.

The more useful test will be less symbolic. Watch whether Comac can lift 2026 deliveries meaningfully above last year's shortfall, whether EASA's Shanghai test-flight campaign produces a firmer certification date than "sometime before 2031," and whether a single non-Chinese airline signs a firm order rather than an expression of interest. Until one of those three things happens, the C919's competition with Boeing and Airbus remains a story about domestic substitution — a very large captive market, not yet a global one.
