Farage returns to Commons via Clacton as Labour edges back ahead of Reform

Clacton-on-Sea, the Essex seafront constituency Nigel Farage vacated and then won back in a record 34-candidate by-election. — John Fielding / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Farage reclaims Clacton in a landslide as Ipsos shows Labour edging ahead of Reform again — but under Burnham, not Starmer, who resigned in June.

Nigel Farage is back in the House of Commons — again — after winning the Clacton by-election he himself triggered five weeks ago. The Essex seat he vacated amid scrutiny of undeclared political donations returned him with a landslide against a field that included 34 candidates, a record for any UK parliamentary contest, and no challenge at all from Labour, the Conservatives, the Liberal Democrats or the Greens.

The result lands the same week that fresh Ipsos polling puts Labour back ahead of Reform UK nationally, 28% to 25% — a reversal from the double-digit deficits Labour was running as recently as the spring. Put those two facts side by side and the picture is not of a stable governing party facing down an insurgent challenger. It is of a Westminster still working through the consequences of a leadership change most of Fleet Street had not fully priced in by midsummer.

A seat, a scandal, and a rematch

Farage resigned as MP for Clacton on 7 July, four days after The Guardian and The Sunday Times reported he had failed to declare donations — including gifts from businessman Christopher Harborne and from George Cottrell, a former Reform aide — received before he entered Parliament. Farage maintains the gifts predated his MP status and required no declaration; he cast the resignation as an invitation for Clacton's voters to render their own verdict rather than leave the matter to a parliamentary standards inquiry.

Voters obliged, decisively. A Survation poll ahead of polling day had Farage on 73% against 20% for Count Binface — the bin-costumed satirist and comedian Jon Harvey, standing with backing from green-energy financier Dale Vince — with the remaining 32 candidates splitting the residue. It is a smaller margin, in relative terms, than the 2024 general election only in the sense that turnout was thin: with the major parties declining to field candidates, Clacton's by-election became a referendum on Farage alone rather than a live contest between governing alternatives.

That is a useful, if unglamorous, data point for reading the result. A commanding win in a seat with no serious opposition confirms Farage's grip on Clacton; it says considerably less about Reform's standing in the 350-odd seats where Labour, the Tories and the Lib Dems will actually turn up.

The Burnham effect, not the Starmer question

Here the story that framed this piece needs updating from where it stood even a few months ago. The "pressure on Starmer" that dominated Westminster coverage through the spring did not stay pressure — it produced a change of prime minister. Keir Starmer announced on 22 June that he would resign as Labour leader after more than 95 MPs had called for him to go or set a departure date, with net approval that had sunk to roughly -46% by the previous November. Andy Burnham, the Greater Manchester mayor who had spent over a year positioned as the challenger MPs actually wanted, won a by-election of his own to enter the Commons, was acclaimed Labour leader on 17 July with backing from more than 94% of the parliamentary party, and was sworn in as prime minister on 20 July — Britain's seventh in a decade.

The Ipsos figures making Labour's three-point lead over Reform this week's headline are consequently a read on Burnham's Labour, not Starmer's. Opinium's tracking from 5 August has Burnham on a net approval of +16 — more popular than any other tested leader, Farage included — in what pollsters have taken to calling the "Burnham bounce." That is a genuinely different political fact than dozens of restive backbenchers grinding down an incumbent, and it changes what the polling narrows: Labour did not claw back ground against Reform by holding its nerve under Starmer, it did so by replacing him.

What the two stories add up to

Taken together, Clacton and the poll swing describe a political system that keeps resolving instability by changing its leading characters rather than by settling into equilibrium. Reform's leader returns to the Commons carrying live scrutiny of his own financial conduct into a chamber where his party still trails in every recent poll of a genuine multi-party contest. Labour's recovery in the polls is real but is barely a month old, built on a leader who has yet to face a Budget, a conference season or a economic shock of his own making.

For markets, the honest read is that neither headline is, on its own, the kind of rupture that moves gilts or sterling — a foregone by-election and an incremental poll shift are Westminster's ordinary weather. What matters is the volatility underneath both: two changes of national leadership inside two years, a governing party whose fortunes swing double digits within a single parliamentary term, and an opposition leader fighting off ethics questions on his way back into Parliament. That backdrop is precisely why gilt yields have stayed elevated near 1998 levels heading into the autumn Budget — investors pricing not any single event but the accumulating cost of a Westminster that cannot yet find a stable equilibrium. The next test lands with Burnham's first Budget this autumn, when Reform's poll strength — and Labour's newfound edge over it — will be measured against tax and spending decisions rather than by-election arithmetic.
