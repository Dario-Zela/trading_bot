US inflation cools to 3.4% in July as Fed officials split on next move

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
July CPI cooled to 3.4% (core 2.5%, a four-year low), cutting September Fed hike odds to 42% even as Cleveland's Hammack pushes for tightening.

US consumer prices rose 0.1% in July, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Wednesday, leaving the annual inflation rate at 3.4% — down from 3.5% in June and the second straight monthly deceleration. Strip out food and energy, and the core rate cooled to 2.5% year-over-year, its slowest pace since March 2021. For a Federal Reserve that spent the summer defending a decision to hold rates rather than raise them, the numbers arrived as useful cover — and immediately reopened a fight among policymakers over what happens next.

The number itself

July's print came in exactly where economists had penciled it, which in a year of volatile inflation readings counted as its own small relief. Energy prices fell 1.5% on the month after a 5.7% drop in June, as the price spike tied to the spring's conflict-driven oil shock continued to unwind — gasoline is still up 24.6% year-over-year, but that's down from 26.7% in June, and the trajectory is what markets are trading on now. Shelter, the stickiest and most closely watched line item in the index, rose just 0.1% for the month and eased to 3.2% annually from 3.3%. Food inflation held flat at 3%.

It is the shelter figure that economists flagged as the most consequential. Housing costs have been the main obstacle to the Fed's 2% target for two years, lagging real-time rent data by months as leases roll over. A sub-0.2% shelter print, if it holds, is the kind of move that lets a central bank stop worrying about inflation and start worrying about growth. Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's, put it plainly: the numbers put inflation "within spitting distance of the Fed's target." Truist's Mike Skordeles was more measured, noting the report "supports our view that the Fed will remain on hold in the near term," while cautioning that a jobs report and an August CPI print still stand between now and the Fed's mid-September meeting.

A Fed chair with cover, and a regional president without patience

The report landed five weeks into Kevin Warsh's tenure as Fed chair, and it gave him something he has lacked since taking over from Jerome Powell in May: a clean data point in favor of standing still. Warsh has spent his early months threading a needle — his press conferences read as dovish even as his prepared remarks stress a strict 2% target, leaving investors to guess which version of the chair will show up at the next meeting. Three FOMC members dissented at July's decision to hold rates, arguing instead for a hike. July's CPI does little to strengthen that camp's case on the data, but it hasn't quieted them either.

Chief among the dissenters is Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, who has been arguing for weeks that the Fed is behind the curve. In a note published just a day before the CPI release, Hammack wrote that "now is the time to act," adding that "the longer we wait to take action to bring inflation back to our 2 percent objective, the more challenging it will be to bring it back down." She has said explicitly that current rates aren't meaningfully restrictive and that a single quarter-point move "probably doesn't do a whole lot for the economy" — code for wanting more than one hike before year-end.

Hammack's position sits awkwardly against Wednesday's data, but the split inside the Fed isn't really about a single month's print — it's about whether the disinflation trend is durable or a temporary lull between energy shocks. Other officials, and most of Wall Street's economist corps, read July's numbers the other way. Washington Post columnist Heather Long noted that outside of gasoline, price pressure is easing broadly, giving Warsh "cover to wait and see what happens this fall before he has to act." Morgan Stanley's Ellen Zentner went further, arguing the in-line reading "will keep the 'no need to hike rates' narrative intact."

How markets moved

Traders came down clearly on the dovish side of that argument. CME Group's FedWatch tool showed the probability of a September rate hike falling to 42% after the release, a sharp retreat from the roughly 52% odds priced in just a day earlier, before the report, when Hammack's comments had pushed hike expectations higher. Treasury yields slipped across the curve. Equities took the news calmly rather than euphorically: the S&P 500 added 0.26% and the Nasdaq Composite 0.54%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average, weighed down by sector-specific moves elsewhere in Wednesday's session, slipped a token 0.04% to close at 53,770.27. The muted equity reaction reflects a market that had already priced most of the good news in — July's number confirmed a trend rather than reversing one.

What the split means from here

The practical stakes of the Hammack-versus-consensus argument are narrower than the rhetoric suggests: a quarter point, maybe two, on a policy rate that both sides agree is somewhere near neutral. But the disagreement matters beyond the September meeting because it is a proxy for a bigger question the Fed hasn't answered — whether the energy-driven inflation spike earlier this year was a one-off shock now fully in retreat, or evidence of underlying price pressure that a few soft shelter prints can mask. Warsh will get two more data points before the FOMC meets in mid-September: a jobs report and one more CPI release. Both will matter more than usual, because the current committee, still finding its footing under a new chair and carrying an active three-member dissent, has shown it does not have a settled reading of its own data.
