US federal budget deficit widens to $432bn in July

The US Treasury Department building in Washington, D.C. — MeanieHyaena / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
July's US budget deficit hit $432bn, 24% above the $348.3bn forecast, pushing the fiscal year-to-date gap past all of fiscal 2025's shortfall.

The US Treasury ran a $432bn budget deficit in July, blowing past the $348.3bn economists had penciled in — a miss of nearly 24%, and one large enough to reset how fast Washington's fiscal math is deteriorating relative to what markets had already priced.

The raw numbers are stark on their own terms. The government collected $334bn in receipts against $766.3bn in outlays, the latter a record for the month of July and up 22% from a year earlier. Some of that gap is mechanical: roughly $99bn of August benefit payments — Social Security, veterans' benefits and the like — landed in July's ledger because August 1 fell on a Saturday, pushing disbursements forward. Strip that calendar quirk out and the adjusted deficit was $333bn, still up 18% on July 2025. Either way, the direction is the same: spending is accelerating faster than revenue is catching up, and tariff collections — meant to be a fiscal offset — actually ran negative for the month, with $33.4bn in refunds outweighing gross customs receipts to leave a net outflow of $8.6bn.

Why the miss matters more than the number

A single month's deficit print rarely moves markets on its own. What makes July's figure a live issue is the size of the surprise relative to consensus. A 24% miss of this magnitude — $83.7bn above forecast — is not noise; it implies economists' models are under-tracking either the pace of outlay growth (defense, interest, entitlements) or the softness in revenue, and probably both. That matters because Treasury's issuance calendar is built off exactly these kinds of projections. Bigger, more persistent misses mean more supply than the curve has discounted, concentrated at a moment when auctions are already testing demand at the long end.

The cumulative picture reinforces the point. Through the first ten months of fiscal 2026, the deficit stands at $1.799 trillion — $170bn wider than the same stretch of fiscal 2025, and already larger than the entire fiscal 2025 shortfall with two months of the fiscal year still to run. The 12-month rolling deficit has reached $1.9 trillion. Interest on the public debt, meanwhile, has climbed to roughly $1.17 trillion for the fiscal year to date, up 15% on a year ago, as the stock of debt outstanding — closing in on $40 trillion — rolls onto higher rates. "The United States has borrowed more in the first ten months of fiscal year 2026 than it did in all of fiscal year 2025," said Maya MacGuineas, president of the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget, adding that the pace now points to more than $2 trillion in borrowing for the full year.

None of this forces an immediate repricing. But it raises the bar for the next data points that will: the Treasury's quarterly refunding announcement, due in the coming weeks, will show whether issuance plans are being revised upward to match the wider gap, and August's Monthly Treasury Statement, out in mid-September, will confirm whether July's overshoot was a one-off tariff-refund distortion or the start of a trend.
