EU begins enforcing AI Act transparency rules, delays high-risk duties to 2027

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, which began enforcing the AI Act's transparency rules on 2 August. — Nuno Nogueira / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.5)
In one sentence
Brussels began enforcing AI Act transparency and deepfake-labelling rules on 2 August while pushing high-risk compliance duties back to December 2027.

Brussels reached a regulatory milestone this month that most companies barely noticed — and that was rather the point. On 2 August, the European Commission's AI Office and national regulators across the bloc began enforcing new transparency rules under the EU AI Act: chatbots must now tell users they're talking to a machine, AI-generated images and video must carry machine-readable marks, and deepfakes need a visible label. At the same time, Brussels quietly pushed back the far more consequential part of the law — mandatory compliance for "high-risk" AI systems — to December 2027.

The combination tells its own story. The EU wants to prove, after years of being mocked as a slow-moving regulator that legislates first and figures out enforcement later, that it can actually police the rules it writes. But it's starting with the cheapest, least contentious part of the AI Act, while giving industry another sixteen months before the expensive obligations bite.

What actually changed on 2 August

The new duties fall under Article 50 of the AI Act, the transparency chapter that applies regardless of how risky a system is deemed. Providers of interactive AI systems — chatbots, voice assistants — must make it obvious that a user is dealing with a machine, unless that's already self-evident. Anyone generating or manipulating text, audio, image or video content with AI has to apply machine-readable markings so the material can be identified as synthetic. Deepfakes get a stricter bar: visible disclosure, not just a hidden watermark. And AI-generated text published to inform the public — a news-style article, for instance — must be labelled if it hasn't had human editorial review.

Companies that ignore these obligations face fines of up to €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is larger — the same penalty tier that applies to breaches further up the AI Act's risk ladder. More than 180 organisations, including major AI labs, have signed the Commission's voluntary Code of Practice on transparency, which offers a degree of presumed compliance in exchange for following its technical playbook on watermarking and labelling.

Henna Virkkunen, the Commission's executive vice-president for tech sovereignty, framed the moment as proof the law can walk and chew gum at once, saying the Act gives "innovators legal certainty while protecting the public interest" and marks "an important step towards AI that people and businesses can understand and trust."

Not everyone is convinced the rules will bite in practice. Alexandru Voica of Synthesia, an AI video company, has pointed to "a worrying lack of media literacy across large parts of Europe, which makes these technical obligations largely ineffective" — labels only help if people notice and trust them. There's also a jurisdictional problem: content made outside the EU by providers with no European footprint can reach millions of users on the continent within minutes, well outside Brussels' reach. And watermarking itself is fragile — it can be stripped or degraded when content is compressed, edited or reposted across platforms, which is more or less what happens to everything on the internet.

The high-risk reprieve

The part of the AI Act that actually worried industry — obligations for "high-risk" systems used in hiring, credit scoring, law enforcement, education and border control — was never going to arrive on schedule. In May, the Council and European Parliament struck a political agreement on what's been dubbed the "Digital Omnibus," the first substantive rewrite of the AI Act since it was adopted in 2024. It pushes compliance deadlines for stand-alone high-risk systems to 2 December 2027, and for AI embedded in already-regulated products — medical devices, cars, industrial machinery — to 2 August 2028.

That's a delay of roughly sixteen months from the original timeline, driven largely by industry complaints that the detailed conformity-assessment infrastructure the law requires — harmonised technical standards, accredited testing bodies, a functioning EU database of high-risk systems — simply wasn't ready. Brussels effectively conceded the point rather than force companies to comply with rules the bloc's own institutions hadn't finished building the plumbing for.

Civil-society groups have pushed back on the sequencing, arguing that transparency infrastructure — the portals and reporting mechanisms meant to make the new labelling rules verifiable — should have been operational before any large-scale enforcement began, rather than being built in parallel. Researchers and advocacy organisations have called on Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and Virkkunen to use the enforcement tools that do exist "with confidence and resolve," a pointed way of saying that starting with the easy rules only counts for something if Brussels follows through.

Why the sequencing matters for business

For companies operating in the EU, the practical read is straightforward: the compliance clock has started, but the expensive part hasn't. Transparency obligations are procedural and largely a matter of labelling and disclosure — costly to engineer correctly at scale, but not the kind of structural overhaul that high-risk conformity assessments demand, with their required risk-management systems, human-oversight protocols and third-party audits. Firms building or deploying AI in regulated sectors now have a defined runway to December 2027 rather than an immediate deadline, which changes budgeting and hiring decisions for compliance teams this year and next.

The risk sits on the other side of that runway. Sixteen months is enough time for companies to treat 2027 as distant and under-prepare, the same pattern that left parts of industry scrambling ahead of this August's transparency deadline despite years of notice. It's also enough time for the political mood in Brussels to shift again — the AI Act has already been amended once under industry pressure, and a second round of lobbying ahead of the high-risk deadline is a safe bet.

The next marker worth watching is less a date than a test: how the AI Office handles its first real enforcement action under the transparency rules now in force. A visible fine, or a visible failure to issue one, will tell companies more about how seriously to take December 2027 than anything in the text of the Digital Omnibus itself.
