Oil nears $90 as Hormuz reopening hopes fade

An oil tanker at berth — global crude markets are being repriced around how much traffic can move through the Strait of Hormuz. — Paul Harrison / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Brent crude topped $89 as fresh shipping attacks cast doubt on a fragile US-Iran plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, still running a fraction of pre-war volumes.

Brent crude pushed above $89 a barrel on Wednesday, its sixth straight session of gains, as fresh attacks on shipping in the Middle East undercut hopes that a US-Iran arrangement to reopen the Strait of Hormuz was close to done. The benchmark is now up roughly 24% since the US-Israel war on Iran began in late February, and within striking distance of $90 for the first time since the conflict started.

The move reverses weeks of cautious optimism. Iran and Oman had reportedly agreed on coordinates for a 60-day interim shipping arrangement — inbound tankers routed through a northern lane in Iranian waters, outbound traffic through a southern lane in Omani waters — that traders read as the first real off-ramp from a crisis that has throttled one of the world's most important oil chokepoints. That optimism is now curdling. Iran attacked several vessels that had used the Oman-approved route, Houthi forces struck a commercial ship in the Bab al-Mandeb strait and killed six people, and US Central Command disabled a Panama-flagged cargo vessel attempting to breach Iran's port blockade. Tehran said this week that Hormuz stays closed until Washington ends the war, lifts sanctions, releases frozen Iranian assets and pays reparations — a list that has grown, not shrunk, since talks began. President Trump countered that the US has "total control" over the strait, a claim Iran flatly disputes.

The chokepoint is still mostly shut

Whatever the diplomatic language, the tonnage tells the real story. Before the war, Hormuz carried roughly 130 vessel transits and 20 million barrels a day — about a fifth of global oil and LNG trade. Kpler and shipping-tracker data cited by traders this week put Monday's crossings at somewhere between six and ten vessels. The US energy secretary has claimed a seven-day average near 9 million barrels a day has been restored; independent estimates put it closer to 7 million. Not a single very large crude carrier loaded in Iran during the first ten days of August, and Iranian crude sitting in floating storage has roughly doubled from July to around 40 million barrels — oil with nowhere to go.

"Markets have not completely lost hope for a deal, but confidence is clearly eroding," said Tim Waterer, chief market analyst at KCM Trade. June Goh of Sparta Commodities said prices look likely to stay pinned in an $85–$90 range "barring new headlines on diplomatic talks" — which, this week, is exactly what traders got, just not the kind they wanted.

The stakes for going further are not trivial. Goldman Sachs has warned that another month of severely constrained Hormuz traffic would push Brent's 2026 average above $100, with prices potentially averaging $120 in the third quarter if the disruption drags on. The US Energy Information Administration, more conservatively, still sees the third-quarter average near $85. The gap between those forecasts is essentially a bet on whether the Oman-Iran shipping lanes get finalised in the coming weeks or the current trickle becomes the new normal.

The next marker is whether Oman and Iran formally sign off on the lane-separation arrangement Qatar has been brokering — expected, if it happens, within days rather than weeks. Until then, every attack on a tanker resets the clock, and the risk premium that briefly looked like it was coming out of oil prices is instead settling back in.
