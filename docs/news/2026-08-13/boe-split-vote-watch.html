BoE's 6-3 split rate cut still reverberating

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the Monetary Policy Committee met on 30 July. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The BoE held Bank Rate at 3.75% in a 6-3 vote on 30 July, but for the first time this cycle the dissent came from hawks wanting a hike, not doves wanting deeper cuts.

The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee held Bank Rate at 3.75% at its 30 July meeting, and the vote that produced that outcome is still doing more to move markets than the decision itself. Six members backed the hold. Three — chief economist Huw Pill, along with Megan Greene and Catherine Mann — voted against it, wanting instead to raise the rate a quarter point, to 4.00%.

That is the detail worth sitting with. For most of this year, dissent on the MPC ran the other way. In February, four of nine members pushed for a cut the majority wasn't ready to make. By June the committee had settled into a comfortable 7-2 hold. Now, five weeks later, the argument has flipped entirely: the holdouts are hawks, not doves, and there are three of them instead of two.

Why the hawks moved

The proximate cause sits a few thousand miles east. Iran's Revolutionary Guard struck commercial shipping off Oman in early July, drawing US retaliation and a fresh round of strikes that has kept the Strait of Hormuz — and global energy supply — under threat (oil is pushing back toward $90 a barrel as reopening hopes fade). Pill, Greene and Mann argued that risk is no longer hypothetical: European gas stocks are below normal for the season and global refining output has fallen, leaving, in the MPC minutes' language, energy prices skewed to the upside.

Governor Andrew Bailey pushed back publicly, telling reporters the committee is not edging toward a hike. "The process of underlying disinflation that was intact prior to the conflict remains in train," he said, pointing to headline CPI, which cooled to 2.6% in June — a 14-month low — and to a labour market that is still loosening: unemployment at 4.9%, private-sector wage growth down to a five-year low of 2.9%, vacancies falling. The majority's read is that a softening jobs market will do more disinflationary work than volatile energy prices can undo.

What it means for the cutting cycle

Both readings can be correct, which is precisely the problem. A committee split three ways between cut-leaning doves, hold-leaning centrists and now rate-hike hawks does not look like one working toward a shared destination — it looks like one waiting for the next data print to break the tie. Ten-year gilt yields pushed through 4.8% in the session after the vote, part of the move keeping them near 1998 highs, as traders trimmed bets on further easing this year.

The next test comes at the 17 September meeting, by which point the MPC will have two more inflation readings and another labour market report in hand — and will know whether the Hormuz standoff has settled or escalated further. A fourth vote for a hike would mark the end of any presumption that the BoE's next move is down.
