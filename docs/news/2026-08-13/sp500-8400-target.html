Wall Street strategists lift S&P 500 year-end target to 8,400

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor — the epicentre of Wall Street's diverging 2026 forecasts. — Eduard Hueber / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Wall Street's "8,400" S&P 500 target belongs to one strategist, Ed Yardeni — the average forecast is 555 points lower, at 7,845.

The most bullish year-end target on Wall Street now belongs to one man: Ed Yardeni, the veteran independent strategist who on August 12 lifted his S&P 500 forecast to 8,400, up from 8,250. That puts him roughly 650 points above the Wall Street average — and squarely at the centre of a debate over whether US equities are pricing in a boom or a bubble.

The index closed at 7,748 the day before Yardeni's call, meaning his target implies another 8% of gains before December. It is also the highest of any major forecaster tracked on the Street, edging out Morgan Stanley's 8,300. RBC Capital Markets sits at 8,150, Goldman Sachs and JPMorgan at 8,000 apiece, and Bank of America — the most cautious of the large banks — at 7,100. The average across Wall Street's strategist community is closer to 7,845, some 555 points shy of Yardeni's number.

That gap matters. Headlines describing "Wall Street's peak-bullish forecast" can read as if the Street has converged on 8,400; in fact it is one independent research shop pulling well ahead of the pack, not a consensus shift. Yardeni Research raised its 2026 earnings-per-share forecast for the S&P 500 to $375, from $330, and lifted its 2027 estimate to $415. The firm also raised the odds it assigns to a continuation of what Yardeni calls the "Roaring 2020s" scenario — sustained growth, controlled inflation, resilient profits — to 80%, from 60%.

The case against, in one ratio

The earnings backdrop is genuinely strong: with about 90% of S&P 500 companies having reported second-quarter results by August 11, aggregate earnings per share were up 46.7% year-on-year, following 19% growth in the first quarter. That is the fuel behind every upgraded target on the Street, not just Yardeni's.

But valuation skeptics point to a different number. John Higgins, chief economic adviser at Capital Economics, has flagged the S&P 500's cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio above 40 for two straight months — a level last sustained in 1999 and 2000, before the dot-com crash erased roughly 30% of the index's value over the following three years on average, per Capital Economics' own historical read. Goldman Sachs' chief global equity strategist, Peter Oppenheimer, has offered a more qualified version of the same worry: "There does not appear to be a valuation bubble, but there may be an earnings bubble" — a distinction that shifts the risk from prices to the durability of the profit growth propping them up. Forward price-to-earnings multiples, at around 21, remain well below the dot-com peak above 24, which is the strongest evidence for Yardeni's camp that this rally differs from 2000.

Yardeni himself puts the odds of a recession derailing the thesis at just 20%, and has said any pullback would be a buying opportunity. He is holding a 10,000 target for the end of 2029.

The next test arrives with third-quarter earnings season and the Federal Reserve's September meeting — both will show whether the profit growth underwriting Yardeni's number, and the market's recent cooling-inflation tailwind, can hold through year-end.
