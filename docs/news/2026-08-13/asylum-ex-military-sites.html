Ministers push ahead with housing asylum seekers on ex-military sites

Cameron Barracks in Inverness, built in the 1880s for the Cameron Highlanders, is being converted to house around 300 asylum seekers. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers are converting two former army sites to house roughly 900 asylum seekers as part of a push to end hotel accommodation by 2029.

Every Sunday morning since November, the Sussex town of Crowborough has staged the same ritual: placards, a slow march to the gates of an army training camp, and a line of police keeping protesters and counter-protesters apart. On the other side of Britain, in Inverness, a Victorian barracks built for the Cameron Highlanders in the 1880s — twin drum towers, a clock tower, the works — sits half-repurposed for a very different kind of arrival.

In one sentence: Ministers are converting two former army sites to house roughly 900 asylum seekers as part of a push to end hotel accommodation by 2029.

Cameron Barracks and the Crowborough Army Training Camp are the government's proof of concept for a bigger bet: that Britain's asylum system can be run out of former military estate rather than the requisitioned Premier Inns and Holiday Inns that have defined it since 2020. Around 300 men are destined for Inverness; up to 540 for Crowborough, where the camp is now close to full and residents typically stay about 80 days before being moved on. Both were announced last October, both slipped their original timetables amid local objections, and both have become fixtures of a familiar protest calendar — thousands turned out in Crowborough at points last winter, while Scotland's first minister, John Swinney, complained the Inverness plan had been "made up on the back of an envelope" with no proper consultation of Highland Council.

The arithmetic behind the barbed wire

The appeal, for a Treasury under pressure, is straightforward: hotels are the most expensive way imaginable to house anyone. The government says asylum accommodation costs have fallen by nearly £1 billion since it took office in 2024, and this month it closed a further 13 hotels — part of a run of 44 shut since April that has taken the total in use from around 400 to fewer than 160. Officials put the latest tranche's saving at £51 million a year; Rachel Reeves has attached her name to the 2029 end-date, framing it as roughly £1 billion a year back for the Treasury once complete. Border security minister Alex Norris put the political framing bluntly: hotels, he said, "were meant to be a short-term stop-gap under the previous government, but they spiralled out of control."

The barracks strategy is meant to scale well beyond Inverness and Crowborough. The Ministry of Defence has floated three more sites — Bicester, Barnham and Linton-on-Ouse — that could together hold 3,750 people, while Crowborough's own approval has already been extended to 2030. Whether that scaling survives contact with the same pattern of late consultation, local anger and slipped timetables seen so far is the open question. Human rights groups have separately warned that dormitory-style barracks in remote locations make it harder for residents to reach legal advice or healthcare — a different objection to the hotel critique, but one that will shadow every new site the Home Office announces.

The next marker is less a date than a headcount: whether the Bicester and Barnham planning applications clear before the next round of hotel closures is announced, expected in the autumn.
