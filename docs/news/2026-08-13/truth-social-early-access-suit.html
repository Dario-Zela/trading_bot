Trump sued over Truth Social's $100,000-a-month early-access posting tier

Truth Social's app icon. The platform's parent company now sells paying subscribers a millisecond head start on presidential posts. — T Media Tech LLC / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Press-freedom groups sued Trump, arguing his Truth Social $100K-a-month early-access tier for market-moving posts is unconstitutional.

Somewhere on Wall Street's more exotic trading desks, a subscription that costs more than most people's annual salary now buys something stranger than data: a few milliseconds' head start on the President of the United States's mind.

On Wednesday, The Intercept and the Freedom of the Press Foundation sued Donald Trump in federal court in the Southern District of New York, arguing that Truth Social's paid early-access tier — a service called Truth API, priced at up to $100,000 a month — is unconstitutional. The suit, filed with the backing of Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington, Yale Law School's Media Freedom & Information Access Clinic, the Public Integrity Project and the firm Altshuler Berzon, names Trump, two aides, and the Executive Office of the President as defendants. It calls the arrangement "extraordinary, corrupt and unconstitutional."

What's being sold

Truth API launched on August 1, built by Trump Media & Technology Group as a licensed real-time feed of posts from ten of Truth Social's most-followed accounts — Trump's own, plus Vice President JD Vance, press secretary Karoline Leavitt and other senior officials. Subscribers pay $100,000 a month, or lock in $60,000 a month with a three-year commitment, for the privilege of seeing those posts before the wider public does. Interim chief executive Kevin McGurn has pitched it bluntly: "Markets already move on Truth Social posts," he told investors, and the feed is aimed at firms "most impacted by the cost of a delay in information" — a description that fits high-frequency trading shops more than casual scrollers. More than ten customers have signed contracts so far, according to the complaint, and McGurn has said the company is in talks with hyperscalers, news organisations and AI developers to expand the client list.

The legal argument

The complaint rests on two constitutional pegs. The First Amendment claim holds that the public and press have an equal right to access official presidential statements, and that charging for a head start on them is a burden that serves no legitimate government interest — let alone one significant enough to survive scrutiny. "There is no legitimate interest, let alone a significant one, in permitting President Trump to profit from selling government information," the filing states. A companion Fifth Amendment claim argues the scheme denies equal protection by making access to the president's public statements contingent on a subscriber's ability to pay. "Every American is entitled to equal access to the president's public statements," said Nikhel Sus, CREW's chief counsel. The Intercept's editor-in-chief, Ben Muessig, put it more pointedly: "Trump is trying to enrich himself by privatizing government information that he has no right to sell. We won't let it stand."

Why it's a markets story, not just a media one

The suit sidesteps insider-trading law, which is narrower and harder to apply to a sitting president's own public statements, in favour of a constitutional access argument. But the practical concern is the same one that prompted Senators Elizabeth Warren and Adam Schiff to write to the SEC seeking an investigation: a data feed sold specifically to firms that profit from being fast creates a two-tier market in information that moves prices, built around posts from tariffs to Fed commentary to geopolitical flashpoints. Boston College law professor Renée Jones, a former senior SEC official, has said the structure looks uncomfortably close to the kind of selective-disclosure conduct securities law was built to prevent — just dressed up as a content licence rather than a tip. Trump holds a roughly 41% stake in Trump Media, now managed through a trust controlled by one of his sons, giving him a direct financial interest in a service built on advance access to his own words.

The case adds a formal legal test to a fight that had, until now, played out mostly in letters and press releases. A Southern District of New York judge will eventually have to decide whether a president's public statements are different in kind from anyone else's licensable content — and whether "pay to read it first" survives constitutional scrutiny when the thing being sold moves markets within seconds. Trump Media has not yet filed a formal response; that filing, and any early hearing date, will be the next marker in the case.
