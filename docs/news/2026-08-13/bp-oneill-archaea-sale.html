BP's new CEO Meg O'Neill sells Archaea Energy in fast-moving reset

Meg O'Neill became BP's chief executive on April 1, 2026, the company's first externally hired CEO and the first woman to lead a top-tier oil major. — Ian Regnard / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
100 days into the job, BP CEO Meg O'Neill is selling biogas unit Archaea and North Sea assets, unwinding the low-carbon division to refocus on oil and gas.

One hundred days into the job, BP's chief executive has a straightforward answer to what the company was doing with the last decade of its clean-energy ambitions: selling it off.

Meg O'Neill, who took over as BP's chief executive on April 1, is putting the biogas business Archaea Energy up for sale and dismantling the standalone low-carbon energy unit that BP built to house its transition bets. The moves, confirmed alongside second-quarter results on August 4, complete a retreat from renewables that began under her predecessor and now looks less like a course correction than a full reversal.

A pivot within a pivot

The arc here spans three chief executives. In 2020, Bernard Looney committed BP to net zero by 2050, pledging to cut oil and gas production 40% by 2030 and build out 50 gigawatts of renewable capacity — a plan Archaea's roughly $4.1 billion acquisition in 2022 was meant to help fill out. It never delivered the returns promised, and the stock lagged rivals that stayed the course on fossil fuels.

Murray Auchincloss, who succeeded Looney, began unwinding the plan in February 2025 under pressure from Elliott Management, the activist fund that built a stake of roughly 5% in BP and pushed for deep cost cuts and a return to core operations. Auchincloss cut renewable investment by about 70%, lifted oil and gas spending toward $10 billion a year, and dropped the 40% production-cut target. It wasn't enough: with the stock down roughly 16% on his watch, Auchincloss was ousted in December, and chairman Albert Manifold — who wanted the company "simpler, leaner and more profitable" — brought in O'Neill, BP's first externally hired chief executive in more than a century and the first woman to run a top-tier oil major. Manifold himself was then removed in May over separate conduct concerns, leaving O'Neill to execute the reset largely on her own authority.

What's actually being sold

Archaea, once framed as a foothold in the biogas market, is now described in O'Neill's own terms as a mismatch for BP's balance sheet. She told analysts the business represented a "capital-intense" approach to lower-carbon molecules, where BP now wants a "capital-light" one — access to the market without owning and building landfill-gas infrastructure directly. BP has already fielded buyer interest.

Archaea is not standing alone. On July 31, BP launched a formal sale process for its North Sea business — five production hubs, including Clair, Clair Ridge and Glen Lyon, employing around 1,100 people — ending six decades of BP operations in the basin. Days earlier, BP exited its stake in the Bay du Nord project off Newfoundland, selling to operator Equinor, which becomes sole owner of a field expected to hold more than 400 million barrels. Internally, the low-carbon unit disappears into a flattened structure: just upstream and downstream, with a trading arm connecting the two. About 700 jobs go with the reorganisation.

"We need to make fewer, better choices and hold ourselves to account," O'Neill said, marking her 100th day. "Investors should be able to rely on us in the same way our customers do."

The money behind the reset

The simplification is inseparable from the balance sheet. BP had already suspended its $750 million quarterly buyback in February and withdrawn its pledge to return 30–40% of operating cash flow to shareholders, leaving the decision on restarting repurchases to whoever took the chief executive job — a decision that landed with O'Neill. The dividend, by contrast, is untouched: BP still intends to grow it roughly 4% a year.

Second-quarter results gave her room to manoeuvre. Underlying profit rose 78% quarter-on-quarter to $5.7 billion, helped by strong trading, and net debt — which had climbed to $25.3 billion by the end of the first quarter — fell by $3.1 billion to $22.3 billion. BP now guides to $8–9 billion in divestment proceeds this year, including roughly $6 billion from the planned Castrol sale, and full-year capital spending of $13.5–14 billion, as it chases a net debt target of $14–18 billion by the end of 2027 — a target Elliott had originally pushed it toward. The market's response was muted rather than enthusiastic: shares hovered around 529p on the day, underperforming the wider FTSE 100.

What to watch

The test now is whether O'Neill can find buyers willing to pay full value for Archaea and the North Sea assets without BP looking like a forced seller — and whether debt actually falls fast enough to justify restarting buybacks before 2027. A formal Archaea sale timeline has not been set, and BP's Aberdeen staff are due further detail on the North Sea process in the coming months. For an industry that spent the early 2020s promising investors a transition story, BP's retreat — the most complete of any major so far — will be read by rivals and activist funds alike as a signal of how much further that story still has to unwind.
