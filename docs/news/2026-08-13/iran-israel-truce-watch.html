Mediators scramble to extend Iran-Israel truce as talks stall

Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif, whose government brokered the Islamabad Memorandum and is now pressing both sides to extend it — NIJATO / Press Service of the President of the Republic of Azerbaijan, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Pakistan is pushing Iran and the US/Israel to extend a 60-day truce past its August 17 deadline as both sides accuse each other of breaching the Islamabad MoU.

The truce that has kept Iran and the United States and Israel from resuming open war since February is entering its final week with no successor agreement in hand — only a promise from both sides that they would rather extend it than let it lapse.

Pakistani officials, who brokered the original ceasefire and the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding that followed, say Washington and Tehran have each signalled consent in principle to extend the 60-day framework past its August 17 expiry. What they have not agreed on is how long the extension should run, or how to bridge the disputes that have already pushed the truce to the brink twice this summer.

The Islamabad MoU, signed on June 17 by President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian with Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif's government standing as guarantor, was meant to convert a shaky battlefield ceasefire into something durable: a phased end to hostilities on all fronts including Lebanon, removal of the US naval blockade within 30 days, toll-free transit through the Strait of Hormuz for the 60-day window, a $300 billion reconstruction fund for Iran, a sanctions-relief timetable, and Iranian commitments on uranium downblending, all under a compliance-monitoring mechanism intended to head off exactly the kind of tit-for-tat breakdown that followed.

It didn't hold. Israel kept striking targets in Lebanon within days of the signing. By June 20 Iran was accusing the US of failing to lift the naval blockade and hinting at closing Hormuz; Trump threatened to invade if it did. By early July, Iran had allegedly targeted commercial tankers in the strait, and the US answered by revoking an Iranian oil-sales waiver and striking more than 80 targets inside Iran. On July 8, Trump himself declared the memorandum "over." Ten days later, Iran's deputy foreign minister, Kazem Gharibabadi, made it official from Tehran's side too: "The US has violated and suspended all its commitments within the framework of the Islamabad MoU," he said. "We also likewise have suspended all of our commitments as a result; we are no longer implementing those commitments."

Washington's rebuttal centres on nuclear compliance rather than the naval blockade. Satellite imagery reviewed by CNN and the Institute for Science and International Security in mid-July purported to show Iran rebuilding facilities struck in February's opening raids — evidence, US officials argue, that Tehran never intended to honour the downblending provisions in the first place. Israel, which was not a signatory but has chafed at the MoU throughout, has said openly it will not tolerate any reconstitution of Iran's nuclear or missile programs.

Islamabad's narrowing room to manoeuvre

What has kept the framework alive despite all this is less trust than exhaustion: neither side has followed through on threats to resume full-scale strikes, and both apparently prefer a messy extension to a formal collapse. Pakistani mediators, joined at various points by Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Qatar and Egypt, have spent August shuttling messages between Washington and Tehran to find extension terms both can live with. But as one regional diplomat close to the process put it earlier this month, Islamabad can facilitate talks and broker statements — it cannot compel either side to actually honour what they sign.

The unresolved core disputes are the same ones that broke the June deal: security guarantees for Iran against further strikes, and who controls navigation through Hormuz, a chokepoint for roughly a fifth of global oil flows. Traders have already priced in some of that risk — crude has drifted toward $90 a barrel on fading hopes the strait reopens on schedule. A clean extension before August 17 would likely ease that premium; a lapse without one would test how much appetite either Washington or Tehran still has for the alternative.
