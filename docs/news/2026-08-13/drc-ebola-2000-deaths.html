DRC Ebola outbreak deaths pass 2,000, fastest-growing on record

A colorized transmission electron micrograph of an Ebola virus virion, imaged by CDC microbiologist Cynthia Goldsmith. — CDC / Cynthia Goldsmith, Public Health Image Library (public domain)
In one sentence
DRC's Bundibugyo-strain Ebola outbreak has passed 2,000 deaths in under three months — the fastest-growing on record, with no licensed vaccine or treatment yet available.

The Ebola outbreak burning through eastern Democratic Republic of Congo has killed more than 2,000 people, and the World Health Organization says it is now the fastest-growing epidemic of the disease ever recorded. The Congolese health ministry's latest figures, reported by UN News and Al Jazeera on August 11, put confirmed cases at 4,381 and deaths at 2,011 across five provinces — with roughly 1,000 of those deaths logged in just the three weeks before that count was taken.[^1][^2]

The virus first appeared in February, according to WHO's outbreak notice, but wasn't formally declared an epidemic until May 15; the WHO escalated it to a Public Health Emergency of International Concern — its highest alert level — two days later.[^3] Fewer than three months on, it trails only the 2014–2016 West African epidemic (11,310 deaths across Guinea, Liberia and Sierra Leone) in total toll, and it got there far faster.[^2]

A strain without a playbook

The culprit is Bundibugyo ebolavirus, a rarer viral species than the Zaire strain behind the 2014–2016 and 2018–2020 outbreaks — the latter of which was brought under control in this same region of DRC using Merck's licensed vaccine, Ervebo, deployed through ring vaccination. No such tool exists for Bundibugyo: there is currently no approved vaccine or treatment for it, per WHO and the US CDC.[^3][^4]

That gap compounds an already difficult response. UN officials cite attacks on at least a dozen health facilities, strikes by unpaid health workers, misinformation, and burial customs involving close family contact with the dead as factors accelerating spread; an estimated 60–70% of deaths are occurring in the community rather than in treatment centers, meaning a large share of transmission chains likely go undetected until after the fact.[^1][^2] Julian Harneis, the UN's senior Ebola coordinator in DRC, has described a response also complicated by armed-group violence, including the M23 conflict in North Kivu.[^1]

Biotech's compressed timeline

With nothing licensed, the outbreak response leans entirely on products still in trials — and DRC's death toll is outrunning them. Gilead Sciences' oral antiviral obeldesivir entered its first efficacy trial as post-exposure prophylaxis on July 14, under the EBO-PEP protocol aiming to enroll roughly 1,000 high-risk contacts in DRC and Uganda; Gilead donated 2,400 bottles of the drug plus 2,000 vials of remdesivir, with initial funding of €3.4 million from the Global Health EDCTP3 partnership and $1 million from Africa CDC.[^5][^6] Separately, WHO's Therapeutics Advisory Group has recommended Regeneron's maftivimab — an antibody component of its already-approved Inmazeb cocktail — for Bundibugyo trials, based on broad cross-strain activity seen in vitro.[^7]

On the vaccine side, a Bundibugyo-specific candidate developed by Oxford University and the Serum Institute of India entered a UK Phase 1 trial on July 24; Health Canada authorized a Phase 1 trial of Moderna's mRNA-1469 six days later, enrolling around 80 volunteers.[^8][^9] Researchers involved say results are still months away — a timeline that all but guarantees this outbreak's current wave will crest, one way or another, before a vaccine is available to deploy.[^10]

Gold, tin and the government ledger

The outbreak's geography matters beyond public health. Its epicenter, Ituri province, and its spread into North and South Kivu sit inside DRC's principal belt for tantalum, tin, tungsten and gold — the so-called 3TG minerals.[^11] Barrick's Kibali mine, Africa's largest gold operation at roughly 673,000 ounces produced in 2025, lies in Haut-Uele province on Ituri's border; Barrick told Reuters in May it had stepped up screening, noting some of its staff and contractors come from the affected province. Alphamin Resources' Bisie mine in North Kivu, which produced a record 18,576 tonnes of tin last year — about 6.3% of global mined supply — sits closer still to the outbreak zone.[^11]

DRC's copper and cobalt heartland, over 1,500km south in Lualaba and Haut-Katanga, remains largely insulated for now and generates the bulk of state mining revenue. But the 2018–2020 Ebola outbreak in this same eastern region offers a cautionary comparison: research from that period found mining household income in affected territories fell nearly 20% within six months, and the share of licensed miners in Mambasa territory operating at baseline dropped from 95% to 27% as formal trade gave way to informal, harder-to-trace channels.[^11] Both Dodd-Frank's Section 1502 and the EU's conflict-minerals regulation require due-diligence tracing of DRC-sourced 3TG minerals; field verification by programs like ITSCI becomes harder to sustain under emergency conditions, raising the risk that more material moves through Uganda, Rwanda and Burundi — corridors already flagged under OECD guidance.[^11]

The next markers worth watching are narrower than the headline death toll: interim safety data from the Gilead and Moderna trials, expected within months; WHO's weekly case counts, which will show whether the outbreak is still accelerating or has begun to plateau; and any operational disclosures from Barrick or Alphamin signaling that screening measures have escalated into output disruption.
