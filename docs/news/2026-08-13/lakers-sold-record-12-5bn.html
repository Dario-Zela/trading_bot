LA Lakers sold for record $12.5bn to Josh Kushner and Bob Iger

Crypto.com Arena in downtown Los Angeles, home of the Lakers since 1999. — Troutfarm27 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Mark Walter is selling the Lakers to Josh Kushner and Bob Iger for a record $12.5bn, a $2.5bn profit just 14 months after buying the team.

Fourteen months ago, Mark Walter completed the largest purchase of an American sports franchise on record, paying the Buss family $10bn for control of the Los Angeles Lakers. On Wednesday, he agreed to sell it — for $12.5bn, to a venture capitalist and a former studio boss, pocketing a $2.5bn profit in barely more than a year.

The buyers are Josh Kushner, founder of the venture firm Thrive Capital, and Bob Iger, who spent parts of three decades running the Walt Disney Company before stepping down for a second time this spring. The two men know each other well: Iger joined Thrive as an adviser after his first Disney exit in 2022, and rejoined the firm again this past April after handing the CEO job to Josh D'Amaro. Buying America's most storied basketball franchise together is, in a sense, a continuation of a partnership rather than the start of one.

"We are deeply honored for the opportunity to become stewards of the Los Angeles Lakers, one of the most iconic sports franchises in the world," Kushner and Iger said in a joint statement, adding that their "long-term commitment is to build on that foundation, compete at the highest level, and serve this extraordinary team, its fans, and the city of Los Angeles."

Iger later told reporters the agreement "came together in three days" — a startlingly short runway for a deal of this size, though Kushner and Iger were reportedly already deep into an unrelated bid for an NBA expansion franchise in Las Vegas before pivoting toward Los Angeles instead.

A very short hold

Walter's Lakers tenure was, by the standards of franchise ownership, a blink. He struck his agreement with the Buss family in June 2025 at a $10bn valuation — itself a record, comfortably eclipsing the Boston Celtics' $6.1bn sale the year before — and the NBA's Board of Governors didn't formally approve the transfer until October 30, 2025. Less than ten months after that gavel came down, he is out again.

Walter, chief executive of Guggenheim Partners, is keeping the Dodgers, the WNBA's Sparks and the PWHL. He is also, according to reporting from CBS Sports and others, currently the subject of federal and SEC scrutiny into roughly $16bn in loans tied to insurance subsidiaries under his holding company, TWG Global — an investigation he denies any wrongdoing in connection with. Whether that inquiry hastened his exit from the Lakers or the numbers simply became too good to refuse is not something either side has said on the record. What is clear is that turning a $2.5bn profit on a nine-figure basketball team in fourteen months is not a normal outcome, even in a market that has spent the past two years re-rating sports franchises as scarce, cash-generative trophy assets.

Who's writing the cheque

Josh Kushner, 40, built Thrive Capital into one of the more consequential venture firms of the past decade, with early stakes in Instagram, Stripe and, more recently, OpenAI. He is also the younger brother of Jared Kushner and already held minority stakes in the Memphis Grizzlies and a small piece of the Miami Heat, making the Lakers his first controlling position in a major American sports franchise.

Iger brings something Kushner's chequebook alone cannot: four decades of Southern California business relationships and a hard-won reputation for stewarding marquee brands, forged over two stints running Disney through the ABC, Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm and Fox integrations. Jeanie Buss, who will remain the team's governor for at least five years under terms carried over from the Walter sale, and the Buss family's residual 15% stake give the new ownership group continuity that a purely financial buyer would have struggled to buy outright.

What a $12.5bn basketball team says about capital

Strip away the celebrity of it, and the Lakers deal reads like most of the other mega-cap transactions crossing desks this year: private capital chasing a small number of assets perceived as genuinely scarce, regardless of financing costs or macro noise. Trophy sports franchises fit that description almost too neatly — there are 30 NBA teams, most of them never for sale, and the Lakers are arguably the single most recognisable global brand among them.

The pattern of quick, profitable flips is becoming less unusual too. Walter's fourteen-month turn from buyer to seller mirrors a broader compression in holding periods for prestige assets, as valuations climb fast enough that even recent purchases look underpriced within a year. Whether $12.5bn proves to be a peak or simply the new floor for the next comparable sale — the Warriors, the Knicks, whichever iconic franchise changes hands next — is the question every league owner and prospective buyer will be watching.

The formal transfer still needs sign-off from the NBA's Board of Governors, whose other 29 owners are expected to treat the vote as a formality when they next meet in New York in September. Barring surprises there, Kushner and Iger will spend the rest of the year figuring out how much of Walter's short reign to keep, and how much of Iger's brand-stewardship instincts to bring to a franchise that has rarely needed rebuilding — just, on current form, a lot more patience on the court than its new owners are paying for off it.
