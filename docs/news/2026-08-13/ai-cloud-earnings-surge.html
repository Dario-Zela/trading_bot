Nebius and CoreWeave surge on blowout AI cloud earnings as rally spreads to Asia

An Nvidia H100 GPU — the accelerator chip powering the AI cloud capacity that Nebius and CoreWeave say is sold out years in advance. — 极客湾Geekerwan / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Nebius jumped 34% and CoreWeave 19% on blowout earnings, with both saying GPU capacity is sold out years ahead — a supply, not demand, problem.

The neoclouds had a very good Wednesday.

Nebius Group closed up 34% on August 12th after reporting second-quarter revenue that grew 454% year-on-year, to $582.3 million, comfortably ahead of the $572.75 million analysts had pencilled in. CoreWeave, the larger and more established of the two AI-infrastructure specialists, rose 19% on revenue of $2.58 billion — up 112% — and a backlog of contracted future business that has swollen to $104.2 billion. Both companies beat on the bottom line too: Nebius posted an adjusted loss of 12 cents a share against a forecast 62-cent loss, while CoreWeave narrowed its per-share loss to $1.14 versus an expected $1.41.

Those are big numbers, even by the standards of a sector that has spent two years redefining what a big number looks like. But the detail that moved the market further than the headline growth rates was a more structural one: both companies indicated that near-term GPU capacity is essentially spoken for, years out.

Sold out, not slowing down

CoreWeave chief executive Michael Intrator told analysts the company had "reached an important inflection point this quarter as our scale began to translate into expanding operating leverage" — corporate language for a business that has stopped worrying about finding customers and started worrying about finding enough silicon. The firm's $104 billion backlog, up 246% year-on-year, doesn't even include a further $25 billion of new customer commitments signed since the quarter closed. Citi analyst Tyler Radke noted that AI infrastructure demand remains strong with capacity largely sold out — a read-through that let CoreWeave raise prices roughly 25% on new contracts without denting order flow, according to reporting from Inc.

Nebius told a similar story with different emphasis. Founder and chief executive Arkady Volozh called it the company's strongest commercial quarter yet, pointing to four AI cloud deals signed in the period with an average contract value north of $1 billion each. Nebius's core cloud unit generated $574.9 million in revenue, up 514% year-on-year, with annualised run-rate revenue now at $3 billion. Volozh went further than most executives are willing to on capacity: he said Nebius could, in principle, sell its entire planned 2027 capacity today on terms similar to its latest deals, but was deliberately holding some back to capture better pricing on shorter-term contracts closer to delivery. The company also lifted its contracted power target to 5 gigawatts by the end of 2026 — a figure that matters because power, not chips, is increasingly the binding constraint on how fast data centres can be built.

That is the piece of this story that separates it from an ordinary earnings beat. When a software company beats estimates, the natural question is how long the growth rate can hold. When a capacity provider says its output is sold out into 2027 and 2028 even as it raises prices, the growth-rate question becomes secondary to a supply one: the constraint is not whether customers want more AI compute, but whether the industry can build data centres and secure power fast enough to sell it to them.

The rally spreads to Tokyo

The move reverberated well beyond Nasdaq. Japan's Nikkei 225 climbed 1.1% on Thursday to close above 68,000 — a one-month high — while the broader Topix rose 1% to a fresh record around 4,180. Chip and AI-linked names led: Kioxia Holdings gained 6.2%, Taiyo Yuden 8.6%, Advantest 5.3%, Ibiden 7.3% and Murata Manufacturing 7.4%.

The Tokyo rally wasn't only a read-through from Nebius and CoreWeave — it also rode on softer US inflation data released the same week, which trimmed the market-implied odds of a further Federal Reserve rate move and gave risk assets room to run generally. But traders in Tokyo were explicit about the AI angle: memory and component makers that supply the same data-centre buildout Nebius and CoreWeave are racing to expand into rallied hardest, suggesting Japanese investors are treating US neocloud results as a demand signal for their own supply chain, not just a US equity story.

What the sold-out order books actually mean

Put together, the two threads point in the same direction. A capacity provider selling out years ahead is not a company managing decelerating demand — it is a company rationing it. That changes the debate that has hung over AI infrastructure stocks for much of 2026, which has centred on whether hyperscaler capital expenditure could plateau once the current build cycle matures. Nebius and CoreWeave's numbers suggest the more binding question is upstream: power procurement, construction timelines and chip allocation, not customer appetite.

It also explains why the rally didn't stay contained to the two earnings-day movers. If demand for AI compute is outstripping the industry's ability to supply it, the beneficiaries run backward through the chain — from the cloud operators, to the chipmakers and memory suppliers that build for them, to the equipment makers behind those. That is roughly the order in which Wednesday's and Thursday's gains landed, from Nebius and CoreWeave in New York to Advantest and Murata in Tokyo.

The next test of that thesis comes from the hyperscalers themselves. Microsoft, Amazon and Google have all signalled further increases to AI capital spending in coming quarters, and any sign of hesitation there — rather than in neocloud earnings — would be the more meaningful indicator that the supply squeeze is easing. Nebius's next scheduled capacity update, tied to its 5-gigawatt power target, is due by year-end.
