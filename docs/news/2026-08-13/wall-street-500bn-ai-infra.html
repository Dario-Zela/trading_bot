Wall Street banks channel $500bn into Nvidia-backed AI infrastructure

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang at the company's CES 2025 keynote in Las Vegas, where he unveiled the Blackwell GPU platform now central to the financing push. — Pronoia / Wikimedia Commons (CC0)
In one sentence
Nvidia and six Wall Street giants target $500bn to finance AI data centres, concentrating institutional capital around one chipmaker as antitrust scrutiny grows.

Six of the biggest names in American finance — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — have agreed to help raise roughly $500bn to build the physical guts of the AI boom: data centres, chips, servers, cooling systems and the power infrastructure to run them. The financing was announced this week alongside Nvidia, whose GPUs sit at the centre of nearly every project the capital is meant to fund.

The headline number is large even by AI-cycle standards, where large numbers have become routine. But the more interesting detail is structural: this is Wall Street formally treating computing hardware — GPUs, racks, the buildings around them — as a distinct asset class, the way it already treats toll roads, airports and power plants.

How the money moves

The arrangement works through what the participants are calling compute financing platforms — vehicles that pool institutional credit, insurance capital and private funds, then lend it to Nvidia's customers so they can build data centres without loading the debt onto their own balance sheets. Nvidia sweetens the arrangement by retaining the option to guarantee up to 25% of any individual deal, which lets borrowers secure cheaper financing than they could get on their own.

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, framed the logic bluntly: "In AI, compute is revenue. We are bringing the world's leading long-term capital providers together to independently underwrite AI infrastructure." KKR's co-chief executives, Joe Bae and Scott Nuttall, put it in asset-manager terms: "Compute has become a critical infrastructure asset ... delivery, not ambition, is the hard part." Apollo's president, Jim Zelter, called modern compute "a scarce, mission-critical asset class."

It is worth noting what the $500bn figure actually is: a target set out in memorandums of understanding, not a signed, binding commitment. The agreements give the six firms and Nvidia a framework to originate deals: nothing so far obliges them to deploy the full sum.

Why the toll-road pitch

Huang has taken to describing GPUs as productive, long-lived, fungible assets — closer to a toll road or a power station than a piece of consumer electronics that depreciates on a three-year cycle. That comparison is doing real work: it is the argument institutional lenders need to hear before they will treat a rack of chips as loan collateral rather than a wasting asset.

The scale of demand underneath it is genuine. Bank of America estimates hyperscalers will spend $860bn on AI capital expenditure in 2026, rising to $1.2 trillion in 2027, and those companies have increasingly turned to bond markets and equity issuance to cover it rather than relying solely on operating cash flow. Financing platforms like this one are, in effect, a new pipe for that spending — one that keeps the debt off the hyperscalers' own books and onto those of pension funds, insurers and private-credit vehicles instead.

Not everyone underwriting the boom is comfortable with where the money is going. Jane Sydenham, a senior investment manager at Rathbones, captured the unease directly: "The worry is that more and more money is going into these projects. Are they all going to earn the right return for the future?" It is a fair question for hardware whose useful economic life — likely three to five years before the next generation of chips makes it obsolete — is far shorter than the toll roads and power plants Huang likes to compare it to.

One vendor, most of the money

What makes this deal more than a financing curiosity is who sits in the middle of it. Nvidia controls somewhere near 90% of the market for high-end AI accelerators, and the compute platforms being built with this $500bn are, almost without exception, architected around its chips and its software stack. Half a trillion dollars of institutional capital is not being spread across competing hardware approaches — it is being routed through a single vendor's ecosystem, with that same vendor helping guarantee the debt.

That concentration arrives as regulators are already circling. The US Department of Justice has escalated its antitrust inquiry into Nvidia from questionnaires to legally binding subpoenas, examining whether the company makes it difficult for buyers to switch suppliers and whether it penalises customers who do not buy exclusively from it. Separate scrutiny has attached to Nvidia's direct equity stakes in AI developers such as OpenAI, with antitrust specialists warning that financial ties of that kind can give a dominant chipmaker reason to treat rival customers less favourably. A $500bn financing programme in which Nvidia is both supplier and guarantor does not resolve that tension — it deepens it, tying the returns of some of the world's largest asset managers to the regulatory fate of one company.

For markets, the read is fairly direct: this is where a meaningful slice of institutional dry powder is now flowing, and it is flowing toward the infrastructure layer rather than into AI software or model companies, where competition remains more open. The capex cycle, in other words, may already be consolidating around its winner rather than spreading the field.

The MOUs signed this week still have to become binding deals, project by project, before the money actually moves — a process likely to stretch over years rather than months. Nvidia's next scheduled test of investor appetite comes on August 26, when it reports fiscal second-quarter earnings and gives Wall Street its next read on whether GPU demand, and the financing built to chase it, still justifies the toll-road comparison.
