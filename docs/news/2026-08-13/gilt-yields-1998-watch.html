Gilt yields hover near 1998 highs ahead of autumn budget

John Healey, appointed Chancellor of the Exchequer by Prime Minister Andy Burnham on 20 July 2026, now faces markets pricing fiscal risk ahead of his 28 October budget. — Richard Townshend / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
UK 30-year gilt yields are hovering near 1998 highs as a new chancellor, John Healey, heads into an October 28 budget under tight fiscal-rule scrutiny.

Long-dated UK government debt is trading like it's 1998 again — not in spirit, but in yield. The 30-year gilt closed at 5.71% on August 12, within a whisker of the multi-decade highs touched earlier this year, and the benchmark 10-year gilt sits close to 5%. For a government eight weeks from its first budget under a new leader, that is not background noise. It is the market setting the terms before the Treasury has finished writing them.

The numbers alone would be a story in a quiet year. This is not a quiet year. Keir Starmer resigned as prime minister on 22 June after losing the confidence of his own MPs; Andy Burnham was acclaimed Labour leader on 17 July and became prime minister three days later. His first significant act was to sack Rachel Reeves as chancellor and install John Healey, the former defence secretary, in her place. Healey has now confirmed his first budget for 28 October — earlier than the 26 November date Reeves had been working toward — and says it will stick to the fiscal rules he inherited.

A new chancellor, an old test

Markets gave Healey's appointment a verdict within hours: 10-year yields rose eight basis points the day he was named, pushing borrowing costs to a two-month high, after Burnham told reporters he intended to use "any flexibility" available within the existing rules. Investors read that as code for more borrowing dressed up as investment — Healey is reportedly drawing up plans to borrow an extra £9bn a year to take stakes in infrastructure assets, spending that can be structured to sit outside the current-budget rule even as it adds to the debt pile. Gilts and sterling have since stabilised somewhat, but traders are withholding any real rally until the budget itself spells out the numbers.

That wariness has precedent. Yields have brushed 1998 territory repeatedly over the past eighteen months — in January and September 2025, then again in May 2026 at 5.78% — each spike tracking a fresh doubt about Westminster's ability to hold its fiscal line rather than any single data print. The Bank of England has added its own pressure: it held rates at 3.75% on 30 July in a 6-3 vote, with three MPC members — Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill — pushing for a hike on inflation risk, a stance that keeps a floor under gilt yields at the long end.

Why the long end won't cooperate

Underneath the politics sits a structural problem the Debt Management Office has been managing for years: pension funds and insurers, once the natural buyers of 30-year-plus gilts, have largely stopped needing them, as defined-benefit schemes mature and de-risk. The DMO has responded by shrinking the share of long-dated issuance to under 10% of the annual programme, down from more than 30% five years ago and the smallest since gilt sales began in 1998 — the same year, incidentally, that yields last sat this high. Thinner natural demand at the long end means every fresh dose of fiscal uncertainty moves the yield further than it once would have.

The next hard data point is Healey's budget on 28 October, when the Office for Budget Responsibility publishes the forecast against which his fiscal rules will be judged. Until then, expect gilt auctions and any further hints of Burnham's "flexibility" to keep doing the talking.
