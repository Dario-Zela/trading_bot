AI is now table stakes for institutions — the next battle is fought over megawatts

Server racks in a modern data centre: for institutional AI, access to this infrastructure is becoming as strategically important as the algorithms running on it. — Carl Lender / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Barclays surveys 410 investors and finds 72% of hedge funds use AI daily — now Andreessen Horowitz warns that power and cooling, not algorithms, will decide who leads next.

The debate over whether institutional investors should adopt AI has been settled. The new question — harder, more physical, and ultimately more consequential — is whether the electricity grid can keep up with what they want to do with it.

That juxtaposition is sharpest when two stories run side by side. Barclays Investment Bank published a survey this month of 410 fixed-income professionals across North America, Europe, the Middle East, and Asia showing that AI has crossed the threshold from experiment to daily infrastructure. Around the same time, Andreessen Horowitz — the venture capital firm that has committed billions to AI's future — published a pointed policy warning: the race for AI leadership, it argues, "could be won or lost on the electrical grid."

For trading firms specifically, the implication is uncomfortable. Competitive advantage no longer flows purely from better models or sharper data. It flows, increasingly, from whoever controls the megawatts.

AI in the daily stack

The Barclays numbers are concrete. Of the 410 fixed-income investors surveyed — hedge funds, long-only managers, and asset owners — 72% of hedge funds now use AI on a daily basis. For long-only managers and asset owners, that figure is 52%. The most common use cases are research and data processing: about 44% of hedge funds apply AI specifically to market data flows. Trade execution and portfolio construction remain largely human, but the surrounding infrastructure — research synthesis, document review, market intelligence — has been substantially automated.

The survey also delivers a useful read on sentiment: only 7% of respondents expect AI to reduce headcount in the near term. Data security and privacy concerns rank as the most significant barrier to wider deployment. Notably, the binding constraint is not technological skepticism. The institutions surveyed have decided AI works. What they are negotiating now is operational risk — who holds the data, where it runs, and on what terms.

The physical stack behind the model

The constraint that the Barclays survey didn't ask about may matter most. AI workloads are physically voracious in ways that traditional software never was. A conventional server rack draws roughly 5 to 10 kilowatts. An AI rack draws 50 to 100 — sometimes more, depending on the GPU density — and generates heat that requires fundamentally different cooling architectures. Liquid cooling, direct-to-chip cooling, and full immersion systems are no longer experimental; they are becoming standard requirements for high-density AI builds. U.S. data centre electricity demand has climbed from 23 gigawatts in 2023 to 42 gigawatts this year, and Barclays' own analysis projects that figure could triple to represent 12% of total U.S. electricity supply by 2028, up from 4.4% in 2023.

The electrical grid is not ready. More than 2,600 gigawatts of new energy projects are currently queued for grid interconnection — a backlog that exceeds the entire installed U.S. power plant fleet more than twice over. New grid connections take five to seven years in many markets. Lead times for high-voltage transformers, the components that make large-scale data centres energisable, have stretched from 12–18 months to 36–48 months or longer in some procurement markets. Roughly half of global data centre projects are facing delays due to power limitations. Gartner estimates that 40% of AI data centres will face outright power restrictions by 2027.

Matt Perault, head of AI policy at Andreessen Horowitz, put it plainly: "When talking to cloud leaders and data centre operators about why there's a compute crunch, it's clear that access to power is often a bigger bottleneck than access to NVIDIA's coveted GPUs themselves." The a16z policy paper frames the structural problem: "Energy could become AI's new moat."

Megawatts as competitive moat

The second-order effect is a stratification of the market. Large hyperscalers — Microsoft, Meta, Google — can negotiate long-term Power Purchase Agreements that lock in energy capacity years in advance, effectively buying out entire regions' future supply. Microsoft has committed $15.2 billion to a UAE AI infrastructure partnership explicitly structured around renewable energy access. Meta's $10 billion Louisiana campus was sited with available grid capacity as a primary criterion. Smaller operators — and the trading firms and asset managers that rely on cloud compute to run their strategies — face volatile spot-market pricing and extended waits.

For trading firms, this creates a performance differential that is structural rather than technical. An institution that secures dedicated compute infrastructure — through direct data centre investment or through cloud providers with locked-in power — will deploy new strategies faster, run more intensive inference workloads, and iterate more quickly on models. A rival relying on shared or constrained infrastructure faces latency that compounds across every deployment cycle. The Barclays survey shows hedge funds already use AI to process market data at scale. The question is whose data gets processed first when power is rationed.

Andreessen Horowitz has moved from analysis to investment. The firm co-led a $140 million Series B for Heron Power, a next-generation grid company, and separately backed Orbital, a startup developing space-based data centre infrastructure designed to bypass terrestrial power constraints. These are not hedged bets — they are explicit wagers that the physical infrastructure layer will determine which AI strategies are executable and which remain theoretical.

What to watch

The Barclays survey captures institutional AI at a moment of consolidation: the tools work, adoption is broad, and the remaining friction is operational rather than philosophical. The constraint that will shape the next phase is outside most investment teams' direct control.

The practical catalysts are specific rather than abstract: grid interconnection queue reform in the U.S. and EU, transformer manufacturing capacity, and the rate at which large-scale power purchase agreements get signed. When the next major trading firm announces a direct data centre investment or a dedicated cloud compute partnership, that will be the signal — not that their models are better, but that they have secured the electrons to run them.
