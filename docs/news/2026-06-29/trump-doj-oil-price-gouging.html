Trump tells DOJ to probe Exxon, Chevron, Shell and BP over pump prices

A Speedway gas station in Minneapolis showing elevated pump prices in March 2022 — a period that prefigured the sharper spike triggered by the 2026 Iran conflict. — Wikimedia Commons / Tony Webster (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Trump told the DOJ to probe Exxon, Chevron, Shell, and BP over pump prices — but no formal investigation has launched, limiting this to political pressure for now.

President Donald Trump on Tuesday named four of the world's largest oil companies — ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, and BP — as targets of a potential federal price-gouging investigation, claiming that pump prices have failed to fall in line with sharply lower crude costs since the Iran conflict subsided.

"We should be, in my opinion, at $2.25 right now at the pump," Trump wrote in a late-night Truth Social post. "The big Oil Companies are not dropping their price at the pump commensurate with the sharply lower prices they are paying for Oil. In other words, customers are being 'gouged'." He added that he had instructed the Justice Department to "immediately start looking into this."

The national average for a gallon of regular gasoline stood at roughly $3.93 on Wednesday — down from $4.51 a month ago but still well above the $2.98 per gallon recorded just before the Iran war began. Brent crude, which surged past $120 a barrel when Iranian forces closed the Strait of Hormuz in early spring, has since retreated by around 36% following a peace agreement that reopened the vital shipping lane.

Why prices lag

The gap between crude and the pump is not new, and industry participants were quick to say so. The American Petroleum Institute said retail fuel prices "don't move in lockstep with crude oil," citing supply disruptions, refining bottlenecks, and inventory cycles that are still resolving after the most severe energy shock in decades. Chevron's chief financial officer was direct: "It's going to take time" for lower crude costs to reach consumers — a view that energy economists broadly support. Crude-to-pump transmission typically takes six weeks or more under normal conditions; after a disruption on the scale of a Hormuz closure, that lag can extend considerably.

Historical precedent offers little comfort for those hoping a DOJ referral will move prices. Federal probes of retail gasoline markets stretching back decades — including an FTC investigation launched after Hurricane Katrina in 2006 — have consistently failed to identify anti-competitive behaviour at scale. The 2006 report found that price increases reflected actual cost increases rather than illegal coordination. No federal statute specifically targets retail gasoline price spikes driven by non-coordinated sellers.

The Justice Department did not confirm a formal investigation launch. A DOJ spokesperson acknowledged that "fuel prices impact the wallet of every American" and characterised rising costs as "a national security issue," but offered no details on investigative scope, timeline, or the standard of evidence being sought.

Markets registered the uncertainty: ExxonMobil fell 2.03% and Chevron dropped 2.57% on the day of the announcement. The moves, while notable, reflect headline risk rather than priced-in regulatory consequence — the absence of any formal filing or prosecutorial framework limits how seriously traders can treat the threat.

The political texture here is also unusual. Oil executives invested heavily in Trump's 2024 return: the industry directed roughly $96 million into his reelection efforts, with total cycle spending estimated at $445 million. The companies now being accused of gouging American consumers are the same ones whose executives sat in the front rows of campaign fundraisers. None of Exxon, Chevron, Shell, or BP issued public comment in response to the allegation.

The next signal to watch is whether the DOJ issues any formal subpoenas or civil investigative demands — the procedural step that would confirm an investigation has moved from presidential statement to institutional action. Without that, the episode remains a political pressure campaign rather than a regulatory event. Watch also for any movement in AAA's weekly national average price; if the downward trajectory continues toward $3.50 or below, the political temperature around the probe may ease before it formalises.
