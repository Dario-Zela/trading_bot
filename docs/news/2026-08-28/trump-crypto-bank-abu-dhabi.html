Trump family's crypto bank draws Abu Dhabi royal backing

Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan, the UAE's national security adviser, whose investment vehicle holds a 49% stake in the entity behind the Trump family's crypto bank. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A UAE royal's $500m stake in Trump family crypto venture World Liberty Financial sits behind its newly OCC-approved trust bank charter, per the WSJ.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency does not, as a rule, publish the names of foreign royals in its bank-charter decisions. That omission is now the story. When the regulator granted preliminary approval on August 14 for World Liberty Trust Company — the banking arm of the Trump family's crypto venture — to hold, redeem and custody its stablecoin, its filings named the investor only as StringZ Holding RSC. The Wall Street Journal has since reported what the OCC left out: StringZ is controlled by Sheikh Tahnoon bin Zayed Al Nahyan, the United Arab Emirates' national security adviser, and it owns 49% of the entity behind the bank.

The arrangement traces back further than the bank charter itself. In January 2025, four days before Donald Trump's inauguration, an investment vehicle tied to Sheikh Tahnoon signed a deal to buy 49% of World Liberty Financial for $500 million — roughly half paid upfront, with $187 million landing directly in Trump family-controlled entities and at least $31 million going to entities tied to Steve Witkoff, the venture's other co-founder. The Trump family's own stake was diluted from 75% to 38% as part of the transaction, though Sheikh Tahnoon's group also picked up two board seats. Eric Trump signed on the family's behalf.

From stablecoin to chartered bank

World Liberty Financial's main product is USD1, a dollar-pegged stablecoin that has grown to roughly $5 billion in market capitalization — the fifth-largest stablecoin in circulation less than a year after launch. Until now, USD1's issuance and custody have run through BitGo Bank & Trust. The charter application, filed in January 2026, would move that function in-house, under World Liberty Trust Company, National Association, a limited-purpose national trust bank proposed for Bay Harbor Islands, Florida.

The charter is narrower than it sounds. It does not permit taking federally insured deposits or making loans — this is a trust bank, not a commercial one. Before it can open, the company still has to clear conditions the OCC laid out: at least $20 million in capital, a qualified internal audit manager, and formal notice of any material change to its business plan. In other words, a fully operational Trump-linked bank is not yet a fact on the ground. A conditionally approved one, backed by an Abu Dhabi sovereign figure, now is.

Why Abu Dhabi wants in

Sheikh Tahnoon is not a passive investor. He chairs G42, the Emirati state-backed AI company, and sits atop Mubadala, one of Abu Dhabi's sovereign wealth funds — institutions that have spent the past two years negotiating access to advanced American AI chips, a deal the Trump administration ultimately approved months after the World Liberty investment closed. USD1 has also shown up on the other side of Gulf capital flows: MGX, another Abu Dhabi vehicle, used the stablecoin to settle a $2 billion investment into Binance last year.

Seen from Abu Dhabi, the calculation is straightforward enough: a stake in a bank bearing the sitting U.S. president's family name buys a kind of political proximity that no amount of conventional dealmaking replicates, at a moment when the relationship between Washington and the Gulf runs through chips, defense and sovereign capital all at once. Company representatives told the Journal that President Trump was not involved in structuring the deal and that it did not influence U.S. policy toward the UAE — a claim that has done little to quiet critics given the chip-export timeline.

The Washington pushback

Senator Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee, had written to the OCC in January urging it to withhold approval unless the president divested his interest in World Liberty. When the conditional charter came through anyway, she called it "the most brazen act of self-dealing our financial system has ever seen," arguing that Trump had become the first president to approve, operate and effectively supervise his own bank. Warren and nine other Senate Democrats — including Chris Murphy, Bernie Sanders and Chris Van Hollen — have since introduced the Ending Presidential Corruption in Banking Act, which would bar senior government officials from owning or controlling a bank. It has no Republican co-sponsors and, with the OCC already having acted, no obvious mechanism to unwind what's been approved.

For markets, the self-dealing argument and the legitimacy argument are two sides of the same coin. A foreign royal with sovereign-fund reach betting $500 million on a crypto venture tied to a sitting president is either a scandal or a signal, depending on where you sit — but either way, it tells traders that a slice of the world's most risk-averse capital now treats U.S. political branding as a feature of crypto infrastructure, not a liability to be priced around. Whether that logic survives due diligence is a separate question from whether it's currently working: USD1's climb to $5 billion says it is, for now.

The next checkpoint is procedural rather than dramatic — the pre-opening conditions the OCC attached to the charter, including that $20 million capital threshold and the audit-manager hire, which World Liberty Trust Company must satisfy before it can actually begin operating. Congressional Democrats have no near-term path to block it; the real test is whether other foreign sovereign investors follow Abu Dhabi's lead into U.S.-chartered crypto banking before the 2026 midterms sharpen scrutiny of the arrangement.
