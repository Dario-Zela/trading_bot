Nvidia's blowout quarter sparks best Wall Street day since April

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, whose earnings-call message that AI demand is "accelerating" drove the year's best chip-sector rally — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nvidia's revenue beat and a first-ever 70%-growth forecast for next year sent the Nasdaq up 1.6% and pulled chipmakers and software stocks along with it.

Nvidia told Wall Street on Wednesday that the artificial intelligence buildout is not slowing down — it's still accelerating — and investors who had spent three months fretting about an AI bubble spent Thursday buying stocks instead. The Nasdaq Composite rose 1.6%, its best session since early August, as chipmakers, software firms and cybersecurity companies rode the coattails of the year's most closely watched earnings report.

The report in question came from Nvidia, whose graphics processors underpin most of the world's AI data centers. The company posted revenue of $96.2 billion for its fiscal second quarter, up 106% from a year earlier and well past the $92.2 billion analysts had penciled in. More consequential than the beat itself was what came next: a forecast for the current quarter of roughly $108 billion, plus or minus 2%, against a Wall Street consensus near $104 billion — and, for the first time in the company's history, a full-year outlook. Nvidia told investors to expect revenue to grow about 70% in fiscal 2028, nearly double the 44% growth analysts had modeled.

The numbers behind the guidance

Data center revenue, the figure that matters most to anyone gauging AI capital spending, came in at $89 billion against expectations of roughly $85.7 billion. Within that, hyperscale cloud providers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google and Meta among them — accounted for $48.7 billion, with AI clouds, industrial and enterprise customers making up the remainder. Adjusted earnings per share of $2.22 beat the roughly $2.09 analysts expected and were more than double what Nvidia reported a year earlier.

Chief financial officer Colette Kress told analysts that customer demand, if Nvidia could fully meet it, would imply revenue growth closer to doubling than to 70% — a distinction that matters because it locates the constraint in supply, not appetite. Memory has become the binding constraint: Nvidia's Blackwell chips carry far more high-bandwidth memory than their predecessors, and suppliers including SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron have told customers their HBM capacity is largely sold through 2027. Nvidia is reportedly testing lower-memory configurations of its next-generation Rubin Ultra chip partly in response to that squeeze — a rare instance of a company redesigning around scarcity rather than cost.

"Demand is accelerating"

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, used the earnings call to press the case that the AI buildout has moved past speculation into paying, productive use.

"AI has reached its inflection point. It's doing useful work. Its tokens are productive and profitable. Now, compute is revenue. And demand is accelerating."

Huang argued that the base of buyers has widened well beyond the handful of frontier labs that drove early demand. "This time last year, one lab alone was driving the buildout," he said. "Today, we have a golden age of new AI labs and startups, multiple frontier labs scaling in parallel, a thriving open-model ecosystem and physical AI coming online — with strong momentum across the U.S. and around the world." Pressed on whether the 70% growth forecast was conservative, he was blunt: "Our demand is much greater than 70%."

Investors took the message at face value. Nvidia shares jumped 8.7% on Thursday, the stock's largest one-day gain since April 2025, adding roughly $442 billion to its market value in a single session — a sum larger than the entire market capitalization of all but a handful of S&P 500 companies.

A rally with company

What distinguished Thursday from prior Nvidia-driven pops was how far the enthusiasm traveled. Broadcom rose 4.5%, Micron, SanDisk and Marvell each climbed more than 4%, and South Korea's SK Hynix and Intel added to Wednesday's gains — a broad chip-sector move consistent with investors betting on a sustained, multi-year capex cycle rather than a one-company story.

Software and cybersecurity names, which had lagged the AI trade for much of the year on worries that generative AI would eventually cannibalize their businesses, also surged. Salesforce climbed roughly 22% — its best day since August 2020 — after posting revenue ahead of forecasts and expanding a product partnership with Anthropic. CrowdStrike gained more than 20% and Okta jumped 28% after both cybersecurity firms raised their outlooks, citing rising demand tied to agentic AI deployments. Adobe and Autodesk rose 5.7% and 6.2% respectively. The rally was narrow in one sense — ten of eleven S&P 500 sectors actually finished lower, with energy, consumer staples and industrials among the weakest — but within technology, the conviction was close to unanimous.

What the quarter settles, and what it doesn't

The results land directly on top of a debate that had been building since spring. Nvidia stock had drifted down from highs above $227 in early August to around $220 amid a broader AI selloff, with OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman telling reporters he believed the sector was in a bubble and that some investors would get "very burnt" when the hype unwound. Concerns centered on capital spending: hyperscalers committing hundreds of billions of dollars to data centers, some of it financed through circular arrangements between chipmakers, cloud providers and the AI labs themselves, with no clear timeline for when that spending turns into profit for anyone but Nvidia.

Thursday's numbers do not resolve that debate so much as reframe it. A revenue base growing 106% year-over-year with demand reportedly outstripping supply is difficult to square with a narrative of overbuilt capacity — for now, the bottleneck is memory chips, not customers. Whether that holds through 2027, when memory suppliers expect their own shortage to peak, and whether the hundreds of billions in hyperscaler capex translates into AI applications that generate comparable revenue, remain open questions that a single earnings beat cannot answer. Nvidia's own next report, due in November, will show whether Thursday's rally reflected a durable reassessment or the market's habit of extrapolating one very good quarter indefinitely. In the nearer term, investors turn to Federal Reserve chair Kevin Warsh's keynote at the Kansas City Fed's Jackson Hole symposium on Friday, where clues on the rate path could determine whether Thursday's gains hold or prove another AI-driven spike in an otherwise cautious market.
