Military veterans told they owe thousands over MoD clerical error

The Ministry of Defence Main Building on Whitehall, home to the department now facing questions over its pension administration. — Harland Quarrington / UK MoD, Crown Copyright, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Over 1,000 UK veterans have been billed up to six figures each after the MoD's pension administrators miscalculated payments for years.

James Uncles spent 35 years as an aircraft engineer in the Royal Navy. He is 71 now, and in February a letter arrived telling him the Ministry of Defence had overpaid his pension by £45,687 since 2021 — and wanted it back. His monthly payment was cut by more than £1,000, close to a quarter of the total, with a further £750 a month to be clawed back for the next five years. He and his wife have put off marking their 50th wedding anniversary while they work out what they can still afford.

Uncles is not alone. More than 1,000 veterans have received similar letters from Veterans UK, the MoD's pensions administrator, and its outsourced payroll handler Equiniti, after officials discovered that two of the armed forces' final-salary pension schemes had been miscalculating payments — in some cases for years. The MoD puts the total value of the overpayments at £5.1 million, spread across a group of roughly 432,000 retired service personnel who rely on the schemes for a guaranteed income in retirement.

"Is it all going to be down to innocent veterans? This scheme is clearly flawed and has been for many years, and in its wake it's leaving hundreds and hundreds of people who are having to change their lives." — James Uncles

A bill nobody earned

The errors trace back to a tangle of technical adjustments rather than one single mistake: national insurance modification calculations, the treatment of guaranteed minimum payments, guaranteed income payments, early departure awards, and pension-sharing settlements handed down in divorce cases. The Forces Pension Society, which advocates for military pensioners, says it has logged 398 confirmed national insurance modification cases alone, with more than a thousand further disputes touching the other categories.

Michael Evans, a 68-year-old retired Royal Navy captain who survived a helicopter crash during his service, was told he owed £16,000, with £414 a month now coming out of his pension. His description of the process was blunter than most: "I can't put into words what it feels like when the dead arm of the state appears from this end of the telescope to be defending its contractors' errors rather than supporting its veterans."

The MoD first spotted the discrepancies in November 2024. Letters to affected pensioners did not start landing until this year — a gap of well over a year between discovery and disclosure that the department has not explained in detail.

Who pays for the mistake

Under Treasury rules, government bodies are required to recover money paid out in error, regardless of who was at fault. An MoD spokesperson said: "Where payments have been made in genuine error, we have a responsibility to recover taxpayer funds and will always seek to do so in a sensitive and proportionate way, including through tailored repayment plans and one-to-one financial and welfare support from veterans services." Veterans UK says its debt recovery team will consider reasonable repayment proposals case by case.

That framing is exactly what the Forces Pension Society disputes. Major General Neil Marshall, the society's chief executive, argues the logic runs backwards: "It seems to us that the requirement to refund the public purse should lie with Veterans UK and Equiniti," not the pensioners who received money in good faith and spent it accordingly. He has called the episode "a failure of leadership, oversight, process and quality assurance," and the society is pushing for consistent, transparent handling of cases and greater use of the Internal Dispute Resolution Procedure — with the Pensions Ombudsman as a last resort for veterans who want to contest individual demands.

The wider pattern

There is nothing exotic about the mechanism at fault here — a payroll system fed the wrong inputs, for years, without anyone catching it. What makes it notable is the asymmetry of consequence: the institution that made the error faces reputational cost and a review; the individuals on fixed retirement incomes face five-year repayment plans and, for some, five-figure lump sums. It is a small but sharp illustration of a broader problem with large administered-benefit systems — public pensions, tax credits, disability payments — where the entity doing the calculating rarely bears the downside of getting it wrong.

The Forces Pension Society expects more cases to surface as it continues collecting reports from members, and the dispute over who ultimately absorbs the £5.1 million is unresolved. The next marker to watch is whether the MoD amends its recovery approach in response to the society's pressure, or whether individual veterans are left to fight case-by-case through the ombudsman route.
