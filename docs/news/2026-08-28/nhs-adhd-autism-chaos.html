NHS bosses warn of 'chaos' in ADHD and autism care

In one sentence
NHS leaders say ADHD and autism assessment waits — now up to 15 years in places — have pushed private providers into doing half of all NHS ADHD assessments.

Health service leaders in England say the system for diagnosing and treating ADHD and autism has become unworkable, with waiting lists so long that some patients face a decade or more before an assessment. NHS England data cited by the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman puts the number of people waiting for an ADHD assessment as high as 803,658 — a backlog that has grown steadily even as ministers acknowledge the health service cannot keep pace.

Complaints to the ombudsman about ADHD and autism care rose from 410 in 2021/22 to 1,257 in 2025/26, according to a review of roughly 3,000 cases spanning six years. Ombudsman Paula Sussex said patients are being forced to "fight their way" through a "complex and confusing system", citing fragmented care, unclear rights to choose a provider, and inconsistent NHS recognition of diagnoses made privately.

The picture varies sharply by postcode. Birmingham children are currently waiting at least 32 months for an autism assessment and 19 months for ADHD, while some other areas report waits stretching to 10–15 years, according to the final report of the independent ADHD Taskforce chaired by Professor Anita Thapar, published in November 2025. Health Secretary Wes Streeting has called the NHS response "falling short" of demand, and when asked in Parliament whether the government was failing to cope with an epidemic, replied: "In a nutshell, yes."

Who fills the gap

The capacity shortfall is already reshaping who actually delivers ADHD and autism assessments. Independent-sector analysis from the Independent Healthcare Providers Network estimates that private providers now carry out at least half of all NHS-commissioned ADHD assessments and more than a third of autism assessments, largely through the NHS's own Right to Choose scheme, which lets patients opt for an alternative accredited provider when local waits are too long.

That outsourcing has a price tag: independent-sector ADHD spending reached roughly £164m against a projected £15.6bn annual NHS mental health budget — a small share of the total, but a growing one, and several integrated care boards have already paused new Right to Choose referrals this year after exceeding their commissioning budgets, including a system-wide freeze in Greater Manchester. The taskforce's own assessment was blunt: NHS services have "inadequate service capacity for all ages" and the condition remains "under-recognised, under-diagnosed and under-treated." Untreated ADHD alone is estimated to cost the UK about £17bn a year in lost employment, education and criminal-justice outcomes — the kind of figure that keeps commercial diagnostic and therapy providers watching the sector closely, even as NHS leaders warn that leaning further on the private sector without matching investment simply shifts, rather than solves, the capacity problem.

A government-commissioned review into mental health, ADHD and autism services, chaired by UCL's Professor Peter Fonagy, was due to report its findings this summer; its conclusions on funding, prevention and service design will shape whether the current strain becomes the new normal or the trigger for a rebuild.
