Global study links long-term air pollution exposure to millions of cancer cases

Beijing's skyline on a clear day and shrouded in smog — the kind of long-term exposure researchers now link to millions of cancer cases worldwide. — Bobak Ha'Eri / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A 34-country study attributes over 18 million cancer cases to PM2.5, NO2 and ozone exposure since 2000, adding pressure for tighter air-quality rules.

A new global analysis has put a number on something epidemiologists have long suspected but rarely quantified at this scale: breathing polluted air for years is driving millions of cancer diagnoses worldwide, in organs far beyond the lungs.

The study, published in Nature Health and led by researchers at the Chinese Academy of Sciences with collaborators from Fudan University and Boston University, tracked 109 million cancer cases across 952 locations in 34 countries between 2000 and 2020. It is among the most comprehensive attempts yet to isolate the cancer burden attributable specifically to ambient air pollution, rather than to smoking, diet or occupational exposure.

What the numbers say

The researchers modelled long-term exposure to three pollutants — fine particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen dioxide and ground-level ozone — against cancer incidence data. Each 10 micrograms-per-cubic-metre rise in PM2.5 was associated with a 16.0% increase in overall cancer risk; the same increment of NO2 carried an 11.7% rise, and ozone 4.23%.

Over the two-decade study period, the team attributed roughly 8.82 million cancer cases to PM2.5, 6.67 million to NO2 and 2.59 million to ozone — a combined burden of more than 18 million cases, though the pollutants' effects are not simply additive given overlapping exposure. The associations extended well past lung cancer to include breast, thyroid, prostate, colorectal, kidney, bladder and pancreatic tumours, and, for NO2, lymphomas. Notably, women showed a stronger pollution-linked risk than men for several cancers, including a roughly 20% increase in NO2-associated breast cancer — far higher than earlier estimates.

The researchers caution the models cannot fully separate pollution's effect from smoking, occupational exposure and socioeconomic conditions, all of which correlate with where people live and breathe. Even so, the scale of the dataset — nearly a thousand locations over 20 years — makes it harder to dismiss the pattern as noise.

Why regulators are watching

The timing sharpens an already live regulatory fight. The World Health Organisation's 2021 air quality guidelines are markedly stricter than most national limits, and the European Environment Agency reports that up to a fifth of EU monitoring stations still exceed even the bloc's current, looser standards. From this year, EU member states are required to file air quality roadmaps for any pollutant above the revised limits — a process this study's findings will likely be cited to justify accelerating. In the US, the EPA's 2024 rule targeting cancer-causing emissions such as ethylene oxide and chloroprene from chemical plants offers a template regulators elsewhere may look to extend toward the broader PM2.5 and NO2 sources highlighted here: vehicle exhaust, industrial combustion and power generation.

For carbon-heavy industries — oil and gas producers, heavy manufacturing, coal power — the study adds a health-cost dimension to an argument previously framed mostly around emissions targets. ESG-focused investors have increasingly pressed for disclosure of downstream health impacts alongside carbon accounting, and quantified cancer-burden data of this kind gives that pressure a harder number to point to, even where it does not yet translate into binding disclosure rules.

The researchers note that 99% of the global population breathes air exceeding WHO guideline levels, with the steepest exposures concentrated in low- and middle-income countries — the same places least equipped to absorb the cost of new abatement rules. The next test comes as the EU's national roadmaps take shape through the rest of this year, and as investors weigh whether health-burden data of this kind belongs in mainstream climate-risk disclosures.
