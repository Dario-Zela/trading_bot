ECB holds rates, leaves September decision open

ECB President Christine Lagarde, whose Governing Council left the door open to a September rate move. — European Central Bank / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The ECB held its deposit rate at 2.25% and refused to say whether June's rate hike was one-and-done, leaving traders to price a coin flip into September.

The European Central Bank left borrowing costs untouched at its July meeting, keeping its deposit rate at 2.25% and, in doing so, keeping traders guessing about whether the eurozone's brief and unusual return to a tightening cycle is finished or merely paused.

The Governing Council's decision was unanimous. It followed a 25-basis-point increase in June — the ECB's first rate hike in nearly three years — made in response to an energy-driven inflation shock after fighting escalated in the Middle East. Having moved once, the Bank chose not to move again, and gave itself room to decide either way come September.

A hold with a hawkish undertone

Alongside the deposit rate, the main refinancing rate held at 2.40% and the marginal lending facility at 2.65%. The accompanying statement was more cautious than dovish: the Governing Council said uncertainty "remains high" and that "the full inflationary impact of the energy shock has yet to play out," language that markets read as leaving the door open rather than closing it.

"Since our decision in June, we have had some relatively benign developments," ECB President Christine Lagarde told reporters, while adding that policymakers would "be particularly attentive to any risk of second-round effects" from higher energy costs feeding into wages and broader prices. The Bank repeated its now-familiar formula — data-dependent, meeting by meeting, no pre-commitment to a rate path — which is central-bank shorthand for: ask us again next time.

Euro-area inflation had eased to 2.8% in June before ticking back up to 2.9% in July, with energy prices running at an annual 10.3% and core inflation, which strips out food and fuel, at 2.5%. Neither reading settles the argument for or against a September hike.

What the market did with the ambiguity

Investors did what investors do when a central bank declines to commit: they priced in the more hawkish outcome. Swaps markets pushed the implied probability of a quarter-point September hike to roughly 92%, up from about 85% ahead of the decision, according to market pricing tracked at the time. The euro initially rallied through $1.1430 against the dollar before giving back the gains to close near $1.1375, a round trip that captured the market's difficulty settling on a direction. European equities wobbled through the session as the hawkish tone competed with relief that rates hadn't risen outright.

ING's Carsten Brzeski put the September calculus bluntly: "Unless oil prices start dropping significantly over the next weeks, the ECB's own macro projections in September will call for another rate hike, loud and clear."

Oil has since done exactly that

That conditional clause has aged into the story's most interesting wrinkle. Brent crude, which had approached $100 a barrel amid the Middle East conflict, has since slid toward $87, and US benchmark crude toward $81, as Iran and Oman reported progress on a maritime arrangement for the Strait of Hormuz and Washington's latest sanctions on Tehran proved milder than markets had braced for. If that decline holds through the ECB's next projections round, the energy-shock justification for a September hike weakens considerably — even as swaps markets, as of the July meeting, were still leaning toward one.

The Governing Council next meets on September 9-10 in Berlin, hosted by the Bundesbank, with fresh staff inflation and growth projections due alongside the decision. Those forecasts, built on whatever oil is doing by early September, will do more to settle the tightening-cycle question than anything said at the July press conference. Until then, EUR pairs and European equity hedges are left pricing a coin the ECB has deliberately declined to call.
