Shell weighs sale of chemicals assets as bidders circle

Construction under way at Shell's petrochemical cracker plant in Beaver County, Pennsylvania — the $14bn plastics complex now among the assets up for sale. — Drums600 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Exxon, LyondellBasell, Apollo and Kuwait Petroleum have made non-binding bids for Shell's four US chemical plants, valued at up to $8bn.

Shell is testing appetite for a sale of its United States chemicals business, and the list of parties said to be examining the assets reads like a roll call of the industry's biggest balance sheets: ExxonMobil, LyondellBasell, private equity firm Apollo Global Management, and the chemicals arm of Kuwait Petroleum Corporation. All four submitted non-binding proposals last month, according to reporting by the Financial Times and Barchart, though Shell has not committed to a sale and stresses the review remains preliminary.

At stake are four plants across Louisiana, Texas and Pennsylvania that turn crude-derived feedstock into the unglamorous building blocks of modern life — ethylene, propylene, benzene and the polymers that end up in packaging, detergents and pharmaceuticals. Bankers at Morgan Stanley are running the process. Some bids reportedly cover the whole US portfolio; others target individual sites.

A discount sale

The numbers illustrate why this looks more like capital surrender than a victory lap. The portfolio is valued at up to $8 billion — a steep markdown against what Shell actually spent building it. The Pennsylvania complex alone, a plastics plant near Monaca that began producing 1.6 million tonnes a year of polymers in 2022, cost roughly $14 billion, more than double its original budget, and has struggled operationally since startup. Selling four plants for a sum smaller than the cost of building one of them is the kind of arithmetic that tends to concentrate a board's mind.

The Deer Park facility near Houston and sites in Norco and Geismar, Louisiana, round out the portfolio. All produce commodity chemicals in a sector that has spent the past several years in a cyclical trough — Shell flagged weak chemicals trading through much of 2026 even as margins ticked up in the second quarter.

Why Shell is walking away

The sale fits a pattern chief executive Wael Sawan has been running since taking over: identify businesses that cannot compete for capital internally, then sell, shrink, partner or close them. Sawan has previously pointed to roughly $45 billion tied up in underperforming units, chemicals and renewables chief among them. Shell's own strategy language calls for "high-grading and selective closures" in Europe and "strategic and partnership opportunities" in the US, with a goal of lower capital employed in chemicals by 2030. Earlier this year Shell sold its onshore wind and solar business to TotalEnergies — the same instinct, applied to a different balance-sheet drag.

For would-be buyers, the logic runs the other way. Exxon and LyondellBasell already run US crackers and could bolt Shell's capacity onto existing networks at, as one report put it, a discount to replacement cost. Apollo's presence signals a financial-buyer angle — take distressed assets private, wait out the cycle. Kuwait Petroleum's interest points to a state oil major seeking a physical foothold in US petrochemicals.

None of this is decided. No binding bids exist, no price is fixed, and Shell can still walk away. The next marker will be whether the non-binding interest hardens into an actual sale process — likely to surface, if it does, around Shell's third-quarter results in late October.
