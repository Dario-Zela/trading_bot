Reform draws level with Labour as Burnham faces backlash over donations U-turn

Andy Burnham, photographed at 10 Downing Street shortly after becoming prime minister in July 2026. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Burnham's approval rating beats his party's, but Labour is tied with Reform UK in the polls and a quiet reversal on a £500k donations cap has fuelled doubts about his reform credentials.

Andy Burnham has spent five weeks as prime minister discovering that popularity is not the same thing as power. Voters like him more than they like his party, more than they like Nigel Farage, and considerably more than they liked his predecessor — yet Labour cannot pull clear of Reform UK in the polls, and the government has just handed its critics a fresh reason to doubt it.

In one sentence: Burnham's personal ratings are strong and Labour is tied with Reform UK in the polls, but a quiet reversal on capping political donations has undercut the reform credentials that were supposed to set his government apart.

A tie that flatters no one

Keir Starmer resigned as Labour leader on 22 June, worn down by two years of falling approval and a local-elections drubbing in May that saw Labour bleed seats to Reform. Burnham, the mayor of Greater Manchester and a long-time internal critic of Starmer's caution, took over on 17 July after winning the leadership contest — becoming the UK's seventh prime minister in a decade.

The early data looked like vindication. An Ipsos poll in late June, taken as Starmer's departure became inevitable, put Burnham as the public's preferred prime minister on 30%, well clear of Farage on 16% and Kemi Badenoch on 13%. By late July, Labour had climbed to 28% in Ipsos's headline voting-intention figure against Reform's 25% — the party's first lead in over a year.

It did not last. A Survation poll conducted on 18 August put Labour and Reform level at 26% apiece, and other trackers have shown the same convergence. Burnham's own net approval remains solidly positive, at +14 in the most recent reading — a rating most Western leaders would take gladly in 2026 — but it has not translated into a party lead. Survation's analysts were blunt about it: "Labour are yet to maintain a definite lead, and confidence in those key issues appears to already be slipping" on immigration and the economy, the areas that did the most damage to Starmer.

The pattern is the story. Seven in ten Britons remain dissatisfied with how the country is being run, and roughly four in ten still have no settled view of Burnham's performance — a leader still being sized up, attached to a party brand that has already been judged.

The cap that wasn't

Into that fragile equilibrium came a story that undercuts precisely the promise Burnham was elected to embody: that his government would govern differently.

In May, while still mayor of Manchester and running to succeed Starmer, Burnham told a campaigner, Shaun Bowler, in an email that he supported capping individual political donations at £500,000, arguing it "would guard against the perception of any one party being unduly influenced or swayed by one person or organisation." It fit a broader pitch: after a decade in which billionaire donors — Elon Musk's flirtation with bankrolling Reform UK chief among them — have unsettled British party financing, a cap looked like an easy, popular commitment.

Once in office, that commitment quietly evaporated. Government sources confirmed this week that Burnham's administration will not back amendments to the elections bill, due back in the Commons for its remaining stages, that would have written a donations cap into law. No public statement announced the change; it surfaced only after campaigners and journalists noticed the government's position had shifted.

The pressure came from inside the tent. The GMB, one of Labour's largest trade union donors, lobbied hard against the amendment, warning it could "undermine their ability to donate to the party" and cautioning of "significant unintended consequences" — a reminder that union money, not just billionaire money, flows into Labour's accounts, and that a cap cuts both ways.

The reaction has been sharp. Labour MP Stella Creasy said "millionaires are increasingly treating British politics as their plaything — splashing the cash to get the outcome they want," while Bowler, the original recipient of Burnham's pledge, said plainly: "words mean nothing if they disappear the moment power is won." Darren Hughes of the Electoral Reform Society added that "huge donations have a corrosive effect on trust in politics" — precisely the sentiment Burnham had tried to harness on his way into Downing Street.

What the gap reveals

Taken together, the polling and the donations reversal point at the same underlying weakness. Burnham was installed to convince voters that Labour could still change — that a leader with a reputation for straight talk and northern grit could out-flank Reform's insurgency by looking less like the political class Farage runs against. His personal numbers suggest that pitch still has an audience. But the moment his government faced a real choice between a popular reform and an institutional interest — in this case, union donors rather than corporate ones — it chose the institutional interest, the same instinct voters punished Starmer for.

That matters for how far a Burnham premiership can travel. Leadership approval is usually the leading indicator that a governing party's fortunes are about to turn; strategists in both main parties have spent years assuming a more likeable leader would eventually drag the numbers with them. Five weeks in, Burnham's ratings have held while Labour's have flattened, which suggests the constraint is not the man but the brand — a decade of unmet promises, a fractured electorate now genuinely split three or four ways, and a party financing system that ties Labour's hands nearly as tightly as it does the Conservatives'.

For markets, a dead-even two-party race with a broadly popular prime minister at its centre is closer to a non-event than a shock: it argues against the kind of abrupt repricing of gilts or sterling that would follow a decisive Reform breakthrough or a Labour collapse. But the donations episode is the more durable signal. It suggests that when Burnham's government meets its first real test of whether reform rhetoric survives contact with donor pressure, rhetoric lost — a result worth watching for the next test, expected when the elections bill returns to the Commons in the coming days and MPs get a formal vote on the amendment Burnham has now declined to support.
