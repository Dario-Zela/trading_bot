Oil's slide drags FTSE lower and pushes BoE rate-hike bets into 2027

London Stock Exchange headquarters, Paternoster Square — the FTSE 100 fell for a second straight session as oil-linked stocks dragged on the index. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The FTSE 100 fell 0.8% Thursday as oil's fourth straight losing day hit Shell and BP, and the same crude slide pushed BoE rate-hike bets from late 2026 into 2027.

London's blue-chip index fell for a second straight session on Thursday, down 0.8% to 10,792.54 — its steepest one-day drop in six weeks — as a fourth consecutive day of falling oil prices dragged on Shell and BP. The move looked, on the surface, like a routine bad day for stocks. Underneath it was something more mechanical: a reminder of how much of the FTSE 100's fate rides on two companies and one commodity.

Shell and BP each dropped roughly 1.5% on the day. Banks were also among the biggest drags on the index, but it was energy that set the tone. Together the two oil majors account for something like an eighth to a seventh of the FTSE 100's total market capitalisation — a concentration that has no real equivalent among the index's international peers, and one that turns a crude-oil rout into an index-wide event almost by arithmetic alone.

A four-day oil slide with a diplomatic root

The proximate cause was Brent crude, which fell for a fourth straight session to around $86.77 a barrel, down roughly 1.2% on the day. US benchmark West Texas Intermediate fell further, extending a five-day losing streak to about $81.10.

The slide has a fairly specific origin. Oil had spent much of August climbing on fears that a US blockade of Iranian shipping, and strikes against Iranian targets, would choke off traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of global oil supply passes. That premium has been coming out of the price all week as Iran and Oman have worked through the details of what Tehran has described as a "joint temporary navigational corridor" through the strait, alongside an agreement to clear mines from the waterway. Markets have read the talks as a credible de-escalation, and crude has given back its geopolitical premium accordingly. For Shell and BP, whose share prices track the crude benchmark more closely than almost any other UK large-caps, the reversal has been just as mechanical on the way down as the spike was on the way up.

Gilts follow oil lower, and so do rate-hike bets

The more consequential move on Thursday was in the bond market. UK 10-year gilt yields slipped toward 5%, hovering near a two-week low of 4.943%, as investors pushed back their expectations for the Bank of England's next rate rise from late 2026 into 2027.

That repricing is the flip side of the same oil story. Through much of August, rising crude had been read by markets as an inflation risk serious enough to justify tightening — traders had at one point fully priced a BoE hike as early as November, with a second move pencilled in for March 2027. UK headline inflation running at 2.9% in July, driven largely by energy bills, gave that view some cover, even with the labour market still subdued. As Brent has come off the boil, so has the urgency. Pricing now shows around 24 basis points of tightening by December and 36 basis points by the Bank's February 2027 meeting, with the September 2027 decision showing barely 4 basis points priced in — implying only about a 15% probability of a move by then.

It is a tidy illustration of how energy prices have become the swing factor in UK rate expectations even as underlying, services-driven inflation stays sticky. Oil doesn't just move Shell and BP's share prices; through the headline inflation channel, it moves the entire curve investors use to price the cost of money in Britain.

The concentration problem

What Thursday's session underlines is less about any single data point than about structure. An index where two energy companies carry outsized weight, and where energy and financials together dominate the constituent list, will always be more exposed to commodity swings than a more diversified benchmark. When oil rallies, the FTSE 100 gets a lift that has little to do with the health of the broader UK economy; when it falls, the index takes a hit that similarly says more about Middle East diplomacy than about domestic consumer demand or corporate earnings. Technology and services stocks — Relx, London Stock Exchange Group and Experian all rose on Thursday, alongside a broader 1% gain for tech shares on the back of Nvidia's blowout results — could not offset the energy and bank weakness, a pattern likely to recur as long as the index's sector mix stays as it is.

The next hard test of these rate expectations comes on 17 September, when the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee delivers its next decision — currently expected to hold the base rate at 3.75%. Between now and then, the path of Brent crude, and whether the Iran-Oman talks over the Strait of Hormuz actually produce a durable shipping arrangement, will do as much to shape that meeting's tone as any UK data release.
