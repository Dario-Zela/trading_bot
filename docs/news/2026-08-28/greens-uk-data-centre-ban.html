Greens call for temporary ban on new UK data centres

A UK data centre in Copley, West Yorkshire — the type of facility the Green Party wants subject to tighter environmental scrutiny before approval. — Humphrey Bolton / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
The Green Party wants new UK data centre approvals frozen over energy and water use, but with only four MPs, it's Scotland's SNP-backed version that could actually happen.

The Green Party wants ministers to stop approving new data centres in England until developers can prove the buildings meet tough environmental standards and local communities have had a say. It is not, in practice, a proposal Parliament is about to adopt — the Greens hold four seats in the Commons — but it lands at a moment when the political cost of the AI boom's appetite for power and water is becoming harder for any party to wave away.

"We need to slam the brakes on the rampant building of new data centres. Data should serve people, not the other way around."

That's Zack Polanski, the Green Party's leader, setting out the case in comments reported by the BBC and LBC this week. His central complaint is about sequencing: since September 2024, data centres have carried Critical National Infrastructure status in England, putting them alongside water, energy and emergency services for planning purposes and making it considerably easier for developers to get large facilities approved, including on green belt land. The Greens want that designation reversed, arguing it lets a warehouse full of servers jump the queue ahead of the communities living next to it.

Water, watts and a drought

The timing is not incidental. Large parts of the UK have spent 2026 under drought conditions, and Polanski's argument leans hard on that: cooling systems for large data centres draw on water supplies, and he wants a firm rule that households, public services and food production always come first when supplies are tight. There is no single official figure for how much water UK data centres use, which is itself part of the Greens' complaint — they're calling for mandatory annual reporting of water and electricity consumption as a condition of any new approval.

The energy numbers are easier to pin down, and they explain why this argument keeps resurfacing. UK data centres already draw around 6% of national electricity supply, up from an estimated 2% in 2024, and the National Energy System Operator says it has received requests for roughly 50 gigawatts of new grid connections from data centre developers — equivalent to Britain's entire peak demand. Forecasts from NESO's Future Energy Scenarios put data centre demand climbing from 7.6TWh in 2024 to as much as 41TWh by 2035.

A ban with teeth, just not in England

The England-and-Wales proposal has no legislative runway. North of the border, though, a similar idea does: the Scottish Greens have pushed for a moratorium on new data centres above 50MW, and in July the SNP backed the call in the Scottish Parliament, where the two parties together command a majority. That makes Holyrood, not Westminster, the place worth watching if a UK moratorium is ever going to move from press release to statute book.

Energy secretary Ed Miliband has so far described the climate impact of data centres as uncertain rather than committing to new restrictions, and Labour has shown no appetite to touch the critical-infrastructure designation it inherited and expanded. The next marker will be whether Scotland's devolved planning guidance, expected later this year, actually caps new capacity — a real test of whether this argument can survive contact with government, rather than opposition, benches.
