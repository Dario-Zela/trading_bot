Judge blocks USPS mail-in ballot rule ahead of midterms

A ballot return box used for mail-in voting — the kind of vote-by-mail infrastructure states say the USPS rule would have disrupted. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Boston judge again blocked a Trump-ordered USPS rule requiring states to hand over mail-voter lists, saying compliance was impossible before the midterms.

A federal judge in Boston has for a second time halted a Trump administration plan to give the US Postal Service new power over how mail-in ballots are handled, ruling that states could not possibly comply with the requirements before November's midterm elections.

US District Judge Indira Talwani issued a 14-day temporary restraining order on Thursday against USPS's newly finalized rule, which would have required state election officials to submit lists of every voter eligible to receive a mail ballot and secure federal pre-approval of ballot envelope designs — down to barcodes unique to each voter, meant to let the Postal Service track and, if necessary, decline to deliver ballots. Any voter missing from the list, or any envelope the agency hadn't signed off on, risked having a ballot go undelivered regardless of whether that person was legally registered under state law.

The rule traces back to an executive order President Trump signed on March 31, directing federal agencies to verify voter eligibility and tighten mail-voting procedures nationwide. States sued almost immediately, and Talwani issued a broad injunction freezing the plan. That injunction survived until August 24, when the Supreme Court, in a 6–3 ruling split along ideological lines, lifted part of it — clearing the administration to proceed with a citizenship-verification list shared by the Department of Homeland Security and to let USPS begin rulemaking on ballot-envelope design. The court left untouched, for now, the more sweeping question of whether USPS can withhold ballots outright from voters not on a federal list.

States say the clock has run out

Within days of the Supreme Court's order, USPS published a Final Rule implementing the eligibility-list and pre-approval scheme, and a coalition of 25 states and the District of Columbia — led by Massachusetts, California, Nevada and Washington — sued again, arguing the rule violates the Constitution's assignment of election administration to the states, ignores USPS's own rulemaking procedures and runs afoul of federal privacy and voting-rights law. State officials told the court their ballot vendors could not redesign and print new envelopes in time for a November 3 election barely nine weeks away.

Talwani agreed. She wrote that the Postal Service had offered no evidence of the mail-voting fraud it cited to justify the rule, and that its interest in "correcting an unsubstantiated problem through likely unconstitutional means" was "dwarfed by the overwhelming risk of pervasive disenfranchisement." The order is temporary — a longer-term injunction depends on a hearing set for September 3, and the administration is expected to appeal quickly, continuing a pattern in which control over the rule has swung between courts twice in a single week.

The underlying dispute — how far a federal agency can go in reshaping state election procedure under executive instruction, without new legislation — is unresolved and likely headed back to the Supreme Court before ballots go out.
