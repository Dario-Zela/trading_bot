US bank regulators to narrow focus onto core financial risks

Michelle Bowman, the Federal Reserve's Vice Chair for Supervision, has led the push to refocus bank examiners on "material financial risks." — Britt Leckman / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
OCC and FDIC finalized a rule defining "unsafe or unsound" bank practices around material financial risk, capping a year-long retreat from ESG, AML and fair-lending oversight.

US bank regulators have spent the past year rewriting the rulebook for what examiners are allowed to flag — and on Thursday they finished the centrepiece. The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency and the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation jointly finalised a rule that, for the first time in the history of American banking regulation, puts a formal definition on the phrase "unsafe or unsound practice." Examiners can only cite it, going forward, when conduct departs from prudent operating standards and has caused — or could reasonably cause — material financial harm to a bank or a real risk to the Deposit Insurance Fund. Policies, procedures and paperwork gaps, on their own, no longer qualify.

That single rule change, first proposed in October 2025, caps a broader campaign by the three main federal banking agencies to narrow supervision down to what FDIC Chairman Travis Hill calls "core, material financial risks." Comptroller Jonathan Gould has framed the goal as ending "check-the-box" exams that distract from bigger threats. Along the way, regulators have eliminated "reputation risk" as a supervisory category — Hill called it "ripe for abuse" and said it "adds no value" — capped examiners' use of confidential "matters requiring attention" notices to material issues only, and are reworking the CAMELS rating system to lean less on subjective judgments about bank management and more on hard financial metrics.

The areas losing attention read like a checklist of Biden-era supervisory priorities. The OCC deferred fair-lending exams and dropped disparate-impact liability, meaning enforcement now requires proof of intentional discrimination rather than statistical patterns. The Fed has rescinded climate-related supervisory guidance that Vice Chair for Supervision Michelle Bowman said "diverted supervisory resources away from risks that are material to safety and soundness." Bowman has also completed a reorganisation of the Fed's own oversight staff, effective July, collapsing several units into four groups after an earlier plan floated cutting the division from roughly 500 employees to 350. Anti-money-laundering supervision is the partial exception: rather than being cut, the Fed proposed in July that banks redirect AML resources toward higher-risk customers rather than spreading effort evenly.

The trade-off regulators are making

Bowman insists the changes sharpen rather than shrink oversight: "Our supervisory approach is not about narrowing our focus — it is about sharpening it. By anchoring our work in material financial risks, we strengthen the banking system's foundation." Bank trade groups, which have long argued examiners used soft categories like reputation and management quality to second-guess business decisions, are broadly supportive.

Not everyone is convinced. Better Markets, a financial-reform advocacy group, has argued that a related proposal to revise CAMELS would let management and governance failures go unpunished until they have already produced financial damage — turning supervision "from its proper role as an early-warning system into an ineffective rearview mirror." The group notes that both the 2008 crisis and the 2023 regional-bank failures were preceded by governance breakdowns that showed up well before the balance sheet did.

The Federal Reserve has not yet issued its own version of the unsafe-or-unsound rule, leaving one of the three agencies still to align with the OCC and FDIC. That filing, along with the Fed's pending AML proposal, is the next marker for how far the retreat from process-based supervision will run.
