Akeso's biliary tract cancer win lifts Summit Therapeutics

Bob Duggan, Summit Therapeutics' executive chairman and CEO, whose company licenses ivonescimab from China's Akeso. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Akeso's Phase III win in biliary tract cancer lifted partner Summit Therapeutics 15% premarket, but the rare indication is a side note next to the lung cancer data that will decide the drug's real value.

Summit Therapeutics shares jumped as much as 15% in premarket trading Wednesday after its Chinese partner Akeso reported that ivonescimab, the pair's bispecific antibody, had won a Phase III trial in biliary tract cancer — a rare cancer of the bile ducts and gallbladder with few effective treatments. Shares settled to close up around 7.75% on the day, a reminder that first reactions to trial headlines tend to run hotter than the numbers that follow.

The trial, called HARMONi-GI1, enrolled 682 patients across China and pitted ivonescimab plus chemotherapy against AstraZeneca's Imfinzi (durvalumab) plus chemotherapy as a first-line treatment. Akeso said the combination hit its primary endpoint — a statistically significant improvement in overall survival — and cleared its secondary endpoints on progression-free survival and response rate too. If the result holds up under full data, it would be the first Phase III trial in biliary tract cancer to show a survival edge over a checkpoint inhibitor plus chemotherapy, the current standard of care.

"Ivonescimab's mechanism of action is different from a simple combination of two drugs," Akeso chief executive Michelle Xia said, framing the result as evidence the drug works differently than the sum of its two mechanisms — blocking both PD-1 and VEGF in a single molecule.

What the data actually shows — and doesn't

The caveat is that Akeso disclosed no hazard ratio, no median survival figures and no detailed data — the full results are being held for an academic conference and peer-reviewed publication. Analysts split accordingly. H.C. Wainwright kept its Neutral rating, noting that without the overall-survival hazard ratio, "we cannot tell whether ivonescimab adds modest survival benefit or materially improves" on durvalumab. Guggenheim, more constructive, reiterated a Buy and $38 price target, arguing the result adds to a growing pattern of consistent efficacy for the drug across tumour types.

That pattern matters because ivonescimab's most-watched trials remain in non-small-cell lung cancer, where it is already approved in China and where Summit is running the pivotal studies that will determine its US and European prospects. Biliary tract cancer, by contrast, is a niche indication — roughly 8,000 new US cases a year, according to American Cancer Society estimates, versus over 230,000 for lung cancer. The global biliary tract cancer drug market is estimated at close to $1 billion, a fraction of what a lung cancer approval would be worth.

The Summit trade

Summit licensed ivonescimab from Akeso in a 2022 deal worth up to $5 billion, with a $500 million upfront payment, covering the US, Canada, Europe, Japan, Latin America, the Middle East and Africa. The arrangement made Summit — helmed by executive chairman Bob Duggan, the biotech investor who took control of the company in 2020 — almost entirely dependent on data generated by its Chinese partner, since ivonescimab is not yet approved outside China.

That structure has cut both ways. Every positive Akeso readout — this is the second Phase III win after lung cancer — de-risks Summit's pipeline without Summit spending its own trial budget. But it also means Summit's valuation moves on data it doesn't fully control and, in this case, hasn't yet fully seen. The stock remains down roughly 38% over the past year and well off its 52-week high of $30, even after Wednesday's pop — a gap that reflects lingering investor doubt about whether ivonescimab's lung cancer data, the asset's real prize, will translate as cleanly in Western trials as it has in China.

Xia acknowledged as much when pressed by Fierce Pharma on investor skepticism toward the drug's headline efficacy numbers: "Scientific questions ultimately demand scientific proof. You can't just make simple linear extrapolations to determine scientific outcomes." She also conceded a more prosaic point about the partnership — "Summit does need more resources and more investments" — an acknowledgment that Akeso is still leaning on its US partner's balance sheet to carry the drug through Western regulatory pathways.

What's next

The near-term catalyst is the release of full HARMONi-GI1 data — the hazard ratio and survival curves that will tell analysts whether this is a genuine best-in-class result or a narrower win against a beatable comparator. The bigger one remains lung cancer: Summit's own Western trials of ivonescimab in non-small-cell lung cancer will determine whether the Akeso partnership pays off at the scale the 2022 deal implied, or whether biliary tract cancer ends up as a solid but secondary addition to a drug whose real test is still ahead.
