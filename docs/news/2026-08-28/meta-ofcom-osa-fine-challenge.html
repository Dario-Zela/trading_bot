Meta challenges Ofcom's Online Safety Act fine calculations in court

The Royal Courts of Justice in London, where Meta's judicial review against Ofcom will be heard in October. — Rafa Esteve / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Meta is suing Ofcom in the High Court over its use of global rather than UK revenue to calculate Online Safety Act fines, with a hearing set for 13–14 October — after the first invoices are due.

Meta has asked the High Court to strike down the formula Ofcom uses to calculate fines under the Online Safety Act, arguing the regulator is measuring penalties against the wrong number entirely — and doing so just weeks before the first invoices are due to land.

The judicial review, filed in London, doesn't challenge Ofcom's power to fine tech platforms, or even the underlying safety rules Meta is obliged to follow. It challenges the arithmetic. Under the Act, Ofcom can fine a company up to 10% of its "qualifying worldwide revenue" — not what it earns in the UK, but its entire global turnover. On Meta's 2025 numbers, that ceiling works out to roughly $16bn. Meta says that's the wrong base entirely, and wants the sum redone using UK revenue only.

A dispute over which number counts

Ofcom's regulatory levy — the running-cost charge platforms pay regardless of any breach — is set at 0.02–0.03% of qualifying worldwide revenue, with a £10m UK revenue floor below which a provider owes nothing. Firms below £250m in global revenue are exempt from liability altogether. It's the same global-revenue logic that underpins the much larger penalty regime for actual violations, and it's modelled closely on how the EU calculates fines under the Digital Services Act and GDPR.

Meta's objection is that the UK isn't the EU, and Ofcom isn't Brussels. "We are committed to cooperating constructively with Ofcom as it enforces the Online Safety Act," a Meta spokesperson said. "However, we and others in the tech industry believe its decisions on the methodology to calculate fees and potential fines are disproportionate. We believe fees and penalties should be based on the services being regulated in the countries they're being regulated in." The company is careful to note that a UK-revenue basis would still let Ofcom hand down "the largest fines in UK corporate history" — this isn't an argument that Ofcom should go easy, just that it should stay in its lane.

Meta is also contesting a narrower but consequential point: how fines are totted up when two services owned by the same company — Facebook and Instagram, in its case — are found jointly liable for the same breach. Bundling them, Meta argues, effectively double-counts the same underlying revenue base against a single infraction.

Ofcom holds its ground

Ofcom isn't budging. A spokesperson said the regulator's fee and penalty structure reflects "a plain reading of the law" and that it would "robustly defend our reasoning and decisions." That's a reasonable reading of the statute as written — Parliament did specify "qualifying worldwide revenue" in the Online Safety Act, not a UK-only carve-out, and Ofcom's position is essentially that it's applying the law Parliament actually passed rather than the one the industry might have preferred.

The timing sharpens the stakes. Ofcom's first invoices under the fee regime are due in September 2026 — matter of weeks away — while the substantive hearing in Meta's case isn't listed until 13–14 October. That leaves a gap in which Meta may be asked to pay under a formula it is actively suing to overturn, unless the court intervenes on an interim basis before then.

Meta isn't litigating alone in spirit, even if it's the only named claimant so far. The Computer & Communications Industry Association, a US trade body whose members include Meta, is expected to intervene, and reporting suggests TikTok, Snap, Pinterest, X and Epic Games share the same objection to global-revenue-based fines without having filed anything themselves. That's not surprising: any of them could face a UK penalty calculated the same way, and none benefits from being the company that draws Ofcom's enforcement attention first.

What a global metric is for

The case is, underneath the procedural detail, a fight over what a national regulator's fines are supposed to accomplish. A UK-revenue cap keeps penalties proportionate to a company's actual UK footprint — a familiar, conservative approach. A global-revenue basis does something different: it ensures the fine is large enough to matter to a company for which UK revenue alone might be a rounding error. Ofcom's approach follows the EU's playbook precisely because Brussels found that smaller, jurisdiction-bound fines didn't move Big Tech's behaviour; global numbers do.

If Meta wins, Ofcom's toughest enforcement lever gets meaningfully blunter just as the regulator is trying to establish that the Online Safety Act has real teeth — and other platforms facing the same exposure would have a ready template to follow. If Meta loses, the ruling would confirm that a UK regulator with a domestic mandate can lawfully size its penalties to a company's entire global business, a precedent regulators well beyond Ofcom would take note of.

The next fixed point is the 13–14 October hearing. Before that, though, is the September deadline — and whether Ofcom sends invoices calculated its way regardless of the pending litigation may say as much about the state of this fight as anything argued in court.
