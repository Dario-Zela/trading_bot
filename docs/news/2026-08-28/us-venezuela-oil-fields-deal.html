US nears deal for stake in Venezuela's vast oil fields

PDVSA's headquarters in Maracaibo — the state oil company at the center of the ownership talks. — Wilfredor / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Axios reports the US is close to a deal for direct ownership in Venezuelan oil fields holding 90 billion barrels, months after Maduro's capture.

Eight months after American special forces seized Nicolás Maduro in Caracas, the United States is closing in on something no previous administration managed in a quarter-century of sanctions, threats and failed diplomacy: a direct equity stake in Venezuela's oil.

Axios reported on 27 August that the Trump administration is nearing an agreement that would give the US ownership in a cluster of more than a dozen Venezuelan oil fields holding roughly 90 billion barrels of proven reserves — a slice of the country's total 303-billion-barrel endowment, the largest in the world, but on its own large enough to more than double the reserve base the US currently claims. Officials briefed on the talks told the outlet a deal is close, though nothing has been signed.

From sanctions to shareholder

The shift in posture only makes sense against what happened in January. On 3 January, US forces captured Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, in a military operation that ended his 13-year hold on power; Trump said Washington would help run the country "until we can do a safe, proper and judicious transition." Two days later, Vice President Delcy Rodríguez — Maduro's former deputy and a fixture of the old government's oil and intelligence apparatus — was sworn in as interim president and immediately signalled she would work with Washington, freeing several detained American citizens within days.

That opening let the administration do quickly what sanctions had blocked for years. In February, the Treasury issued general licenses reopening contract talks with PDVSA, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright flew to Caracas — the highest-level US energy visit to Venezuela in nearly three decades — to tour fields alongside Rodríguez and promise a "dramatic increase" in output. Chevron, which had kept a foothold through the sanctions years via a joint venture in the Orinoco Belt's Carabobo block, has since forecast a 30–40% jump in its Venezuelan production this year.

What's actually on the table

The fields under discussion now go further than reopening old joint ventures. Axios reports Secretary of State Marco Rubio has led talks with Rodríguez's government toward US ownership — not just operating licenses — in fields previously controlled by Venezuelan insiders (some since indicted in the US) and by Chinese state-backed firms. One structure discussed is a lease running as long as 100 years, with private international firms, including American companies, handling day-to-day development and returning a larger share of revenue to Caracas than under the PDVSA-dominated system Maduro left behind.

Rubio has been explicit about the strategic logic: he has said the US "would not allow" China, Iran or Russia a significant foothold in Venezuelan oil going forward, and displacing Chinese interests from these specific fields appears to be as much the point as the barrels themselves. The White House has said it expects "dramatic interest" from Western oil companies once the fields formally change hands, though the majors have so far held back — ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips both still carry the scars of Venezuela's 2007 nationalizations and are wary of committing capital before a deal is final. Smaller, faster-moving operators, including SLB, Hunt Oil and Pacific Coast Energy, have been more willing to sign near-term service agreements with PDVSA in the meantime.

Why Washington wants it now

The timing is not incidental. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve sits near a 40-year low, drawn down over successive administrations and never fully rebuilt. Wars in Ukraine and Iran have kept Middle Eastern supply routes and Russian barrels under a cloud, pushing the White House to look for reserves it can influence outside the Gulf and outside OPEC+ discipline. Venezuela's heavy, sulphurous crude is costlier to refine than benchmark grades, but it sits a few days' tanker journey from the US Gulf Coast rather than halfway around the world.

The scale of what it would take to make those barrels useful is its own story, though. Independent estimates put the investment needed to lift Venezuelan output toward 3 million barrels a day — from around 1.25 million now — at roughly $180 billion over the next decade, with a minimum of $50 billion in capital spending over 15 years just to hold current production flat. That is a bet on political stability in a country that spent the 2010s in economic collapse, and on an interim government whose long-term legitimacy is still being tested.

What to watch

No agreement has been signed, and Axios's own sourcing describes the deal as close rather than done. The next concrete marker is whether Rubio's State Department and Rodríguez's government produce a signed framework — expected, if it comes, in the weeks ahead — spelling out the ownership structure, the lease term and which companies get first rights to develop the fields. Oil markets, already jumpy over Middle East supply and a recent slide in crude prices, will be watching for confirmation of both the deal's size and whether Exxon or Conoco decide the political risk has finally eased enough to come back in.
