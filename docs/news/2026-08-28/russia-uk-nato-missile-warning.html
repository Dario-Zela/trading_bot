Russia warns UK is 'playing with fire' as CIA chief delivers Nato warning in Moscow

CIA Director John Ratcliffe, who made a previously unannounced visit to Moscow to warn Russian officials against attacking NATO member states. — Official portrait / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Russia threatened UK and French assets over Storm Shadow missile tech shared with Ukraine, even as the CIA director secretly warned Moscow off attacking NATO.

Two messages travelled between Washington and Moscow this week, on different channels, in different registers, but pointing at the same underlying fact: the war in Ukraine has entered a phase where every side is escalating support and rhetoric simultaneously, while quietly keeping a line open in case things go wrong.

On one channel, the Kremlin's spokeswoman went public with a threat. On the other, the director of the CIA flew to Moscow — unannounced, unpublicised until reporters spotted the plane — to deliver a warning of his own, in person, to Russian intelligence officials. Neither move cancels the other out. Together they describe the shape of the relationship: adversarial, arms-length, and still talking.

'Playing with fire'

The public threat came from Maria Zakharova, spokeswoman for Russia's foreign ministry, after Britain confirmed it would let the manufacturer MBDA share classified technical data with Ukraine so it can build its own Storm Shadow cruise missiles — known in French service, and in Ukrainian hands as of this transfer, as SCALP. Prime Minister Andy Burnham authorised the release during his first visit to Kyiv since taking office, and Paris, which had already licensed its share of the intellectual property, wants a Ukrainian production line running by the end of the year.

Zakharova called the decision "playing with fire" and said any British or French military installations or equipment — in Ukraine "or beyond its borders" — could now be considered legitimate targets if Ukraine strikes Russian territory with British-derived weapons. The threat is broader than the missile itself. Storm Shadow, a deep-strike weapon designed for hardened targets like bunkers and command posts, has flown against Russian territory before under UK and French oversight; what changes here is that Ukraine would eventually make the missile itself, reducing its dependence on foreign stockpiles and, in Moscow's reading, formalising Western co-ownership of the war's most sensitive weapons programme. The new production line is not expected to deliver missiles before 2027, which gives the threat a rhetorical charge well ahead of any physical one. Neither London nor Paris has publicly answered Zakharova's warning.

The Ratcliffe channel

The second track surfaced almost by accident. Reporters in Moscow noticed a US Air Force jet at a city airport and a diplomatic motorcade moving through town on Tuesday; it turned out to be CIA Director John Ratcliffe, on his first known visit to Russia since taking the job, holding talks with Russian intelligence counterparts rather than President Vladimir Putin. Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed the meetings took place "through the intelligence services," called such contacts "a positive phenomenon," but added that US-Russia relations remain in "a profound crisis" and that it was "too early to say" what would come of it.

According to US officials briefed on the trip, Ratcliffe's central message was a warning against any Russian move on NATO territory — a message shaped by a US intelligence assessment that Putin may authorise a small-scale test of alliance resolve within the next few years: a drone incursion, a cyberattack, a limited land probe, most plausibly aimed at Estonia, Latvia or Lithuania. That assessment did not come from nowhere. Over the past several months NATO states have logged a Russian drone crashing into an apartment building in Romania, a missile crossing briefly into Polish airspace, and a pattern of sabotage and cyberattacks that Warsaw's own president has described as a deliberate test of how fast the alliance reacts. Ratcliffe also raised Iran, warning of further US sanctions if Russia does not help reopen the Strait of Hormuz to shipping. Trump, asked about the trip, called it "semi-routine" and said "something may come out of it," a reference to a possible opening on ending the war rather than any breakthrough already achieved.

Managing, not resolving

The two threads read, at first, like contradictory signals — Russia threatening British assets even as its own intelligence chiefs sit down with America's — but they are more consistent than they look. Moscow's public threats are aimed at deterring the West from arming Ukraine further; Washington's private warning is aimed at deterring Moscow from testing NATO directly. Both governments are simultaneously raising the stakes in Ukraine and working to ensure that neither side stumbles into a NATO-Russia confrontation neither wants. That the CIA director can still fly into Moscow for a face-to-face conversation with Russian intelligence officials, even as the two countries maintain no meaningful formal diplomacy, is itself the more important data point for markets and governments watching for the tail risk in this war: a direct NATO-Russia clash. As long as that channel exists, the base case remains a grinding, high-casualty war contained within Ukraine's borders rather than a wider one.

What happens next will be visible well before any missile is built. Watch for whether Ukraine and its European backers can stand up the SCALP production line on the stated end-of-year timeline, for any further Russian drone or missile incidents along the Baltic or Polish frontier that would test Ratcliffe's warning in real time, and for whether Trump's "something may come out of it" translates into any renewed push toward talks on ending the war itself.
