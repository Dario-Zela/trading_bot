Trump's plan for a new federal AI regulator stalls

The White House, where a draft executive order creating a new AI self-regulatory body has stalled awaiting sign-off. — Martin Falbisoner / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
A White House draft order to create an industry-run AI regulator modeled on FINRA has stalled, leaving federal AI rules undecided despite broad industry backing.

The Trump administration has spent much of 2026 flip-flopping on how — or whether — to put formal guardrails on artificial intelligence. The latest attempt has quietly stalled.

According to The Information, White House staff have been circulating a draft executive order in recent weeks that would create a new self-regulatory body for companies building the most advanced, "frontier" AI models. The order needs sign-off from a chain of senior officials, and ultimately the president himself, before it can be finalized. That buy-in hasn't materialized, and there's no public timeline for when — or if — it will.

A regulator modeled on Wall Street's own

The proposal isn't a government agency in the traditional sense. It borrows the structure of FINRA, the industry-funded body that polices stockbrokers under the SEC's supervision. Under the draft concept, AI labs would fund and staff a board that includes independent technical experts, set testing protocols and capability benchmarks for frontier models, and coordinate safety reviews with federal agencies — starting on a voluntary basis, with room to become mandatory later for any model sold in the US.

The idea gained real momentum after Google DeepMind chief executive Demis Hassabis published a framework on July 14 calling for a "Frontier AI Standards Body." It drew an unusually broad coalition of backers: Microsoft's Satya Nadella and Mustafa Suleyman, Block's Jack Dorsey, Box's Aaron Levie, OpenAI's Sam Altman, and Elon Musk all signed on. Even David Sacks — Trump's former AI and crypto czar, and the White House's most consistent voice against formal AI rules — called it "better than having the government trying to regulate frontier AI directly." Bloomberg has reported that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent helped develop the concept inside the administration, with the draft under review by Chief of Staff Susie Wiles.

Why it's stuck

That unusual industry consensus hasn't been enough to move it through the White House. The administration has a recent history of pulling back from AI rules at the last minute: in May, Trump abruptly scrapped the signing of a separate executive order on frontier-model review and AI cybersecurity after Musk, Meta's Mark Zuckerberg and Sacks all lobbied him overnight, arguing it would slow the US down against China. A pared-back version — voluntary, with no penalty for skipping it — was eventually signed on June 2, asking companies to submit advanced models for review 30 days before release.

The skepticism runs deep in the administration's own ranks. On July 3, Sriram Krishnan, then still a senior White House AI adviser, told the Financial Times flatly that there would be no "FDA for AI" under Trump, warning that licensing requirements would put "sand in the gears" of the industry. Critics on the other side argue the SRO concept, however it shakes out, has no teeth: AI safety researcher Yoshua Bengio has said a voluntary body needs "a clear and precise roadmap to transition from voluntary to mandatory" or it risks becoming self-policing in name only.

What it means for markets

For an industry that has priced in years of hypergrowth, the practical question is compliance cost and legal exposure — and both remain unanswered. A binding federal regime, even an industry-run one with teeth, would give AI companies a clearer liability shield and a single national standard to build against, rather than the patchwork of state rules the administration's December executive order already tried to preempt. No regime at all leaves that patchwork, and the litigation risk that comes with it, in place. Each stalled draft just extends the interval in which neither outcome is certain.

The next marker to watch is whether the administration revives the draft order at all, or lets the informal, voluntary review process set up in June harden into the de facto policy by default. Krishnan's exit from the White House removes one of the loudest anti-regulation voices from the room — whether that changes the political math inside the administration, rather than just its personnel, is the open question.
