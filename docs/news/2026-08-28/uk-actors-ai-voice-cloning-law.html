Actors including Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas call for UK law on AI voice cloning

Hugh Bonneville, one of around 80 performers backing the Save Our Voices Now campaign, pictured at the 2017 Berlinale. — Martin Kraft / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Around 80 UK actors, led by Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, are petitioning the government to make voice ownership a statutory right as AI cloning outpaces the law.

Hugh Bonneville's voice has narrated period dramas, corporate videos and, most famously, six years of life at Downton Abbey. It took a stranger three seconds of audio and an AI model to make a passable copy of it — one that could be typed into saying anything at all.

That's the demonstration behind Save Our Voices Now, a campaign launched this week by around 80 UK performers, including Bonneville, Matt Lucas, Nicola Coughlan, Luke Evans and Derry Girls' Siobhan McSweeney. They've signed an open letter to Prime Minister Andy Burnham and backed a Gov.uk petition asking for something that sounds almost quaint in the age of generative AI: that a person's voice be recognised, in law, as theirs.

In one sentence: Around 80 UK actors, led by Hugh Bonneville and Matt Lucas, are petitioning the government to make voice ownership a statutory right as AI cloning tools outpace existing law.

The gap in the law

The campaign's core complaint is narrow and specific: UK law was not written with voice-cloning software in mind, and it shows. Dr Mathilde Pavis, a media law academic who has advised on the issue, told the BBC that existing statute comes "close" to protecting a person's voice, face and identity but doesn't fully cover any of them, because the relevant rules predate synthetic media by decades. There is no clean equivalent, in England and Wales, of a US-style right of publicity that treats a voice as property someone can be sued for stealing.

Campaign founder Peter Caulfield frames the stakes bluntly: "In just three seconds, with the right AI system, your voice can be cloned, stolen and reinterpreted without your knowledge." Bonneville's own framing is plainer still: "My voice is unique, it's mine, it's part of my identity. So is yours. That's worth speaking up about."

The numbers behind the campaign aren't limited to actors, either — a survey cited by the group found 28% of UK adults report having been targeted by a voice-cloning scam, from fake kidnap calls to bank-fraud attempts using a cloned relative's voice. That's the argument the campaign is leaning on to broaden its appeal beyond the entertainment industry: this isn't just a dispute over dubbing rights, it's a consumer-protection gap.

Actors already feeling it

For performers specifically, the harm is more immediate. Equity, the performers' union, says it has filed claims on behalf of more than 20 members whose voices were allegedly used without consent — enough, according to the union representative handling voice-artist cases, to be "taking up so much of my time." Separately, Equity has been negotiating with the BBC, ITV, Disney and the industry body Pact over AI provisions in broadcast contracts; talks have stalled, and the union has warned it will go to "the courts if necessary" if members' rights keep being breached in the training of AI voice and likeness models.

McSweeney's complaint is less about any single incident and more about pace: "legislation, contracts, working practices and ethics, and artists' rights are not being considered or protected as quickly as AI technology is progressing," she said, calling it "an existential problem" that needs legislative attention urgently.

What other countries are doing

The UK campaign is explicitly pointing overseas for a template. Denmark is pushing through legal reforms that would give citizens ownership over their own face, body and voice, with the right to demand takedowns of unauthorised AI content and to seek compensation when it isn't removed. The US has moved further still, with several states enacting publicity-rights statutes specifically aimed at unauthorised AI voice and likeness use, following high-profile disputes such as Scarlett Johansson's 2024 clash with OpenAI over a chatbot voice she said sounded uncannily like her own.

Britain, by contrast, has no equivalent bill before Parliament yet — the campaign is asking for one, not responding to one. That puts this squarely in the pre-legislative stage: a lobbying push and a petition, not a government commitment.

What's next

Gov.uk petitions that clear 10,000 signatures get a government response; 100,000 triggers a debate in Parliament, though neither guarantees legislation follows. The more concrete pressure point is the stalled Equity negotiation with broadcasters, which the union has signalled could end up in court if it doesn't move. Whichever forum resolves first — the petition process, a court claim, or a government bill — will set an early precedent for how UK law treats a voice as something a person can own, rather than just something a company can generate.
