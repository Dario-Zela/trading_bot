Former Barclays boss denies allegations involving Epstein's 'Snow White'

Jes Staley, then chief executive of Barclays, in New Delhi in July 2016. — Prime Minister's Office, Government of India / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Jes Staley told a US House committee he did not have sex with a woman dressed as Snow White at Epstein's apartment, per a transcript released 26 August.

Jes Staley has spent the best part of five years insisting his friendship with Jeffrey Epstein, however ill-advised, never crossed into anything sinister. This week that defence acquired a rather more specific — and stranger — footnote: Snow White.

The former Barclays chief executive told US lawmakers he did not have sex with a woman who appeared at Epstein's Manhattan townhouse dressed as the Disney princess, according to a transcript of a closed-door congressional interview released by the House Oversight Committee on 26 August. Asked directly, Staley said: "No, I did not." Pressed further, he allowed for something less categorical: "I may have seen her in apartment [sic] and prompted that email. But I was not with a person dressed as Snow White outside of potentially crossing at his apartment."

The email in question dates to July 2010, when Staley — then head of JPMorgan's investment bank — wrote to Epstein: "That was fun. Say hi to Snow White." Epstein wrote back asking which character he'd like next; Staley replied, "Beauty and the Beast." Lawmakers also produced a separate exchange in which Epstein directed the woman to buy a Snow White costume, and in which she told him the costume-wearing had led to sex twice. Asked whether he and Epstein used Disney princesses as code names for arranging encounters, Staley said no.

A friendship that has already cost him a career

Staley spent 34 years at JPMorgan before taking the top job at Barclays in December 2015. He resigned in November 2021 after the Financial Conduct Authority opened a probe into how he had characterised his relationship with Epstein, whom he had called one of his "deepest" and "most cherished" friends in emails exchanged while running JPMorgan's private bank. The regulator later found Staley had misled it about the closeness and duration of that friendship — including contact in the days around his appointment as Barclays CEO — and in 2023 banned him from senior UK financial roles and fined him £1.8m. Britain's Upper Tribunal upheld both the ban and the fine in June 2025, and Staley did not seek to appeal further.

What the newly released transcript adds is texture, and a second thread: Staley told the committee he repeatedly shared confidential, market-sensitive JPMorgan information with Epstein, including details of the bank's Federal Reserve dealings during the 2008 crisis, a $44bn figure on private-bank inflows, his own pay, and pending deals. He also said Epstein was, for a period, named a trustee of his estate, and that he told Epstein the bank had flagged his large cash withdrawals and designated him a high-risk client.

Why the vetting question won't go away

None of this is new in the broad sense — JPMorgan and Barclays have both been sued, settled and reviewed over their Epstein ties, and Staley's regulatory ban was already public. What the transcript does is put a name-brand bank's internal risk flags on the record: a client the bank itself considered high-risk was, on Staley's account, told as much by the executive who kept the relationship alive. That is the detail regulators and boards elsewhere in the sector — still tightening onboarding and conflicts-of-interest rules in the years after Epstein's 2019 arrest and death in custody — will find hardest to wave away, since it describes a control that existed and was worked around rather than one that was simply missing.

Staley, through his lawyers, has consistently maintained he was deceived by Epstein about the true nature of his conduct and that nothing in the relationship was criminal on his part. The House Oversight Committee's Epstein files project is ongoing, with further witness transcripts expected as the investigation continues; whether any of it reopens scrutiny of JPMorgan's own oversight — the bank paid $290m in 2023 to settle claims it enabled Epstein's trafficking — is the next thing worth watching.
