AstraZeneca's Tezspire clears Phase 3 hurdle, but stock stays discounted

In one sentence
AstraZeneca's Tezspire hit every mark in a Phase 3 esophagitis trial, but shares stayed 14% off their highs as investors weigh a July pipeline setback and Dupixent's head start.

AstraZeneca and Amgen delivered exactly the kind of news a pharma investor is supposed to like on Thursday: a late-stage trial that hit every endpoint it was designed to hit. Tezspire, the asthma drug the two companies have spent a decade building into a broader inflammatory-disease franchise, cleared its Phase 3 test in eosinophilic esophagitis, a chronic condition in which immune cells inflame the esophagus and make swallowing painful or dangerous. The stock's reaction was a shrug.

AstraZeneca shares slipped roughly 2% in pre-market trading despite the result, and by Thursday's close they were still changing hands at $164.75 — about 14% below where they stood before a different trial, for a different drug, blew up in July. That gap is the real story here: strong data in a competitive market was not enough to move the needle, because the market's doubts about AstraZeneca right now have little to do with Tezspire and everything to do with what came before it.

What the trial actually showed

The Phase 3 study, code-named CROSSING, tested tezepelumab — sold as Tezspire — in adults and adolescents with eosinophilic esophagitis, or EoE. Both doses tested produced statistically significant improvements in the disease's two co-primary measures: histologic remission (a reduction in the inflammatory cells that damage esophageal tissue) and the frequency and severity of dysphagia, the difficulty-swallowing symptom that drives most patients to a doctor in the first place. Benefits were visible by week 24 and held through week 52, with a safety profile in line with Tezspire's existing approvals. It is, on its own terms, a clean win — EoE would be the drug's third approved indication, after severe asthma and (pending) chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps.

The commercial stakes are real. Tezspire generated $1.13 billion in AstraZeneca sales and $1.48 billion for Amgen in 2025, and AstraZeneca's second-quarter 2026 revenue from the drug was up 45% to $390 million. GlobalData analysts have pegged peak sales potential above $4 billion if the EoE indication and other label expansions come through. That would make Tezspire one of AstraZeneca's more important growth assets — just not, in the context of the company's stated ambitions, a decisive one. Management has set a target of $80 billion in total revenue by 2030, against which even a $4 billion peak-seller is a single brick in a very large wall.

The shadow of Wainua

The more immediate explanation for investor indifference sits in July. AstraZeneca's Wainua, a gene-silencing heart drug developed with Ionis Pharmaceuticals, failed to beat placebo in reducing cardiovascular death and clinical events in a Phase 3 trial for transthyretin-mediated amyloid cardiomyopathy — a market some analysts had sized above $15 billion. The stock fell 9.55% in a single session, wiping roughly £19 billion off the company's market value. It was an unusually clean miss for a drug that had shown positive earlier-stage signals in a disease area with validated competitors already on the market, which is precisely why it rattled confidence in the wider pipeline rather than just the one asset.

As one analysis of the stock's post-Tezspire move put it, one clinical win does not erase a major pipeline scar. With more than 20 Phase 3 readouts expected across AstraZeneca's pipeline over the next 18 months, investors appear to be treating each one as a data point in a longer verdict on whether the $80 billion target is credible — not as a standalone catalyst worth re-rating the stock over.

Racing an incumbent that already won

There is a second, more structural reason Tezspire's EoE data hasn't excited the market: Dupixent got there first. Sanofi and Regeneron's drug is already the first and only approved biologic for eosinophilic esophagitis, with more than 1.4 million patients on it across all its approved uses and quarterly sales of €4.17 billion, up 30.8% year-on-year in the most recent quarter alone — a single quarter that dwarfs Tezspire's entire annual haul. Physicians treating EoE already have a familiar, reimbursed, well-studied option. Tezspire's job, if it wins approval, will not be to create a market; it will be to carve share out of one Dupixent already owns, in a disease area where switching an established biologic is a slower and more conservative process than launching into open territory.

That dynamic — a well-run trial that expands a drug's label into a market its rival already dominates — helps explain why good data alone isn't repricing the stock. Investors are pricing execution risk against an entrenched competitor, not just clinical risk against a placebo.

The next real test for the shares is less about Tezspire than about whether AstraZeneca can string together clean readouts elsewhere in that pipeline. A regulatory filing for the EoE indication is the near-term milestone to watch; further out, the next Phase 3 data drop — in any therapeutic area — will tell investors whether July's scar is healing or whether the market has quietly recalibrated how much credit AstraZeneca's pipeline deserves.
