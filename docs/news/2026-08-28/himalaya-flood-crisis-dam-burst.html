Himalayan flood crisis deepens as China warns of fresh glacial dam burst

Tsho Rolpa, one of the Himalayas' largest glacial lakes, in Nepal's Rolwaling Valley near the region hit by this week's floods. — Hrishav398 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
China warns a Nepal-Tibet border lake swollen with 3 million cubic metres of meltwater could breach around 1 September, deepening a flood crisis that has killed 469 and left 1,300 missing.

The barrier lake that formed on the Nepal-Tibet border after Wednesday's glacier collapse has begun to overflow, Chinese state broadcaster CCTV reported Friday — a full week ahead of the breach that authorities on both sides of the frontier had been bracing for. China's water resources ministry says roughly 3 million cubic metres of meltwater and debris runoff, more than 1,200 Olympic swimming pools' worth, is flowing into the already swollen lake, with peak inflow expected around 1 September and rainfall forecast over the coming week likely to worsen the picture.

"The volume of water is continuing to increase, and so is the risk," Zhu Jinfeng, a researcher at the ministry's hydrology department, told CCTV. Nepal's disaster management authority issued its own warning to "residents of the lower banks, passengers, rescuers engaged in rescue operations, and all concerned" to stay away from riverbanks. Rescue teams that had been working the debris fields along the Bhote Koshi and Lhende rivers, including a 13-person crew led by Pawan Koirala, have themselves relocated to higher ground.

In one sentence: A second glacial-lake breach is now feared on the Nepal-Tibet border within days, compounding a flood disaster that has already killed at least 469 people and left more than 1,300 missing.

A glacier that gave way twice

The chain of events began at 8:40am local time on Wednesday, when a large section of glacier broke away roughly 5,200 metres up in the mountains above the Lhende river on the Tibetan side of the border, plunging some 1,200 metres onto the valley floor and gathering rock and sediment as it fell. The debris dammed the river; when that natural dam gave way, the surge tore downstream through Nepal's Rasuwa district, destroying homes, hydropower plants and border infrastructure before mud and floodwater struck Gyirong Port in China's Shigatse prefecture. The US Geological Survey had initially attributed a 4.4-magnitude seismic signal to an earthquake, then revised its assessment: the signal came from the landslide itself.

It is the second time in 14 months that the same stretch of river has produced a catastrophe. A glacial outburst down the Lhende in July 2025 destroyed the Nepal-China Friendship Bridge at Rasuwagadhi — the crossing that handles some 70% of Nepal-China trade — and killed more than a dozen people. That bridge had only recently been rebuilt.

At least 19 bridges and roughly 40km of road have now been destroyed across the two countries. Nepal has barred onward travel into affected border areas; China has closed Shigatse's crossings to Chinese visitors. More than 500 villagers and stranded tourists have been evacuated so far, according to Chinese authorities, with further relocations under way ahead of the anticipated breach.

A toll still climbing, and a search called off

The confirmed death toll passed 469 on Friday, with more than 1,300 people still unaccounted for — among them over 500 foreign nationals from at least 32 countries, reflecting how popular the Rasuwa trekking and pilgrimage corridor is with foreign visitors in late August. Nepal's tourism board has identified 33 British citizens among the missing, including a Hindu priest from the South London Sanatan Mandir and Community Centre in Coulsdon, Croydon. Roughly 90 Americans, dozens of Australians and around 30 Canadians are also unaccounted for, alongside more than 100 Indian nationals.

King Charles said he and Queen Camilla were "heartbroken" by the disaster, offering his "most profound sympathy to all those who have so tragically lost their loved ones," as the UK government pledged £5 million in aid. Pope Leo has also offered prayers for the dead and missing. But the overflow now reported at the barrier lake has forced rescuers to suspend search operations in the debris fields — precisely the terrain where survivors would most plausibly still be found — trading the search for the missing against the risk of sending crews back into the path of a second flood.

Why a swelling lake matters beyond the mountains

Glacial lake outburst floods, or GLOFs, are not new to the Himalayas, but their frequency is rising as warming temperatures accelerate glacier retreat and destabilise the moraine and ice dams that hold meltwater back. What makes this event different is the compounding: a first collapse that killed hundreds and severed cross-border infrastructure, followed within days by a second hazard building behind an unstable natural dam on contested, thinly monitored terrain. Every rebuild decision now facing Nepali and Chinese engineers — the Friendship Bridge, hydropower plants, the Gyirong crossing — has to reckon with a river system that has demonstrated it can do this twice in little over a year.

The next fixed point is 1 September, the date Chinese hydrologists have flagged for peak inflow and the highest breach risk. Whether the lake fails catastrophically or drains more gradually will determine whether Rasuwa and Shigatse face a second wave of destruction on top of a death toll still being counted, and it will shape how seriously both governments treat early-warning systems for a river that has already proven itself the most dangerous stretch of the frontier.
