BEYOND THE TAPE · AVIATION · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-28 · 3 min read

LaGuardia controllers left work early before deadly collision, timecard fraud probe finds

An Air Canada Express Bombardier CRJ-900 — the aircraft type involved in the fatal LaGuardia collision — on approach at Baltimore-Washington International Airport. — Acroterion / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
An FAA review found two LaGuardia controllers left their shift early via a so-called 'early shove' hours before a fatal Air Canada collision, prompting firings and union pushback.

The Federal Aviation Administration is moving to fire two air traffic controllers at LaGuardia Airport after finding they left their shift roughly an hour early on the night a regional jet collided with a fire truck, killing both pilots — a practice controllers call an "early shove," and one the agency now treats as timecard fraud, according to the Wall Street Journal.

The collision happened just before midnight on March 22, when Air Canada Express Flight 8646, a Jazz Aviation-operated Bombardier CRJ-900 carrying 76 people, was cleared to land on Runway 4 as a Port Authority fire truck was waved across the same strip of tarmac. The truck had no transponder, so the tower had no automated way to track it. Controllers shouted "stop, stop, stop" as the jet closed in at around 90 knots; one firefighter later told investigators he didn't realise the warning was meant for him until he saw the plane bearing down. Both pilots died. Thirty-nine people were hurt, including the two firefighters riding in the truck.

The shove before the crash

The FAA's finding doesn't slot neatly into that timeline. The two controllers under investigation left before the accident occurred, and the agency has stopped short of saying their early exit changed who was working, or what those on duty were doing, when the jet hit the truck. What it has established is the backdrop: a punishing shift. Thunderstorms had pushed traffic at LaGuardia to more than twice its scheduled volume that evening, and the departures left the remaining controllers stretched thinner still. One was running both ground and local control simultaneously — a combination a 1997 rule was meant to prevent before midnight, following an earlier runway accident — while also managing an unrelated aircraft that had reported smoke in the cockpit minutes before the collision.

"Early shoves" are, by multiple accounts, an open secret in a controller workforce that often works six-day weeks to cover chronic staffing gaps — leaving a little sooner than the timesheet says, tolerated as an informal release valve. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy framed the LaGuardia cases differently: "We have no choice but to act," he said. "We will not tolerate fraud, and we are taking appropriate action to hold folks accountable." NATCA, the controllers' union, has been notably restrained in response, saying only that it is "actively discussing these allegations with FAA leadership" and that such discussions are "the appropriate forum for addressing the matter" — while pointing to the "extraordinary dedication" of controllers working under strained conditions.

What's next

The National Transportation Safety Board's investigation, opened the night of the crash, remains open; a preliminary report in April flagged tower staffing and the missing transponder without assigning cause. A final NTSB report — likely to weigh in directly on whether staffing and workload played a role — is still pending, and will carry more weight than any timecard dispute in determining what actually failed that night.
