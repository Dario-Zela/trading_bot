Colman's put up for sale as owner switches to American mustard

A jar of Colman's Original English Mustard, bearing the brand's Royal Warrant dating to 1866. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Colman's mustard is being sold off to clear competition concerns in Unilever's $44.8bn merger with McCormick, owner of rival French's.

There is a particular kind of corporate irony that writes itself, and the sale of Colman's mustard is one of them. The Norwich brand that has sat in Britain's fridge doors since 1814 — the one with the bull's head crest and the Royal Warrant granted by Queen Victoria in 1866 — is being put up for sale by Unilever. The reason is French's, the American mustard brand. Colman's owned French's once, back in 1926. Now French's owner is the reason Colman's has to go.

The mechanism is a $44.8bn merger, not a change of heart. Unilever is combining its entire foods arm — Hellmann's, Knorr, Marmite and the rest — with McCormick, the US spice and condiment giant, in a deal that creates a roughly $65bn business built around McCormick's name. Unilever chief executive Fernando Fernandez has described it as "unlocking trapped value" and turning what remains of Unilever into a €39bn "pureplay" home and personal care company, sharpening the portfolio toward what he calls high-growth categories. Foods, in that framing, becomes someone else's problem — or rather, McCormick's.

Colman's is the casualty of the merger's own tidiness. McCormick already owns French's, one of the biggest mustard brands in the world; Unilever brings Colman's, one of the biggest in Britain. Put the two together and a competition regulator has an obvious objection, so the companies got ahead of it. "A decision has been taken to market the Colman's brand and assets to potential buyers in order to proactively seek to address potential competition concerns," was the driest possible way Unilever found to say: one of these mustards has to be sold before anyone signs off on the rest. Rothschild has been retained to run the process, and people close to it describe the brand's value as "not material" against a deal this size — a faintly brutal way to describe two centuries of trading history.

A brand that outlasted its own factory

Colman's has already survived one uprooting. Production ran from Carrow Road in Norwich for 160 years before moving to Burton upon Trent and a plant in Germany; the last jar came off the Norwich line in July 2019, stamped with a small commemorative label reading "Norwich's Last. By Its Finest." The brand became part of Reckitt & Colman in 1938 and passed to Unilever in the 1995 demerger, where it has sat since as a solid, unglamorous earner — the kind of brand that shows up in every merger deck as a heritage asset rather than a growth story, which is precisely why it is the one being cut loose now rather than kept.

Whoever buys it inherits a name with more recognition than momentum: a fixture of Sunday roasts, respected rather than fashionable, competing against a global rival — French's — that will soon sit inside a $65bn combined group with considerably deeper pockets. The McCormick-Unilever merger is expected to close later this year, and Colman's new owner, whoever emerges from Rothschild's process, will need an answer for how a 212-year-old English mustard tin stays on shelves next to that.
