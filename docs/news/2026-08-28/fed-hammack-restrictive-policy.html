Fed's Hammack says policy must stay restrictive despite balanced labour market

Beth Hammack, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, has dissented in favour of a rate hike at the Fed's last meeting. — Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Cleveland Fed's Hammack says the labor market is balanced but inflation progress will be slow, so policy needs to stay restrictive — open-minded on pace, not on direction.

Cleveland Federal Reserve president Beth Hammack went on Fox Business Network on Thursday and delivered a message traders have heard from her before, just with the inflation numbers attached this time: the labour market is fine, and that is precisely why the Fed has no business easing up.

"The job market is broadly in balance," Hammack told Fox Business's Edward Lawrence, a notable concession given that July payrolls fell by 23,000 and unemployment sits at 4.1%. But she paired it with a inflation forecast that gives little comfort to anyone hoping for cuts: prices ending this year near 3%, with "maybe mid-twos at best" in 2027. "We need restrictiveness in policy," she said, adding that she goes into every meeting "open-minded" about how fast the Fed should move.

A hawk among dissenters

Hammack's framing only makes sense against the backdrop of the July 28–29 FOMC meeting, where the committee held its target range at 3.5%–3.75% — but not unanimously. Hammack, Minneapolis Fed president Neel Kashkari and Dallas's Lorie Logan all dissented in favour of a quarter-point hike, the first three-way directional dissent at the Fed since September 2016. Their argument: with headline inflation running at 3.4% in July, more than five years above target, policy isn't tight enough to finish the job. New Fed chair Kevin Warsh, who took over in May, held the line at hold but signalled the committee would "not hesitate to act where necessary."

The market has moved on without her

What's shifted since July is the data, not Hammack's stance. The weak July jobs report knocked the wind out of hike expectations that had briefly touched 61% on the CME FedWatch Tool; by Thursday, futures markets were pricing roughly a 30%-34% chance of a September hike against about 70% for another hold. Hammack's appearance reads as a rearguard action — reminding investors that a soft labour print doesn't automatically clear the path to cuts, let alone rule a hike back in, so long as inflation keeps landing above 3%.

That is the tension now sitting under the September 16–17 meeting: a Fed chair trying to keep options open, a trio of regional presidents on record wanting more restraint, and a market that has swung from pricing hikes to pricing patience within a month. The next reads that matter are August payrolls, due the Friday after Labor Day, and the August CPI print in mid-September — either could reopen the argument Hammack is still making on television.
