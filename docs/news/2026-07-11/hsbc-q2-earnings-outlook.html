HSBC sees strongest US earnings growth since the pandemic recovery, picks Amazon, Microsoft, Meta

In one sentence
HSBC forecasts 22% US Q2 earnings growth — the fastest since the post-pandemic recovery — concentrated in energy, tech and Amazon, Microsoft and Meta.

Wall Street's earnings analysts spent much of the past year hedging. Guidance was murky, tariff exposure was unclear, and the AI capital-spending boom looked to some like a bill that would eventually come due. HSBC Global Investment Research is now breaking from that caution in a notably specific way: its analysts expect second-quarter US corporate earnings to grow roughly 22% year-on-year, the fastest pace since the immediate post-pandemic recovery, when reopening comparisons made growth rates look artificially heroic. This time, HSBC argues, the acceleration is real — and it is concentrated in a handful of sectors and companies that happen to include some of the most heavily owned names in the market.

A revision that moves the wrong way

Earnings estimates typically drift downward as a quarter approaches — analysts trim numbers, companies guide conservatively, and the eventual "beat" is manufactured as much by expectations management as by performance. HSBC's Nicole Inui, head of equity strategy for the Americas, has flagged the opposite happening this cycle: consensus EPS forecasts have been revised upward heading into the print, an unusual pattern that HSBC reads as a signal of genuine strength rather than a sandbagged bar.

The growth is not evenly spread. HSBC expects energy and information technology to post double- and triple-digit earnings gains, with semiconductors and tech hardware doing much of the lifting alongside oil and gas producers benefiting from firmer crude prices. Materials names round out the list of standout sectors. Strip those out, and the picture looks far more pedestrian: HSBC puts earnings growth for the rest of the market at around 5%, a reminder that this is a story about a small number of very large, very profitable companies rather than a broad-based recovery.

Inui's own framing is that the acceleration is not the kind of speculative excess that ends badly. "Fundamentally strong sectors and dominant market players," is how she has characterized the source of the growth — a distinction between concentration built on cash flow and concentration built on hope. It is also the same logic behind HSBC's decision in May to lift its year-end S&P 500 target to 7,650 from 7,500, alongside an 8% upgrade to index-level EPS estimates, with strategists sketching a path to 8,000 if AI-driven margins keep expanding and the rest of the market starts to catch up.

The stock list tech investors already know

HSBC paired the sector call with a list of ten Buy-rated names it considers best positioned for the earnings season now underway: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, AbbVie, Caterpillar, Marriott International, Vertiv, NextPower and Wells Fargo. The rationale, notably, is company-specific rather than a single thematic bet — though the through-line running under most of the picks is the same one driving the sector forecast: artificial intelligence infrastructure spending and the cloud businesses that monetize it.

Microsoft's inclusion rests on what HSBC calls a re-acceleration in Azure's AI build-out as infrastructure scales and ecosystem partnerships deepen customer retention. Amazon's case leans on AWS capacity expansion, in-house chip development, and partnerships with Anthropic and OpenAI, paired with margin improvement in the retail business. Meta's pitch is narrower and more mechanical: AI-driven ad targeting is lifting monetization per user, while custom silicon and early signs of compute monetization help offset the spending. Both Microsoft and Alphabet have already shown some of this in hard numbers this year — Microsoft's fiscal first-quarter revenue rose 18% to $77.7 billion with Azure growth around 40%, while Alphabet's first-quarter revenue climbed 22% to $109.9 billion.

The rest of the list is a reminder that HSBC's AI thesis extends past the household names. Vertiv, which makes power and cooling systems for data centers, gets a mention specifically because roughly 80% of its revenue is tied to that build-out. Caterpillar's inclusion cites data-center construction demand alongside its traditional mining and infrastructure business. AbbVie, Marriott and Wells Fargo are the outliers — included for company-specific drivers (drug franchise durability, travel demand, net interest income) rather than the AI-capex theme running through the rest of the list.

Why the margin of safety is getting thinner

The tension in HSBC's own numbers is worth sitting with. US equities are trading near record valuations, and the case for holding them at those multiples has rested heavily on the assumption that earnings would eventually grow into the price. A 22% EPS print, if it materializes, does exactly that — it closes some of the valuation gap analysts have been uneasy about since late last year. But it does so by leaning further into the same handful of names that already dominate index weightings, which cuts against the standard argument that expensive markets are safer when their gains are broadly distributed.

That is the real stakes of HSBC's call. If energy, tech and materials deliver as forecast while the rest of the market limps along at 5%, the S&P 500's valuation premium becomes even more dependent on a shrinking set of companies continuing to execute flawlessly on AI infrastructure spending that has yet to be tested by a slowdown. A miss from any of Microsoft, Amazon or Meta this earnings season would not just move those individual stocks — it would undercut the sector-level thesis HSBC is using to justify the market's overall valuation.

What comes next

Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Amazon report second-quarter results within the same two-week window in late July, giving the market a concentrated stress test of HSBC's thesis almost immediately. Energy earnings, tied to crude price moves through the quarter, and early materials reports will offer the first read on whether the acceleration HSBC is forecasting outside mega-cap tech actually shows up, or whether the "broadening" bulls have been waiting for stays confined to the same five or six names.
