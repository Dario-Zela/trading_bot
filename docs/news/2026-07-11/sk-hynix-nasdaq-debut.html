SK Hynix's Nasdaq debut marks the largest-ever US listing by a foreign company

The Nasdaq MarketSite tower in Times Square, where SK Hynix executives rang the opening bell on July 10, 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
SK Hynix's ADRs rose 13% on Nasdaq debut, capping a $26.5bn listing — the largest-ever US market entry by a foreign company.

SK Hynix closed its first day on the Nasdaq up 13%, at $168.01 a share, capping a listing that raised $26.5 billion and became the largest-ever U.S. market debut by a foreign company. The South Korean chipmaker — the dominant supplier of the high-bandwidth memory that feeds Nvidia's AI accelerators — priced its American depositary shares at $149 apiece, drew orders for more than seven times the shares on offer, and walked away with a listing bigger than Alibaba's $25 billion Nasdaq debut in 2014, the previous record holder.

A record built on a supply squeeze

The mechanics of the offering matter less than the demand behind it. SK Hynix sold 177.9 million ADRs, each representing one-tenth of a common share, and the deal was covered several times over before pricing. SK Group chairman Chey Tae-won rang the opening bell in Times Square alongside chief executive Kwak Noh-jung, who thanked investors for their "trust and support" and pledged to keep pushing what memory technology can do. Behind the ceremony sits a blunter fact: SK Hynix controls something close to 60% of global revenue in high-bandwidth memory, the stacked-chip technology that sits inside every Nvidia AI accelerator shipped this year, and Kwak has separately warned that 2027 could bring the tightest memory supply the industry has seen. Scarcity, not sentiment, is doing the heavy lifting here — AI-related chip sales at the company have roughly tripled year over year, and HBM now commands three to five times the price of conventional memory.

That scarcity is also why the timing looks deliberate. SK Hynix and Micron both crossed the $1 trillion valuation mark this year as the memory market re-rated around AI demand, and SK Hynix's own market cap moved past $1 trillion within hours of trading. Bringing a U.S. listing to market now, at the peak of investor appetite for anything touching the AI supply chain, is the kind of thing that only works once.

The discount the debut is meant to close

The more interesting number sits underneath the pop: even after SK Hynix's shares rallied, the company trades at roughly 5.9 times forward earnings against Micron's 7.8 times — despite the two effectively running a duopoly in advanced memory and SK Hynix holding the larger share of the more lucrative HBM segment. Traders and analysts have taken to calling the gap the "Korea discount," a catch-all for the reasons foreign capital has historically underweighted Korean equities: a retail-heavy home market, opaque governance structures at the country's conglomerates, and geopolitical risk tied to Korea's exposure to China and to the broader semiconductor trade fight.

That framing is worth treating with some caution — geopolitical risk is real for SK Hynix, which still runs fabs in China, but it is hardly absent for Micron, which sells into the same AI supply chain and carries its own China-market restrictions. A cleaner explanation is simply access: until Friday, U.S. institutional investors who wanted direct, dollar-denominated exposure to the world's leading HBM supplier mostly couldn't get it without navigating the Korea Exchange. The Nasdaq listing removes that friction. Whether it closes the valuation gap will show up over the coming months in whether the ADRs trade in line with the Seoul-listed shares or continue at a discount once the debut-day enthusiasm fades — SK Hynix is due to complete a second listing on the KOSPI on July 29.

What the appetite signals

Seven-times oversubscription on a $26.5 billion deal is a specific, measurable data point, and it lands at a moment when investors have been hunting for ways to bet on AI infrastructure beyond Nvidia itself. SK Hynix's pitch — first to mass-produce HBM3, first to ship HBM3E, already sampling next-generation HBM4E for Nvidia's Rubin Ultra platform due in 2027 — reads as a "picks and shovels" argument: memory manufacturing requires capital and process expertise that new entrants cannot quickly replicate, which is a different kind of moat than a software company's. That argument evidently found a large and willing audience in New York. It is also being read in Seoul and Taipei as a template. A market with a $26.5 billion appetite for one Korean chipmaker's ADRs is a market other Asian semiconductor firms will now be pricing meetings with their bankers around.

What to watch

The near-term test is whether SK Hynix's ADRs hold their debut-day gain once trading settles and whether the valuation gap to Micron narrows in the weeks after the KOSPI listing closes the loop on July 29. Micron reports fiscal third-quarter earnings later this summer, which will offer the first direct read on whether the memory pricing cycle Kwak has been warning about — tightening supply into 2027 — is showing up in guidance from the U.S. side of the duopoly as well. If it is, the debate over which company deserves the higher multiple gets a lot more concrete.
