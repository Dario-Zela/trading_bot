US senators say they've reached agreement with Trump on Russia sanctions bill

Sen. Lindsey Graham, lead author of the Sanctioning Russia Act, says the White House has approved the bill's latest draft. — U.S. Senate / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Senators say they have a deal with Trump on a Russia sanctions bill, but the waiver-authority language and a floor vote both remain unresolved.

Four senators who have spent more than a year trying to force a vote on Russia sanctions say they have finally gotten what they needed most: a nod from the White House. Republicans Lindsey Graham of South Carolina and Roger Wicker of Mississippi, together with Democrats Richard Blumenthal of Connecticut and Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire, announced on Friday that they had reached agreement with the Trump administration on updated language for the long-stalled Sanctioning Russia Act.

That sounds like a breakthrough, and in Washington terms it is one — the bill has sat in limbo for more than a year despite gathering more than 80 co-sponsors, a veto-proof majority, because Senate Majority Leader John Thune declined to bring it to the floor without assurance the White House wanted it there. What the announcement does not do is specify what changed in the bill's text, or commit Trump to actively pushing for its passage.

The gap the headline skips over

The legislation, as originally drafted, would let Trump impose tariffs of up to 500% on countries that keep buying Russian oil, natural gas or uranium — a list topped by China and India, the two largest importers of Russian crude — with carve-outs possible for nations aiding Ukraine's war effort. That structure has not changed. What has been under negotiation is narrower but consequential: how much discretion the bill hands Trump to waive sanctions or set tariff levels himself, rather than have Congress dictate them.

Shaheen, the Senate Foreign Relations Committee's top Democrat, said as recently as this month that "there isn't an agreement on language" on that point, and Democratic caution has hardened since the Supreme Court struck down Trump's global tariff authority in March. Friday's statement suggests negotiators found wording both sides can live with, but neither the senators nor the White House released the text, and a spokesperson for the administration did not respond to requests for comment on the record. Graham has told Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky the bill has White House backing; Trump himself has not said so publicly since he first endorsed the effort in January.

Thune's position hasn't moved: the Senate will act only once it is certain the administration wants it to, and with midterm-year floor time scarce, that calculus still rests with the White House rather than the 80-plus co-sponsors already on record. A companion House bill cleared the 218-signature threshold this week to force a vote there, which may be the more immediate pressure point.

For markets, the honest read is that nothing has actually shifted yet. Blumenthal himself pointed to the reason the deal became politically easier to strike: oil prices have eased since tensions with Iran cooled, making a tariff threat against Russian crude buyers look less inflationary than it would have looked in the spring. Whether that threat becomes law — and whether Beijing and New Delhi treat it as credible if it does — is the actual catalyst to watch, not this week's framework announcement.
