Rio Tinto looks fully priced on cash flow, cheap on earnings

A Rio Tinto mine and processing plant — the miner's cash flow profile is at the centre of a valuation split among analysts. — Marcin Wichary / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Rio Tinto is up 68.5% over five years, but DCF models call it 27% overpriced while earnings multiples call it cheap versus peers.

Rio Tinto has handed shareholders a 68.5% return over the past five years, and 59.4% over the past twelve months alone. That kind of run usually invites a simple question — is it too late to buy? — and usually gets a simple answer. This time it doesn't.

Run the numbers through a discounted cash flow model and Rio Tinto looks stretched. Using a two-stage free-cash-flow-to-equity approach built on the miner's roughly $6.7 billion in trailing twelve-month free cash flow, the model puts intrinsic value at around £53 a share — against a market price that implies the stock is trading about 26.7% above that mark. On projected cash generation alone, in other words, the market has already priced in more than the discounted flows justify.

Run the same stock through an earnings multiple and the picture flips. Rio Tinto trades at roughly 14.8 times earnings, below the metals and mining industry average of 16.1x and well under the wider peer group's 30.8x. A valuation model tailored to Rio Tinto's growth and risk profile puts a "fair" multiple closer to 21.5x — which would suggest the stock is cheap, not expensive. Tally up the standard valuation checks and Rio Tinto clears just three of six, a split score that reflects a genuinely split picture rather than a rounding error.

Where the models disagree

The divergence is not noise. DCF models are forward-looking and sensitive to the shape of future cash flows; earnings multiples are backward-looking and sensitive to what the market is currently willing to pay for a dollar of profit elsewhere in the sector. Rio Tinto's problem sits squarely in the gap between the two: the market is paying a below-average multiple for its current earnings, but analysts assessing what those earnings translate into as discounted cash further out are less convinced the growth holds up.

UBS put a name to that concern this year, flagging a "growth gap in the copper pipeline after 2030." Copper output is on track to rise from roughly 883,000 tonnes in 2025 to around 1 million tonnes by 2030, driven by the Oyu Tolgoi ramp-up in Mongolia and Kennecott in Utah. Beyond 2030, the bank sees fewer clear projects ready to fill the gap — Resolution, La Granja and Nuevo Cobre are all longer-dated, and Winu is comparatively small. Rio already holds a 17% stake in Argentina's Los Azules project, a low-cost operation targeting first copper in 2030, and was reported in May to be weighing a larger position there.

That copper question is precisely what a DCF model is built to capture and a trailing P/E ratio is not. It helps explain why the cash-flow lens flags Rio Tinto as fully priced — it is discounting a growth profile that thins out mid-decade — while the earnings lens, comparing today's profits to today's price, still calls it a discount to peers.

For traders leaning on either signal in isolation, that is the practical takeaway: the multiple says buy, the discounted cash flow says the market has already paid for the easy part. The next real test of which model has it right lands with Rio Tinto's half-year results and any update on Los Azules, where a bigger stake — or a decision to walk away — will tell the market how seriously the company takes UBS's post-2030 gap.
