US oil rig count holds flat at 445 as gas rigs edge up

A horizontal drilling rig of the type used in U.S. shale oil and gas extraction. — Meredithw / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA)
In one sentence
U.S. oil rigs held flat at 445 even as crude firmed on Hormuz tensions, with gas rigs up just one to 127.

The number that usually moves markets didn't move at all. Baker Hughes reported Thursday that the U.S. oil rig count held at 445 for the week, exactly where it sat the week before. Natural gas rigs ticked up by one, to 127. Add in miscellaneous rigs and the total came to 581, against 580 a week earlier — a rounding error dressed up as a data release.

What makes the stall notable is the backdrop. Crude has firmed over the past week, with West Texas Intermediate trading near $71-$72 a barrel — up roughly 3.5% — as renewed U.S.-Iran friction has choked shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, a route that carries about a fifth of the world's oil and gas trade. Higher prices are supposed to be the signal that gets drillers moving. This week, they didn't.

Discipline, not direction

The flat print fits a pattern that has held for most of 2026. In the Permian Basin, the country's most active shale patch, the rig count has stayed in the 300-310 range through the first half of the year even as spot prices ran well above what operators had budgeted for. Public producers — the Exxons, Chevrons and Diamondbacks of the sector — have kept capital guidance roughly where it was rather than chase the rally, funneling the extra cash flow into dividends, buybacks and debt paydown instead of new wells. Private operators, who answer to fewer shareholders, have been somewhat more willing to add rigs, but not enough to move the national count.

Part of the reluctance is that the current price strength looks geopolitical rather than structural — a risk premium tied to tanker traffic near Iran, not a shift in the underlying supply-demand balance. Producers who spent the past three years unwinding the growth-at-any-cost habits of the shale boom are in no hurry to reverse course on a headline that could evaporate with a diplomatic de-escalation. At 445 rigs, the oil count remains well below the roughly 498 that Baker Hughes data have averaged since 1987, and a small fraction of the 1,609 peak reached in October 2014.

Gas rigs are a smaller, quieter story. Henry Hub spot prices have drifted between $2.95 and $3.30 per million BTU in recent weeks, pressured by a five-year storage surplus near 185 billion cubic feet and seasonal maintenance at Freeport LNG's export facility. A single added rig is not a vote of confidence so much as noise around a market with ample supply and no urgency to add more.

The next Baker Hughes count lands Friday, July 17, alongside the EIA's weekly petroleum inventory report. Until either the Hormuz risk premium proves durable or storage surpluses start narrowing, the more useful number may not be the rig count itself but how long operators are willing to sit on their hands while it holds.
