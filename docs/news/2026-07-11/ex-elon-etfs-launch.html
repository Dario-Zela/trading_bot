New 'Ex-Elon' ETFs strip Tesla and SpaceX from major indexes

Tesla's Gigafactory 1 near Sparks, Nevada — one of the assets a new breed of ETFs is designed to exclude. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Subversive Capital has filed 'Ex-Elon' ETFs (QQNE, SPNE) that strip Tesla and SpaceX from the Nasdaq-100 and S&P 500, betting founder risk is now investable.

The pitch is almost insultingly simple: buy the market, minus one man.

Subversive Capital, the boutique ETF shop better known for funds that mimic the stock trades of Democratic and Republican members of Congress, has filed with the SEC to launch two new products built around a single exclusion criterion. The Nasdaq-100 Ex-Elon Enterprises ETF (ticker: QQNE) and the S&P 500 Ex-Elon Enterprises ETF (SPNE) will track their namesake benchmarks with one modification — they strip out any company "founded, controlled, or led by Elon Musk," starting with Tesla and the newly public SpaceX.

What's actually being excluded

The mechanics are narrower than the headline suggests. QQNE would hold the Nasdaq-100 minus Tesla and SpaceX, which together account for roughly 8.4% of that index's weight. SPNE would hold the S&P 500 minus Tesla alone — SpaceX isn't in the S&P 500 — worth on the order of 2% of that benchmark. Both funds reserve the right to drop future Musk-adjacent companies from their portfolios, naming Neuralink and the Boring Company as candidates should either go public. The filing's own language frames the rationale in risk-management terms: Musk-linked companies, it says, carry "corporate governance concerns, political risks, and heightened share-price volatility" that some investors would rather not underwrite simply by buying an index fund.

That last point is the more interesting one. An investor who buys VOO or QQQ today is, by construction, also buying Musk's governance style, his Twitter/X output, his political ventures and the volatility that comes with a chief executive who runs multiple public companies simultaneously. Subversive's bet is that a slice of the market wants exposure to mega-cap growth without inheriting that particular bundle.

Why the timing isn't a coincidence

The filing lands three days after SpaceX itself joined the Nasdaq-100, a remarkably fast turnaround for an index that historically required three months to a year of trading history before admitting a new name. Nasdaq changed its own rules in March to allow companies onto the index within 15 trading days of an IPO — a change that arrived just in time for SpaceX's June listing, the largest IPO on record, which raised $85.7 billion and briefly valued the company near $2.1 trillion. Because only a small fraction of SpaceX's shares are currently freely tradeable, its float-adjusted weight in the index started small, but J.P. Morgan estimated the inclusion alone would force roughly $4.3 billion of passive-fund buying as index trackers rebalanced to match.

In other words: millions of index-fund holders who had no opinion on SpaceX, and no ability to opt out, suddenly owned a stake in it the moment the rules changed. That forced, mechanical exposure is precisely what Subversive's new funds are pitched as an escape hatch from.

Gimmick or genuine risk factor?

Not everyone is convinced this is more than marketing. "They may well attract some money that doesn't think too hard, but these kinds of narrow-cast, micro-ideas aren't really 'for' anyone," said Dave Nadig, president and director of research at ETF.com. "Fun marketing, not a real investment thesis." Morningstar's Jeffrey Ptak was similarly guarded, warning that such a product "might not serve a legitimate investing purpose or cost an arm and a leg for a very marginal benefit," even as he allowed that issuers face pressure to differentiate in an increasingly crowded ETF market. Nate Geraci of NovaDius Wealth Management put a finer point on the fragmentation risk: "if we're now in an ETF world where issuers are excluding single companies from major indexes based on investor sentiment toward one man, we may be slicing things a little too thin."

History offers a caution on both sides. Tuttle Capital's Inverse Cramer Tracker ETF, a similarly personality-driven fund, shut down within a year of its 2023 launch for lack of assets. But Subversive's own congressional-trading funds, NANC and KRUZ, found a durable audience by turning political sentiment into a tradeable product — proof that a narrow thesis can work if the underlying anxiety is real and sustained rather than a passing news cycle.

That's the actual test here. Founder-concentration risk — the idea that an index investor is really making a bet on a handful of individuals rather than diversified corporate management — has been a talking point in equity research for years, applied to Musk, to Mark Zuckerberg's dual-class control of Meta, to Jensen Huang at Nvidia. What's new is that a fund issuer is willing to underwrite that thesis as a product rather than a caveat in a research note, effectively pricing founder dependency as a factor to be hedged rather than merely disclosed.

What comes next

QQNE and SPNE are slated to begin trading September 21, 2026, structured as actively managed funds under the Tidal Trust I umbrella, competing against index trackers like SPY and QQQ that charge only a few basis points. Whether investors pay up for the exclusion — and whether the funds gather enough assets to survive their first year, the threshold that killed the Inverse Cramer fund — will be the real verdict on whether founder-risk hedging is a durable category or a one-cycle novelty. The nearer-term catalyst to watch is SpaceX's share-lockup expiration, which will free up more stock for trading and could push its Nasdaq-100 weight, and the case for funds like QQNE, higher still.
