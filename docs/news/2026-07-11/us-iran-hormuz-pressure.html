US presses Iran on Hormuz attacks as Tehran vows no surrender

Satellite view of the Strait of Hormuz and the Musandam Peninsula, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil trade passes. — NASA MODIS / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US demands Iran publicly declare Hormuz open and attacks halted; Tehran's negotiator vows no surrender, keeping oil's war-risk premium alive.

The Strait of Hormuz has spent the past two weeks doing the opposite of what a 20-billion-barrel-a-year chokepoint is supposed to do: it has emptied out. Tanker traffic through the passage, which normally carries something like a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, was running at roughly 40 transits a day in early July, against a pre-war average of 125 to 140. The gap is the clearest measure of how fragile the truce between the United States and Iran has become, and why crude has been climbing even as neither side wants, on paper, to reopen the war.

A truce that keeps slipping

The current standoff sits on top of a war that formally ended only three weeks ago. US and Israeli strikes on Iran began on February 28; a ceasefire followed in early April after five weeks of fighting; and on June 17 the presidents of the United States and Iran signed the Islamabad Memorandum, a mediator-brokered deal giving both sides 60 days to negotiate final terms. Its headline commitments were straightforward — Iran would reaffirm it is not building a nuclear weapon, hostilities would stop on all fronts, and the Strait of Hormuz would reopen to normal traffic.

That 60-day clock is now roughly three weeks from expiring, and the interim has not gone smoothly. On July 6 and 7, Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired on three commercial vessels transiting the strait — a Qatari-owned LNG carrier, the Al Rekayat, which caught fire in its engine room and had to be evacuated, a Saudi-flagged supertanker, and a third ship struck the following morning. It was, by the UN's shipping monitor, the worst single day of attacks since the memorandum was signed, and it prompted fresh US strikes in response. President Trump said afterward he considered the truce effectively over.

Washington's ultimatum

That episode is the backdrop to the demand US officials confirmed to Reuters on Friday: that Iran issue a public statement declaring every lane of the strait open and its forces no longer firing on ships. "They're either going to give us that statement or we're not having a good outcome for them," one senior official said, describing the choice in blunt terms. Iran's own explanation for the July attacks, relayed to Washington, was that they came from "an errant part of their system" — language US officials read as an admission of an internal split between hardliners still fighting the war on their own terms and a more pragmatic camp trying to hold the memorandum together.

Diplomacy has not stopped despite the exchange of fire. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, was expected in Oman on Saturday for talks that Omani mediators hope can stabilize the Hormuz question specifically. Separately, Al Arabiya reported that a four-way call involving the United States, Iran, Pakistan and Qatar was in the works, with US and Iranian technical teams expected to meet in Pakistan on Sunday. Neither report carries confirmed details of what would be discussed, and Gulf mediators — Qatar and Pakistan chief among them, with Turkey, Egypt and Saudi Arabia also making calls — appear to be doing as much improvisation as negotiation, scrambling to keep the memorandum from unraveling rather than advancing it.

Tehran's line: no surrender

Against that mediation effort, Iran's own negotiating team is not projecting flexibility. Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, the parliament speaker who has emerged as Iran's chief interlocutor with Washington, said this week that "this conflict will never end with Iran's surrender," and that Tehran is "prepared for an all-out defense" the moment the Americans "betray the understanding." He added that he had told US Vice President JD Vance directly, in negotiations, "we have no trust in you" — and that in his view, only those "prepared for war" are equipped to negotiate with Washington at all.

"This conflict will never end with Iran's surrender."
— Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, Speaker of Iran's Parliament

That rhetoric lands as bargaining position more than new escalation — Ghalibaf is restating a line Tehran has held since the ceasefire, not announcing a break from it. But paired with the July 6-7 attacks and Washington's public-statement demand, it illustrates why traders have stopped treating the Islamabad Memorandum as a settled outcome and started pricing it as a conditional one, renewable roughly every time a projectile hits a tanker.

What the market is actually pricing

Brent crude has held above $76 a barrel, on track for a weekly gain of near 6%, with the premium tied directly to Hormuz uncertainty rather than to demand fundamentals. That is the mechanism worth watching: the strait doesn't need to close to move the price, it only needs traffic to stay thin and the question of who controls it to stay open. Near-standstill shipping volumes are already a supply story even without a formal blockade, and every cycle of attack, strike and demanded statement resets the clock on how much premium the market is willing to hold.

The next concrete markers are close together — Araghchi's Oman meeting and the US-Iran technical session in Pakistan, both expected within days, followed by whatever comes of the mooted four-way call. Behind all of it sits the harder deadline: the Islamabad Memorandum's 60-day negotiating window closes in mid-August, and neither Washington's ultimatum nor Ghalibaf's defiance suggests the two sides are converging faster than that clock is running out.
