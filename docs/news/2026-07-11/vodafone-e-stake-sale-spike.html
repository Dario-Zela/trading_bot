Vodafone shares jump 13% as e& exits $6bn stake, lifting FTSE 100

Vodafone's Newbury headquarters, days after e& agreed to sell its 16% stake to French billionaire Xavier Niel — Matt Wakeman / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Xavier Niel's Vega bought e&'s entire 16% Vodafone stake for $6bn at a premium, sending shares up 13% and lifting the FTSE 100.

Vodafone Group shares jumped almost 13% in London on Friday, their sharpest single-day move in months, after the UAE telecoms group e& agreed to sell its entire stake in the company for roughly $6 billion. The buyer is not a rival operator or a private equity consortium, but a single French billionaire: Xavier Niel, the founder of Iliad, who now becomes Vodafone's largest shareholder.

The move sits at the odd intersection of an exit and an endorsement. A major investor is walking away with cash in hand, and the stock is rallying anyway — which tells you more about who's buying than who's selling.

The mechanics of the deal

e& agreed to sell its full 16.21% holding in Vodafone — just under 3.95 billion shares, representing 17.13% of total voting rights — to Vega, an investment vehicle controlled by Niel's family. The agreed price was 112.5 pence per share, a roughly 13-15% premium to Thursday's closing price of 97.76p, putting the total consideration at about £4.4 billion, or $5.95 billion. The package includes cash plus a final dividend of 2.02p per share that Vodafone is due to pay on July 30.

For e&, the sale closes out a position it built over four years. The Emirati group bought an initial 9.8% stake in 2022 for about $4.4 billion as part of a stated ambition to become a global telecoms and technology investor, then quietly added to it. On Friday the company framed the exit as "actively managing its investment portfolio while creating value for shareholders" — corporate language for taking a premium and moving on. Analysts read it more bluntly as e& stepping back from that global-platform ambition and banking a gain instead.

Why a billionaire buying looks like a vote of confidence

The reason the stock didn't just tread water on the news — the reason it jumped — is the identity of the buyer. Niel is not a passive allocator. He built Iliad into France's disruptive fourth mobile carrier in 2012 and now holds telecom assets across nine European countries, including Ireland's eir, with a combined base of roughly 50 million subscribers and more than €10 billion in annual revenue. Buying into Vodafone gives him simultaneous exposure to two of Europe's largest markets, the UK and Germany, where Vodafone has struggled with subscriber losses and a stretched balance sheet.

Niel said in a statement that Vodafone was "a compelling investment opportunity, underpinned by quality assets, strong brands, leadership positions and a diversified geographic footprint," and that Vega was "ready to contribute our deep sector expertise and operational know-how." Vega separately said it intends to be "a committed, long-term shareholder," and — pre-empting the obvious next question — stated it does not intend to make an offer for the rest of Vodafone's share capital. Vodafone, for its part, said it would engage with the Niel family group as a supportive, long-term shareholder.

That framing matters for how the market is pricing the deal. A 16% stake changing hands at a premium is one thing; a buyer with an operating track record in the exact markets where Vodafone is under the most pressure is another. Morgan Stanley flagged that the real signal to watch is not the stake itself but how involved Niel's team becomes in Vodafone's operations, particularly in Germany, where the group's cable business has been a persistent drag on earnings.

A calmer FTSE, for once

The rally in Vodafone was large enough to move the whole London market. The FTSE 100 closed up 24.84 points, or 0.2%, at 10,497.29 on Friday, with Vodafone — up 12.3p to 110.1p — doing most of the lifting alongside a jump in easyJet on a separate takeover approach from Apollo. Traders described the session as calmer than recent ones, a notable shift after weeks in which the index has been jostled by Middle East tensions and rate speculation. A single stock-specific catalyst producing an orderly, well-defined index gain is itself a small tell: when the market can trace a move to one transaction rather than macro noise, it tends to hold.

What to watch

The transaction still needs regulatory sign-off, expected to conclude by the end of the year, and Vodafone's 2.02p dividend lands on July 30 regardless of how the stake-sale timeline plays out. The more interesting date sits further out: whenever Niel's team starts showing up in Vodafone's German restructuring conversations, or pushes for board representation, the market will get its first real read on whether this was a passive portfolio play or the opening move in something more active. Until then, the 13% jump stands as the market's best guess — that a telecoms operator with a track record of turning around distressed carriers just put $6 billion behind the idea that Vodafone's problems are fixable, not terminal.
