MoD papers say UK police should have kept the airbase trial of US personnel

In one sentence
Ministry of Defence papers reportedly indicate UK police should have kept jurisdiction over a trial involving US personnel at a British airbase, raising fresh questions over handovers.

Ministry of Defence documents indicate that British police should have kept a criminal case involving US personnel at a UK airbase, rather than letting American military authorities take it over, according to the Guardian. That would turn a jurisdictional handover, usually treated as routine, into a question about whether the decision was made correctly. The Tribune has not seen the papers. The details below rest on that single report and on earlier coverage of similar cases.

What is claimed

The Guardian's account, as summarised, is that the documents point to UK police having been entitled to retain jurisdiction over the trial. A US force then took it on. The case, the defendant, the base, the charges and the dates of the papers could not be confirmed independently. No government response has been located. Any reading of the papers beyond that one-line finding would be guesswork.

Why the handover matters

US forces in Britain operate under the 1951 NATO Status of Forces Agreement. In broad terms, it gives the US the primary right to prosecute its personnel for offences committed on duty, or against US property or dependants. In other cases, UK authorities have the first call. The US can ask for jurisdiction, and the UK can agree. The system relies on British police judging, case by case, whether to keep an investigation or let it go.

That judgment has been under scrutiny since June. Reporting then described how Cambridgeshire police let the US Air Force take over an investigation into pilot Jacob Wulfson. He was later convicted at a court martial at RAF Lakenheath in Suffolk of strangling a woman, Sarah Steele, and acquitted of other charges. He received six months' detention and was dismissed from service. Steele told the Guardian that the force did not consult her about the handover. A US Air Force spokesperson said it had "negotiated jurisdiction over this case with the local police using all available facts at the time."

The government and police chiefs have since opened a review of why serious cases involving US personnel have been transferred. Deputy Prime Minister David Lammy has pressed Washington for an explanation. It is not established that the newly reported MoD papers concern the Wulfson case. The material found does not say.

What would change if the papers hold

Until now, ministers have been able to describe handovers as decisions for operationally independent police forces. Government papers saying the police were entitled to keep a case would complicate that. The question would shift from whether a force exercised its discretion to whether it understood its own position, and whether the MoD, which manages the UK side of visiting-forces arrangements, advised it properly.

The next markers are practical. One is whether the Guardian publishes or the government confirms the documents. Another is whether the existing review takes them into account. A third is whether police are told to take Crown Prosecution Service advice before ceding any case, a step ministers have already urged.
