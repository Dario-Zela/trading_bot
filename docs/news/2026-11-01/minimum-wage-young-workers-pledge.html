Ministers face claims minimum wage plans for young workers break a pledge

In one sentence
Ministers face claims that planned minimum wage rises for young workers fall short of a Labour pledge to equalise pay, easing the cost for retailers and hospitality firms.

The government promised to end the lower minimum wage for young workers. Planned changes to pay rates for that group may now fall short of that promise, and ministers are being accused of breaking it. If they do, retailers, pubs and restaurants, which employ many of the affected workers, would not face the full cost increase that equalisation implies.

The row turns on a manifesto commitment to remove age bands from the minimum wage so that younger adults are paid the same as older ones. At present the statutory rate for 18-to-20-year-olds is £10.85 an hour, against £12.71 for those aged 21 and over, a gap of £1.86. Reports this week say the youth rate is due to rise by about 3.9%, to roughly £11.27. Those figures are as reported and have not been checked against an official announcement. On that arithmetic, the gap would narrow only slightly and would remain well above £1 an hour.

A pledge without a date

The government's defence rests on timing. Torsten Bell, a junior minister, told BBC radio: "We're committed to our manifesto... but that manifesto did not set out the timeline." He said voters who read it as a first-term promise were making "an understandable mistake".

Unions are unconvinced. The GMB's line is that this is a manifesto promise and that young people should not be paid less for the same work. Earlier this year ministers were reported to be considering dropping the equalisation pledge altogether, citing record youth unemployment. Ministers have not confirmed that, and the position now seems to be a slower pace rather than abandonment.

Who carries the cost

Employers in retail and hospitality argue that rising payroll costs make them more cautious about hiring inexperienced staff, and they have pointed to youth unemployment as the consequence. Those sectors employ a large share of under-21s, so the pace of any narrowing matters to them more than to most. A slower path leaves their labour bill lower than full equalisation would. A faster one would add to costs already raised by earlier increases in employer National Insurance and in the adult wage floor.

That is also why the dispute has limited direct market relevance. No costings or sector earnings estimates have been published alongside the reports. The effect on listed retailers and hospitality groups is therefore a matter of direction, not size.

What to watch

The test is the formal announcement of the youth rate for April, expected alongside the wider minimum wage decision. It will show whether ministers set a date for equalisation or only a smaller step. A date would answer the charge of a broken pledge, and its absence would sharpen it. Unions and business groups will also use the Low Pay Commission's evidence on youth employment to argue their cases.
