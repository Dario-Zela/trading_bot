Cost-of-living protests in Germany turn to looting

In one sentence
Looters hit shops in Frankfurt after largely peaceful youth cost-of-living marches in 34 German cities, prompting officials to warn of French-style unrest.

Shops were looted in Frankfurt on Saturday night after a day of largely peaceful, youth-led demonstrations over the cost of living. The protests ran across Germany, and the violence there was limited. It still gave Europe's largest economy an unfamiliar public-order headache.

What happened

The marches were called by a campaign group, Gen Z regelt, and took place in 34 cities, among them Berlin, Frankfurt and Munich. The organisers' demands were broad. They covered underfunding in schools, the high cost of living, public spending cuts and military service. For most of the day the demonstrations were orderly.

In Frankfurt that changed after dark. At least two stores were looted and several more damaged, according to early reports. One of the targets was a gun shop. Police in Berlin, Cologne and Hamburg also reported violence. Water cannon were used to disperse protesters, and in Hamburg some barricades were set on fire.

The figures are thin. The reports so far give no reliable count of arrests, injuries or damage, and they were all filed on the day. The scale should be treated as provisional.

The official reading

The political response was quick, and it looked abroad. Hesse's interior minister, Roman Poseck, pointed to France: "We all have those images from France in our minds — looting, burning houses, burning police vehicles, and attacks on police officers." That is a comparison with last month's French unrest, which Berlin itself condemned. It shows how worried officials are about the pattern spreading.

The federal interior ministry said parts of Germany's far-left extremist scene, and some groups linked to foreign extremist movements, had joined efforts to mobilise support. That suggests officials see the violence as the work of a militant fringe attached to a wider movement, not of the organisers. The reports contain no independent confirmation of who did the looting.

The student movement has also begun to split. The national school student umbrella organisation, BSK, has distanced itself from the initiative. It plans its own day of protest on 20 November.

Why it matters

By the standard of the past decade, this was not a large episode: a handful of looted shops in one city, set against 34 marches. The significance lies in the setting. Germany has largely been spared the street violence that has periodically hit France and Britain. A price squeeze there has mostly produced strikes, petitions and orderly marches.

The composition of the crowd also matters. These were young people whose grievances run from school budgets to conscription, and the cost of living is only one item on the list. That makes the movement harder for any government to answer with a single policy, such as an energy-price measure. Looting by a minority may also hand ministers an easy way to change the subject from the demands to law and order.

What to watch

The first test is the official tally. Arrest numbers and damage costs from Frankfurt, Hamburg and Berlin will show whether Saturday was an isolated flare-up or the start of a pattern. The second is whether the organisers condemn the violence. The BSK's separate protest on 20 November will show how much of the student movement stays on the streets, and whether it does so peacefully.
