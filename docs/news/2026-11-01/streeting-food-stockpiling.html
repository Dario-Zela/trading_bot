Streeting says he began stockpiling food weeks ago

In one sentence
Defence secretary Wes Streeting says he began stockpiling food and water weeks ago to lead by example, but he named no specific threat behind the advice.

Wes Streeting, the defence secretary, has said he began stockpiling food and water several weeks ago, and that he did it partly to set an example. The disclosure, reported by the Guardian on Saturday, comes as the government urges households to think about their own resilience. It names no specific threat.

What he has put aside

According to the Guardian's account, Streeting keeps bottled water, a first aid kit and a battery-powered torch. His cupboards hold baked beans, sweetcorn, red kidney beans and tuna, along with protein bars and nuts. He said he started "several weeks" ago in order to "lead by example".

He added: "I'm glad that people are beginning to think about their own household resilience." The list is the sort of thing civil-contingency advice has long suggested: shelf-stable food, water and basic light and first aid. It contains nothing unusual.

The threat, such as it is

The Guardian piece does not point to an imminent event or a particular adversary. Streeting frames the broader task in the familiar terms of deterrence: to deter adversaries and, "should deterrence fail, to be ready to defeat them". The personal stockpile appears as an illustration of that mood, not as a response to a warning.

Earlier remarks give some context. The Farmers Guardian reported on 23 October that Streeting had warned Britain must prepare for food shortages and supply-chain disruption, citing cyber attacks from hostile states and, according to its subheading, climate shocks. Its headline said he had been accused of scaremongering. Its full text is paywalled, so who made that accusation is not established here. Other outlets have described a government "Be Ready" checklist built around a three-day disruption. That detail comes from secondary reports, and the Guardian article as reproduced does not mention it.

So the signal is real but loose. A cabinet minister is publicly describing his own preparations and encouraging others to follow. That is a policy position. It is not evidence of any new intelligence about a specific plot.

The money behind the message

The remarks sit alongside a spending argument. The Guardian reports that Chancellor John Healey wants defence spending at 3% of GDP by 2030 and 3.5% by 2035, with Whitehall departments asked to find 1% savings to help pay for it. The detailed route to 3% is due at next year's spending review. Streeting, who took over the defence brief after Healey left it in June, said that "spending more money badly is not virtuous", a line aimed as much at the Ministry of Defence's own procurement record as at the Treasury.

Seen that way, a defence secretary talking about tinned tuna serves a purpose. Public acceptance of higher military spending is easier to win if the threat feels close to home. Critics will say the same logic is what makes "scaremongering" an easy charge.

What to watch

The test is whether the government attaches anything concrete to the rhetoric: a named risk assessment, updated official household guidance, or a supply-chain resilience plan with dates. The next fixed point is the spending review, where Healey's path to 3% will have to be costed. Until then, the story is a minister's pantry and a mood, not a disclosed threat.
