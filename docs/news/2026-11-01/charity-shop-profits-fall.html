BEYOND THE TAPE · HIGH STREET · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-11-01 · 3 min read

Charity shop profits slump as resale moves online

In one sentence
UK charity retailers are reported to be earning sharply less as online resale apps pull the best donations away, though the scale of the fall is unconfirmed.

The charity shop is one of the high street's more durable institutions. It has outlasted department stores, travel agents and most of the banks. Its business model is simple: people give away clothes, volunteers sort and price them, and the proceeds go to a hospice, a cancer charity or an animal rescue. That model is now reported to be under strain, as the same wardrobe-clearing instinct is increasingly satisfied by selling online.

The Guardian reports that profits at UK charity retailers have fallen sharply, and it points to two causes: resale platforms and changes in how people donate. The summary available to this desk gave no figures, named charities or underlying report. The scale of the fall, and which charities are worst hit, could not be independently checked, so this piece does not put a number on it.

A model built on free stock

The economics have always rested on one unusual advantage: the stock costs nothing. A charity shop pays no wholesale bill for its jumpers and jeans, and much of its labour is unpaid. What it does pay for is rent, rates, energy and a small core of paid managers. The margin between those costs and what a £4 shirt fetches funds the charity's actual work.

That arrangement works only if the donations are good. A rail of faded polyester sells slowly, while a decent coat or a recognisable high-street label moves fast. This is where the pressure appears to come from.

The best items are going elsewhere

Apps such as Vinted, Depop and eBay have given households a reason to hold on to anything with resale value. A donor who once dropped off a bag of unwanted clothes can now photograph the best pieces, list them and be paid within days. What remains for the charity shop tends to be the lower-value material: plenty of volume, little margin.

This is the logic behind the Guardian's framing, though the mechanism is not proven here. Without the retailers' own accounts, it is not possible to say how much of any fall in profit comes from weaker donations, how much from higher running costs, and how much from shoppers themselves moving online. Those are different problems with different remedies, and the headline cause may be only one of several.

Many charities have not stood still. Several large chains already run their own online shops or sell selected items through third-party marketplaces, and some sort donations to separate the valuable from the ordinary. Those steps add cost and skill to an operation designed around volunteers and bin bags.

What is at stake

The effect on charities depends on how much they rely on their shops. For some, retail is a sideline to fundraising appeals and legacies. For others, it is the single largest source of unrestricted income, the money that can be spent wherever the need is greatest. A weakening there is hard to replace quickly.

The backdrop is a familiar one. The high street is still being reshaped by online buying, as the story of how Poundland grew from a market stall to a high-street giant reminds readers. Charity shops had seemed to sit outside that story, protected by a supply of free goods and a loyal customer base. If resale platforms are now eating into that supply, the protection is thinner than it looked.

The next evidence will be the annual accounts of the larger chains, along with any figures from the Charity Retail Association, the trade body. Those should show whether the decline is broad or concentrated, and whether online sales are offsetting any of the loss in shop income.
