Binance says it will cover 90% of losses after a reported $6.3bn hack, with North Korea-linked groups the prime suspects

In one sentence
Binance is reported to be covering about 90% of user losses from a $6.3bn theft blamed on North Korea-linked hackers, but the figure, pledge and attribution are all unverified.

Binance has said it will cover about 90% of user losses after a theft reported at $6.3bn, according to BBC reports, with North Korea-linked hacking groups named as the prime suspects. None of this has been independently confirmed. The $6.3bn figure, the 90% coverage pledge and the attribution all rest on a small number of BBC reports, and our own checks could not corroborate them. What follows treats each as a claim, not a fact.

What is being reported

The outline is simple. A theft of roughly $6.3bn is said to have hit Binance, and the exchange is said to be promising to make users whole for about nine-tenths of what they lost. The BBC's World, Business and Technology desks have carried versions of the story. The technology report is the one that names suspects.

The gaps are large. The mechanism of the theft has not been established in anything we could verify. Nor have its date and the precise population of affected users. It is also unclear what the remaining 10% means in practice: whether it is a haircut borne by customers, a deductible, or a figure still to be finalised.

The size is the first reason for caution. At $6.3bn, this would be more than four times the largest crypto theft on record that we can cite with confidence, the roughly $1.5bn taken from the exchange Bybit in February 2025. That does not make the figure wrong. It does mean a number of this order needs a primary confirmation, from the exchange or from on-chain analysts who can trace the funds, before it is treated as settled.

Can a backstop of this size hold?

The more interesting question, if the report is accurate, is financial. Exchanges that hold customer assets typically point to some form of reserve or insurance fund as the answer to a hack. Those funds were sized for incidents measured in tens or hundreds of millions of dollars. Covering 90% of a $6.3bn loss would mean finding about $5.7bn, on the reported figures, from the exchange's own balance sheet.

Whether Binance can do that is not something the available reporting answers. The arithmetic shows what is at stake. The pledge is only as good as the assets behind it, and the market will want to see them: a proof-of-reserves statement, a timeline for repayment, and clarity on whether payments are in cash, in the stolen tokens or in exchange credit. A promise to cover losses that arrives without those details is a statement of intent, not a guarantee.

There is also a precedent worth noting. After the Bybit theft, the exchange said it would make customers whole, and it did so by borrowing and sourcing replacement assets quickly. Whether a similar approach scales more than fourfold is the open question. A coverage promise of 90% rather than 100% also suggests, at minimum, that the exchange has judged full cover to be out of reach or unwise. That is an inference from the reported figure, not something the exchange has been quoted as saying.

Why North Korea is the first name on the list

The attribution follows a pattern. Groups linked to North Korea have been blamed for the largest crypto thefts of recent years, and investigators tend to name them early when a case involves a very large sum.

The US government attributed the 2022 theft from the Ronin Network bridge, about $615m, to the Lazarus Group. The FBI attributed the Bybit theft to North Korean actors. Both cases involved sophisticated compromises of the systems that manage keys or approvals, rather than a flaw in a single smart contract. Both were followed by rapid laundering through chains of swaps and mixers. These details are from memory and were not re-checked for this article.

The pattern matters for two reasons. First, it shapes how regulators respond: a theft attributed to a state-linked group is treated as a sanctions and national-security matter as well as a consumer-protection one, which changes who investigates and how recovery efforts are coordinated. Second, it shapes what exchanges can realistically recover. Funds taken by state-linked actors have rarely been returned in meaningful amounts.

None of that proves who did this. The BBC describes North Korea-linked groups as the prime suspects. That is a hypothesis, and it is single-sourced. Formal attribution has usually come from US agencies days or weeks after an incident, and from blockchain analysis firms sooner. Neither has been seen here.

What would settle it

Three things would turn a reported story into a confirmed one. The first is a statement from Binance itself giving the loss figure and the terms of the 90% pledge. The second is on-chain evidence: traceable wallets holding or moving the stolen funds, which analytics firms normally publish within hours. The third is a government attribution, most likely from the FBI, naming the actor.

Until those arrive, the claim that Binance can absorb a loss of this size remains untested, and the figure itself remains unverified.
