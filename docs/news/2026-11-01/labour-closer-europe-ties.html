Senior Labour figures argue Britain's future lies closer to Europe

In one sentence
Labour's senior figures, now led by PM Andy Burnham, are openly arguing for closer EU ties, but no policy or market move has yet followed.

Britain's political argument about Europe has moved a long way from where Labour began in government. The idea that the UK's future lies closer to the EU is now voiced openly by the party's most senior figures. That includes the prime minister, though the prime minister is no longer Keir Starmer, as some of the reporting behind this story still implies.

Who is saying what

The framing that Rachel Reeves is Chancellor and Starmer is Prime Minister is out of date. Starmer was ousted by Labour MPs in July, and Andy Burnham became prime minister. Both of the earlier statements are still on the record.

In May, as he fought calls to resign, Starmer pledged to put Britain at the heart of Europe. In July, in what was widely expected to be her last major speech as chancellor, Reeves told the Mansion House that no alliance mattered more than the one with the EU. She said Britain should be "much bolder" in pursuing closer ties. She had called the EU "the biggest prize" for UK trade in February. Reports suggest she will not stay on under Burnham, though I could not confirm any Cabinet appointment.

The sharpest statement has come from Burnham himself. At the party conference at the end of September he said Brexit had done "more harm than good" and had brought a decade of low growth. He put several options on the table, including the single market and the customs union. He said Labour could in time go as far as proposing full membership, after weighing the options.

What has actually changed

So far, rhetoric has moved faster than policy. Burnham is still bound by the 2024 manifesto, which ruled out rejoining the customs union or single market and ruled out free movement. His remarks suggest those red lines may not last. Stella Creasy, a Labour MP, welcomed the move as the removal of the red lines. The analyst Anand Menon cautioned that Burnham had only proposed starting a conversation.

Opponents see a back door. Reform UK's Richard Tice accused Burnham of trying to return Britain to Brussels by stealth, and the Conservatives called him backward-looking. A Survation poll in June found 55% of voters would back rejoining in a new referendum, which helps explain why the party is willing to say it.

The claim that this is tied to a shift in the US relationship is plausible but, in the sources I found, not attributed to any specific US policy or event. The market angle is similarly unproven. I found no reported move in gilt yields or sterling in response to these remarks. The 10-year yield's position near 5.4%, covered elsewhere in this edition, is driven mainly by the Budget and the fiscal outlook, not by Europe.

What to watch

The nearest concrete test is the UK-EU summit. It was due in Brussels in July but was postponed when Starmer fell. An EU official has said the bloc is aiming for November, though no date is confirmed. The agenda covers food and animal-health standards, a youth mobility scheme and the linking of emissions-trading systems. Talks have reportedly stalled over British concerns about the EU's "Made in Europe" industrial plans. A signed agreement, or a rewrite of the manifesto's red lines, would turn a tone shift into policy. Until one of those happens, markets have little reason to reprice it.
