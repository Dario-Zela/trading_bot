Analysis: how Vučić's fall is said to mirror Orbán's

In one sentence
Orbán lost power in Hungary in April and Vučić conceded defeat in Serbia's 25 October election, but the two cases differ in how each man fell.

Two of Europe's longest-serving strongmen have now been voted out within seven months of each other. Hungary's Viktor Orbán lost power in April after 16 years. Serbia's Aleksandar Vučić conceded defeat on 25 October, when the student-backed Student List won the snap parliamentary election. The comparison drawn by analysts is real, but the two cases differ in one important respect: Vučić has lost his party's grip on government, not his name from the ballot paper.

What has actually happened

Orbán's Fidesz had governed Hungary since 2010. In April it lost to Péter Magyar, and Vučić publicly congratulated the winner. Analysts quickly noted what the loss meant for Belgrade. Tonino Picula, a European Parliament rapporteur, said Vučić and the Bosnian Serb leader Milorad Dodik had lost a strong patron. The Atlantic Council argued that Orbán had cast himself as the sponsor of like-minded governments such as Vučić's.

Vučić's own exit came in stages. In June he said he would step down as president, which he was barred from seeking a third term in. He resigned on 27 September, about eight months before his term was due to end, to run for prime minister. Parliament Speaker Ana Brnabić became interim president. Meduza described the move as an attempt to stay in power by changing office.

That plan failed at the polls. Preliminary results gave the Student List about 52.7% of the vote and roughly 140 of 250 seats, with the SNS-led list on about 39.2% and 104 seats. Counting of votes cast abroad shifted the totals slightly, to 141 and 103. Turnout was reported at about 69%. On election night Vučić said: "It is clear that we have lost these elections." He blamed independent media and alleged foreign interference. The figures are preliminary and sources differ slightly.

Where the parallel holds

The pattern is that both men built systems around a dominant party and a loyal media, and both were beaten by an opponent who built a broad coalition outside the old opposition. Magyar came from inside Fidesz's orbit. Serbia's students had no established party. Their list was shaped by university students, though none of them stood as candidates.

The trigger in Serbia is a matter of record. The movement grew from the collapse of a canopy at Novi Sad railway station on 1 November 2024, which killed 16 people and was widely blamed on corruption. Today is the second anniversary of that disaster. In Hungary, voters were reported to have punished corruption, a sluggish economy and rising inflation. In both countries, the complaint was about how the state functioned, not only about who ran it.

There is also a regional link. Orbán was Vučić's most useful ally in the EU, shielding Belgrade from pressure in Brussels. Political scientist Florian Bieber said Vučić lost a key EU ally. Some reports, unconfirmed, say Orbán helped persuade Vučić to concede. No independently verified evidence supports claims that Orbán stopped a coup attempt.

Where it breaks down

The cases are not identical. Hungary's change came through a conventional multi-party campaign. Serbia's came after two years of street protest, and observers cited deficiencies in the process and a failure to ensure free and fair conditions. Vučić and the SNS deny the accusations. The student movement also now has to govern, having never done so.

What to watch

The immediate tests are procedural. A presidential election must be held within 90 days of Vučić's resignation, which means a vote by late December. The new majority must then form a government and decide how quickly to dismantle the SNS's hold on state institutions. The EU, which has had an uneasy relationship with Belgrade, will also have to decide how to treat a government that campaigned against corruption but has no record in office. Whether Serbia's change lasts as long as the system it replaced is what will decide if the Orbán comparison holds.
