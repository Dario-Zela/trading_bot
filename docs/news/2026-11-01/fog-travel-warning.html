Thick fog brings hazardous driving into next week

In one sentence
Forecasters warn widespread fog may keep driving hazardous into next week, but no official alert level, regions or incidents have been confirmed.

Forecasters are warning that widespread fog will make driving hazardous across parts of the UK and that the disruption could continue into next week. The warning is a forecast. No crashes, closures or cancellations have been reported alongside it.

What is known

The warning comes from BBC UK's weather coverage. It says fog is widespread and that hazardous driving conditions are expected to persist beyond the weekend. Checks against other sources did not turn up more detail. The warning does not give:

  • the regions most affected;
  • the visibility levels forecast;
  • the dates on which conditions are expected to improve;
  • whether the Met Office has issued a formal alert, or at what level.

That last point matters. The Met Office uses a colour-coded system, and a yellow warning for fog means some disruption is possible but the conditions are not considered life-threatening. An amber warning would mean a higher likelihood of delays and cancellations. Until a warning is published on the Met Office's warnings page, "into next week" describes a general expectation, not an official finding.

Why fog is a road hazard

Fog is defined as visibility below 1,000 metres, and forecasters call it dense when visibility falls below 200 metres. At those distances drivers have little time to react to slow or stationary traffic. The usual advice is to cut speed, use dipped headlights, leave a larger gap and avoid sudden braking. Fog patches tend to be uneven, so visibility can drop sharply over a short stretch of road, which is part of what makes it dangerous.

Fog also affects more than roads. Low visibility can slow flights and, on some routes, rail services. The warning does not say whether any of that is expected this time.

Why the duration matters

Overnight fog that lifts by mid-morning is a routine autumn event, and for most commuters it means a slower journey. It becomes a larger story only if it lingers through several days, covers a wide area and prompts official warnings. The forecast points to that possibility without confirming it. Persistent fog often reflects stable, high-pressure conditions that hold moist air near the ground. The warning does not describe the weather pattern behind it, so how long it lasts is uncertain.

What to watch

The next update to the Met Office's warnings page and the regional forecasts due on Monday morning will show whether any alert has been issued and for which areas. Motorists planning early journeys can check those and local traffic reports before setting off. If a yellow or amber warning appears for several consecutive days, the story changes from a weather brief to a travel-disruption story. If the fog lifts and no warning is issued, it stays a routine seasonal hazard.
