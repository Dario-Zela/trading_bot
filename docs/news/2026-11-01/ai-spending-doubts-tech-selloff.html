AI doubts widen: Alphabet's $500bn drop, near-$1tn of capex and murky suppliers weigh on US tech

In one sentence
Alphabet lost over $500bn in value after results pointed to AI search summaries hurting ad sales while AI spending pushed free cash flow negative, feeding wider tech doubts.

Alphabet lost more than $500bn of market value in a day this week, in the sharpest single-session fall in the company's history by that measure. The cause was not a vague worry about the future. It was a set of quarterly results that suggested Google's own AI-generated search summaries are already costing it advertising money, at the same moment its AI spending is turning free cash flow negative.

That sequence matters for how the episode should be read. The fall has been described as a sentiment move, with investors pricing a risk that AI answers will displace paid links. The reporting available points to something more concrete: a company that disclosed pressure on ad sales, a profit decline and a cash outflow in the same release.

What Alphabet reported

Alphabet shares fell nearly 13% to a six-month low after the results, according to Anadolu Agency, taking market capitalisation from $3.97tn to $3.46tn. The fall was reported on 30 October.

The headline numbers were mixed rather than weak:

  • Quarterly revenue rose 25% year on year to $127.6bn.
  • Net profit fell 25%.
  • Free cash flow turned negative, with outflows of more than $9bn, which Anadolu attributes to surging AI investment.
  • YouTube advertising sales of $11.2bn missed projections, according to coverage of the BBC's report.

The search-specific claim is that Google's default AI summaries of queries are reducing advertising sales. Philipp Schindler, Alphabet's chief business officer, is reported as saying that the company's "AI Mode" is eating into the money made from ads on conventional results. Neither source gives a figure for search advertising revenue or its growth rate, and that is the number the thesis ultimately rests on. Without it, the scale of any cannibalisation cannot be measured from what has been published so far.

Kim Scott, a former Google ads executive quoted by the BBC, argued that Google would rather cannibalise its own search business than let rivals do it. Tracy Woo of Forrester said the results show the difficulty of funding very large AI investment while protecting established businesses.

The spending side of the ledger

The second thread is the cost of the build-out. Alphabet's cash outflow arrived alongside weak free cash flow at Amazon, which reported sales up 11% to $201bn but free cash flow of negative $42bn, according to Anadolu. Amazon shares fell about 6% in after-hours trading. Microsoft also updated investors the same week and kept spending heavily.

A BBC Business headline put the combined AI capital expenditure of US tech firms at close to $1tn and linked it to a sharp fall in tech shares. The underlying breakdown by company, and the percentage declines on the day, could not be confirmed from the material reviewed, so the $1tn figure should be read as the BBC's characterisation rather than a verified sum. Earlier this year Alphabet was reported to have earmarked $175bn to $185bn for capital expenditure in 2026, mostly for AI data centres.

The arithmetic of the worry is simple. Spending is rising faster than free cash flow can absorb it, and the revenue meant to repay it, in Alphabet's case search advertising, is the line the new technology may erode. Revenue growth of 25% shows the franchise is not shrinking. The market's objection is to what it costs to grow and what the growth is made of.

Murkier corners

A third BBC Technology report, on little-known firms said to be feeding fears of an AI bubble, points to thinly disclosed suppliers and financing vehicles at the edges of the build-out. The article text was not retrieved, and no company names or amounts are confirmed here. The general concern is nonetheless familiar: when the large buyers of AI hardware and data-centre capacity spend at this scale, the credit and counterparty risk sits with smaller entities whose finances are harder to inspect. A separate Benzinga item said the S&P 500's rally had stalled on AI valuation worries, but it named no gauge or index level and could not be corroborated.

What to watch

The test is in the next filings. Alphabet's detailed segment disclosures, in particular search and other advertising revenue growth against its recent trend, will show whether AI summaries are shrinking the ad business or merely changing its shape. Capital expenditure guidance and the direction of free cash flow will show how long the outflows persist. Until then, the roughly $500bn fall prices a risk that Alphabet's own results have begun to make visible but have not yet quantified.
