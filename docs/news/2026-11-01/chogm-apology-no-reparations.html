Burnham open to a formal slavery apology but rules out reparations as the King speaks in Antigua

In one sentence
Ahead of the King's CHOGM speech in Antigua, Prime Minister Burnham says he is open to a formal slavery apology but rules out the reparations Caribbean states are pressing for.

Britain is edging toward a formal apology for its part in the transatlantic slave trade, and it is doing so while closing the door on the payments that Caribbean governments want most. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has said he is open to an apology. He has ruled out reparations. The remarks come as the King prepares to speak in Antigua, where Commonwealth leaders gather for their biennial summit, known as CHOGM.

The distinction is the central fault line between London and much of the Caribbean. It will shape how the summit's debate on slavery lands.

A line drawn in advance

The government has not set out the wording, timing or form of any apology. Burnham's position, as reported, is therefore a statement of direction rather than a commitment. Even so, the sequencing matters. By signalling a willingness to apologise and a refusal to pay before the summit opens, ministers have tried to set the terms of the argument before Caribbean leaders can.

The refusal on reparations is not new. The long-standing UK position is that the slave trade was abhorrent and that its effects are still felt, but that Britain will not pay compensation for acts that did not break domestic law at the time. One Foreign Office source was reported as saying there is no legal case for reparations because slavery was lawful when it was practised. What has shifted is the apology, which previous governments have also avoided. Burnham's openness to one is the movement here.

The King in Antigua

The King's speech comes at the end of a nine-day Caribbean tour that began in the Bahamas. Buckingham Palace said the tour would not shy away from "the darkest days of our past". Ahead of the trip, the King was expected to acknowledge the "great suffering of the past", and he confirmed that Commonwealth leaders will discuss the legacy of slavery at the summit. Antigua's CHOGM runs from 1 to 4 November. The text of his speech had not been published at the time of writing.

Palace insiders had suggested an apology from the King himself was unlikely. That fits constitutional practice. The King acts on ministerial advice, and the government had not advised him to offer an apology or reparations. The Prime Minister's comments change that calculation only if ministers choose to act on them, and an apology delivered by a government would carry a different weight from one delivered by a monarch. At the previous CHOGM, in 2024, the King stopped short of an official apology and did not engage directly with the subject.

What the Caribbean wants

The pressure comes chiefly from CARICOM, the Caribbean community of states, which said it would push to put reparations on the summit agenda. Its argument is that acknowledgement without material redress treats a continuing harm as a closed chapter. For these governments an apology is a starting point and, if it comes without money, may be read as a way of ending the conversation.

The Commonwealth's own secretary-general, Shirley Botchwey, has said an apology "wouldn't hurt". The remark is mild, but it comes from the head of the organisation hosting the debate, and it suggests the Secretariat sees value in the gesture even if it will not satisfy the most insistent members.

There is a reason the apology is the easier concession. It has no direct fiscal cost and no obvious legal consequence, though governments often worry that an admission might later be used in claims. Reparations imply budgets, precedents and a long list of other historical grievances. Ministers also face a Treasury with little spare room, a point made elsewhere in this edition's coverage of gilt yields and the Budget.

What to watch

Two things will show how far the position has moved. The first is the wording of the King's speech: whether it acknowledges the suffering of the past in general terms or addresses the trade and its legacy more directly. The second is the summit communiqué. Caribbean states will want language on reparatory justice, and Britain will resist anything that implies a commitment to pay. If the communiqué records a divergence, the apology will have narrowed the gap in tone and left the dispute over money untouched.

The longer-term test is whether Burnham's openness becomes a formal act, with a date, a venue and a text. Until then the government has offered the Caribbean a gesture it has not yet made, in exchange for a demand it has already declined.
