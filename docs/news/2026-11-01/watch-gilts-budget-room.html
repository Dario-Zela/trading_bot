Gilts and rates: 10-year yield near 5.4% keeps Budget room tight

In one sentence
The 10-year gilt yield stayed above 5% in October, near 5.4% and over two points above the FTSE 100's dividend yield, squeezing the Treasury's room before the Budget.

The cost of lending to the British government has stopped being a background number. The 10-year gilt yield was near 5.4% at the end of October and, on the figures cited here, stayed above 5% throughout the month. That is the setting for the Burnham government's Budget: the sums are being done around what debt costs, not around what ministers would like to spend.

The yield and the dividend

Two figures carry the argument, and both come from single sources that have not been independently checked.

The first is the yield itself. Trading Economics, which tracks the benchmark 10-year gilt, put it near 5.4% and above 5% for all of October 2026. A yield is the annual return an investor locks in by buying the bond at today's price. It is also, roughly, the rate at which the Treasury must refinance as older, cheaper debt matures.

The second is the comparison. AJ Bell's Q3 2026 dividend dashboard, as cited in the discovery notes, has the 10-year gilt yield exceeding the FTSE 100's dividend yield by more than two percentage points in October. If the gilt sits at about 5.4%, that implies a dividend yield of roughly 3.4% or lower. That is the writer's arithmetic, not a published number, and it moves with each day's price.

The gap matters because the usual trade-off has flipped for income seekers. A government bond is a near-certain payment. A share dividend is a choice made by a board. When the safe asset pays two points more than the risky one, the pull on savers and pension funds is towards gilts. That demand can help the Treasury sell its debt. It also shows how much the state now pays to borrow.

What it does to the Budget

Higher yields feed straight into the public finances. New borrowing is priced at the prevailing rate, and maturing debt is rolled over at that rate too. Every extra tenth of a percentage point across a debt stock of this size is a recurring bill, one that competes with tax cuts and departmental spending for the same headroom.

That is the sense in which the Budget is being drafted around debt service. The headroom against the government's fiscal rules is the figure that decides what is affordable, and a higher yield shrinks it. The Budget date and the precise rules in play were not confirmed in the material available for this piece, so neither is stated here. The direction of travel needs no confirmation: with yields at this level, any new commitment has to be paid for by tax rises or cuts elsewhere.

What is not established

The wider context is also less certain. Reports that the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank raised rates this autumn, and that markets expect more, could not be checked: no meeting dates, sizes of move or market pricing were available. Nor were the date of the Bank of England's next decision or the current Bank Rate. If such tightening has happened, it would add upward pressure on gilt yields and narrow the Bank's room to hold rates steady. That is conditional, and this piece does not rely on it.

The core claim stands on the two sourced figures alone. Both are single-source, and both are worth re-checking against the Bank of England and Debt Management Office series before they are treated as settled.

What to watch

The next test is the gilt market's reaction on the day of the Budget statement, and in particular whether the 10-year yield falls back below 5% or stays near 5.4%. The Office for Budget Responsibility's accompanying forecast will then show how much of the government's headroom survives at these yields, and the Bank of England's next rate decision will show whether the central bank sees the rise as imported or home-grown.

  2. AJ Bell — Q3 2026 dividend dashboard
