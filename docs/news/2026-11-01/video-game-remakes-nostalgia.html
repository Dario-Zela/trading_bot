Why publishers keep betting on video game remakes

In one sentence
Remakes of 1990s and 2000s classics sell fast on built-in fan bases, so publishers see them as a safer bet than new franchises, though cost data is scarce.

Walk through a games shop's release calendar, or its digital storefront, and the same titles keep turning up. Resident Evil 4 is back. So is Final Fantasy VII, in instalments. Oblivion, first released in 2006, returned in 2025 as a polished remaster. Publishers keep going back to the catalogue, and the sales figures explain why.

The numbers behind the nostalgia

The broadest estimate comes from the research firm Ampere Analysis, as reported by other outlets. It found that remasters and remakes released between January 2024 and September 2025 drew 72.4 million players and earned about $1.4bn, counting full-game sales and in-game purchases. The report is not public, so the figure is second-hand.

The individual cases are better documented. Capcom's remake of Resident Evil 4 sold more than 3 million copies in its first two days in 2023. It passed 7 million by the end of that year, 9 million by January 2025, and later 10 million, according to Capcom's own updates. Square Enix said Final Fantasy VII Remake shipped 3.5 million copies in three days and later passed 7 million units across its PlayStation 4, PlayStation 5 and PC versions. Circana analyst Mat Piscatella said Oblivion Remastered sold more copies in its first days than the original managed in its first 15 months. Accounts differ on the exact window.

The pattern is consistent. Remakes tend to start fast, because the audience already exists and already knows the product.

Cheaper, or just safer?

The more careful claim is that remakes are safer, not necessarily cheaper. One industry analysis cited by trade press says remakes cost more and take longer to build than a straight remaster. They can still be sold at full price and keep players engaged for longer. I found no reliable budget figures for the three games above. Publishers rarely disclose them, so cost comparisons with new franchises are inference, not data.

The case for safety is stronger. A remake comes with a fan base, a sales history and a finished design. Much of the creative risk, the question of whether the game is any good, was settled decades ago. A new franchise has none of that. Most of the successful remakes draw on titles from the 1990s and early 2000s, when today's core buyers were growing up.

Demand looks real as well. A YouGov survey found 65% of British gamers and 70% of Americans said they were fairly or very likely to buy a remake.

What it costs elsewhere

The worry is where the money goes. A studio busy rebuilding a 2005 game is not making something new. Critics inside the industry argue that the trend crowds out original work. That is hard to prove from the outside, since nobody publishes the projects that were never funded. Investors will see the effect in earnings reports instead, in how much of a publisher's release slate is back catalogue and how much is new intellectual property.

The next test is whether the hit rate holds. Remake sales are strongest when the original is beloved and the rebuild is faithful. A weak remake of a mid-ranking title would show whether the strategy has limits. Publishers' next quarterly results, and the release lists they attach to them, will show how far they are willing to lean on it.
