Ministers weigh a partial ban on backyard fireworks

In one sentence
UK ministers are reportedly weighing a partial ban on private fireworks over noise complaints, but it is only a proposal and nothing changes this Bonfire Night.

Ministers are reported to be weighing a partial ban on fireworks set off in private gardens, a proposal that has surfaced four days before Bonfire Night on 5 November. According to the Guardian's politics coverage, the stated driver is growing public complaints about noise. No decision has been taken, and what follows is a proposal under consideration, not policy.

What is on the table

The word "partial" matters. The reporting points to curbs on private use, not an end to fireworks as such. Organised public displays, which are run by trained operators and licensed in advance, are the obvious category to leave alone. How a restriction would be drawn is not settled in what has been published so far. It could turn on the loudness of the product, the hours of use, or who is allowed to buy and fire them. A partial ban could mean any of these, and each would fall differently on households.

The idea is not new. Earlier this year, UK-wide measures were reported to be under review, including limiting the noisiest fireworks to professional displays. That review was said to involve a 12-week public consultation led by the consumer protection minister, Kate Dearden. That account comes from regional press coverage as relayed by an aggregator, and the details could not be checked against a primary government document. Campaigners have long argued for lowering the legal noise ceiling, currently 120 decibels, to 90. A retailer's blog from late 2025 claimed the government had no plans to ban sales. That is a commercial source, and it predates the latest reports.

Why the noise argument has grown

The complaints are familiar: late-night bangs that distress pets, young children and veterans, and a seasonal stretch in which fireworks are let off for weeks around 5 November rather than on a single evening. Animal charities have been among the louder voices. Backers of the proposals in Parliament have called the review a "major step forward".

There are also arguments on the other side. Bonfire Night is among the oldest fixtures in the British calendar, and many households treat a garden display as part of it. Retailers would lose a large share of their seasonal trade. Any rule on private use would also need enforcing, and police and councils already field complaints they find hard to act on after the fact.

What would change, and what comes next

If a restriction on private use went ahead, it would change what a household can legally set off at home. It would also set a precedent for how noise is regulated around a tradition-heavy event. Existing law already limits sale and use. Fireworks cannot be set off between 11pm and 7am, with later cut-offs on a few dates, and noise is capped at 120 decibels. A partial ban would therefore sit on top of a framework that regulators say is hard to police.

Nothing will change for this Bonfire Night. Any new rules would need to run through consultation and, if they were to bind, legislation or secondary regulations. The thing to watch is whether ministers publish a formal proposal or consultation response setting out which products or settings would be covered. Until then, the reported plan remains an option under consideration.
