Slot venues could dodge the Budget gambling tax rise by registering as bingo halls

In one sentence
The Guardian reports slot-venue operators may avoid a planned Budget gambling tax rise by registering as bingo halls, which would cut the revenue the Treasury expects to raise.

A planned Budget increase in gambling tax could raise less than the Treasury expects. The Guardian reports that operators of high-street slot venues, sometimes called slot farms, may be able to avoid the higher charge by registering as bingo halls. If that route is open, the venues the policy is aimed at could sidestep it. The revenue pencilled in for the Treasury would fall short by the same amount.

The Guardian's report is the only source for the claim. No figure for the shortfall has been published, and the details of the mechanism have not been independently confirmed. The Treasury's revenue forecast and the exact size of the planned increase also remain unverified. What follows is the context that can be checked, and what the loophole would mean if it is real.

Why the label matters

British gambling is taxed by category, not by what a customer actually does. Machine Games Duty, which applies to slot-style machines, has been charged at 20% on higher-stake machines. Bingo was taxed separately, and bingo duty was due to be abolished from April 2026, a change the Treasury and the Bingo Association publicly welcomed. The tax system therefore treats a machine in a bingo hall differently from one in other premises. A venue that can move from one category to the other can change its tax treatment without changing much about how it trades.

That is the pressure point the Guardian describes. If a higher rate is attached to some venue types and not to bingo, the definition of bingo becomes the thing that decides who pays. Bingo halls already host a large number of gaming machines. Mecca Bingo's owner, Rank Group, has said machine duty is central to its economics.

What the industry has been saying

The tax debate ahead of the Budget was already heated. Trade reports put forward a proposal to double the rate on higher-stake machines from 20% to 40%, with a suggested yield of about £458m. A left-leaning think tank was reported to have made a similar estimate. Rank Group has warned that a rise could close venues and affect around 2,000 jobs, roughly 30% of its UK workforce. Buzz Bingo was reported to be exploring a sale amid the same fears. These are industry warnings and projections, not Treasury figures.

The loophole claim cuts across that argument. Operators warning of closures and a Treasury counting on new revenue would both be working from a premise that the tax lands where it is aimed. If venues can reclassify, it may land less heavily on the slot farms, while the genuine bingo operators still carry a share of the burden.

What to watch

Two things would settle it. The first is the Budget documents, in particular the Treasury's costings, which would show whether the revenue estimate allows for operators changing category. The second is any response from HMRC or the Gambling Commission on how a bingo premises is defined and licensed. A tightened definition in the Finance Bill would suggest the Treasury accepts the gap is real.
