Mahmood expected to tighten indefinite leave to remain rules

In one sentence
The Home Secretary is expected to tighten settlement rules, raising the question of whether Labour is reforming policy or positioning against Reform UK on immigration.

Shabana Mahmood is expected to tighten the rules on how migrants win the right to stay in Britain permanently. The move would deepen a shift in Labour's immigration policy that began under her predecessors and has accelerated since she became Home Secretary in the September 2025 reshuffle. The substance matters to the hundreds of thousands of people on work and family visas. The timing matters to Labour, which is competing with Reform UK for voters who rank immigration among their top concerns.

The details of what Ms Mahmood will announce were not confirmed at the time of writing. The broad direction is already on the record.

What settlement means

Indefinite leave to remain, usually called settlement, is the status that lets a migrant live, work and claim most benefits in the UK without time limit. It is also the usual gateway to British citizenship. Under the baseline rules it has typically followed five years of qualifying residence.

In late 2025 the government proposed "earned settlement". It would extend the baseline qualifying period to ten years, with routes to shorten it for contribution through work, taxes or public service. Law firms and the Immigration Law Practitioners' Association (ILPA) describe the plan as unprecedented, because ten years has until now applied mainly to fallback routes rather than to the standard path.

Legal commentary describes how the reductions would work. Higher earners could cut the wait: £50,270 a year sustained for three years would bring it to five years, and £125,140 a year for three years to three. Community volunteering is also cited as a route, though ILPA reads the plan as allowing only one reduction to be relied upon. Claiming public funds for more than a year would lengthen the wait, to as much as twenty years, according to one firm's summary.

The sources do not agree on the legal status of these changes. Some describe them as still proposed. ILPA reports that they were set out in a Statement of Changes to the Immigration Rules in March 2026, applying to most applications made from 26 March 2027. Whichever is right, the practical point is the same: the five-year norm is on its way out, and any new announcement would sharpen or accelerate that.

Policy or positioning?

The case that this is substantive policy rests on scale. Settlement is the point at which a temporary worker becomes a permanent resident, so doubling the baseline wait changes who can plan a life in Britain. It also affects employers, who rely on predictable routes to keep skilled staff, and public services such as the NHS and social care, which recruit heavily from overseas. Reports that the Home Secretary wants the new rules to reach people who have not yet received settlement would extend the effect to migrants who arrived expecting the old terms. That retrospective element is the part most likely to draw legal challenge and employer complaint.

The case that it is positioning rests on the political calendar and on how the policy is built. A long default wait with generous exemptions for high earners lets ministers say they have toughened the system while sparing the sectors that complain loudest. Net effects depend on how many people qualify for shortened routes, and that detail tends to emerge long after the headlines. The two readings are not mutually exclusive. A rule can be real and also be chosen, and timed, for what it says to voters.

The evidence for the political reading is circumstantial but consistent. Reform UK has made settlement a signature issue, and Labour's moves have tracked its pressure. Ministers know that a policy phased in over several years will not change net migration before the next general election. Its value then lies in the signal.

What to watch

Three things will show which reading holds. The first is the published criteria for shortened routes, in particular the earnings thresholds and whether public-sector workers such as nurses and care workers qualify. The second is whether the rules apply to people already in the country, which would turn a forward-looking reform into a disruption for existing visa holders. The third is the response from employers and from Reform, which has called for far harsher limits. If Reform dismisses the changes as insufficient, Labour will have to decide whether to go further, and how far toward Reform's position it is prepared to move.

The next fixed point is the Home Office's own publication of the rules and any accompanying impact assessment, which should show how many people the department expects to be affected.
