How Poundland grew from a market stall to a high-street giant

In one sentence
Poundland's journey from a £1-only shop in 1990 to a restructured national chain shows why mid-market and single-proposition retailers struggle on today's high street.

Poundland has outlasted the chains around it, but it has not come through unchanged. The shop that began in 1990 with one rule, that everything cost £1, now sells at several price points and has just been through a rescue. The route from a Midlands start-up to a restructured national chain says a good deal about why shops in the middle of the market keep going under.

Editor's note: the headline's "market stall" origin could not be independently confirmed for this piece. What follows relies on the widely reported outline of the company's history, and the details should be checked against the BBC's retrospective.

One price, no argument

The formula was borrowed from the American dollar store. Poundland opened in Burton upon Trent in 1990, and the name was the whole pitch. Shoppers needed no price tags, no comparison and no mental arithmetic. For the retailer, the limit on the shelf price meant buying had to be ruthless: suppliers were pushed to hit £1 exactly, and pack sizes and product specifications were adjusted to fit.

That discipline made the chain easy to understand and easy to roll out. Through the 2000s and 2010s it spread across the country, often into the units that department stores and high-street names had left behind. It also moved abroad under the Dealz brand in Ireland and Poland.

Buying the neighbours

Expansion was not only organic. Poundland bought the rival 99p Stores in 2015, and in 2016 the South African conglomerate Steinhoff took it over in a deal valued at roughly £600m. Rivals fell away in the same period. Poundworld collapsed in 2018, and Wilko followed in 2023. Poundland was meanwhile dropping the single price it was named for. It introduced items at £1.50, £2, £5 and beyond, because a fixed £1 had become harder to hold as costs rose.

That shift is the part of the history that explains the present. A chain built on one price is exposed to every cost it cannot pass on, from wages and rents to freight. Once it starts selling above £1, it competes with supermarkets, B&M and Home Bargains, which offer wider ranges and bigger stores. The name stayed fixed while the proposition drifted.

What the history explains

In 2025 Poundland went through a restructuring that closed a swathe of loss-making shops and passed control to the turnaround investor Gordon Brothers, in a deal reported as costing a nominal £1. The detail is worth confirming, but the direction is clear: a store estate that grew fast on cheap, empty high-street units had to be cut back.

The pattern matches the other casualties. The chains in trouble tend to be those that are neither the cheapest nor distinctive enough to justify a trip, and that carry leases signed in better times. Poundland's survival rests on being understood at a glance, which is also what made it vulnerable once the price promise went.

The next test is the retailer's own trading updates. They will show whether the smaller estate, with a wider price ladder, can turn a profit when household budgets are still stretched.
