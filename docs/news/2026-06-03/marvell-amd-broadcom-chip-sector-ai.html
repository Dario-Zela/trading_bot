Jensen Huang puts Marvell in the trillion-dollar club as Barclays reframes the AI trade around CPUs

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, whose COMPUTEX appearance with Marvell's CEO sent shares to a record close on Tuesday — Daniel Torok / White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Nvidia's Jensen Huang crowned Marvell 'the next trillion-dollar company' at COMPUTEX, sending shares 32% higher as Barclays says CPUs are the AI trade's next act.

Marvell Technology shares closed at an all-time high on Tuesday — the company's best trading session since listing — after Nvidia's chief executive Jensen Huang turned a product stage in Taipei into something closer to a market event. The gap between the two companies' sizes, Huang implied, is temporary.

"The next trillion dollar company, ladies and gentlemen," Huang told the Computex audience on Monday, gesturing toward Marvell CEO Matthew Murphy beside him on stage. The remark circulated quickly. By Tuesday's close, Marvell had gained 32.52% — a record single-day move — lifting its market capitalisation from roughly $191 billion to approximately $234 billion. Reaching $1 trillion from that base would require something close to a fourfold increase. Markets appeared content to price the direction, not the distance.

What makes the moment worth examining past the headline figure is its source. Huang runs Nvidia, the company that has defined and largely captured the GPU infrastructure trade for three years. That he would publicly anoint a networking and connectivity chip maker as the sector's next landmark is a claim about which layer of the stack he believes remains undervalued.

Huang's reasoning, delivered at Computex, centres on topology. "When you take a computing problem, and you disaggregate it into a lot of parts, and you distribute it across the entire data center, what's necessary is connectivity," he said. Marvell builds the custom ASICs and networking silicon that stitch data-centre fabric together — the circulatory system for AI workloads distributed across thousands of nodes. If compute continues to scale out horizontally, that market grows with it.


Marvell Technology's Santa Clara campus. The company's connectivity chips are central to the case Huang made from the COMPUTEX stage.

The CPU thread Barclays is pulling

The endorsement arrived in the same week Tom O'Malley, Barclays' semiconductor analyst, raised his price target on Advanced Micro Devices to $665 from $500 — maintaining an Overweight rating — and argued that the AI hardware trade has been defined too narrowly for two years running.

O'Malley's thesis: the infrastructure cycle is broadening. The first phase was almost exclusively about GPUs; Nvidia dominated that conversation and most of Wall Street followed it there. But as AI workloads mature from model training toward inference and, more recently, agentic applications that run multi-step tasks continuously, the relationship between CPUs and GPUs is shifting. CPU-to-GPU ratios are tightening — meaning demand for high-performance processors beyond the GPU is rising as a share of the total compute stack.

AMD's own management provided some grounding. On the Q1 2026 earnings call, CEO Lisa Su said the company was "seeing strong momentum as inferencing and agentic AI drive increasing demand for high-performance CPUs and accelerators." That is not a hedged comment — it names the specific workload category pulling CPU demand higher.

For institutional investors who have been concentrated in GPU suppliers and light on the broader silicon infrastructure, the Barclays call and the Marvell rally appearing in the same week point toward the same conclusion: the AI infrastructure trade has a second act, and it does not belong exclusively to Nvidia.

Broadcom and the quarterly test

The third thread — and the one with the shortest fuse — sits at Broadcom. The company reports fiscal second-quarter results on June 3, with consensus revenue at $22.1 billion, up 47% from a year earlier. AI chip revenue is expected to reach $10.7 billion for the quarter, roughly 140% higher year-on-year.

Jay Woods, chief global strategist at Freedom Capital Markets, put the stock's position plainly ahead of the print: Broadcom "may as well be" a member of the Magnificent Seven, given its trillion-dollar valuation and index weighting. The stock entered the week at $477.74, up nearly 39% year to date, with JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, Susquehanna and UBS all clustered near a $500 price target.

The earnings print matters beyond one company's quarterly figures. Broadcom provides custom silicon and networking infrastructure to the AI clusters of Google, Amazon, Meta and others. If its numbers corroborate what Huang described from the Computex stage — disaggregated compute at scale, connectivity as the binding constraint — it cements what is beginning to look like a sector-wide upgrade cycle for chip infrastructure, not just for GPU suppliers.

If the numbers disappoint, the logic that is currently levitating Marvell and AMD alongside Broadcom gets harder to sustain at current valuations. That is the risk embedded quietly in a cluster of sell-side price targets all sitting just above the current share price.

Broadcom's Q2 figures and forward guidance land after the US close on June 3. For AMD, the next concrete data point is a potential custom-silicon roadmap update at a summer analyst event — the catalyst that would give O'Malley's CPU thesis specific product numbers rather than a directional argument. For Marvell, the question is whether Huang's COMPUTEX endorsement translates into disclosed design wins, or remains, for now, a conviction call from a well-placed peer.
