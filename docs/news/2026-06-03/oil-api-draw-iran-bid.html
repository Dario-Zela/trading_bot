API crude draw runs 87% above forecast as US-Iran deal speculation adds a geopolitical bid

Crude oil storage tanks — the pace of US drawdowns over six consecutive weeks points to tighter-than-expected domestic supply conditions. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US crude stocks fell 6.75M barrels last week — 87% above the 3.6M-barrel consensus — marking a sixth straight draw as Iran deal uncertainty keeps a geopolitical bid in oil.

American Petroleum Institute data released Tuesday showed US crude stockpiles fell by 6.750 million barrels in the latest week — nearly double the consensus draw of 3.600 million barrels and 87% larger than markets had expected. If confirmed by the Energy Information Administration on Wednesday, it would mark the sixth consecutive weekly drawdown in US commercial crude inventories, a string of declines that increasingly suggests either demand is running hotter than assumed or domestic supply constraints are tighter than the models allowed.

WTI futures settled near $94 a barrel on Tuesday, up roughly 1.7% on the session. Brent closed around $96, holding gains from a third straight positive session. Both benchmarks have been whipsawed since the Iran conflict began in early 2026 — Brent hit nearly $120 a barrel at its peak — and have since retreated as ceasefire diplomacy advanced, only to swing on each fresh headline out of the negotiating rooms.

The inventory beat adds a clean fundamental argument to a market that has been running almost entirely on geopolitical noise. A draw that large, sustained over six weeks, points to real-side tightness: refiners are running product through the system at a pace that outstrips replenishment. With the Strait of Hormuz still operating at reduced commercial capacity, domestic stocks are absorbing pressure that would ordinarily be shared with imported supply.

The Strait Factor

The geopolitical overlay is doing work that the fundamentals alone would not justify. More than 14 million barrels per day of Gulf crude remain effectively shut in while peace talks between Washington and Tehran drag across their core disagreements — uranium enrichment limits, sanctions sequencing, verification protocols. President Trump said this week that a memorandum of understanding to reopen the Strait "could happen over the next week," a timeline Iranian officials have not confirmed and which their state media greeted with headlines designed to suggest the gap remains wide.

Traders are pricing that uncertainty in both directions. A deal that reopens the Strait would release an estimated 100 million barrels of stranded tanker cargo onto a market already bidding up the risk premium, a potential price shock downward. Oxford Economics analyst Bridget Payne offered the counter-scenario: if the closure extends into the late summer, OECD commercial stocks could reach a critical threshold by mid-September, "triggering a price spike towards $150 per barrel."

Goldman Sachs noted that oil market positioning is "significantly more limited than at the start" of the conflict, a sign that speculative money has stepped back from a trade where the range of outcomes remains unusually wide. That positioning restraint means that when the resolution — in either direction — comes, the move in futures could be larger than the headline number implies.

The EIA's official inventory count, due Wednesday morning, will either validate or trim Tuesday's API figure. The direction of the revision matters less than the cumulative trend: six draws in a row is a meaningful signal that the market's supply-demand balance, even without the Strait fully open, is tighter than consensus assumed heading into summer driving season. The next hard catalyst is the next round of US-Iran talks, which negotiators from both sides have indicated could resume before month-end.
