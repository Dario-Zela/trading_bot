Goldman's Solomon calls AI funding a 'greed'-driven frenzy

Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon in New York, November 2019 — U.S. Department of Defense / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Goldman CEO David Solomon says markets are in 'greed' mode and probably 'earlier in the cycle than later' as OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX prepare trillion-dollar IPOs.

Goldman Sachs CEO David Solomon said on Monday that investors have tilted decisively into "greed" mode, a characterisation he offered as markets prepare to absorb the largest fundraising wave in AI history — and one that carried an implicit note of caution from the bank advising many of those deals.

Speaking to CNBC's Leslie Picker on June 2, Solomon was blunt: "We are definitely in a moment where there's more greed than there is fear." The remark draws on the classic Wall Street sentiment spectrum — fear and greed as the two poles between which rational pricing gives way to instinct — and came as three of the largest anticipated public offerings in market history edge toward their roadshows.

OpenAI, Anthropic, and SpaceX are each preparing listings at valuations that, combined, approach $3.6 trillion — roughly the GDP of France. Anthropic just closed a $65 billion Series H at a post-money valuation of $965 billion; OpenAI is generating $25 billion in annualised revenue at an $852 billion private valuation; SpaceX is eyeing a raise of up to $75 billion as early as next week at a $1.8-2 trillion price tag. Goldman analysts project total 2026 IPO proceeds could reach $160 billion, roughly four times last year's figure.

Solomon's evidence that markets can absorb this: Alphabet's $80 billion follow-on equity offering — the largest of its kind on record — which he called his "first actual concrete data point" for what public investors are willing to commit at scale. "There's plenty of liquidity in the system if the world continues to remain as optimistic," he said.

Exuberance with a caveat

Solomon did not predict a crash. He acknowledged the cycle explicitly: "Greed can turn into fear very quickly, but that doesn't mean it will." And he offered a view that may unsettle the more bearish camp: "Exuberance can go on for big periods of time. There's a good chance that we're earlier in the cycle than later."

His advice to capital-hungry companies was equally direct: "When capital's available, if you're capital consumptive and it's available, take the capital."

For traders, the significance lies not in what Solomon said but who is saying it. Goldman is the underwriter and adviser of choice across this deal pipeline. The bank profits when equity is issued, and its CEO has now publicly named the mood as "greed" — language that typically precedes a recalibration, even if timing is anyone's guess. Short seller Michael Burry, in a May 2026 Substack post, drew comparisons to the final months of the 1999-2000 bubble. Morgan Stanley analysts have pushed back, arguing bubble fears are "misplaced" given that today's AI leaders generate real revenue and margins their dot-com predecessors did not.

The immediate test is the SpaceX roadshow, expected to begin as early as June 4. If that $75 billion raise is absorbed without visible stress — and if OpenAI follows in September as signalled — Solomon's earlier-in-the-cycle thesis gets a hard data point. If demand frays under the weight of three concurrent trillion-dollar offerings, the greed-to-fear turn he flagged could arrive faster than the current sentiment would suggest.
