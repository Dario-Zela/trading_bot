Warsh installs Project 2025 veterans at the Fed, mapping the administration's monetary direction

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh delivers remarks at his swearing-in ceremony in the East Room of the White House, May 22, 2026. — White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Warsh's first Fed hires — including Project 2025's Fed chapter author Paul Winfree — signal a conservative policy direction without yet committing to its most radical proposals.

Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh made his first staff additions to the central bank on June 2, choosing two conservative policy veterans as temporary contractors for policy analysis and planning — a choice that maps the intellectual orbit in which his chairmanship intends to operate.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the appointments. Paul Winfree, president of the Economic Policy Innovation Center, and Daniel Heil, a policy fellow at Stanford's Hoover Institution, will join as contractors. Warsh has known both men for years, having worked with them on research and writing projects. Neither appointment has been made permanent.

Two Contractors, One Telling Signal

Winfree is a veteran of the first Trump White House, where he served as Deputy Assistant to the President for Domestic Policy and co-authored the administration's inaugural budget proposal alongside then-OMB Director Mick Mulvaney. He went on to chair the Fulbright Foreign Scholarship Board and most recently founded the Economic Policy Innovation Center, a pro-Trump think tank that has concentrated on monetary and fiscal reform. He holds a PhD in economics from Queen's University Belfast.

Heil built his career on the federal budget and antipoverty programs, advising Jeb Bush's 2016 presidential campaign on tax policy before settling in at the Hoover Institution, where his recent work has examined federal healthcare costs and Social Security reform.

The temporary nature of both positions leaves room for interpretation — Warsh has said he has not decided whether to make them permanent. But the pairing signals something about scope. Heil's expertise sits largely outside the Fed's traditional remit of monetary policy and bank supervision; his appointment suggests Warsh is building something broader than a conventional policy shop.

The Blueprint on the Table

Winfree's hiring carries ideological weight that goes beyond résumé. He is the named author of the Federal Reserve chapter in the Heritage Foundation's ninth edition of Mandate for Leadership — the policy compendium that provided the framework for Project 2025. That chapter canvassed a range of structural reforms, most notably a proposal to replace the Fed's dual mandate of maximum employment and price stability with a narrower directive to "protect the dollar and restrain inflation." It also addressed the Fed's communication practices, its balance sheet, and the broader question of central bank independence.

The chapter explored terrain that goes further than anything Warsh has publicly endorsed. But its author is now inside the building.

Alignment, Distance, and What to Watch

Warsh has been careful to mark distance from the most aggressive Project 2025 proposals. At his swearing-in ceremony on May 22, he sent a memo to more than 20,000 Fed employees pledging to respect "the best of the Fed's traditions, while also looking for change." He has spoken in measured terms about honoring both sides of the dual mandate — a direct public departure from Winfree's Project 2025 position.

His stated reform priorities cluster around structural matters: reducing the Fed's $6.7 trillion balance sheet, scaling back highly specific forward guidance, and reconsidering how the central bank measures inflation. Warsh has for years been a vocal critic of large-scale asset purchases — he has called them "reverse Robin Hood," arguing they support financial asset prices while doing comparatively little for the broader economy and widening wealth gaps.

Still, the gap between stated positions and staffing choices invites scrutiny. The Senate confirmed Warsh 54-45 in May — the most contested confirmation in the Fed's history — in part because of concerns about whether he would use the chair to advance the administration's monetary preferences. Hiring the architect of Project 2025's Fed overhaul as a first act does not simplify that question.

The Fed's first rate-setting meeting under Warsh's chairmanship is scheduled for June 16-17. Whether Winfree and Heil transition from contractors to permanent staff will be an early and concrete signal of how deep the Project 2025 influence is intended to run. A Supreme Court ruling on presidential removal powers — which could affect the legal standing of Fed board members — remains pending and adds a further layer of institutional uncertainty. Investors watching rate expectations should track both.
