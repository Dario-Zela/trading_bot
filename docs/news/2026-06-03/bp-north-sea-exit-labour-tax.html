BP weighs full North Sea exit as Labour's windfall tax makes the economics unworkable

BP Clair Ridge platform hull west of Shetland — BP holds a 45% stake in the Clair field, the largest oilfield on the UK Continental Shelf — Grahame C Anderson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BP's £2bn North Sea sale to Ithaca Energy collapsed over decommissioning liabilities, as Labour's 78% EPL tax rate makes the basin's assets increasingly hard to price and sell.

BP has been hunting for the door to the UK North Sea for the better part of a year. Late last month, it looked like it had found one. Talks with Ithaca Energy over a package of North Sea assets valued at close to £2 billion reached an advanced stage before collapsing — a breakdown that leaves BP with a thinning list of options and a tax liability that is not getting smaller.

The deal that didn't close

The assets on the table were never named publicly, but BP's North Sea portfolio is a matter of record: a 45% stake in the Clair field — the largest oilfield on the UK Continental Shelf, lying 75 kilometres west of Shetland — plus the Eastern Trough Area Project hub in the central North Sea, and a collection of associated interests. Together they produce roughly 120,000 barrels of oil equivalent per day, about 5% of BP's 2.3 million barrel global daily output.

Ithaca was the natural buyer. The Aberdeen-based company is one of the largest independent North Sea operators, a FTSE 250 constituent majority-owned by Israel's Delek Group, and already a joint operator with BP on the Vorlich oilfield. The commercial logic was tidy; the negotiating positions were not.

The talks broke down over decommissioning liabilities, according to people familiar with the discussions. Late-life North Sea fields carry end-of-life commitments — plugging wells, removing platforms, restoring seabed — that run into hundreds of millions of pounds. Under UK law those liabilities cannot be abandoned; they transfer with the asset. The tax-relief structure for decommissioning costs gives both parties strong incentives to fight over the allocation, and when that allocation is unresolved the effective price of the field becomes undefined. Valuation gaps that appear bridgeable on headline numbers become unbridgeable once the decommissioning overhang is priced in. That appears to be what happened.

BP confirmed it is still exploring a transaction, leaving open the possibility of a revised deal with a different counterparty or a restructured terms package. It declined to comment on the specifics of the Ithaca discussions.

A 78% rate that reshapes what any asset is worth

The collapsed talks cannot be read in isolation from the fiscal backdrop. When the UK Labour government raised and extended the Energy Profits Levy in late 2024, the effective marginal tax rate on North Sea production climbed to 78% — a composite of 30% corporation tax, a 10% supplementary charge, and a 38% EPL surcharge — with the levy's sunset date extended to March 2030. BP quantified the incremental cost to its business at £539 million in its 2025 annual report.

That rate restructures the economics on both sides of any transaction. For a seller, high taxation compresses the post-tax cash flows that determine what an asset is worth on a discounted basis. For a buyer, the arithmetic is identical in reverse: paying close to £2 billion for a basin where 78 pence of every marginal pound flows to the Exchequer is a categorically different proposition from the same asset at a 40% rate. Decommissioning liabilities, discounted at a lower post-tax cost of capital, loom correspondingly larger. The result is a structural gap between what sellers need and what buyers can justify — and it is widening, not narrowing.

The broader pattern reflects this. Chevron and ConocoPhillips have trimmed North Sea exposure in recent years. UK Continental Shelf production today runs at roughly 20% of its year-2000 peak. A licensing moratorium on new exploration, which took effect in November 2025, has stripped out the upside optionality that once compensated buyers for accepting mature-field risk. What remains is a basin offering declining output, a decommissioning liability curve stretching decades forward, and a tax structure that limits the return on capital for anyone who stays.

A $20 billion target and a shrinking runway

BP's new chief executive, Meg O'Neill — who replaced Murray Auchincloss in April following pressure from activist hedge fund Elliott Management — has committed to $20 billion in asset sales by end-2027 against $27 billion in net debt. The North Sea was supposed to contribute meaningfully to that figure. The failed Ithaca talks set the timetable back.

A full exit from the North Sea — where BP has operated continuously for more than 60 years — remains under active internal review, according to people with knowledge of the process. Scottish First Minister John Swinney has said he is "very concerned" by the reports. The political response has been predictable; the commercial response has not yet materialised.

The government's position has softened at the margins. Chancellor Rachel Reeves has signalled the Energy Profits Levy could be wound down by 2027, and UK officials have held bilateral discussions with North Sea producers on the levy's future. Whether that timetable is short enough to close the valuation gap on a £2 billion deal — and to do so before BP's divestiture deadline tightens — is the question the next few quarters will answer. For a prospective buyer like Ithaca, the uncertainty itself may be reason enough to wait for clearer terms from government before revisiting the table.
