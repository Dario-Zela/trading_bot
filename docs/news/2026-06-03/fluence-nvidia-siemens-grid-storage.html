Fluence Energy surges as Nvidia and Siemens back grid-storage blueprints for AI data centres

A grid-scale battery energy storage installation — the category of infrastructure that Siemens' new reference architecture embeds at the heart of every major AI data centre design — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Siemens and Nvidia named Fluence Energy's SmartStack as the sole battery partner in a 136MW AI data-centre blueprint, sending shares up ~47% over three sessions.

On Monday afternoon, shares of Fluence Energy climbed a further 5.1%, the second consecutive session of notable gains after the electricity storage specialist announced a three-way collaboration with Siemens and Nvidia to design the power infrastructure for next-generation artificial intelligence data centres. The stock has now risen more than 47% across three trading days — a cumulative move that reflects something beyond ordinary market noise: two of the world's most formidable industrial names have put a formal stamp on grid-scale battery storage as non-negotiable infrastructure for the AI era.

Blueprint for a 136-Megawatt Future

The centrepiece of the announcement is a reference architecture — an engineering blueprint — developed by Siemens for data centres built around Nvidia's DSX Vera Rubin NVL72 platform, the company's next-generation rack-scale AI supercomputer. Fluence's SmartStack battery system is the sole battery energy storage component specified throughout the design.

The blueprint covers the complete electrical pathway for a 136-megawatt facility — 100 megawatts of IT load — from a 34.5-kilovolt utility connection through medium- and low-voltage distribution, all the way to individual rack interfaces. It is built to Tier III concurrent maintainability standards, meaning components can be serviced without taking the facility offline. Prefabricated power skids from Siemens shorten commissioning time, and a modular architecture allows capacity to scale from tens of megawatts to hundreds without a wholesale redesign.

One specification stands out: the design calls for two to three hours of battery discharge capacity. Most analysts modelling battery storage in data centres had assumed roughly one hour. That gap implies more hardware per facility and more revenue per deployment for Fluence — a detail the market has clearly noticed.

What SmartStack Actually Does in an AI Factory

Battery storage in data centres is often described in vague terms. In Siemens' design, SmartStack has specific, named functions.

The first is ride-through: when the utility grid experiences voltage or frequency fluctuations — increasingly common as grids absorb more variable renewable generation — the batteries maintain steady power delivery to computing equipment. The second is black start: in a full outage, the system can restart operations without waiting for grid restoration. The third is load smoothing: AI workloads consume electricity unevenly, with sharp spikes during model inference, and batteries absorb those surges so the facility does not hammer the grid or overload transformers.

"By providing essential capabilities like voltage and frequency ride-through, black start, grid demand response, and AI load smoothing," said Jeff Monday, Fluence's chief growth officer, the SmartStack technology addresses "one of the most operationally challenging aspects of running dense AI computing infrastructure at scale."

Fluence's position in the Nvidia ecosystem is unusually concentrated: the company is the only named battery energy storage provider across all seven infrastructure OEM configurations in Nvidia's partner network. That exclusivity — even if the reference architecture is not a binding purchase agreement — gives Fluence a significant first-mover position in specification discussions with hyperscalers and co-location providers who use these blueprints as starting points for facility design.

The Bottleneck Has Shifted

Until recently, the limiting factor in building out AI data centres was chips: supply was constrained and delivery times stretched. That pressure has eased. The constraint is now, in a growing number of markets, power.

In July 2024, a single voltage fluctuation in Northern Virginia disconnected sixty data centres simultaneously — creating a 1,500-megawatt power surplus in an instant and illustrating in stark terms how poorly the grid handles concentrated, spiky AI demand. Gartner has estimated that power shortages will restrict 40% of AI data centres by 2027. Site selection for new facilities has already shifted from questions of fibre access and latency to a search for available megawatts.

Fluence sits at that intersection. The company ended its most recent half-year with $5.6 billion in contracted backlog and reported order intake of $2 billion for H1 FY2026, double the comparable period in 2025. It has signed master supply agreements with two major hyperscalers and expects its first order from those agreements to land during the current quarter. Full-year revenue guidance stands at $3.2 to $3.4 billion.

The Siemens relationship is not new — the German industrial conglomerate co-founded Fluence as a joint venture with AES Corporation in 2018, before Fluence listed publicly in 2021. What is new is the scale and specificity of the commercial alignment. The reference architecture formalises Fluence as the natural battery choice for anyone adopting Siemens' power design — a distribution channel that Fluence's sales team could not replicate on its own.

For Siemens AG itself, the architecture represents an extension into a data-centre market that has been reshaping capital spending across the industrial sector. The company's stock has posted gains across the past day, week, month, and three-month periods, a run that reflects broadening investor recognition of Siemens' exposure to grid infrastructure demand.

The Market's Verdict — and Its Caveats

The initial market reaction was dramatic. Fluence shares surged approximately 44% on the first day of trading after the announcement, jumping to roughly $27.15. The stock remains about 12% below its February 2026 peak of $32.23 — a reminder of how far it fell before this week's recovery.

Analysts have been more measured. The consensus Wall Street rating on FLNC is Hold, with a mean price target of $18.47 — implying significant downside from current levels if the partnership excitement fades without translating to revenue. The critical caveat: a reference architecture is a blueprint, not an order book. Hyperscalers could adopt Siemens' electrical systems and source batteries from a competitor. Nothing legally compels them to use SmartStack.

Fluence's most recent quarterly results offer mixed evidence on execution. Q2 revenue came in at $465 million against a consensus expectation of $622 million, with supply-chain delays and commodity volatility cited. Insider selling has also drawn scrutiny: management and early investors are reported to have sold more than $400 million of stock over a recent three-month period — a figure that sits uneasily alongside the bullish messaging of the current announcement cycle.

The clearest near-term test is whether Fluence converts one of its two hyperscaler master supply agreements into an actual purchase order this quarter — the company's own guidance says that is expected. If that order materialises, it begins to close the gap between blueprint-level validation and revenue-level reality. Until then, the partnership confirms that the world's largest chip company and one of its most powerful industrial groups have decided battery storage belongs at the centre of every serious AI data centre design — a bet on infrastructure that would have seemed premature two years ago.
