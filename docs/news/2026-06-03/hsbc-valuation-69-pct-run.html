HSBC's 69% annual run leaves valuation with little room for disappointment

HSBC's global headquarters at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC's 69% annual run has pushed its P/E to 15x against peers at 11x, leaving fair value roughly where the stock already trades.

HSBC Holdings shares have climbed 68.7% over the past twelve months, carrying the stock from the mid-800p range to around 1,400p on the London Stock Exchange. The recovery has been real and, in operational terms, earned. The harder question — the one that matters for anyone assessing the stock today — is whether that recovery is now fully priced.

At roughly 1,399p as of the close on 2 June, HSBC trades on a trailing P/E of 15.2x. That compares unfavourably with European banking peers, whose average multiple sits at 11.4x, and with a broader global bank cross-section averaging 10.8x. Simply Wall St places a fair P/E for HSBC at 10.8x — implying the market has already awarded a premium the bank has not yet fully grown into. The most-followed narrative valuation — a discounted cash flow approach using an 8.29% discount rate applied to projected earnings — arrives at an intrinsic value of £13.67. The current price of £13.99 sits barely 3% above that figure. For a stock that has almost doubled in twelve months, the margin for error is thin.

Two models, one uncomfortable message

The valuation picture is not straightforward. The P/E lens and the excess-returns model deliver sharply different verdicts. The latter capitalises HSBC's return on equity above its cost of capital and calculates intrinsic value at £21.66 — implying the stock is 35% undervalued. That reading is anchored to an average ROE of 15.93%, which Q1 2026's 18.7% return on tangible equity helps to support. In a rising-earnings environment, the excess-returns model earns its optimism. In a mean-reversion scenario, it flatters.

The composite score tells a colder story: Simply Wall St rates HSBC 2 out of 6 on its valuation checklist. Analysts are less pessimistic — consensus sits at Moderate Buy, with average 12-month targets clustering between 1,375p and 1,467p — but that implies perhaps 5% upside at the last close, which is not the kind of cushion that follows naturally from a 69% annual run.

The operational foundation beneath the share price is not contested. The strategic pivot from underperforming European and US businesses toward Asia and the Middle East is producing results. Total wealth balances reached $1.57 trillion in Q1 2026, up 12% year-on-year; Asian client assets grew 14% to $1.07 trillion, and net new money inflows accelerated to $39bn in the quarter from $23bn a year earlier. Net interest margins held at 1.60%, broadly flat in a choppy rate environment. The difficulty is that the market knows all of this. A P/E of 15x against peers at 11x is not a sceptical market awaiting evidence; it is a market that has priced the transformation in.

Any shortfall — prolonged stress in Hong Kong commercial real estate, an unexpected rate cut compressing Asian margins, or a stumble in wealth inflows — lands against a multiple that provides no buffer. The bank's half-year results, due in August, will be the first real test of whether Q1's 18.7% RoTE is the new baseline or a high-water mark. The direction of short rates across Asia, and any update on Hong Kong property exposure, will do more to settle the valuation debate than any model.
