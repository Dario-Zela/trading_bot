New York lawmakers move to impose a one-year data centre construction freeze

Server racks inside a large-scale data centre — the type of facility New York's one-year moratorium would put on hold — Wikimedia Commons / CC BY-SA 3.0
In one sentence
New York lawmakers are set to vote on a one-year halt to large data centre construction, citing grid strain and rising electricity rates in the AI buildout era.

New York's state legislature is on course to pass a one-year moratorium on new large-scale data centre approvals — a move that would make it the first state in the country to impose such a statewide ban.

Assembly Speaker Carl Heastie said this week that lawmakers "intend to pass it," referring to legislation (S10642/A11560) co-sponsored by state Sen. Kristen Gonzalez and Assemblymember Didi Barrett. The chamber was expected to vote before the legislative session closes on Friday. The bill cleared its initial hurdles after lawmakers trimmed what had originally been proposed as a three-year freeze down to one year — enough to secure the votes, short enough to survive the political room.

The math behind the freeze

The immediate driver is electricity demand, and the numbers are striking. The New York Independent System Operator currently has 48 new large-load projects in its interconnection queue, totalling more than 11 gigawatts. Data centres account for the bulk of that load. Projected data-centre electricity demand alone could reach 7,000 megawatts by 2030 — enough to power seven million homes. Set against that, electricity rates in New York rose 13 percent in 2025, with data centre construction cited as a contributing factor.

The NYISO's own vice president acknowledged the pressure plainly: "It's a concern because we're not adding commensurate supply to meet that forecasted demand."

The bill targets facilities designed to draw at least 20 megawatts, which captures hyperscale cloud and AI infrastructure projects rather than ordinary commercial server rooms. During the one-year pause, the Department of Environmental Conservation would produce a generic environmental impact statement, and the Public Service Commission would assess how data-centre loads affect rates for residential and commercial ratepayers.

A governor with reservations

Governor Kathy Hochul has not committed to signing the bill and has positioned herself as sceptical of statewide action. Her view is that data-centre siting is "a local decision for municipalities" and a matter of land-use law rather than state regulation. She has separately floated a different approach: require large facilities to either generate their own power or pay a premium that shields residential customers from cost pass-through.

Without her signature, the bill dies. With it, New York becomes the first state to formally pause AI-era data centre expansion on grid-capacity grounds — a precedent that would invite litigation and legislative mimicry in roughly equal measure.

Cloud operators with live construction programmes in Virginia, Texas, and the southeast have limited immediate exposure to a New York pause. The longer-run signal is harder to ignore: if electricity grids are treated as a rationed public resource, permitting timelines for new AI infrastructure will lengthen materially in states that follow New York's lead.

Hochul has offered no public timeline for her decision. The session closes Friday.
