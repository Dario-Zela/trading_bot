S&P 500 chases a nine-day winning streak not seen since 1995

The trading floor of the New York Stock Exchange, where S&P 500 constituents are among the most actively traded names. — Carol M. Highsmith / Library of Congress
In one sentence
Nine straight daily gains and chasing a tenth not seen since 1995—history shows such streaks are followed by six- and twelve-month returns well below the market's long-run average.

The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at 7,612, logging its ninth consecutive daily gain — and positioning itself, on Wednesday, for a milestone not seen since September 1995. Only eight times in 55 years of market data has the index extended a daily run to ten sessions. None of those came after 2001.

A nine-day streak is uncommon but not unprecedented; this one is the seventeenth of its kind since 1970, with May 2025 still fresh in the record books. It is the tenth day that represents historically thin air — and that is the threshold the market is now chasing.

What the data say

The forward-return record after ten-day streaks is, in a word, unimpressive. Across eight prior instances, average returns lagged the market's baseline by a margin that widened the further out the measurement went. One month later, the index underperformed its historical average by 0.28 percentage points — a number small enough to dismiss. Six months later, the gap was 5.2 points, with the S&P 500 averaging a return of negative 0.70% against a historical norm of 4.5%. Twelve months out, the average gain was 2.14%, compared to the long-run expectation of roughly 9%.

Win rates confirmed the drag. The index closed higher six months after a ten-day streak in only four of eight cases — a coin flip. That figure is meaningfully below the market's unconditional hit rate over comparable periods.

The caveat is the sample. Eight data points, spread across decades and macro regimes, make clean statistical conclusions difficult. September 1995 was followed by a 16% advance over the next twelve months, as the early phase of the technology supercycle overwhelmed any mean-reversion impulse. October 1989 worked the other direction: approximately 15% losses over the same window, amplified by credit stress and the brief but sharp October mini-crash. The dispersion between those two endpoints should give anyone building a contrarian trade around streak length reason to pause.

The rally's foundations

The run that brought the index to this point rests on two pillars: AI-driven chip demand and an earnings season that has outpaced even elevated expectations. Roughly 85% of S&P 500 companies have beaten estimates this quarter, with an average positive surprise approaching 17% — more than double the long-run average. Semiconductor names have been the sharpest contributors, a theme echoed in the week's broader chip-sector moves.

Valuation has tracked the price gains. The index trades near 21.2 times forward earnings, above historical averages but not wildly so given the earnings acceleration. The complication is the inflation backdrop: April CPI came in at 3.8%, well above target, and the Federal Reserve has held rates rather than cut. That leaves the real yield environment as a latent weight on multiples, particularly if the summer data prints surprised on the upside.

What to watch

If Wednesday's session produces another positive close, the index will have completed a streak that, by the historical record, preceded below-average returns in five of eight subsequent six-month windows. Whether this instance resembles 1995 or 1989 will depend less on streak mechanics than on what inflation does next. The June CPI print, due in the second week of the month, is the most immediate variable: a reading that sustains or exceeds 3.8% would test whether current valuations can hold without a rate-cut catalyst.
