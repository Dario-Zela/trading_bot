BP's rich valuation tested by UAE gas expansion bet

In one sentence
BP trades at 31.6x earnings versus a 13.6x sector average, with a new UAE gas stake cited as justification but no disclosed cost or timeline yet.

BP shares have handed investors a 92.7% total return over the past five years — nearly triple money on dividends and price appreciation combined, and enough to make the stock look, on paper, like one of the better calls in European energy. The trouble for anyone buying today is that the good news is already priced in, and then some.

BP trades at roughly 31.6 times earnings, against an oil-and-gas sector average nearer 13.6 times and a peer average of 11.8 times, according to Simply Wall St's valuation screen. Even the site's own modelled "fair" multiple — an estimate of what BP's growth and returns ought to command — comes in at 23.3 times, still well short of where the stock actually sits. Its composite valuation score is a middling 3 out of 6: cheap on some measures, such as price-to-sales at 0.6 times versus a peer average of 2.2 times, and rich on others. Cash-flow models that discount BP's expected future earnings even suggest the shares are undervalued. Multiples built on what the company is earning right now say the opposite. That tension is the whole story.

The premium's justification

The case for paying up rests substantially on where BP is putting new money to work, and the most concrete recent example is in Abu Dhabi. On June 24, BP signed a concession agreement with ADNOC to take a 10% stake in the Bab Gas Cap project, its first entry into upstream gas resources in the emirate. The project targets reservoirs sitting above the existing Bab onshore oil field and is designed to produce up to 1.5 billion cubic feet of gas a day, alongside condensates — part of a UAE push toward gas self-sufficiency by 2030 that sits inside ADNOC's broader $150 billion capital programme for 2026 through 2030. ADNOC Onshore retains 60% of the concession; TotalEnergies, CNPC, Inpex, ZhenHua Oil and GS Energy hold the remainder.

It is a real project with a real partner list. What it does not yet have, publicly, is a cost figure attributable to BP, a first-gas date, or an estimate of what it adds to group cash flow. Analysts citing the deal as a reason to pay a premium multiple are, for now, extrapolating from strategic intent rather than disclosed numbers.

What the multiple is actually pricing

A 31.6 times earnings multiple on an integrated oil major is unusual enough to demand an explanation beyond "the company signed a concession." BP shares have pulled back from an April high near $47 to around $37, and the stock's market capitalisation sits near $96 billion. Investors holding through the five-year run have been rewarded; investors buying at today's price are betting that either earnings catch up to the multiple, or that projects like Bab Gas Cap — plus BP's other recent upstream gas moves in Angola — deliver enough incremental cash flow to make the current price look reasonable in hindsight. Neither of those outcomes is guaranteed by the concession signing itself, which is a starting gun, not a delivered barrel.

What comes next

The near-term test is whether BP or ADNOC put a capital or production timeline to Bab Gas Cap in coming disclosures — likely candidates being BP's next quarterly results or ADNOC's own investor updates on its 2026–2030 spending plan. Until a figure attaches to the project, the UAE expansion functions as a narrative supporting an already-expensive stock rather than a quantified catalyst. Whether that gap gets filled with numbers, or simply with more narrative, is what will decide if BP's premium holds.
