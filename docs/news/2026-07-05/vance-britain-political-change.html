Vance says Britain needs political change, pledges support for next PM

U.S. Vice President JD Vance, whose comments on British political leadership came in a Sunday Times interview published July 4. — Official White House portrait / Emily J. Higgins
In one sentence
VP Vance called Britain "failed by its leadership" and hopes the next PM delivers reform, as Labour's contest to replace Starmer nears its conclusion.

U.S. Vice President JD Vance has told Britain, in effect, that it has earned its own instability. In an interview with The Sunday Times published on July 4 — the 250th anniversary of American independence — Vance said the country had been "failed by its leadership for a long time" and voiced hope that whoever succeeds Keir Starmer as prime minister will finally deliver the "significant structural change" he believes British voters have been demanding for years.

The remarks land at a delicate moment. Starmer announced on June 22 that he would step down after barely two years in Downing Street, triggering a Labour leadership contest that is expected to instal Andy Burnham, the mayor of Greater Manchester, as Britain's seventh prime minister in a decade. Nominations open July 9, with the party aiming to have a successor in place before the summer recess.

"What I see is six prime ministers in the last few years. What that says to me is that something is very broken about British politics and that people are really crying out for significant structural change."

A backhanded compliment

Vance was careful to frame the criticism as affectionate rather than hostile. He called Britain an "amazing place" with "the most amazing people in the world" outside the United States, and said his comments — which he conceded could sound "provocative" — came "from a perspective of love and admiration." On the question of Burnham specifically, Vance was notably noncommittal, saying he did not know much about the Manchester mayor but that Washington would "work with them and work with them as successfully as we can," whoever ends up leading the country.

That studied vagueness is itself informative. Vance's intervention is the latest in a run of unusually blunt commentary from the Trump administration about British governance. President Trump predicted Starmer's resignation days before it happened, writing on Truth Social that the outgoing prime minister had "failed badly" on immigration and energy policy, and had urged him to "open up the North Sea" to oil and gas drilling. When asked about Burnham directly, Trump was more pointed still, calling him "extremely liberal" and predicting that a Burnham government would resist expanding North Sea production — a read Burnham has pushed back on, saying he holds no "fixed position" on the issue.

Migration, free speech, and a widening rift

The Sunday Times interview did not arrive in a vacuum. Vance has spent months criticizing European governments broadly, and Britain specifically, over migration policy and what he characterizes as the erosion of free speech — comments that grew sharper after the fatal stabbing of a British teenager, Henry Nowak, which Vance argued was a consequence of European elites failing to "stand their ground" on immigration. Downing Street has previously accused foreign politicians, implicitly including Vance, of trying to "interfere" in British democracy and "stir up division," a sign that the relationship has grown more openly adversarial even as both governments insist the alliance remains intact.

That tension is the real substance behind Vance's latest remarks. His language about "support" for the next prime minister is conspicuously short on specifics — no mention of trade terms, defense commitments, or the tariff arrangements the Trump administration negotiated with Starmer's government earlier in its term. What is clear is the administration's preference: officials in Washington want a British government more aligned with their positions on energy production and migration enforcement, and are signaling — through Trump's broadsides and Vance's more diplomatic framing — that they intend to make that preference known regardless of who wins the Labour contest.

What happens next

For markets, the immediate takeaway is limited. Sterling and UK gilts showed little reaction to the interview itself, since Vance offered no concrete policy shift, tariff threat, or investment pledge to price in. The more consequential test comes once Labour's leadership process concludes, expected before the summer parliamentary recess, and a Burnham government — if that is indeed the outcome — sets out its own positions on North Sea licensing and immigration enforcement. Washington's tone toward London from that point, rather than anything said on July 4, will be the better signal of whether U.S.-UK trade and defense cooperation cools or holds steady.
