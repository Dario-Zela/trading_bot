Football regulator says he would quit over political interference in the Man City case

In one sentence
IFR chair David Kogan says he would resign if politicians meddle in the Manchester City case, while insisting no pressure has yet been applied.

The man charged with policing English football's finances has told the government where he stands if politicians try to steer the Manchester City case. David Kogan, chair of the Independent Football Regulator (IFR), says any political interference in how his body operates would end with him leaving the job. The threat is conditional. Kogan says no pressure has been applied. But he chose to make it public just as the most consequential financial-rules case in the sport's history nears its next stage.

What Kogan said

Speaking in Manchester at the launch of the IFR's draft State of the Game report, Kogan was blunt about his own position. "Our independence is absolute, and my independence personally is absolute," he said. He added that he had made clear "last year that any form of political interference in the way we operate is going to result in my resigning from this role." Asked by BBC Sport what he would do if politicians pressed him, his answer was two words: "I would resign."

He was equally direct about the present. "To be crystal clear, we've come under absolutely no political pressure whatsoever on this or any other topic," he said. So the resignation threat describes a hypothetical, and it restates a position he says he took when the regulator was being set up. What is new is the setting. He is repeating it now, with City's case live and a prime minister who has spoken about it.

The case and the Burnham remarks

City were found by an independent commission, in a ruling on the Premier League's charges, to have breached the league's financial rules. Reports differ on the count: the club were convicted on 114 of the 115 charges, so coverage refers to both figures. The club are appealing, and a separate appeal board will hear it. A sanctions hearing, which could produce a heavy fine or a points deduction, would follow later.

The political friction began with Prime Minister Andy Burnham. He told the BBC he would be concerned if City's owners chose to sell the club. Downing Street then sought to calm things. A spokesperson said that "there can't be any suggestion that anyone is above the rules" and that "if wrongdoing is established, those responsible should face the appropriate consequences". Burnham has since said he will not get directly involved in the process.

The remarks still landed badly in some places. Some Premier League clubs were reported to be astonished by them and to feel they had put the regulator in a difficult position. Shadow sports minister Louie French urged ministers to "keep politics out of football" and to ensure the commission is free of outside influence. David Bernstein, a former City chairman and former FA chair, said it was "absolutely vital that this independent regulator can perform independently and without political interference."

What the regulator can do, and when

The IFR, created by the government in 2025, has the power to assess whether owners, directors and senior executives are suitable. Kogan has said it will use that power when there is clear evidence of misconduct by individuals. On City, it is waiting. The regulator will let the appeal run its course and then act on legal advice.

That raises the stakes of the process. If City's owners were ever disqualified, the reporting suggests Sheikh Mansour would be given a deadline to sell. If he missed it, a third party would take over the sale at a price the club would not control. Few decisions in English football would carry more money, or be more tempting for ministers to comment on.

The regulator is also exposed on cost. Kogan said that if it faces "a long-running legal challenge, we will clearly go back to government" for support. Chief executive Richard Monks said the IFR would take a firmer line on clubs using loopholes in financial rules, and would "just say no" to schemes such as selling stadiums or assets to an ultimate owner. A regulator that may depend on the Treasury to fund a court fight against a well-resourced club is the sort of arrangement that makes the independence question practical rather than abstract.

There is a history here too. Kogan's own appointment drew criticism. He is a former Labour donor, and Keir Starmer was rebuked by his ethics adviser over signing off the appointment, which Starmer later called an "unfortunate error". Kogan's public vow to walk out is partly a reply to that doubt: a statement that, whatever its origins, the body will not take instructions.

What to watch

The next markers are procedural. The IFR is due to set out its levy plans next week, which will show how it intends to pay for itself and for the government's set-up costs. Any extraordinary legal action would come from outside that budget, which is where the funding relationship with ministers would be tested. And City's appeal will decide when the regulator has to act. Until then, a resignation threat that has not been triggered is mainly a marker, and the first real test is whether ministers keep their comments to the principle that no one is above the rules.
