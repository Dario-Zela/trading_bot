Bank of America fined $1.2bn over money laundering failings, with a separate OFAC settlement

In one sentence
Bank of America agreed to pay over $1.2bn to settle US AML failings, plus an $11.6m OFAC sanctions settlement, with enforcement aimed at compliance controls.

Bank of America has agreed to pay more than $1.2bn to settle US allegations that its anti-money laundering programme failed for years, in a package announced by the Treasury on 13 October. The package also includes a much smaller sanctions settlement. The bank admitted that it "willfully violated" the Bank Secrecy Act. The announcement came less than a day before the lender's third-quarter earnings.

The two actions differ in scale by a factor of about a hundred. They also point the same way: regulators are examining whether the machinery of compliance works, and the individual bad payment is a secondary concern.

What the regulators found

The larger case rests on two agencies. The Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) said Bank of America "failed to implement and maintain an AML program that met the minimum requirements of the BSA". It said the bank failed to mitigate and report priority national-security risks, among them Chinese money-laundering networks and illicit finance on the southwest border. As a result, it did not file timely reports on more than $5bn of suspicious transactions. Treasury said more than $1bn of that unreported activity touched national-security priorities.

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) added its own penalty of $750m, the largest civil money penalty it has imposed on a bank. The OCC found the bank's programme deficient and ineffective. It said the bank remained out of compliance after a December 2024 cease-and-desist order. Comptroller Jonathan Gould said "egregious and continued BSA violations warrant the OCC imposing its largest CMP ever assessed against a bank". Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the actions "send a clear message that even the biggest financial institutions are not above the law".

Reports differ on how the headline figure is built. Some outlets attribute the $1.2bn to FinCEN, while others describe it as a combined total across agencies in which the OCC's $750m counts toward the sum. The agencies' own releases would settle it. The bank, for its part, says its third-quarter results will carry a non-tax-deductible charge of $1.2bn, roughly 17 cents a share. That charge is the number investors can use. Treasury describes the penalty as the third-largest in its history.

The sanctions settlement

The Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) announced a separate settlement of about $11.6m. It covers 475 apparent violations of sanctions programmes involving Russia, Belarus, Iran, Burma, Somalia, narcotics and global terrorism. OFAC characterised the conduct as "non-egregious" but largely not voluntarily self-disclosed. The bank processed millions of dollars of payments that apparently breached those programmes.

At under 1% of the AML penalty, the OFAC sum is small. Its significance lies in the pattern. Sanctions screening and suspicious-activity monitoring are different controls run by different teams, and both are cited against the same institution in the same announcement.

What it signals

The cases point to enforcement aimed at controls rather than individual transactions. The OCC's order dates from December 2024, and the penalties flow from that order and FinCEN's investigation, so the bank was under supervisory pressure for almost two years before this resolution. The FinCEN allegation is about volume and process: $5bn of unreported activity implies a monitoring and escalation failure. A single rogue transfer would not produce that.

For other large lenders, the practical reading is that remediation after a consent order is itself examined. Missing deadlines carries a price. Compliance and risk teams at peer banks will likely test their own suspicious-activity reporting and sanctions screening against the categories Treasury named. Those are cross-border Chinese networks, border-related finance and the Russia and Iran programmes.

The bank's response was measured. "Financial crime compliance is serious, and we are responsible for getting it right," it said, adding that it had worked with regulators to resolve the matters.

What to watch

The first test is the third-quarter earnings report, where the $1.2bn charge will appear against the bank's results and where management will be asked about remediation costs and any further regulatory conditions. The second is the full text of the FinCEN and OCC orders, which should show the monitorship or programme requirements attached. Investors tracking regulatory costs will also look for whether other large banks flag AML provisions in their own filings this season.
