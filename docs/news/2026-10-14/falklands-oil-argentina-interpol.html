Argentina escalates Falklands oil claim as UK asks Interpol to reject arrest requests

In one sentence
Argentina's criminal cases against the Sea Lion oil developers have reached Interpol, and the UK says the arrest requests are politically motivated.

Argentina is using criminal law and international policing to try to stop an oil project in waters around the Falkland Islands. Britain has now asked Interpol to reject Buenos Aires's requests for arrests. The dispute over who owns the islands, which Argentina calls the Malvinas, is old. What is new is that it has become a fight over who may drill, and who may be detained for helping.

According to a Guardian report, British officials wrote to Interpol's headquarters in Lyon, France, urging caution over Argentine requests to detain people tied to the Sea Lion oil project. The UK argues that the requests, including those aimed at British nationals, are politically motivated. UK officials reportedly described Argentina's actions as the most serious escalation since the 1982 war.

A field that could change the islands

Sea Lion lies in the North Falkland Basin in the South Atlantic. Reports say it could triple the islands' GDP, which explains why it matters to both sides. It is developed by the Israeli company Navitas and its British partner, Rockhopper Exploration.

President Javier Milei has vowed to block the project. His government has opened criminal proceedings against Navitas and Rockhopper. One report puts the number of individuals and corporate partners facing proceedings or sanctions at around 60. That figure comes from a single secondary account and I could not confirm it independently.

The UK reads the campaign as intimidation. Its aim, in this view, is to frighten those involved and put off future investors, rather than to win a case in court.

Why Interpol

The arrest requests are red notices. These ask member countries to locate people and provisionally arrest them pending extradition. A red notice is not an international arrest warrant. Interpol screens requests, and each member state decides for itself whether to act on one.

That design is the source of the British worry. Interpol's constitution bars it from activities of a political character under Article 3, and the organisation has reportedly reaffirmed that commitment. Even so, a notice that slips through, or a country that detains someone on the strength of an Argentine request, could expose executives, contractors or officials to arrest while travelling.

Andrea Clausen, chief executive of the Falkland Islands government, said Argentina's use of Interpol posed a threat. She added: "One would hope that Interpol wouldn't necessarily take action."

The sovereignty backdrop

Argentina has long claimed the islands. Britain's position rests on the wishes of the residents: in a 2013 referendum, 99.8% voted to remain a British territory. The 1982 conflict lasted 74 days and killed 649 Argentine military personnel, 255 British military personnel and three islanders.

A second report says Argentina was preparing to seek arbitration under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. It adds that UK government sources are confident such a tribunal would not rule on sovereignty. I found no confirmation of this in other coverage, and the details of any filing remain unclear.

What to watch

The immediate test is whether Interpol accepts or rejects any of the Argentine requests, and whether it says so publicly. Beyond that, any case that reaches a tribunal would show whether Argentina can frame the licences as a legal matter rather than a sovereignty one. A ruling either way would affect how states use international policing against companies and individuals tied to disputed territory. The source reporting so far is largely one Guardian story and its rewrites. It gives no names of those targeted and no dates for the requests.
