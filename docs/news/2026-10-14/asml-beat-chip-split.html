ASML beats and guides higher while Korean memory stocks fall on fears of Chinese competition

In one sentence
ASML reportedly beat Q3 EPS by about 19% and guided FY2026 sales to €44-45bn, while Korean memory stocks fell on China fears; figures rest on one unverified report.

ASML, the Dutch company that makes the lithography machines every leading-edge chip factory depends on, has reported third-quarter earnings well above expectations and told investors to expect a stronger fourth quarter. On the same day, Korean memory-chip makers sold off on fears of Chinese competition. The two moves point in different directions, and the gap between them is the most useful thing in the day's chip news.

All of ASML's figures below come from a single wire-service report, carried by Benzinga. They have not been checked against the company's own release, and several details need that check.

The numbers

ASML's reported third-quarter earnings were $14.88 a share against a consensus of $12.47, a beat of about 19%. Net sales for the quarter were put at $13.42bn.

Guidance is where the report is most striking. ASML sees fourth-quarter sales of €14.2bn to €15.2bn and full-year 2026 sales of €44bn to €45bn.

The currencies do not line up. ASML reports in euros, and the report gives the quarterly figures in dollars and the guidance in euros. The $13.42bn may be a conversion, or it may be a euro figure mislabelled. The difference matters for the arithmetic below.

Take the figures at face value and treat the quarter as roughly 13.4bn in euro terms. The fourth-quarter midpoint of €14.7bn would then be about 10% higher than the third quarter. Subtracting the fourth-quarter range from the full-year range implies the first nine months of the year totalled somewhere between €28.8bn and €30.8bn. That is consistent with a business that has been accelerating through the year rather than one that has just jumped.

It is a plausible ramp. It still rests on the unverified assumption that the quarter and the guidance are in the same currency, and the company's own statement of its assumptions will settle it.

Why a single supplier matters

ASML is the only maker of extreme-ultraviolet lithography machines, the tools used to print the finest circuit patterns on advanced chips. Its customers include the biggest foundries and memory makers. Because chipmakers order tools well ahead of building capacity, ASML's order book and guidance are read as an early signal of how much the industry intends to spend.

A 19% earnings beat and a full-year outlook at that scale suggest customers are still committing capital, and that AI-related demand is feeding through to equipment orders. It is a read on intentions, and it does not show that end-demand will arrive. Equipment makers have in the past been paid for capacity that later proved surplus.

The memory counter-current

Korea's KOSPI index fell sharply, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix among the casualties. The stated cause was fear of Chinese competition in memory chips. Applied Materials, a US toolmaker that supplies memory and logic fabs, has also fallen for six consecutive sessions, according to the source summary.

The sources do not say what triggered the memory fears. No new Chinese product, price cut or capacity announcement is cited. Without one, the move looks more like sentiment than a repricing of the market, though the report does not rule out a catalyst it fails to name.

The Applied Materials decline is an indirect signal. The company makes equipment and not memory, so its slide says investors are marking down future fab spending, or at least the memory share of it.

This is where ASML's guidance and the Korean sell-off could be reconciled, or collide. If Chinese memory supply grows, incumbents may cut their own spending plans. Chinese expansion would also require tools. ASML's EUV machines are subject to export controls, so Chinese fabs are largely limited to older lithography. Whether the incumbents or the challengers buy the tools, ASML's order book is probably less exposed than Samsung's pricing. The same cannot be said of every equipment supplier.

Barclays' decision to raise its SAP price target to $243 was reported the same day, but the source gives no date, rating or rationale, and the figure is in dollars for a stock primarily listed in euros. It is routine analyst activity and not evidence of anything about the chip cycle.

What to watch

The first test is ASML's own release, which should confirm the currency of the sales figure and set out the assumptions behind the fourth-quarter range. The second is whether any concrete Chinese memory development, such as pricing or capacity, appears to explain the Korean sell-off. If it does not, the retreat in Samsung and SK Hynix will be hard to separate from the wider debate over how much of this year's chip rally rests on a narrow base of AI-linked names. ASML's fourth-quarter results, due in January, will show whether the guided ramp arrived.
