Rwanda chosen to host F1 in 'landmark' move

In one sentence
Formula 1 has signed a 10-year deal for an African Grand Prix in Rwanda, but the fee, first-race year and funding are unpublished, so the sportswashing question remains open.

Formula 1 will go to Rwanda. The sport announced on 13 October a 10-year agreement for a Formula 1 African Grand Prix, presented by Visit Rwanda. It would be the first world championship race on the continent since Kyalami in 1993. The harder question is what Rwanda is paying for, and what it gets in return. Neither side has published the terms.

What has been agreed

The race is to be held on a purpose-built circuit at Bugesera, south of Kigali and beside the new international airport that is still under construction. It is planned to FIA Grade 1 standard, the licence F1 requires. Former driver Alex Wurz's design firm is reported to be involved. The project sits under the Rwanda Development Board.

A good deal is missing. F1 has given a decade rather than a date, and the first race is reported to fall in the 2030s. Neither side has disclosed the hosting fee or who funds the track. Nor has F1 said which round would be dropped from a calendar that already has 24 races and, from 2027, new ones in Portugal and Turkey.

Stefano Domenicali, F1's chief executive, promised "lasting economic and social benefits through tourism, investment, skills development and local business growth". President Paul Kagame said the day "belongs to Africa".

One correction to the framing: Rwanda would not be the first sub-Saharan host. Kyalami in South Africa staged grands prix from 1967 to 1985 and again in 1992 and 1993. What is new is the return to the continent after more than three decades.

Why Rwanda, not South Africa

South Africa looked the front-runner a year ago. Its Kyalami bid relied on ministers lining up private pledges, with one reported target of more than $100m from six firms. Rwanda arrived with one plan and a government behind it. Kagame announced the bid himself in December 2024, at the FIA's prize-giving week in Kigali. F1's own pitch is demographic: it says Africa has more than 100 million fans under 35.

The sums matter because they decide what kind of deal this is. Experts have said a purpose-built circuit could cost billions. Domenicali said in 2024 that F1 wanted Africa but needed "the right investment, and the right strategic plan". A state that pays a high fee and underwrites the track is buying something beyond a sporting event.

The sportswashing question

Critics of Kagame's government have long said it uses sport to burnish its image while restricting free speech and political opposition. The government rejects the charge. Its spokeswoman, Yolande Makolo, has said F1 would move Rwanda "from being a consumer of sport to a participant in the business of sports", part of a strategy of using sport for development.

There is also the conflict in eastern Congo. The DRC's foreign minister has urged F1 to drop Rwanda, warning of a "blood-stained association". F1 said it was monitoring developments. The SI report on the announcement does not mention human rights or the DRC, which suggests F1 did not address them in the release.

Whether this is simply commercial expansion or reputation-laundering depends on who pays. A fee funded largely by taxpayers, with the government as the main beneficiary, would sit closer to the Gulf model that F1 already has. A commercially backed track with open books would not. The contract is the evidence, and it is unpublished.

What to watch

Three things will settle it: the first-race year, the published fee and funding split, and whether F1 says anything on rights and the DRC beyond monitoring. Which existing race gives up its slot will test how far the sport's growth push overrides its established hosts.
