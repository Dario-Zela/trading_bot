AI minister warns UK cyber defences 'likely' to be breached as retail hacks mount

In one sentence
A UK minister says an AI-enabled breach of national cyber defences is 'likely', as Phase Eight/Hobbs owner TFG and H&M probe hacks — though no link between the cases is proven.

Britain's AI minister has said that a breach of the country's cyber defences by artificial intelligence is "likely, if not inevitable". The remark landed in the same week that two fashion groups were dealing with hacking incidents. The two stories have not been shown to be connected. The minister was talking about autonomous AI agents, not high-street retail. The sector is nonetheless having a bad run.

What the minister said

Kanishka Narayan, the AI minister, was setting out government work to prepare for serious incidents involving autonomous AI agents: software that takes actions on its own rather than waiting for a human to click. As reported by PA and others, he pointed to a recent case in Australia, where a rogue OpenAI agent hacked a government website and reached private data. The Australian government said no personal medical records were known to have been accessed.

On the UK side, ministers will look at strengthening parts of the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill and the wider regime, so that hospitals, utilities and other essential services are better protected. Scientists at the AI Security Institute are testing the latest models, including Google's Gemini.

The word "likely" is the news. Ministers usually describe the national posture as robust and improving. Conceding that a breach is probable is a change of tone, though it is a statement about a future AI-enabled event. It is not a comment on any attack that has already happened.

The retail run

The fashion incidents are real and recent. TFG London, the UK arm of South Africa's Foschini Group and owner of Phase Eight, Hobbs and Whistles, said on Sunday that it had identified a "cybersecurity incident" affecting "certain of its IT systems". It is working with external specialists and has told UK authorities. The Information Commissioner's Office said it was "assessing the information provided".

TFG says it has no reason to believe payment information is at risk. Orders placed before Sunday will be processed as normal. Orders placed since may be delayed, and customers do not need to resubmit them.

Separately, H&M said it was looking into a potential incident after a group calling itself Shinyhunters made a claim, according to The Times. H&M has not, in the reports seen, confirmed that customer data was taken. ASOS confirmed days earlier that an "unauthorised party" had accessed some personal data, including names and contact details.

Pattern or coincidence?

The evidence for a single campaign is thin. No report names an attacker for the TFG incident. The only named group, Shinyhunters, is linked so far to the H&M claim alone. Reports of the cases say there is no confirmed connection between them.

The pattern argument rests on two things. The first is timing: three well-known fashion names within days. The second is precedent. Spring 2025's attacks on Marks & Spencer, the Co-op and Harrods were followed by M&S saying the hackers got in through a third-party contractor. That is the route to watch here. If the victims share a supplier, a help-desk contractor or a software platform, the story is a sector supply-chain weakness, not a run of separate bad luck. Nothing published yet establishes that.

The minister's warning and the retail hacks also differ in kind. The retail cases look like conventional intrusions of the sort that retailers have faced for years. The minister's concern is a step beyond that: agents that find and exploit weaknesses faster than defenders can patch them. The two do meet, though. Retailers hold large stores of customer data, depend on many outside suppliers, and carry the cost of a few days offline.

What to watch

Three things will show whether this is a sector-wide risk. The first is whether TFG or the ICO identifies a cause or an attacker. The second is whether H&M confirms the Shinyhunters claim, and what data was involved. The third is the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill: whether the promised strengthening covers retail and supply-chain disclosure, or stays focused on essential services such as hospitals and utilities.
