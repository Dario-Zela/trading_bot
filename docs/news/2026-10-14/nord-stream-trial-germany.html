German trial of Ukrainian accused over Nord Stream sabotage begins

In one sentence
A former Ukrainian officer is on trial in Hamburg over the 2022 Nord Stream blasts, the first court test of who carried out an attack no state has formally attributed.

A former Ukrainian military officer went on trial in Hamburg on Wednesday over the sabotage of the Nord Stream gas pipelines. It is the first time the evidence has been tested in open court, four years after the blasts. The defendant is identified under German privacy rules only as Serhii K. He is 50 and the only person in the dock. He denies any involvement, according to his lawyer.

The case is narrow in form: one man, one court, a trial expected to run into February 2027. It also carries a heavier question. The attacks on 26 September 2022 have never been formally attributed by any state. A courtroom is now being asked to do what diplomacy has not.

What happened in the Baltic

Swedish seismologists recorded several blasts on 26 September 2022, roughly 17 hours apart. They damaged the Nord Stream 1 and Nord Stream 2 pipelines and caused four leaks. The explosions took place in the Swedish and Danish exclusive economic zones. Neither pipeline was carrying commercial gas flows that day. Nord Stream 1 had supplied about half of Germany's gas imports before Russia curtailed deliveries. Nord Stream 2 had been built but never certified for operation.

The damage was at once an energy story and a security one. It came seven months into Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, and it removed any prospect of the pipelines being quietly restored. It also unsettled confidence that undersea infrastructure in NATO waters could be protected.

The case against Serhii K.

German federal prosecutors allege that Serhii K. led a small team that included divers and an explosives expert. They say he planned the operation with other military personnel on behalf of unidentified Ukrainian state entities. The indictment says he entered Germany via Poland on a forged Ukrainian passport about three weeks before the attack. The group is said to have hired a sailboat in Rostock and sailed to waters near the Danish island of Bornholm. There they allegedly attached timed, military-grade explosives to the pipelines.

The charges are being an accomplice to a war crime of attacking civilian objects, together with destruction of infrastructure and causing an explosion. Whether a gas pipeline owned largely by a Russian state company counts as a protected civilian object in an armed conflict is a legal question the court will have to address.

He was arrested in Italy in August 2025 and extradited to Germany in November. Reporting from earlier this year says the Federal Court of Justice, in keeping him in custody, found with a high degree of probability that the blasts were carried out on the instructions of a foreign state, which it identified as Ukraine. I could not confirm the date or exact wording of that ruling.

The defence calls the case political and says the evidence is thin. Reports say it plans to submit written statements from two of his military commanders. Serhii K. is a serviceman and says he cannot disclose details of his activities. He is not expected to testify himself.

A second suspect, identified as Volodymyr Z., a trained diver, was arrested in Croatia, and a Croatian court has approved his extradition to Germany. A Polish court refused in October 2025 to extradite a suspect of the same name, and Berlin signalled it would not challenge that ruling. Reports about his status conflict, and he is not on trial in Hamburg.

What a verdict can and cannot settle

Ukraine's government denies any role, and President Volodymyr Zelensky has rejected the allegations. Russia denies blowing up its own pipelines. It said on Monday it expects a "just verdict" naming both the perpetrators and those who ordered the operation. Moscow has long wanted attribution to point westward, and a German court describing a Ukrainian state operation would not satisfy that demand. It would nonetheless give Russian officials a judicial finding to cite.

The case has strained relations between Berlin and Kyiv, though Germany remains one of Ukraine's strongest backers. A conviction that says Ukrainian state bodies ordered the attack would force the German government to decide how far to carry that finding into policy. An acquittal, or a verdict that leaves the question of who gave the orders open, would leave the attribution question where it has stood since 2022.

A courtroom also works within limits. Judges rule on one defendant's conduct, not on the responsibility of a state, and they hear only the evidence that is put before them. The defence's reliance on military commanders' statements suggests that questions of authority and orders will be argued indirectly.

The next markers are procedural. Sixteen hearing days are scheduled through 17 December, with more planned for January and February 2027 and no verdict date set. The first witness testimony, and any ruling on how the court treats the "civilian object" charge, will show how far the trial can go in answering the question of who ordered the attack.
