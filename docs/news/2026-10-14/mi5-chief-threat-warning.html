MI5 chief warns of Russia, Iran and AI threats but plays down Russian sway over Brexit

In one sentence
MI5's Ken McCallum named Russia 'aggressor number one' and flagged Iran and AI, but declined to confirm Russian interference in the 2016 Brexit vote and warned against making trolls look '10ft tall'.

The head of MI5 used his annual threat update on Tuesday to rank Russia as the first of the UK's state adversaries, to describe Iran as an expanding danger on British soil and to warn that artificial intelligence is changing the stakes for terrorism. It was also the day he was asked about a live political dispute, and his answer drew more attention than the threat assessment itself. Sir Ken McCallum, the director general, declined to confirm that Russia interfered in the 2016 EU referendum and warned against making Russian trolls "look 10ft tall".

The question arose because Prime Minister Andy Burnham said on Sunday that Russia had interfered in the 2016 vote. Moscow denies it. McCallum did not contradict the prime minister outright. But on the evidence he cited, he did not back the claim either.

The Brexit question

McCallum's answer separated two things that are often run together: attempts to influence British debate, and evidence that those attempts changed an outcome. Russian efforts to shape UK opinion are not new, he said. He pointed to the tools involved: state media, trolls and bot farms. Reuters quotes him as saying it is public knowledge that Russian outlets such as RT "came in pretty strongly around the time of the referendum". He described the online trolling as "comparatively unimpressive". He also stressed that the referendum result itself is not in dispute.

His caution was tactical as well as evidential. As reported by WION, he said:

"I am a little bit anxious that we don't play into our adversaries' hands by making these trolls look 10ft tall."

The logic is that overstating Russian reach does some of Moscow's work for it. It sows doubt about democratic results and flatters influence operations that may have achieved little.

The position is consistent with the official record. In July 2020 Parliament's Intelligence and Security Committee found that the government had not assessed whether Russian activity affected the 2016 vote. It also noted that, when asked for evidence, MI5 supplied a submission of just six lines. The committee's criticism was that nobody had looked properly, not that interference had been proved. McCallum's answer stays on the same ground. Russian activity is documented, but its effect on the result is unproven.

The threat picture

The rest of the speech was a broader accounting of hostile pressure. On Russia, McCallum used the phrase "aggressor number one". He said Moscow is escalating "hybrid" activity such as sabotage, cyber attacks and disinformation, while taking care to stay below the level that would trigger a NATO response. MI5 now devotes more resources to hostile states than at any point in the Cold War, he said, and the state-threats caseload has quadrupled over a decade.

He also made a case that Russia is weaker than it looks. About 1,000 Russian intelligence officers and embassy staff have been expelled since the invasion of Ukraine, which has forced the Kremlin to rely on paid intermediaries. "So we see them scrambling to pay criminals to do their bidding," he said, adding: "These are not the actions of a great power with the strategic upper hand." That fits his Brexit remarks: a service describing a diminished adversary has little reason to inflate its past successes.

On Iran, he reported more than 50 arrests in the UK over the past year linked to Iranian activity. Tehran uses criminal proxies against dissidents, Iranian media outlets and Israeli interests, and much of the targeting has fallen on Jewish communities. The notable shift is towards "harder targets, like military sites". He did not address the RAF Fairford case directly in the speech. Seven people were arrested and bailed after three suspicious vans were seen near the Gloucestershire airbase. He told reporters, "We have seen Iranian interest beyond RAF Fairford in the UK." He warned that those causing disorder on British streets, "whether hired thugs here or their paymasters overseas, will meet an unflinching response". Russia and Iran both routinely deny hostile activity in Britain.

AI and the terrorist threat

On AI, McCallum described a technology that intensifies the contest between attackers and defenders in cyberspace and allows propaganda to be tailored to its audience. MI5 has caught would-be attackers using it for reconnaissance and attack planning, and AI-enabled drones are a new problem. His gravest concern is AI-assisted development of biological weapons, a danger he said has not yet fully emerged. "These risks are real," he said, adding that human history "has given us no experience of facing an actor which is smarter than we are". MI5, GCHQ and MI6 are working with the UK's AI Security Institute.

Terrorism remains the other half of the workload. The national threat level was raised to "severe" in April, meaning an attack is highly likely, for the first time in five years. Al Qaeda and Islamic State still inspire plots, but the most notable trend, McCallum said, is extreme right-wing terrorism, where "extremist versions of anti-immigration and anti-Muslim grievance are tipping into terrorist intent". Many cases involve young people radicalised online. One was a 13-year-old whose case helped an international partner prevent a lethal attack.

What to watch

The disagreement is unlikely to stay at the level of one press question. Burnham's claim and the head of MI5's more guarded account will both be tested if opposition MPs or the Intelligence and Security Committee press for an assessment that the 2020 report said had never been done. For now, the security service's line is that Russian influence attempts are real, that their success is unproven, and that exaggerating them carries its own cost.
