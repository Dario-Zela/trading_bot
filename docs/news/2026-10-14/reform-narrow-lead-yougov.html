Reform holds narrow poll lead over Burnham's Labour as bounce fades

In one sentence
A two-week-old YouGov poll with Reform on 25% and Labour on 24% is within the margin of error, and Survation's contemporaneous six-point Labour lead argues against a clear fade.

A single opinion poll is carrying more weight than it can bear. A YouGov survey published around 30 September put Reform UK on 25% and Labour on 24%. That is a one-point lead for Nigel Farage's party over a government that has had a new prime minister for less than three months. Al Jazeera cited it as a sign of how fragile the "Burnham bounce" is. The poll is now two weeks old. One poll is not a trend.

What the poll says, and what it doesn't

Andy Burnham was declared Labour leader on 17 July and took over from Keir Starmer as prime minister on the following Monday. He had come to the leadership by an unusual route. He was the sole candidate, backed by 379 of Labour's 403 MPs, and had returned to Westminster only weeks earlier by winning the Makerfield by-election.

The early polling rewarded the change. In a YouGov survey on 9–10 August, Labour led Reform for the first time since Reform took the polling lead in early 2025. Labour was on 24%, up two points on the previous week, and Reform on 22%. That is the "bounce" in question.

The late-September poll is a different matter. Reform at 25% and Labour at 24% is a gap of one point. A national sample of a couple of thousand people carries a margin of error of roughly two to three points on each party's share, so the gap is well within the noise. The result is consistent with a tie, and with Labour still narrowly ahead. It does not show Reform in front. Compared with August, Reform has gained about three points and Labour has not moved, which is more informative than the headline gap. But that comparison also rests on one pollster's samples.

The other pollsters disagree

The wider evidence at the end of September does not support a clear fade. Survation, with fieldwork on 29 September, had Labour on 29%, up two points, and Reform on 23%, down one. That is a six-point Labour lead, from a different firm with a different method. A YouGov seat projection a week earlier showed Labour as the largest party on 241 seats but short of a majority, with the Conservatives on 130 and Reform on 123.

Pollsters differ in how they weight and prompt respondents, so their levels often diverge by a few points. What matters is the direction across several of them at once. One firm showing Reform gaining while another shows Labour gaining is the pattern that the sampling-error arithmetic would predict. It is not a reversal.

Tim Bale, a politics professor at Queen Mary University of London, has cautioned that the bounce is not "necessarily long-lasting". He has also noted that little of Labour's gain came from voters switching from Reform. That points to a more modest process. Labour was recovering support from its own earlier defectors, such as Greens and Liberal Democrats, rather than winning over Reform's base. Gains of that kind can drift back as the novelty of a new leader wears off.

What would make it a story

A fading bounce would matter beyond this one poll. It would show whether a change of leader can hold support once the early attention settles, which is the question every governing party that swaps its leader mid-term eventually faces. But the evidence so far does not settle it.

The test is whether the next batch of polls from YouGov, Survation, More in Common, Opinium and others shows Reform level with or ahead of Labour. Two or three consecutive readings in the same direction, from different firms, would carry real information. A return to the previous gap in YouGov's own weekly series would carry some. Until then, the one-point lead is a data point to track, not a finding.
