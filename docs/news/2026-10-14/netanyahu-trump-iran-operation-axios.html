Netanyahu and Trump discussed a major new Iran operation before elections, Axios reports

In one sentence
Axios reports Trump and Netanyahu discussed a major new Iran operation before elections; the White House calls it false, and Bessent keeps saying Iran's economy is collapsing.

Benjamin Netanyahu and Donald Trump discussed launching a major new military operation against Iran before the Israeli and American elections, Axios reported on Tuesday. The White House called the report false. The Israeli prime minister's office confirmed regular contact between the two leaders but would not say what they discussed. The story rests on Axios's own sourcing, and no second outlet has independently confirmed it. Other outlets have repeated Axios's account without adding to it.

What Axios reports

According to Axios, the conversation took place last week. The outlet cites two US officials, one Israeli official and a fourth source. Netanyahu is said to have pointed to Israeli intelligence suggesting Iran is taking worrying steps to rebuild its nuclear programme. Some of the follow-on coverage mentions a site known as Pickaxe Mountain, which it says Iran may be using for uranium enrichment. That detail is secondhand and unverified.

Trump, in one US official's account, told Netanyahu to "go ahead and get ready". The same official said he made no commitment on whether or when an operation would happen. Axios says the call came before Trump said publicly that he would not strike Iran ahead of the US midterms in November.

The report describes a split inside the administration. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth and US Central Command leaders are described as the strongest advocates of resuming attacks. Most of Trump's other advisers reportedly prefer to keep up economic and other pressure for now. The Israeli military and CENTCOM remain at a high state of readiness and are finishing preparations in case Trump changes his mind.

Axios also reports that Israel's chief of staff, Eyal Zamir, warned that fighting in this window could force the postponement of Israel's 27 October election, because of the Iranian missile response it would invite. That detail shows how tightly the military and political calendars are bound together.

The denial and what it leaves open

A White House official told Axios the reports were false and that Trump had ruled out attacks before the midterms. The denial and the report can both be accurate. A leader can discuss an option, ask an ally to prepare, and then decide against acting. Axios itself says the call preceded Trump's public statement. The denial therefore addresses the current decision, not whether the conversation took place.

The report is also a statement about what officials were willing to say to a reporter. Leaks of this kind can come from people who want the option kept alive, from people who want it closed off, or from people trying to signal to Tehran. The sources are anonymous, and Axios does not say which of these applies.

The economic track

The same week, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Iran's economy is "collapsing", according to the triage summary for this piece. No figures or document accompanied that remark in the material available. It fits a line he has taken for months. On 31 August, at a G20 finance meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, he said Iran's economy could collapse "within weeks or months", though not inevitably, and that the outcome depended on its leadership changing course. He added: "I would think that they are lashing out kinetically because they are losing economically."

That claim has an administration campaign behind it, which it calls "Operation Economic Outcast". Last month the US sanctioned five sectors of the Iranian economy: aviation, digital assets, gold, technology and shipping. It also sanctioned 60 individuals and vessels. It then penalised the UAE branches of Egypt's Banque Misr over alleged Iran ties. Bessent has said further steps could include cutting a bank off from the dollar system, and that new sanctions would likely come weekly.

The pairing of the two stories invites a reading in which the economic argument prepares the ground for force. That reading is an inference. The evidence for it is the timing and the shared audience, not any statement linking the two. The Axios account points the other way on one point. It says most advisers favour pressure instead of strikes, which makes the economic campaign look like the alternative to military action and not its prelude. Bessent's own framing is that economic pain is meant to bring Tehran to negotiations. Collapse language can serve either purpose, and the reporting does not show which one is in play.

The wider setting

The US and Israel first struck Iran on 28 February this year, and the confrontation passed the six-month mark in late August. The costs have reached American households. In July, overall US energy prices rose 14.7% from a year earlier and petrol rose 24.6%, according to figures cited at the time. Those numbers help explain why a pre-election strike would be a gamble for the White House. Renewed fighting could push oil higher and complicate the Federal Reserve's task. Treasury yields are already near 5.2%, and Fed officials are split on further rate rises.

For Netanyahu the arithmetic differs. An election on 27 October gives him reasons to want the security question settled, and Zamir's warning shows that his own military sees the risks. For Trump, the midterms cut the other way. His public promise to hold off before November is the clearest constraint, and a reversal would have to be explained to voters.

Regional spillover is already visible. Missiles were intercepted near Riyadh this week after a Saudi-led coalition strike on a Yemeni fuel depot, and Britain's MI5 chief has named Iran among the main threats to the country.

What to watch

The first marker is Israel's 27 October vote and whether Netanyahu's government signals any change in posture before it. The second is the US midterms in November, which Trump has tied to his promise of restraint. Between them, the tests are practical. Watch for an on-record confirmation or a second outlet matching Axios's account. Watch also for the next weekly round of Treasury sanctions, and for any movement of US forces. A bank cut off from the dollar system would show the economic track hardening. A strike order would show it had been overtaken.
