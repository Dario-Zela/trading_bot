Treasury yields hold near 5.2% as Fed officials split on whether more hikes are needed

In one sentence
The 10-year Treasury yield is near 5.22% even as Williams sees inflation near 2% in 2027, while Collins reportedly sees more hikes ahead and Goolsbee flags AI-driven pricing.

Long-dated US Treasury yields are sitting above 5%, and the Federal Reserve's own officials do not agree on what that should mean. The 10-year yield was around 5.22% on Wednesday, the 30-year around 5.57% and the 2-year about 4.80%. John Williams, president of the New York Fed, said inflation should approach 2% in 2027. A bond market that demands more than 5% to lend for ten years is not pricing a smooth return to target.

What the curve says

The quoted yields put the 2-year to 10-year spread at roughly 42 basis points and the 10-year to 30-year spread at roughly 35. Both are positive, so the curve slopes upward: investors want more compensation the longer they lend.

That shape is consistent with a market that sees the policy rate as close to its peak but is unwilling to price a quick return to low rates. A 4.80% two-year yield suggests short rates are expected to stay high. A 5.57% thirty-year yield suggests investors also want a wide cushion against inflation or fiscal supply further out.

The yield levels alone do not show which of those worries dominates. A yield embeds expected policy rates, expected inflation and a term premium, and the quoted figures cannot separate them. What they do show is that the long end has not moved toward Williams's 2027 forecast. If it had, long-dated yields would be easier to explain with a falling-rate path. They sit where they would if investors doubted the path.

A divided Fed

Williams's comment is the optimistic pole. He sees inflation nearing 2% in 2027 and no need for a rate rise that year.

Boston Fed President Susan Collins is reported to see it differently. Bloomberg, via Benzinga, reports her saying rates will likely need to rise further this year to rein in inflation. That account has not been independently verified. She is said to be focused on how broad price pressures are across the economy, which is a conditional stance: more hikes if inflation is widespread, fewer if it is concentrated.

The two are not strictly contradictory. Collins is talking about this year and Williams about 2027, and a central bank can raise rates now precisely so that it need not later. But the gap in emphasis matters to a bond market trying to price the path. One official points to a destination, the other to unfinished work on the way there.

The AI variable

A third voice adds a different source of pressure. Austan Goolsbee, president of the Chicago Fed, has said that AI-boom pricing 'jumps out of its lane', according to a Semafor report republished by Benzinga. The framing ties inflation to AI-related demand for power and hardware. The republished item gave no figures or dates, and the original Semafor piece should be checked before any numbers are attached to the claim.

If the argument holds, AI investment is an input to rate-setting and not only a technology story. Heavy demand for electricity and chips can lift prices in specific sectors, and those increases can spread into the broader measures the Fed targets. That would fit Collins's test of how broad price pressures are. It would also give long-dated investors a reason to doubt a clean path back to 2%, and the Fed's own projections do not obviously account for it.

Why the gap matters

Long-term yields are benchmarks for mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs and the discount rates used to value equities. A 10-year yield above 5% raises the hurdle for each of them. If the Fed's outlook proves right and inflation nears 2% in 2027, long yields have room to fall. If Collins's more cautious reading proves right, they may stay where they are or rise.

The evidence for any turn is thin today. This is one day's yield levels set against one official's forecast, and the Collins and Goolsbee remarks are secondhand. The next inflation readings and further Fed speeches are the likeliest catalysts. The question for them is whether price pressures look broad, as Collins's test implies, or contained, as Williams's forecast assumes. The long end will show which view investors are adopting.
