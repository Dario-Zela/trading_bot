UK will not put tariffs on Chinese vehicles, says Jonathan Reynolds

In one sentence
Jonathan Reynolds is reported to have ruled out UK tariffs on Chinese vehicles, but early-October reports of a tariff package leave it unclear whether this is settled policy.

Britain's government has signalled that it will not impose tariffs on Chinese-built vehicles, including electric cars. If the position holds, the UK would stand apart from the European Union, which has put anti-subsidy duties on Chinese EVs. The remarks are attributed to Jonathan Reynolds, and the only source for the new report is the Guardian's politics coverage.

That source gives no date, figures or direct quotes. It does not say whether this is a formal policy decision or a remark made in passing, and the weight of the story depends on which it is.

A position that has moved before

The stance fits a pattern, but the pattern has not been steady. In 2024, Reynolds said the UK had no plans to follow the EU's tariffs on Chinese electric vehicles. He pointed to the absence of any request from the domestic industry to the Trade Remedies Authority, though he did not close the door entirely. Reporting from late September this year, as relayed by Auto Express, had him again saying there were no plans to raise duties on Chinese car imports.

Early October brought a different signal. The Times reported that ministers were drawing up a package of possible tariffs, out of concern about subsidised Chinese cars being sold in Britain, and that they were prepared to match the EU's levy. Reuters said it could not immediately verify that report. The government's line was that no tariffs had been imposed and no final decision made. Separate reporting said the EU had urged the UK to take a tougher line.

The new remarks therefore read as a reversal of that early-October reporting, or a rebuttal of it. The brief does not say which.

What is at stake

Chinese brands now hold roughly 16% of the UK new-car market, according to industry reports. Chinese vehicles currently face the UK's standard 10% import duty, without the additional anti-subsidy layer the EU applies.

The choice has costs either way. Tariffs would protect domestic producers and their suppliers from price competition, but would raise prices for buyers and risk retaliation, which matters in an industry that exports heavily. Leaving the market open favours consumers and fleet buyers shifting to electric models, but puts pressure on British assemblers and component makers. A firm no-tariff position would shape pricing and investment decisions across that chain. It would also mark a divergence in trade policy from Brussels.

What to watch

The test is whether the position is written down. A statement from the Department for Business and Trade, or the absence of any referral to the Trade Remedies Authority, would show whether this is settled policy. Any further briefing on the tariff package the Times described would show the same. Until then, the reported stance is best treated as a signal rather than a decision.
