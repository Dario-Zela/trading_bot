Vatican says pope diagnosed with lung cancer, surgery successful

In one sentence
The Vatican says Pope Leo XIV has lung adenocarcinoma and that surgery to remove his right lower lung lobe went well, but it has not disclosed the stage or lymph-node status.

The Vatican said on Tuesday that Pope Leo XIV, 71, has lung cancer. The diagnosis came after surgeons at Rome's Gemelli Hospital removed the lower lobe of his right lung. The Holy See described the operation as "successful" and said it had "no complications".

The announcement ended a short run of disclosures. On Sunday, 12 October, the Vatican said a nodular lesion had been found in the pope's lung and that he would have surgery. In his Sunday blessing, Leo said: "The doctors believe it's best to take action, so, following their advice, I've decided to have surgery to remove it." The reports do not say how the lesion was detected.

What the Vatican has said

The operation took place on Tuesday, 13 October. According to the Vatican's medical bulletin, as reported by CBS News, it used a video-assisted technique, and the lower lobe was removed "to ensure a radical resection". The surgery lasted about 90 minutes, and the pope was in the operating room for roughly three hours. The team was led by Dr Stefano Margaritora, Gemelli's chief thoracic surgeon.

The bulletin said Leo "regained consciousness normally" and was taken to a room on the hospital's tenth floor. He is staying in a room near the usual papal suite at the hospital because the papal apartment is being renovated, according to the reports.

The cancer was identified as lung adenocarcinoma. This is a common form of non-small-cell lung cancer, and it is the point where the account is thinnest.

What has not been said

The Vatican has not disclosed the stage of the cancer, or whether it has spread. It has not said whether lymph nodes were involved, or whether the pope will need further treatment. Dr David Jones of Memorial Sloan Kettering told CBS News that more pathology information is needed to determine next steps, including the lymph-node question.

The word "successful" therefore needs care. It describes the operation. On the evidence published so far, it is not a statement about the disease. Before surgery, oncologists cited in early reports said the prognosis would be "excellent" if the nodule proved cancerous and was removed at an early stage. That was a conditional assessment made without pathology results, and the Vatican has not confirmed that the cancer was early-stage. These reports all date from the same day, and later bulletins may change the detail.

Handling duties and what comes next

The Vatican Press Office has cancelled the pope's audiences through 20 October. Under Vatican protocol, cardinals and aides will take on his duties as needed. The recommendation after this type of procedure is typically to avoid air travel for about three weeks. That bears on a South American trip planned to begin on 6 November. There is no word yet on whether it will go ahead.

The next markers are the Gemelli medical bulletins, and above all the final pathology findings on staging and lymph nodes. Those will indicate whether further treatment is planned, and the Vatican's decision on the November trip will be the first public sign of how it expects the recovery to go.
