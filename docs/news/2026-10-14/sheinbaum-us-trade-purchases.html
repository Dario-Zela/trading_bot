Sheinbaum vows to buy more US goods to secure a trade deal

In one sentence
Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum has pledged to buy more US goods to help close a trade deal, though terms, volumes and any US response remain unreported.

Mexico's president has said she will buy more American goods as part of an effort to close a trade agreement with Washington. The pledge, reported by Bloomberg, offers purchases as a concession rather than a change to tariffs or rules. Few details have been made public. No volumes, dollar values, product categories or deadlines accompanied the report, and no US counterpart was quoted.

A familiar argument

The offer fits a position Claudia Sheinbaum has been developing for months. Late last month, Spanish-language outlets reported that she argued the US trade deficit with Mexico should be narrowed by Mexico importing more, rather than by curbing Mexican exports. That framing gives Donald Trump something to point to. He has long treated the bilateral deficit as the measure of whether trade with Mexico is fair.

The pressure behind it is concrete. Mexico faces a 50% US tariff on steel, aluminium and derivative products, and a 25% tariff on vehicles, according to September reporting. Both hit sectors central to the integrated North American supply chains that run through Mexican factories. Mexico is negotiating directly to remove or reduce those duties.

The tone from Washington has not been warm. One account of the president's remarks has him calling the existing trade agreement 'irrelevant' and saying the US does not need products made in Mexico or Canada. Sheinbaum avoided a public confrontation and pointed instead to the investments US companies have made in Mexico. That has been her consistent approach since early 2025. After a call with Trump in May of that year she described a good conversation, while saying no specific agreement had been reached.

What a purchase pledge does and does not do

Buying more American goods costs Mexico relatively little, and may largely formalise existing trends. The US is already Mexico's dominant supplier of fuel, machinery, agricultural products and components. A headline commitment could therefore be met in part by trade that would happen anyway. Whether Washington accepts that as a real concession depends on terms that have not been published.

The approach also differs from what other partners have been asked for. Purchase pledges treat trade as a ledger to be balanced. Rules-based agreements, such as the USMCA, work by fixing tariffs and origin requirements. If purchase commitments become a standard bargaining chip, they would sit awkwardly beside those rules, and the effect on the planned review of the North American pact is unclear.

What to watch

The report rests on a single source, and the details, including whether Mexico has put a figure on its offer, remain to be confirmed. The first test is whether the US side responds with relief on the steel, aluminium or vehicle tariffs. The next marker is any published text of an agreement or a joint statement setting out purchase amounts and the tariff cuts offered in return. Without tariff relief, the pledge would be a one-sided gesture.
