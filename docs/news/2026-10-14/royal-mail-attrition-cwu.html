Royal Mail losing 400 postal workers a week, union says

In one sentence
The CWU says Royal Mail is losing about 400 workers a week, up from 330, as a new delivery system rolls out; Royal Mail has not confirmed the figure.

Royal Mail is losing about 400 postal workers a week, according to the Communication Workers Union (CWU). The union ties the departures to a new delivery system that is still being rolled out, and the claim lands at an awkward moment: Christmas is approaching, and the company has been under Czech ownership since mid-2025.

The figure is the union's, and Royal Mail has not confirmed it. The CWU puts the current rate at roughly 400 leavers a week, up from about 330 before the new system was introduced earlier this year. The workforce is about 83,000, so 400 a week is under 0.5% of staff. Over a year it comes to around 20,000 people. The union says most leavers are believed to be new starters on part-time contracts, but that departures of staff on legacy contracts have also risen. It has not published the underlying data.

The system behind the exits

The system is called DM26. It reorganises delivery routes, groups workers into teams of three, and delivers second-class post only every other weekday. Royal Mail says it is in about 1,000 of its 1,200 delivery offices. The CWU says it works in about 500 offices but has run into significant problems in hundreds of others, and it has told members to pause further rollout until a plan is agreed with the company.

Martin Walsh of the CWU put the staffing argument bluntly:

If a boat is already taking on water, adding more passengers is unlikely to solve the problem.

The BBC, which first reported the claim, spoke to more than 30 postal staff. Several said parcels were being prioritised and letters left behind, and that time was lost sorting mail into first and second class. Eight workers who tracked their step counts said their daily totals had risen by a quarter or a third since the switchover, often to about 25,000 a day. One former carrier in Bedfordshire, Bryn Dymott, left in July after nine years. He said he could not face another "shambolic" Christmas. These are anecdotes from a self-selected group of workers, and they do not amount to a measure of attrition.

The company's case

Royal Mail says the reforms are part of a plan for a more "efficient and financially sustainable" service. It says the rollout is 90% complete and expects it to be running in every office by Christmas. It also says it now delivers to 95% of addresses daily, up from 88% before the rollout. It is hiring an additional 22,000 temporary workers for the festive peak.

Those claims can be checked in a way the attrition figure cannot. Royal Mail's delivery performance is reported to Ofcom, which has fined the company more than £37m over the past three years for missing delivery targets. The regulator says it will keep holding the company to account. Postal Services Minister Kate Dearden has called the improvement plan "a step in the right direction" that must deliver tangible improvements for customers. A group of MPs was due to meet Ofcom.

What the number would mean

Royal Mail's owner, Daniel Křetínský's EP Group, completed its takeover of the parent company, International Distribution Services, in 2025 in a deal worth about £3.6bn. A golden share held by the UK government protects the headquarters location and UK tax residence. Separately, Royal Mail announced on 7 October plans to cut 2,500 head office and support roles by the end of 2027. The cuts and the attrition claim are different things, but both bear on how lean the owner intends the network to be.

If the 400 figure holds, the arithmetic is uncomfortable. Churn among new part-timers is a normal feature of the sector, but a rising rate among experienced legacy staff would mean the loss of the route knowledge that peak-season delivery depends on. A rate that keeps rising while a new rota is still being introduced would also make the pause the union is demanding harder to resist.

The next data points are Royal Mail's own Christmas delivery performance, the Ofcom quality-of-service figures, and whether the company agrees to the union's request to pause rollout before its self-imposed Christmas deadline.
