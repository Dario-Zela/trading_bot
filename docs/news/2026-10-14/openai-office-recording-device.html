Saudi man charged with planting 'sophisticated' recording device in OpenAI office

In one sentence
A former OpenAI engineer, Saudi national Abdulrahman Alfozan, is charged with hiding an audio recorder in an executive conference room; he has not been shown to have taken any model data.

A Saudi national who worked as an engineer at OpenAI has been charged in a US federal court with secretly recording conversations at the company's San Francisco offices. Prosecutors say he hid a covert audio recorder in an executive conference room. The charge is an accusation, not a finding, and none of the allegations has been tested in court.

The man, Abdulrahman Alfozan, is 35. The Justice Department says he joined OpenAI as an engineer in July 2025. He was arrested on 9 October and made his first appearance in federal court on 13 October. He is charged with using, or attempting to use, a device to intercept oral communications. The maximum sentence is five years in prison and a $250,000 fine. The US Attorney's Office for the Northern District of California is prosecuting, and the FBI is investigating.

What prosecutors allege

According to the Justice Department, a recorder was found on 30 September plugged into a USB port on the back of a television in an executive conference room. It was wrapped in toilet paper, apparently to conceal or muffle it, and was thought to have been installed around 28 September. Its contents were encrypted.

A second, similar recorder was reportedly found on 5 October in a bag that Alfozan had left unattended in another OpenAI building.

The complaint also cites other evidence:

  • CCTV footage: It allegedly shows him near the executive conference room on several dates, though he had no authority to be there. On 5 October he allegedly looked behind the television.
  • Chatbot prompts: Between 24 and 30 September, a chatbot account linked to his spouse allegedly received prompts about building audio recorders and encrypting their storage. Some allegedly referred to specifics of the first device.
  • Earlier queries: He had allegedly used a productivity tool to look up building addresses and meeting-room details.

OpenAI has said it used its own models to analyse the device and trace where it came from. That, it says, pointed to a specific employee who had planted it a few days earlier. The company referred the matter to the FBI and fired him.

What is not alleged

The Justice Department says the aim was to capture sensitive discussions about OpenAI's "super intelligence" technology. The release does not give a personal motive, and the sources reviewed do not say who, if anyone, he was working for. Nor does the Saudi nationality, on its own, establish a state link, so that should not be read into the case.

OpenAI says it has no indication that he improperly accessed model weights, source code or customer data. By the company's account, then, no technical secrets were taken. The charge covers recording conversations, and no recording has been described as successfully captured and removed.

Senior officials framed the case in national-security terms. FBI Director Kash Patel said Alfozan "allegedly concealed a covert recording device inside his employer's executive conference room." US Attorney Craig Missakian said: "For us it is a matter of national security."

Why it matters

Most public discussion of AI-lab security is about hacking: stolen model weights, compromised accounts, poisoned data. This case points at an older and simpler route. Executives at frontier labs discuss strategy, safety decisions and research direction in ordinary rooms, and an insider with a badge and a USB-powered recorder does not need to defeat any network defences.

The case also shows the limits of what the public record supports. The device was reportedly crude, and the prosecution leans heavily on mundane evidence: camera footage and chatbot logs. Whether it amounts to organised espionage or something narrower will depend on what the encrypted recordings contain, and on whether investigators find anyone behind the engineer.

The next step is the court process in the Northern District of California, where a grand jury indictment or further hearings would show how much of the complaint prosecutors can support. The questions to watch are whether the recordings are decrypted, and whether any third party is named.
