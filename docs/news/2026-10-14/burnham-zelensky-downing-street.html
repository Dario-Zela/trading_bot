Burnham hosts Zelensky at No 10 in show of 'rock-solid' support

In one sentence
Burnham hosted Zelensky at No 10, promising faster missile deliveries and an LNG sanctions push, while the US intelligence-sharing dispute remains reported but unconfirmed.

Andy Burnham received Volodymyr Zelensky at Downing Street on Wednesday. The Prime Minister used the visit to announce faster deliveries of British air-defence missiles and to describe UK backing for Ukraine as "rock solid". The meeting is routine on paper. It comes days after Washington struck a diesel deal with Moscow that Kyiv has openly resented, and it is being read against that.

One caution first. The triage notes for this piece said Washington's intelligence-sharing stance was "restated" during the visit. The reporting reviewed here does not support that wording. The coverage of the visit itself, including the Brit Brief account of the Downing Street programme, does not mention intelligence sharing. The intelligence question belongs to a separate, earlier strand of reporting, set out below.

What Burnham offered

Burnham, who took office in July, framed the visit in terms of British security as much as Ukrainian. "Our rock-solid support to Ukraine is vital for keeping us safe here in the UK," he said. He added: "We are with you for as long as it takes," and, "Russia's aggression cannot be allowed to succeed on our continent."

The substance is modest but specific. The UK will speed up deliveries of British-made lightweight multi-role missiles, which are meant to help Ukraine counter jet-powered drones. They form part of an existing £1.6bn package to supply more than 5,000 of the missiles. No separate figure was given for the accelerated tranche.

Sanctions were the second strand. Burnham and his ministers will push for a full international maritime services ban on Russian liquefied natural gas exports. Chancellor John Healey is due to press the case at the International Monetary Fund summit in Bangkok.

The two have met before. Zelensky visited a naval base in Portsmouth in July, and Burnham travelled to Kyiv in August. Wednesday's talks fit a pattern of early and frequent contact, which suits a new prime minister keen to show continuity with his predecessors on Ukraine.

The Washington backdrop

The awkward context is American. On 9 October Donald Trump said he had agreed with Vladimir Putin on supplies of Russian diesel to US and global markets. Zelensky has been angry about it. He said subsequent Russian attacks on civilian targets amounted to a "thanks" from Putin. Trump has also called for Ukraine to have a new leader and announced what he described as an energy-infrastructure ceasefire, which Zelensky said "was news to him".

The intelligence thread sits inside this. Axios reported, citing a Ukrainian official, that US envoys Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner warned that Washington could end intelligence sharing if Kyiv kept striking Russian oil refineries. The Financial Times reported similarly, citing people familiar with talks in Miami. A US official denied that any threat was made, saying the envoys stressed that Washington supplies Ukraine with munitions and intelligence. A separate US official told Axios the diesel deal was struck after Zelensky ignored repeated requests to halt the refinery attacks.

Those accounts rest on anonymous officials, and Ukraine has not officially confirmed the threat. Trump's own line, reported in the days since, is that intelligence sharing with Ukraine continues. That is a restatement of an existing position and not a change in it. A senior Ukrainian official has said Kyiv will keep hitting Russian refineries, and the Ukrainian team cut short its US trip.

What the visit does and does not show

There is a temptation to treat the Downing Street meeting as a rebuke to Washington. The evidence does not go that far. Burnham's quoted remarks are about Russia and British security, and none of them, in the reporting available, mentions the American deal or the intelligence dispute. The sanctions push on Russian LNG does cut across a US decision to ease the market for Russian diesel, but the government has not presented it that way.

The precedent is instructive. When Zelensky met Keir Starmer in Downing Street in March 2025, the day after the Oval Office confrontation with Trump and JD Vance, London's message was one of reassurance to Kyiv. The present visit has the same function, with the difference that this time the strain with Washington is about oil, targeting and leverage, not a televised row.

The practical limit is also familiar. Britain can deliver missiles, sanctions pressure and political cover. It cannot replace American intelligence and targeting data, which is why the unconfirmed threat to the flow of that material matters more to Kyiv than any single communiqué.

What to watch

The first marker is Healey's pitch on Russian LNG in Bangkok, which will show whether other governments will follow London on a maritime services ban. The second is whether Washington's public line on intelligence sharing holds if Ukraine continues to strike Russian refineries, as Kyiv says it will. A confirmed change in either would turn a routine visit into a genuine sign of divergence.
