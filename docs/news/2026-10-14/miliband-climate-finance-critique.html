Miliband says the global financial system is failing developing countries

In one sentence
Foreign secretary Ed Miliband told the IMF and World Bank meetings in Bangkok that the global financial system is failing poorer countries, and proposed debt, crisis-finance and restructuring reforms.

Britain's foreign secretary has put the structure of global lending at the centre of his first big international pitch. Speaking in Bangkok on Wednesday, ahead of the IMF and World Bank annual meetings, Ed Miliband said the international financial system "is failing to serve developing countries on debt, on resilience and on climate". He called for a "new deal" for governments struggling under heavy debt.

The framing needs a correction on one point. Miliband, who was previously energy secretary, now holds the foreign affairs brief. He took the UK's seat on the World Bank board himself, rather than leaving it to the development minister, Kirsty McNeill. That choice signals how much weight he places on the issue.

The account here rests on the Guardian's report as carried by AOL and a second syndicated copy. The speech text itself was not independently checked.

What he is proposing

The speech is more specific than a general complaint about the system. The reported proposals are:

  • Longer repayment and cheaper refinancing. Multilateral development banks, the World Bank among them, would use their balance sheets to stretch repayment periods and refinance at better rates, creating room in government budgets.
  • Crisis money arranged in advance. Funding would be "teed up in advance of crises", so governments get support within days rather than months.
  • An overhaul of the "common framework". This is the process for restructuring the debts of countries that cannot pay, and it is widely seen as slow and cumbersome. The UK wants to stop private creditors holding up negotiations, as happened in Ethiopia's case.
  • A pitch to private capital. Miliband argues that a healthier system for emerging economies will draw in more private investment, a stated goal of World Bank president Ajay Banga.

He described the status quo as "not just against our values but against our national interest". He also invoked the 2005 Gleneagles summit, where Britain's G8 chairmanship led to large-scale debt write-downs for more than 30 countries. Resources are tighter now, so the emphasis falls on refinancing and process reform rather than blanket cancellation.

He spoke at the launch of a Rockefeller Foundation report. It calls for a "surge" in affordable IMF and World Bank financing for debt-burdened countries. Campaigners put the problem in stark terms: in sub-Saharan Africa, more than three times as much is spent on debt servicing as on education.

Doubts about the cost

Tim Jones, policy director at Debt Justice, welcomed the focus but set a test. Unless private creditors share the costs, he warned, "public money will just bail out private lenders" rather than funding investment. The proposals, as reported, say private lenders should not be able to stall talks. They do not say whether those lenders would have to accept losses.

There is also a credibility question at home. Labour's 2024 manifesto promised to restore Britain's development reputation, but Keir Starmer later cut the aid budget to pay for defence. Moves that mostly redeploy multilateral balance sheets cost the Treasury little. That may be part of their appeal, and it is also the point critics will probe.

What comes next

The next test arrives this week. Chancellor John Healey is due to set out the UK's G20 priorities in Bangkok, including mobilising more resources from IMF and World Bank balance sheets. Britain takes over the G20 presidency next year. That forum includes China, India, South Africa and Brazil, and it will discuss sovereign debt. Whether Miliband's proposals become G20 outcomes will depend on whether private creditors, and China as a major official lender, accept the changes.
