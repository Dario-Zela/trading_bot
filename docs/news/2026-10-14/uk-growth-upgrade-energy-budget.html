UK growth forecast upgraded, but the energy shock is expected to bite in 2027 as retailers lobby on Budget costs

In one sentence
The OECD and IMF have raised UK 2026 growth forecasts, but expect energy prices to slow growth in 2027, leaving rate and Budget choices harder as retailers lobby on costs.

Britain's economy is growing faster this year than forecasters expected. The same forecasters say that will not last. Both the OECD and the IMF have raised their 2026 UK growth estimates, and both attach a warning: higher energy prices are expected to weigh on activity in 2027. For investors the question is how much the upgrade is worth against the slowdown that follows it.

The upgrade, and its limit

The OECD lifted its 2026 UK forecast to 1.1% from 0.9%, citing solid domestic demand in the second quarter. It expects growth to ease to 1.0% in 2027, with energy prices and borrowing costs both still in play. The IMF moved its 2026 figure to 1.0% from 0.8%. It also warned that higher energy prices will push headline inflation above target for a time, and that this could delay the return to the 2% goal.

On the OECD's numbers, the 2027 drag is modest. A 0.1-point fall in growth from one year to the next is hardly a cliff. The point is that the 2026 gain is not carrying over: the stronger year does not lift the one that follows. The IMF's 2027 figure is not quoted here.

The central forecasts are the milder end of the range. EY's July outlook put 2026 growth at 0.9% and 2027 at 1.2%. It also ran a downside case in which the Strait of Hormuz stays closed until early or mid-2027. In that scenario UK GDP growth falls to 0.5% this year and GDP contracts by 0.2% in 2027. Those scenarios rest on very different assumptions about energy prices and how long they last, and they should be read as bounds, not as predictions.

Why energy matters more for the UK

Energy costs reach the UK economy through two channels, and both bear on rate expectations. The first is inflation. Higher gas and fuel prices lift the headline rate directly, which gives the Bank of England less room to cut. The second is demand. Households and firms spending more on energy have less to spend elsewhere, which is the mechanism behind the 2027 slowdown the OECD describes.

That combination is the awkward one for gilts and sterling. Slower growth would normally argue for lower rates. Inflation held above target by energy argues against. A central bank facing both tends to move slowly, and the forecasts' own language, that the return to target may be delayed, points the same way. Markets will be weighing which force the Bank judges the bigger one.

Retailers press their case

The cost side is arriving through the Budget. The British Retail Consortium, backed by a letter from bosses at more than 80 high street firms including Tesco, Sainsbury's and John Lewis, has urged Chancellor John Healey to restore the employer National Insurance threshold to £6,000 from £5,000. The 2024 Budget raised the employer rate to 15% and cut the threshold from £9,100 to £5,000.

The BRC says those changes added more than £2.3bn to retailers' costs, with minimum wage increases adding a further £4.1bn. It says the sector has shed 115,000 jobs in two years. Helen Dickinson of the BRC said "soaring employment costs" are making it harder to offer entry-level jobs. The group also wants lower energy bills for shops and an exemption from the higher business rates multiplier.

The government has pointed to a £2.5bn youth employment support package, including £3,000 payments covering wages for six months for people out of work long term, and lower hiring costs for under-21s and apprentices.

The link to the growth story is direct. Retailers are among the heaviest users of low-paid, young labour, and they would absorb the energy shock on top of payroll costs. If the OECD's slowdown arrives as forecast, a sector already cutting jobs is where it would show first. The cost to the Treasury of a threshold rise has not been published in the material reviewed here, and it is the number the Chancellor will weigh against a tight fiscal position.

What to watch

The Budget is the next fixed event, and the forecast the Office for Budget Responsibility publishes alongside it will give an official 2027 growth figure to set against the OECD's 1.0%. The Bank of England's next decision will show whether policymakers are leaning towards the inflation risk or the growth risk. Energy prices, and in the downside case the Strait of Hormuz, will decide which of the forecasters' scenarios turns out nearer to right.
