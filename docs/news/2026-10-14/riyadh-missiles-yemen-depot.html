Missiles intercepted near Riyadh after Saudi-led coalition hits Yemen fuel depot

In one sentence
After a Saudi-led strike on a Yemeni fuel depot, missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh were intercepted, raising the risk of a wider conflict near Gulf oil infrastructure.

A Saudi-led coalition strike on a fuel depot near Sanaa on Tuesday night was followed within hours by missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh and other Saudi cities. The Saudi military says it intercepted them. The exchange is the latest in a conflict that has been moving steadily toward Saudi territory since July, and it now lands on the energy infrastructure that oil markets watch most closely.

The account below rests largely on statements from the coalition and the Houthi movement, relayed by the BBC and The National. Neither side's claims have been independently verified, and the reported counts of projectiles differ between outlets.

The exchange

The coalition said it hit the al-Sabaha facility of the Yemen Petroleum Company near Sanaa on Tuesday, 13 October. Before the strike, spokesman Maj Gen Turki al-Maliki published an order on social media, with a map, urging civilians to leave the area. The Houthi-run company said the site "does not contain any stock of petroleum products." According to The National, the coalition also said it struck fuel-loading piers at the Red Sea port of Hodeidah. Al-Maliki's justification was that the Houthis were using civilian infrastructure to support military operations and supply their arsenals.

Al-Maliki then said that missiles and drones aimed at Riyadh and other cities had been intercepted, giving a figure of four missiles and two drones near the capital. The National, citing the same spokesman, reported a smaller tally: three missiles launched toward Saudi Arabia on Monday evening and Tuesday, one aimed at Riyadh and two at Khamis Mushait. The two accounts may describe different time windows. They have not been reconciled.

No casualties were reported from the interceptions. Fires did break out at an Aramco administration building in Dhahran, which the kingdom's ministry described as limited. The Houthis had earlier named the Riyadh airport and the Aramco refinery in Riyadh, along with Aramco's headquarters in Dammam, as targets. They have declared Saudi oil installations "legitimate targets" and warned civilians working there to stay away.

A ceasefire that unravelled

Yemen's civil war began in 2014, when the Houthis ousted the internationally recognised government from Sanaa. A Saudi-led coalition intervened in 2015. An informal truce held for roughly four years, until July, when the Houthis declared a "maritime embargo" on Saudi Arabia and began attacking Saudi airports, oil facilities and tankers. They cited a Saudi blockade and an air strike on Sanaa airport.

The escalation since has been concrete. A missile strike on King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on Saturday killed 13 people and injured 308, according to The National; the BBC reported that another person died in a similar attack the following day. In September the Houthis seized part of Yemen's south-western Red Sea coast near the Bab al-Mandab Strait, a chokepoint for global shipping. Last week, pro-government forces regained territory overlooking the strait with Saudi air support.

The human cost inside Yemen is also rising. The UN says hundreds have been killed since August, including at least 54 civilians, half of them children. More than 216,000 people have been displaced, and suspected cholera cases rose from 217 to 647 in three weeks. In Sanaa, residents queued at petrol stations on Tuesday afternoon, fearing shortages after the coalition's warning about the depot.

Energy and diplomacy

The backdrop for markets is a tight one. The BBC links the Houthi advance to a rise in oil prices that followed six months of war between the US, Israel and Iran. None of the reports reviewed gives a price figure for Wednesday, and the reaction in crude is not yet established.

Political reaction has been quick. Saudi Arabia's cabinet, chaired by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, said the Houthis would "bear the consequences." Mahdi al-Mashat, head of the Houthi Supreme Political Council, vowed to keep fighting. President Trump said the Houthis were "behaving very badly" and trying to drive up oil prices. He did not say whether he would help Saudi Arabia retaliate. The IMF chief warned of "potentially negative spillovers for everybody, everywhere." Egypt's foreign minister, Badr Abdelatty, telephoned his Iranian counterpart, Abbas Araghchi, to press for an immediate halt to attacks on Saudi Arabia, saying there could be "no justification" for them.

Life in Riyadh has not stopped. Residents told The National that roads were busy and offices open, though schools have moved to remote learning and travel has been disrupted.

What to watch

The nearest tests are practical. One is whether the Houthis follow through on their threats against the Riyadh refinery and Aramco's Dammam headquarters, which would turn a contest over Yemeni territory into a direct threat to export capacity. Another is whether the Hodeidah piers and the Bab al-Mandab coastline become the next focus of fighting, because that is where Red Sea shipping is exposed. A third is whether diplomatic pressure, through Cairo or Tehran, produces any restoration of the truce. Crude prices over the coming sessions will show how seriously traders judge those risks.
