Rupert Lowe set to earn as much from X posts as from being an MP

Rupert Lowe, independent MP for Great Yarmouth and leader of Restore Britain — Laurie Noble Photography / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rupert Lowe's declared X earnings are closing in on his £98,599 MP salary, exposing gaps in disclosure rules built for boardrooms, not platform payouts.

Rupert Lowe donates his MP's salary to charity, he says, so he wouldn't be in it for the money even if he wanted to be. But the money is arriving anyway — just not from Parliament.

A Guardian analysis of the register of MPs' financial interests shows the independent member for Great Yarmouth has earned over £105,000 from posting on Elon Musk's X since he was elected in July 2024, more than half of it in 2026 alone. His hourly rate has climbed sharply too: £377 on average last year, £932 since January, and £1,476 since the start of June — a rate that, extrapolated across a working year, would outstrip what most MPs take home from Parliament altogether.

MPs' basic salary rose to £98,599 in April, after a benchmarking-plus-cost-of-living increase from the Independent Parliamentary Standards Authority. Lowe's projected 2026 X income, on current trend, comes in at just over £93,000 — closing in on that figure, and on course to overtake it if the summer's pace holds.

A declaration, not a category

What makes the figures visible at all is a quirk of the disclosure system rather than any deliberate transparency push. MPs must register outside earnings, and X's revenue-sharing programme pays creators per post based on engagement from verified subscribers — so Lowe's declarations list not just the sums but the hours logged against each payment, letting anyone do the hourly-rate maths the Guardian did.

That register was built for consultancy fees, directorships and after-dinner speeches — discrete, contracted, easily labelled outside interests. A platform payout that fluctuates with algorithm changes and post virality doesn't map cleanly onto those categories, and there's no requirement for the Commons authorities to track or aggregate social-media income across the House the way they might a shareholding or a directorship. Lowe's case is visible mainly because he is, by a wide margin, the highest earner from X among sitting MPs; a dozen smaller, less newsworthy payouts elsewhere could sit in the register indefinitely without anyone noticing the pattern.

The politics of the platform

Lowe's posts — many amplifying demands for mass deportations — draw the kind of engagement X's payout formula rewards, on a platform owned by Elon Musk, who has previously boosted Lowe's content and that of Restore Britain, the party Lowe founded in June 2025 after leaving Reform UK amid bullying allegations the CPS later found insufficient evidence to pursue. Researcher Ambrose Pym, tracking the payments, put it plainly: a foreign billionaire simultaneously paying and algorithmically boosting an ideologically aligned British politician is, in his words, "strikingly under-reported."

Lowe's own defence is the one he's given before: "If I wanted to make money, I would not be in politics. I donate my entire net MP salary to local charities in my constituency." That may well be true, and it doesn't touch the underlying question — not about Lowe's motives, but about whether a disclosure regime designed for boardrooms can keep pace with a Commons where a platform's algorithm can now out-pay the state. IPSA's next scheduled salary review, and whatever the Committee on Standards makes of the growing pile of platform-income disclosures, will be the places to watch for whether the rules catch up.
