NHS England to offer free at-home HPV testing kits

An NHS self-test kit, swabs and instructions — illustrative of the mail-order format the HPV self-sampling programme will use. — Unsplash
In one sentence
NHS England starts mailing free HPV self-testing kits to under-screened women from 26 August, but trial data show mailed-only kits get far fewer returns than in-person offers.

From Tuesday, millions of women in England who have fallen behind on cervical screening will be offered a way to test themselves at home rather than in a clinic. NHS England begins sending self-sampling HPV kits by post to anyone more than six months overdue for a smear test — a vaginal swab, done in private, sealed and dropped in a postbox for laboratory analysis.

The logic is straightforward. Cervical screening only works if people show up for it, and fewer are showing up. Coverage across England was 68.8% in 2024, well short of the 80% the programme treats as the floor for "acceptable" performance, and down from around 76% in 2010–11 [[Nuffield Trust, NHS England]]. Roughly 4 million eligible women and people with a cervix are not up to date. Around 3,300 people are diagnosed with cervical cancer in the UK each year, and nearly all of it is preventable if high-risk HPV — the virus responsible for almost every case — is caught early [[Cancer Research UK]].

A familiar gap

Eligible people will be invited via the NHS app, text, email or letter, and can then order a kit online. The swab tests only for high-risk HPV strains; a positive result triggers an invitation for a conventional clinician-taken sample, since self-testing does not yet check for the abnormal cells that HPV can cause. Dr Sue Mann, NHS England's national clinical director for women's health, called it a "gamechanger," and Health Secretary Yvette Cooper framed it as evidence of "the future NHS we are building." Cancer Research UK's chief executive, Michelle Mitchell, was more measured, noting screening "isn't always easy for everyone to take part" in — embarrassment, pain, and lack of time are the barriers most often cited by non-attenders.

What the evidence actually shows

The strongest data behind this rollout comes from YouScreen, a 2021 trial across 133 GP practices in north and east London that mailed or opportunistically offered kits to 27,000 under-screened women [[YouScreen, eClinicalMedicine]]. It is worth reading the fine print. When kits were handed out in person during a GP visit, 65.5% of women returned a sample. When kits were simply mailed with no human contact, the return rate fell to 12.9%. Encouragingly, the people who did respond were broadly representative of the hardest-to-reach groups — 64% from ethnic minority backgrounds, 60% from the most deprived fifth of the population — which is the more important finding for equity. But the net effect on overall screening coverage at participating practices, over an average seven and a half months, was a modest 1.6 percentage points.

NHS England's extrapolation — up to 400,000 more people screened a year nationally — assumes a scale-up that has not yet been tested outside trial conditions, and Tuesday's rollout leans mostly on pure mail-out rather than the in-person offer that drove YouScreen's best results. The programme sits inside a wider NHS ambition to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem by 2040. Whether cost, uptake and follow-up capacity all hold together at national scale is the question worth watching as the first results come in from local screening data over the coming year.
