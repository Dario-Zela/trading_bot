TikTok agrees $400m settlement over children's privacy violations

The Robert F. Kennedy Department of Justice Building in Washington, D.C., which announced the $400 million TikTok settlement. — Pelajanela / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
TikTok will pay $400m to settle DOJ claims it collected children's data without parental consent — its third such fine in seven years, and about 1% of a year's revenue.

TikTok has agreed to pay $400 million to settle a federal lawsuit accusing it of hoovering up children's personal data without their parents' permission — one of the largest sums ever extracted from a company under America's child-privacy law, and still barely a rounding error against what the platform earns in a single week.

The deal, announced by the Justice Department on August 22, closes a case first filed in 2024 by the DOJ and the Federal Trade Commission against TikTok and its Chinese parent, ByteDance. The core accusation was straightforward: TikTok knowingly let millions of children under 13 open ordinary accounts — outside the restricted, ad-free "Kids Mode" meant for that age group — and then collected and kept their names, videos, location data and viewing habits without the parental consent the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) requires. When parents asked TikTok to delete their kids' accounts, the government alleged, the company often didn't.

What the government said TikTok did

COPPA, passed in 1998, requires any service aimed at or knowingly used by children under 13 to get verifiable parental consent before collecting personal data from them. The DOJ's complaint described "massive-scale invasions of children's privacy": TikTok, it said, had inadequate systems for catching and removing underage accounts, and continued gathering data on kids it knew were too young to be there.

Under the settlement, TikTok pays $300 million up front. The remaining $100 million is contingent on a court vacating a separate 2019 consent decree — a legacy of Musical.ly, the lip-sync app TikTok's parent bought and rebranded in 2018. Associate Attorney General Stanley E. Woodward Jr. called the outcome "a major victory for American children and parents." TikTok, for its part, said much of what the suit described concerned "past events and practices" that were either "factually inaccurate or have been addressed," and pointed to newer age-verification tools and parental controls it has rolled out since.

Not TikTok's first fine, and not likely its last

This is the third time TikTok or its predecessor has been punished for the same underlying problem. In 2019, Musical.ly paid the FTC $5.7 million — a record at the time — for the identical failure to seek parental consent from under-13 users. In September 2023, Ireland's Data Protection Commission fined TikTok €345 million (roughly $370 million) under EU law for mishandling children's accounts and defaulting them to public settings. Each fine has been larger than the last, each has followed a fresh finding of the same conduct, and none has stopped the underlying business — built on maximizing time-on-app and ad-targeting data — from operating largely the same way.

That pattern is the real story behind the number. Regulators are getting more aggressive and better resourced on child-data enforcement, and $400 million sounds punitive until it's set against what TikTok actually makes. The company's global revenue is projected near $33 billion in 2025, with U.S. ad revenue alone forecast to hit $14.5 billion in 2026. A $400 million settlement is roughly 1% of one year's global revenue — closer to a cost of doing business than a deterrent.

A company also unwinding at the ownership level

The settlement lands as TikTok is mid-transition on a separate front entirely. Under the divest-or-ban arrangement finalized in January 2026, a consortium including Oracle, Silver Lake and the Gulf investment fund MGX took a controlling stake in TikTok's U.S. operations, restructuring who actually runs the app that 170 million-odd Americans use. That deal was about national-security concerns over Chinese ownership, not child privacy, but it puts new operators in charge of the compliance systems the DOJ just spent two years picking apart — and gives them a fresh incentive, coming off a $400 million bill, to show regulators the age-gating and data-deletion machinery actually works this time.

What happens next is less about this settlement than about the ones still coming. State attorneys general and the FTC continue to pursue child-safety and addictive-design claims against TikTok and rival platforms separately from COPPA, and Congress has repeatedly floated — without passing — stiffer penalties for repeat data-privacy offenders. Until the price of violating child-privacy law rises closer to what platforms earn from the data itself, settlements like this one are likely to keep arriving on a predictable multi-year cycle, each one bigger than the last, each one absorbed and moved past.
