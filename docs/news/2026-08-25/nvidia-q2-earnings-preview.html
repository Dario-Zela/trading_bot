Nvidia's Q2 report looms as verdict on the AI trade

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's founder and CEO, whose commentary on demand will be scrutinised alongside Wednesday's earnings numbers. — Presidential Office (Taiwan) via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
Nvidia reports Wednesday with consensus near $91-95bn revenue; the guidance for the following quarter, not the beat itself, will decide whether the AI trade holds or rotates.

Nvidia reports fiscal second-quarter earnings after Wall Street closes on Wednesday, and the number that matters most is not the one at the top of the press release. Consensus has revenue landing around $91-92bn, roughly in line with the company's own guidance of $91bn plus or minus 2%, and comfortably above $95bn if the beat pattern of the last three years holds. That would mean growth of roughly 93-95% from the $46.7bn Nvidia posted in the same quarter last year — a figure so large it has become almost routine to type, which is itself part of the problem now facing the stock.

Nvidia closed last week near $215 a share, down from an all-time high above $236 set earlier this summer, with a market capitalisation hovering around $5tn. That drawdown is the market's way of asking a question it can't yet answer: can a company already generating $81.6bn in a single quarter keep compounding at close to 100% a year, or has Wall Street simply extrapolated a good run into infinity? Wednesday's report, and more specifically the guidance Nvidia gives for the quarter after that, is the closest thing to a direct answer investors are going to get before the autumn.

Why the bar keeps rising

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Nvidia's data-centre business has roughly doubled year over year for eleven straight quarters, driven by hyperscalers — Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta and, increasingly, OpenAI's own infrastructure partners — racing to build out the compute needed for large language models and, more recently, "agentic" AI systems that act semi-autonomously. Every quarter that growth continues raises the base it has to grow from next time. A 95% increase on $46.7bn is a very different lift than a 95% increase would be on $10bn, and at some point compute demand, however voracious, runs into the physical limits of power, fabrication capacity and the willingness of customers to keep spending.

That last point has become the sharpest edge of the debate. Nvidia has reportedly discussed providing financing guarantees worth roughly $250bn to backstop OpenAI's data-centre leasing commitments — the kind of arrangement that lets a customer buy more Nvidia chips than its own balance sheet would otherwise support. Short-seller Michael Burry and Bernstein analyst Stacy Rasgon have both flagged this as "circular financing": revenue that looks organic in Nvidia's income statement but is partly underwritten by Nvidia's own capital. It does not make the chips shipped any less real, but it complicates the question of how much underlying, self-funded demand actually exists beneath the headline growth rate.

Jensen Huang, Nvidia's chief executive, has waved off bubble comparisons directly. "There's been a lot of talk about an AI bubble," he told analysts on a recent call. "From our vantage point, we see something very different" — pointing to three overlapping platform shifts, from CPU to GPU computing, from traditional machine learning to generative AI, and now to agentic AI, each of which he argues justifies its own wave of infrastructure spending on its own terms.

China, competition and the margin question

The report will also show how much Nvidia has managed to claw back in China. Washington's export licensing restrictions on H20 chips forced Nvidia to take a $4.5bn charge in the first quarter of this fiscal year and to guide with essentially no data-centre compute revenue from China baked in — the company said at the time it was "effectively foreclosed" from that market. Any commentary suggesting a partial reopening, or conversely a hardening of the restrictions, will move the stock independently of the topline beat.

Competition is the other thread. AMD's Instinct MI350 now matches Nvidia's B200 on raw FP8 throughput, and its next-generation MI400, built on TSMC's 2nm process and anchored by a 6-gigawatt supply deal with OpenAI, is due later this year. AMD still holds only a low single-digit share of the data-centre AI GPU market against Nvidia's dominant position, and Nvidia's CUDA software ecosystem remains a formidable switching cost for customers. But hyperscalers are also building their own custom silicon — Amazon's Trainium, Google's TPUs, Microsoft's Maia — precisely to reduce dependence on any single supplier, Nvidia included. None of this threatens Wednesday's number. All of it bears on the guidance for the quarter after, which is what the stock will actually trade on.

What a beat, or a miss, would mean

A number in line with or above the $91-95bn range, paired with guidance that holds near triple-digit growth, would likely be read as confirmation that AI infrastructure spending has further to run — supportive not just for Nvidia but for the wider basket of AI-linked names, chip equipment makers and power infrastructure plays that have moved in sympathy with it on both sides of the Atlantic. A miss, or guidance that so much as hints at deceleration into the mid-double-digits, would land differently precisely because valuations have priced in continuation rather than moderation. At a forward price-to-earnings ratio in the mid-30s to low-40s, Nvidia does not have much room to disappoint without a re-rating.

The report lands the same week as the PCE inflation reading and continued parsing of Fed Governor Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole remarks, giving markets two distinct catalysts to digest within days of each other — one macro, one company-specific, both capable of moving the same AI-adjacent trade. Nvidia's numbers arrive first. What Huang says about demand into next year, more than the quarter just closed, will likely decide which way the AI trade leans into September.
