Scottish National Investment Bank posts 'painful' £138m loss

In one sentence
Scotland's taxpayer-backed investment bank posted a record £138m annual loss after three early bets failed, reviving scrutiny of its risk strategy.

Scotland's state-owned development bank posted a net loss of £138m for the year to March 2026, its steepest since launch, as three early-stage bets on Scottish technology firms collapsed and a clutch of others were written down.

The Scottish National Investment Bank (SNIB), capitalised with £2bn of Scottish government money over ten years and mandated to invest where private capital won't, said £65m of the loss was "realised" — crystallised by the outright failure of three portfolio companies: laser manufacturer M Squared Lasers, electric-vehicle charging firm Trojan Energy, and satellite and digital-services group Krucial. A further £85m was unrealised, reflecting markdowns on long-term holdings including rocket firm Orbex and medical-device maker PneumoWave, both now in administration processes expected to conclude in the current financial year. The total loss of £137.5m compares with £58.4m the year before — a 135% jump.

"These figures are painful for the Bank," chairman Willie Watt said, adding the results were "not where we want to be."

Chief executive David Ritchie called the outcomes "regrettable and disappointing," while pointing to a more encouraging operating picture underneath the writedowns: income of £32.3m against operating costs of £20m, leaving an operational profit of £12.3m once the investment losses are stripped out. The bank also recorded its busiest year for deployment, committing £374m to Scottish ventures and taking cumulative investment past £1.2bn across 53 companies since it was founded in 2020 — a portfolio it says has helped crowd in £1.9bn from third-party investors.

A mandate built to lose money sometimes

SNIB was designed from the outset to take risks conventional lenders avoid, financing early-stage and long-horizon projects — clean energy, life sciences, housing — on the theory that patient, mission-driven capital can unlock growth that purely commercial investors won't fund. That mandate makes some losses structurally inevitable rather than a sign of mismanagement on its own; venture-style portfolios routinely see a handful of investments fail while a few outsized winners carry the average. The problem for SNIB is that, six years in, the winners haven't yet shown up in the accounts, while the losers — Orbex's failed rocket ambitions, the collapse of bottle-return scheme operator Circularity Scotland, and now three more write-offs — have piled up in public view.

Political fallout

Because the money is taxpayers', the losses land as a governance story as much as a financial one. Opposition politicians seized on the figures: Scottish Conservative finance spokesman Craig Hoy said the scale of losses raised "serious questions" about how the SNP government's flagship bank is run, and Holyrood's finance committee has been pressed to hold an urgent evidence session. Deputy First Minister Kate Forbes has previously defended the bank's remit directly, arguing after an earlier Orbex writedown that taking on risk private investors refuse is precisely SNIB's job — a defence that will be tested again as the finance committee digs into this year's numbers. The bank says the recurring losses have already triggered an internal review of its risk and due-diligence processes.

What's next

The finance committee scrutiny session will test whether Holyrood accepts the bank's own framing — that a development bank absorbing early losses on a maturing portfolio is working as intended — or concludes the risk controls need tightening before more public money follows. With Orbex and PneumoWave's administrations due to close out in the current financial year, next year's results will show whether the losses are finally troughing, and whether any of the bank's larger, later-stage bets are close to producing the returns that have so far stayed on paper.
