Shell draws Exxon, LyondellBasell and Apollo bids for $8bn US chemicals arm

Shell's Pennsylvania petrochemicals complex under construction along the Ohio River, January 2019. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell has drawn non-binding bids from Exxon, LyondellBasell, Apollo and Kuwait Petroleum for its $8bn US chemicals arm, a steep discount on the $14bn it sank into just one plant.

Shell has spent two decades building out a US chemicals footprint anchored by a $14bn plastics complex on the Ohio River. It is now prepared to sell the whole thing for perhaps $8bn — and the queue of interested buyers includes two of the industry's biggest names.

The company has received non-binding indicative offers for its American chemicals business from ExxonMobil, LyondellBasell, Apollo Global Management and the chemicals arm of Kuwait Petroleum Corporation, according to the Financial Times. The bids, submitted last month, cover both an outright sale of the entire unit and piecemeal acquisitions of individual sites. Shell is running a parallel process for its European chemicals assets, though those are expected to fetch considerably less.

A discount on $14bn

The US portfolio spans four sites in Louisiana, Texas and Pennsylvania that turn natural gas liquids into the building blocks of plastics, detergents and packaging. The centrepiece is the Monaca petrochemicals complex in Beaver County, Pennsylvania — an ethane cracker that began operating in November 2022 after roughly $14bn of Shell capital, with capacity to produce up to 1.6m tonnes of polymers a year.

An $8bn valuation for the whole US business would mark a steep write-down against that spending, even accounting for the other three sites. It is the kind of gap that shows up when a company builds for one set of market conditions and sells into another. Shell sanctioned Monaca in 2016, betting that cheap Marcellus shale gas would make it one of the lowest-cost polyethylene producers in the world. The plant arrived into a global market that had, by then, been flooded by new Chinese and Middle Eastern capacity.

Chief executive Wael Sawan has been blunt about the result. He has described the current stretch of depressed chemical margins as "one of the longest we have seen in a very, very long time," and told investors last year that Shell would "deal with" a chemicals business that is "not delivering adequate returns." His verdict on the unit's fit within Shell is equally direct: "we do not believe we are the natural owners of this chemicals portfolio." Shell has flagged roughly $45bn of capital tied up across chemicals and renewables that it considers underperforming, and reporting earlier this year suggested it wants to cut global petrochemical capacity by more than 60%, to around 3.7m tonnes annually.

Buyers circling a bad market

What is striking is not that Shell wants out — oil majors have been reassessing chemicals for years — but who wants in. ExxonMobil and LyondellBasell are not opportunistic outsiders; they are two of the largest integrated producers already operating in the same commoditized markets Shell is fleeing. Their interest, alongside a private equity bid from Apollo and a state-backed one from Kuwait, points to a straightforward calculation: distressed assets bought at a discount to replacement cost can still generate returns even when the sector itself is unprofitable, provided the buyer has the balance sheet and operating scale to ride out the cycle.

That scale question is precisely why a deal is not straightforward. Polyethylene margins have been mostly negative on an integrated basis since mid-2022, squeezed by a wave of Chinese and Middle Eastern capacity additions that has left global ethylene supply growing faster than demand — a mismatch analysts expect to persist beyond 2027. If ExxonMobil or LyondellBasell were to acquire Shell's plants outright, regulators would need to weigh whether combining that capacity with their existing US chemicals operations meaningfully narrows competition in specific product lines, particularly polyethylene. That is one reason bidders have structured offers around both a full sale and a break-up into individual sites — a company such as Apollo could take pieces regulators would wave through, while Exxon or LyondellBasell circle the strategic pieces that fit their existing networks.

What the exit signals

The broader pattern here matters more than any single sale price. Shell built Monaca as a bet that low-cost feedstock could offset commoditization; it hasn't. That an integrated major with Shell's scale, trading capability and balance sheet cannot make US chemicals work is a harder signal for the sector than a mid-sized producer struggling — it suggests the margin compression is structural, not a company-specific execution problem. Sawan's capital is instead flowing toward liquefied natural gas and upstream projects where Shell believes it retains a genuine cost advantage, echoing similar recalibrations already under way at Exxon and Chevron, both of which have leaned toward carbon capture and gas over chemicals expansion.

For the rest of the sector, the read-through is a consolidation wave rather than a broad retreat: majors and financial buyers with the balance sheet to absorb a multi-year downturn are positioned to pick up capacity cheaply, while smaller, higher-cost producers caught in the same margin squeeze without Shell's optionality may not get buyers this willing. A binding agreement is not expected immediately — Shell has given no formal deadline — but the next marker will be whether the non-binding offers convert into a definitive deal, and whether regulators signal early resistance to a buyer such as Exxon or LyondellBasell taking the Monaca site outright.
