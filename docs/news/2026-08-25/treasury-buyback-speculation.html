Speculation over Treasury's $1tn account fuels buyback bets

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, whose department is weighing whether to tap its nearly $1 trillion General Account to fund expanded bond buybacks. — U.S. Department of the Treasury
In one sentence
An unconfirmed report that Treasury could drain its $1tn cash account to fund bond buybacks briefly lifted stocks and eased yields Monday.

Wall Street spent Monday morning in a familiar sulk — tech stocks dragging the Nasdaq lower, yields still smarting from a summer of upward drift — until a single, unconfirmed report gave traders something to chew on. CNBC, citing two Treasury officials, reported that the department could tap its General Account, which holds close to $1 trillion in cash, to help fund an expanded programme of long-bond buybacks. Within minutes, the Dow turned positive, Treasury yields eased and gold ticked higher. Nobody at Treasury would confirm the plan on the record.

That a source-based report with no official sign-off was enough to move markets says as much about investor nerves as it does about the policy itself. The 10-year yield fell more than 3 basis points to 4.704%, and the 30-year — which touched levels last seen in 2007 just days earlier — eased to 5.234%. The S&P 500 pared its loss to roughly 0.1%, and the Dow added about 150 points. None of it amounted to a rally so much as a flinch away from the week's worst fears.

What's actually on the table

The backdrop is a buyback programme Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced only last week, doubling the per-operation ceiling on purchases of longer-dated debt from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, with the larger operations running September 9 through November 4. The move was aimed squarely at the 10- to 30-year part of the curve, where yields have been climbing for weeks on concerns about deficit issuance and, more recently, doubts about the Federal Reserve's independence ahead of Kevin Warsh's prospective arrival as chair.

The open question has been how Bessent pays for it. The consensus assumption was that Treasury would fund the purchases by selling more short-term bills — a rotation Bessent himself has dubbed a "Treasury Twist," a nod to the Fed's 2012 Operation Twist. Monday's report offered a second possibility: drawing down the General Account, which Bessent has built up to roughly $950 billion, well above the $550-600 billion range the Biden Treasury had targeted. Officials wouldn't say how much of that cushion might be used, or when a decision would be announced.

Why a rumor moved a market

Draining the TGA would, mechanically, push cash back into the banking system and could ease the liquidity strains that have been showing up in short-term funding markets. That's the logic behind Monday's price action — more liquidity, marginally more support for risk assets. But the scale argument cuts the other way: even a full trillion dollars is a rounding error against a Treasury market measured in the tens of trillions, and the buyback programme itself, at $4 billion a pop, is tiny next to the pace of new issuance needed to fund the deficit. One market skeptic described the broader buyback push as policy jawboning with only superficial capital behind it.

That gap between the size of the gesture and the size of the market it's meant to influence is precisely why the report landed as it did. Investors have spent weeks parsing every Bessent statement for signs the administration will lean on Treasury tools to do what looks increasingly like quasi-monetary-policy work, at a moment when the Fed's own credibility is under political pressure. A report that isn't even confirmed was enough to move the tape because there is so little else to hold onto.

What's next

Treasury has given itself until early November to run the expanded buyback window, so any decision on using the General Account will likely surface well before then — possibly alongside routine debt-issuance guidance. In the meantime, the market's attention shifts to Nvidia's earnings and Friday's PCE inflation print, plus Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole appearance, all due this week. Any of the three could do more to move yields than another round of buyback speculation.
