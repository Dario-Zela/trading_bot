Burnham's early agenda: mayors gain planning powers as Labour edges ahead in polls

Andy Burnham, whose government will consult next week on giving regional mayors call-in powers over major planning decisions. — House of Commons / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Burnham will let mayors in 13 English regions override councils on big developments, as Labour posts its first poll lead over Reform UK in over a year.

Andy Burnham has spent three decades in politics learning that the space between a good idea and a built house is usually occupied by a planning committee. As prime minister, he now intends to shrink that space — by handing himself and 12 other regional mayors the power to reach past local councils and wave through the developments those councils won't.

A consultation opening next week will propose giving mayors in Greater Manchester, the West Midlands, Liverpool City Region, the West of England and nine other combined authority areas so-called "call-in" powers over major planning applications — the same tool London's mayor has held for two decades. The threshold, according to reporting in Property Week and the Local Government Lawyer trade press, is set at developments of more than 150 homes, commercial schemes above 15,000 square metres, or any building taller than 30 metres, roughly ten storeys. Cross that line, and a mayor could direct a council to approve a scheme it had rejected — or, just as pointedly, force a refusal on a project the council had waved through.

From Manchester to Downing Street

The policy carries an unmistakable signature. Burnham built his political brand as Greater Manchester's mayor arguing that England is too centralised and that power should flow outward from Whitehall to the regions. Handing metro mayors call-in powers looks, on its face, like devolution. But it is devolution of a specific and pointed kind: power moving away from the 300-plus district and borough councils that currently hold the planning pen, and toward 13 mayoral offices — Burnham's old job, writ larger.

The justification is arithmetic as much as ideology. Labour inherited a manifesto pledge to build 1.5 million homes this parliament, a target that requires roughly 300,000 completions a year against a backdrop of chronic underdelivery. Ministers argue that local planning committees, susceptible to organised objector groups and short electoral cycles, are the single biggest bottleneck between permission and shovels in the ground. Give a mayor — elected on a mandate covering a whole city region, less exposed to a single ward's objections — the power to break deadlock, the thinking goes, and stalled schemes start moving.

Burnham himself has framed it as a corrective that cuts in more than one direction. Speaking to the Sunday Times, he said: "Sometimes challenge does need to be made to local decision-making, and actually it cuts both ways. It's not all about Nimbyism. Sometimes it's the other way round — it's a local authority putting forward something that's unacceptable in terms of the impact on the community… so it's a two-way street." In other words: mayors could just as easily block an over-dense scheme a council had nodded through as force through one it had blocked.

The pushback

That framing has not neutralised the objection at the heart of the policy, which is that it removes a democratic backstop. The chair of the Local Councils Network said local planning authorities hold "unrivalled knowledge of local communities" and that "this expertise should not be dismissed lightly," warning that weakening council accountability "has the potential to diminish local places" without necessarily producing the high-quality homes communities want. The Green group leader on the Local Government Association went further, describing the move as evidence that the government's real agenda "is not handing power down, but taking it away from local communities and local councils." Conservatives and Liberal Democrats have raised similar objections, arguing that a council rejecting a scheme is often reflecting exactly the kind of local accountability devolution was meant to strengthen, not sideline.

For the property and construction sectors, the calculus is more straightforward: predictability. A developer sitting on a stalled 200-unit scheme in a combined authority area gains a second route to permission if a local committee balks. Property Week's coverage notes that ten of England's fourteen devolved mayors have already backed the plan — a signal that, whatever the democratic objections, the people who would actually wield the power are keen to have it. The detail that will matter most to housebuilders and commercial developers is not in dispute yet: exactly how the thresholds get drawn, and how liberally mayors use the tool once they have it, will determine whether this reshapes delivery timelines or sits largely unused as a backstop.

A government finding its footing

The planning announcement lands as Burnham's Labour, barely a month into his premiership after replacing Keir Starmer, shows its first sustained polling recovery. Ipsos polling conducted between 30 July and 4 August put Labour three points ahead of Reform UK — 28% to 25% — the party's first lead over Nigel Farage's insurgents in more than a year. PollCheck's tracker shows the same reversal. Burnham is also personally more popular than either Farage or Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch as preferred prime minister, though Ipsos found seven in ten voters still dissatisfied with how the government is running the country, and four in ten with no settled view on Burnham at all.

The two threads — a poll bounce and a planning shake-up — are not unconnected. A new prime minister's early agenda is where a governing style gets set, and Burnham has chosen to lead with something concrete and popular in the abstract: more homes, built faster, with councils no longer able to sit on schemes indefinitely. Whether that reads as decisive government or high-handed centralisation will depend heavily on where the thresholds land and how the first call-ins are used once mayors have the power. A three-point poll lead is not a mandate; it is a head start that a botched rollout — or a mayor seen overruling a community on a genuinely unpopular scheme — could erase quickly.

What's next

The consultation opens next week and will run for a period yet to be confirmed; its outcome will fix the final thresholds and any exemptions for conservation areas or green belt land. Housebuilders and property investors will be watching those numbers closely, since a threshold set low enough to catch mid-sized schemes would matter far more to the sector than one confined to genuinely major developments. Burnham also faces an autumn Budget in which he has declined to rule out tax rises — a test of whether the planning bounce and the Budget arithmetic pull the polls in the same direction, or apart.
