US restaurants experiment with no-tipping, all-inclusive pricing

A tip jar at a Washington, DC eatery — the fixture a small number of US restaurants are trying to retire. — Ser Amantio di Nicolao / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A handful of US restaurants have dropped tipping for all-inclusive pricing, but tax rules and well-paid servers are stalling wider adoption.

The pitch is simple enough to fit on a chalkboard: one price, no math, no awkward pause while the machine asks whether you'd like to leave 18, 20 or 25 percent for someone who refilled your water twice. At La Cigale, a 14-seat counter restaurant in San Francisco's Glen Park, dinner is $140 a head — tax and tip included, full stop. "What you see is what you pay," the restaurant tells diners. "We do not accept tips."

A scattering of restaurants across the US has spent the past few years running the same experiment. Nightshade Noodle Bar outside Boston went tip-free five years ago. Dirt Candy in New York's Lower East Side got there first, in 2015, and has stuck with it. The pitch to staff is as tidy as the one to diners: a flat, predictable wage — La Cigale pays roughly $40 an hour, Dirt Candy about $30 — instead of income that swings with the size of the room and the mood of the crowd.

It is, on paper, an easy sell. Survey after survey finds Americans exhausted by what pollsters have taken to calling tip fatigue: nearly nine in ten say tipping culture has become excessive, and a majority tell researchers they'd happily pay higher menu prices to make the tip screen go away. And yet the no-tipping restaurant remains a curiosity rather than a category — a handful of names that keep recurring in write-ups precisely because there are so few of them to write up.

The one that turned back

The more instructive story is the one that didn't stick. Talulla, a restaurant in Cambridge, Massachusetts, dropped tipping in 2020 and spent five years trying to make the sums work. Co-owner Danielle Ayer raised menu prices 23 percent to cover it — enough, she said, to get through the winter months, but not enough to run on year-round. The restaurant reverted to tipping in September.

The mechanics behind that reversal say more about the model's limits than any amount of survey data. Higher listed prices mean higher reported revenue, which means a bigger sales-tax bill — a cost that voluntary tips, largely outside that accounting, don't carry. "Operating a non-tipped restaurant is more expensive overall," Ayer said. Separately, Cornell researchers Shuo Wang and Michael Lynn ran a simulation with 318 diners and found people judge how expensive a restaurant is almost entirely by the menu price on the page, largely ignoring the size of the final bill. Fold the tip into the price and the restaurant looks pricier, even when the total a diner pays is identical or lower.

Who actually loses

The deeper obstacle is that tipping, for the workers who are good at it, already pays well. The National Restaurant Association puts median hourly earnings for tipped servers at $27, and in busy markets the top end runs to $40 or $60 an hour, according to Derek Simms, who runs a hospitality group in Texas. Flatten that into a fixed wage and the restaurant's best servers — the ones with the regulars and the big Saturday-night tables — take a pay cut to subsidise everyone else. "There's no incentive for the servers to be attentive and give extra-good service," Simms argued, a view echoed by other operators who worry that a flat wage removes the sharpest tool restaurants have for making sure someone hustles.

Washington has just made the arithmetic worse for reformers. A federal provision exempting tip income from income tax took effect this year, making a dollar earned as a tip worth more to a server than a dollar earned as service-charge revenue, which the IRS treats as ordinary wages and taxes accordingly. Cornell's Lynn, who has studied tipping for decades, doesn't see the practice going anywhere soon: "The economic disadvantages of eliminating tipping outweigh the advantages."

That is the pattern beneath the trend piece: diners say they want the simplicity, but restaurants that try it run into tax treatment, perceived pricing and a workforce with every reason to resist. The restaurants sticking with no-tipping tend to share a trait — a tight-margin, high-control format like a chef's counter or a tasting menu, where the owner can price precisely and doesn't have a large front-of-house staff to keep onside. That's a niche business model, not evidence of a shift in the mainstream one.

None of this shows up as pressure in the numbers that matter for the industry — menu inflation, labor costs, restaurant margins. What would change that is either a labor market tight enough that operators need a wage story to hire, or a change to how service charges are taxed that closes the gap with tips. Neither is currently in motion. Until one is, the interesting restaurants stay interesting precisely because there are so few of them.
