BEYOND THE TAPE · HOUSING · By L. Bordeaux · 2026-08-25 · 4 min read

Reform UK says it would evict social housing tenants over unkempt gardens

Richard Tice, Reform UK's deputy leader, unveiled the party's social housing plan alongside chairman Lee Anderson. — House of Commons / Laurie Noble, via Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Reform UK unveiled a housing policy prioritising British-born applicants and threatening eviction for tenants who don't maintain their gardens.

There is a particular kind of policy that exists mainly to be quoted, and Reform UK's new social housing plan is a fine specimen. Unveiled this week by deputy leader Richard Tice and party chairman Lee Anderson, it promises to put British-born workers under 35, married couples with children and military veterans "at the front of the queue" for council homes — while threatening tenants who let their gardens go to seed with eviction.

"We make no apology," Anderson said, defending a proposal that draws explicitly on a hazier version of postwar council housing, when tenancy agreements were said to enforce tidy hedges and painted front doors. "If after that you didn't keep it up to reasonable standard, you were out." What "reasonable standard" means in 2026 — an overgrown lawn, a broken fence, a skip left too long in the driveway — the party has not said.

The pledge, in full

Tice's presentation leaned heavily on numbers: a promise to build 50,000 affordable social homes a year, funded, he said, without new taxes or borrowing — a claim that invites obvious scepticism given the party has yet to publish costings. Priority in the allocation queue would run through young British workers, married couples (civil partnerships included, Tice noted, in a nod to the party's professed enthusiasm for marriage) and veterans, with those on benefits pushed down the list.

The more eye-catching plank is the pledge to evict non-UK nationals from social housing entirely within three months of a Reform government taking office, which the party claims would free up roughly 540,000 homes — a figure well above outside estimates, which put the number of non-UK citizens in social housing closer to 470,000. Combined with the garden-maintenance clause, it reads less like a housing white paper than a campaign leaflet with a planning application's vocabulary.

Why it likely can't work as written

Reform UK holds no seats in government and no mechanism to enact any of this before an election Westminster watchers do not expect before 2029 at the earliest. That has not stopped the substance from drawing fire on legal grounds. The Equality Act 2010 already bars discrimination in housing allocation on grounds including race and nationality, and campaigners were quick to note that a blanket eviction of foreign nationals — many of whom hold indefinite leave to remain or refugee status with full legal rights to social housing — would almost certainly require primary legislation to survive a court challenge, legislation Reform has not drafted, let alone tested for compatibility with existing equalities law.

Shelter, the housing charity, argued that ranking applicants by nationality and marital status ahead of housing need would leave more families in temporary accommodation for longer, not fewer. Labour called the package "unworkable and unserious" and put a £10 billion hole in its costings, though neither side has published detailed modelling to back their numbers.

The politics underneath the policy

What the plan does credibly do is signal where Reform intends to fight the next election: a message aimed squarely at voters frustrated with housing waiting lists, dressed in the language of order and reciprocity — keep your garden tidy, and the state keeps its side of the bargain. It is a strategy, not a piece of legislation, and its authors know the difference; a policy announcement made from opposition costs nothing to promise and nothing to fail to deliver.

The next test of how far that strategy travels is less about garden fences than about polling. With Reform continuing to poll competitively against Labour and the Conservatives, the party has every incentive to keep issuing headline-grabbing pledges on immigration and housing well before it has to explain how any of them survive contact with the Equality Act — or a spending review.
