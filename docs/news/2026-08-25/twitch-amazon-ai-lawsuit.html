Twitch and Amazon hit with class action over AI training on streamer content

Twitch, the Amazon-owned livestreaming platform now facing a class-action suit over AI training on creator content. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A Twitch streamer with under 1,000 followers is suing Amazon in a class action alleging it trained AI on millions of creators' livestreams without consent since 2024.

A Twitch streamer from Connecticut has taken Amazon to federal court over what livestreaming's fine print apparently allowed all along: feeding millions of hours of broadcast footage into the company's AI training pipeline, without asking first.

Warren Pandiscia, who streams LEGO builds and video games to an audience of just over 900 followers, filed a class-action complaint on August 20 in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. He alleges that Twitch and its parent company copied and used streamers' broadcasts, clips and chat logs to train Amazon's generative AI models — and that this had been going on since as far back as 2024, long before anyone using the platform was told.

What triggered it

The practice only came into public view on August 12, when Twitch Support posted on X that the platform had "added a setting that lets you opt out of having your channel content used to train generative AI content models across Amazon." The post linked to an FAQ page and, notably, did not amount to much fanfare: the complaint says Twitch "sent no email, displayed no pop-up notification, and made no announcement before enabling the setting for every account."

The setting is opt-out, not opt-in — every streamer's content is fair game for AI training unless they dig into settings and switch it off. Asked about that design choice on a Twitch "Patch Notes" livestream, the company's chief product officer, Mike Minton, was candid: "if it was opt-in, nobody would opt in." It is the kind of remark that tends to end up quoted back in a courtroom, and it duly has.

The claims

Pandiscia's suit argues Twitch and Amazon breached both implied and express contracts with creators, were unjustly enriched, and violated California's Unfair Competition Law by turning streamers into what the complaint calls "free training stock for separate commercial AI products." He is seeking an injunction, plus damages, restitution and disgorgement of any profits Amazon made from the practice. Because the suit is filed as a class action, its scope could extend far beyond one small streamer to the millions of people who have broadcast on Twitch since 2024.

Why this case is different

Authors, visual artists and musicians have spent the past few years suing AI developers — including Meta, Stability AI and OpenAI — over training data scraped from books, art and songs. Twitch's case pushes that fight into new territory: livestreaming is bigger, messier and far more distributed than a catalogue of novels or paintings. Millions of ordinary people, not just professional creators, generate that footage every day, often with no formal copyright registration and no leverage over a platform's terms of service. If courts treat unlicensed video training the same way they've begun treating unlicensed text and image training, the exposure for Amazon — and any other platform hosting user-generated video, audio or livestreams — grows by an order of magnitude.

Amazon and Twitch had not issued a public response to the complaint at the time of writing.

The case now heads toward an early scheduling conference in the Northern District of California, where Amazon is expected to move to dismiss or compel arbitration under Twitch's terms of service — a strategy that has had mixed success against other AI-training suits. Whether the opt-out setting Twitch rolled out on August 12 shields the company for content collected afterward, while leaving it exposed for everything scraped before, is likely to be the case's first real battleground.
