Trump threatens 50% tariffs on Canadian autos and steel; Carney open to talks

President Trump announced the 50% tariff on Canadian autos, parts and steel via Truth Social, effective January 1, 2027. — Daniel Torok / White House (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump set a 50% tariff on Canadian autos, parts and steel from January 2027; Carney says Canada won't negotiate until the US attitude changes.

Donald Trump said Sunday that the United States will raise tariffs on Canadian vehicles, auto parts and steel to 50%, effective January 1, 2027 — doubling the current 25% levy on Canadian auto imports and locking in the existing 50% steel tariff for another year. Vehicles assembled in the United States are exempt. The announcement, delivered on Truth Social, followed the collapse of trade talks between Washington and Ottawa and immediately split equity markets along an unusually clean line: automakers down, steelmakers up.

In one sentence: Trump set a 50% tariff on Canadian autos, parts and steel from January 2027, and Carney says Ottawa won't return to talks until Washington's "attitude" changes.

A five-month fuse

The mechanics matter more than the rhetoric here. Trump is not imposing the tariff today — he is announcing that it takes effect on January 1, 2027, invoking Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 rather than the emergency powers used in earlier rounds of this trade fight. That gap gives markets nearly five months to price the change in gradually rather than absorb it as a shock, and Monday's trading session showed the repricing already underway. Ford fell roughly 3–4%, Stellantis dropped as much as 4.2%, and General Motors slipped 1–2% as investors marked down North American production networks that assume tariff-free parts crossing the Windsor–Detroit corridor multiple times before a vehicle is finished. Steelmakers moved the other way: Cleveland-Cliffs jumped more than 6%, Nucor gained around 3–4%, and Steel Dynamics rose over 2%, all benefiting from reduced competition from Canadian imports. Trade lawyer Barry Appleton's framing is worth keeping in view: the tariff is collected at the US border, from US car dealers and US buyers, not from the Canadian treasury — the incidence falls on the American side of the line even as the diplomatic fight is styled as Canada versus the US.

Cleveland-Cliffs' reaction carries an extra wrinkle: the company bought Canadian steelmaker Stelco for $2.5 billion in November 2024, so a chunk of its book sits on the Canadian side of whatever line gets drawn — a reminder that "long steel, short autos" is a cruder trade than the sector labels suggest. Steel names had also whipsawed the week before on reports that Washington and Ottawa were nearing a deal to cut Canadian steel and aluminum tariffs from 50% to 25%, which knocked the same stocks lower days before Trump's announcement reversed the move entirely. For anyone building a systematic signal around this dispute, that whipsaw is the tell: headline risk here is genuinely two-sided until a deal is signed or the January deadline passes.

Trump's case, Carney's answer

Trump's stated grievance is familiar: he says Canada has been "ripping off" the United States for years, pointing to a roughly $60 billion trade deficit and to Canada's own 25% tariff on non-USMCA-compliant American vehicles, which he calls discriminatory. The dispute sits inside a broader unwind of the trade architecture that has governed North American manufacturing since 2020 — Washington confirmed in July that it would not renew USMCA for another 16-year term, opting instead for annual reviews that were meant to be routine and have instead become the venue for this fight.

Prime Minister Mark Carney's response was notable mostly for what it didn't do: escalate the rhetoric or offer a concession. Asked to react, Carney called the move unsurprising — "not a surprise," in his words — and cast it as further evidence of what he described as the administration's underlying endgame rather than a genuine opening bid. On the prospect of talks, he set a condition rather than a date: "When the Americans go to the negotiating table first, with the right attitude towards our industries and in a true partnership, of course we'll come to the negotiating table." He was pointed about what he wouldn't accept — "an attitude at the negotiation table that Canada is a subsidiary of the United States." Ottawa is not waiting on Washington to move first. Carney has confirmed retaliatory tariffs take effect September 8, matching the US measures "dollar for dollar" across steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp, paper and electronics — a broader basket than autos alone, which telegraphs that Canada intends to spread the pain across sectors with political weight in the US rather than mirror Trump's target list directly.

What the market is actually pricing

Auto tariffs at 50% are close to prohibitive for a supply chain built on parts crossing the border repeatedly before final assembly — Canadian imports of US motor vehicles were already down roughly 22%, or $5.6 billion, in the year to March 2026 under the current 25% rate, well before Monday's escalation. A doubling to 50% is not a proportional extension of that pressure; it is closer to a redesign incentive, pushing automakers toward reshoring final assembly into the US rather than absorbing the cost. That is the trade several analysts flagged over the weekend, and it is consistent with the equity reaction: the market is not treating this as noise to fade but as a genuine input to where the next capital-expenditure cycle in North American auto manufacturing gets allocated.

Goods affected are a relatively modest 5% of Canada's total exports to the US by one estimate, which limits the macro damage to Canada even as it concentrates pain in specific Ontario and Quebec manufacturing communities. The bigger unknown is whether the five-month runway produces a deal or simply becomes the period in which both sides stake out positions for a longer fight. Watch September 8, when Canada's retaliatory tariffs land, and the pace of any renewed USMCA review talks between now and January — the next dated catalyst that could reprice both baskets in either direction.
