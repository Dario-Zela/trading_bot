Markets hold steady as Warsh's Jackson Hole test and PCE loom

Kevin Warsh at the White House on May 22, 2026, the day he took office as Federal Reserve Chair. — Official White House Photo by Daniel Torok
In one sentence
Markets sit in a narrow range ahead of Wednesday's PCE inflation data and Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's Friday Jackson Hole speech, his first major test since a rocky transition from Powell.

Wall Street spent the week doing very little, and doing it on purpose. The S&P 500 has been pinned in a band barely 100 points wide since Monday, its 50- and 200-period moving averages nearly converging — the kind of chart that reflects hesitation rather than direction. Treasury yields have eased off recent highs but stayed elevated, with the 10-year near 4.73% and the 30-year above 5.27%. Nobody wants to place a bet before Friday.

That is when Kevin Warsh, five months into the Federal Reserve chairmanship, delivers his first address to the Jackson Hole Economic Symposium — the closest thing central banking has to a proving ground. Wednesday brings the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, personal consumption expenditures data for July. Between the two, traders are treating this as a genuine coin flip on which event moves markets more, and the narrow trading range is the clearest evidence of that uncertainty: nobody has the conviction to take a position ahead of either.

A chair still finding his footing

Warsh arrived in the job carrying a reputation as an inflation hawk, built partly on his own criticism of predecessor Jerome Powell. Before taking office, Warsh argued repeatedly that rising long-term Treasury yields were a symptom of Powell's credibility problem — that markets no longer trusted the Fed's inflation-fighting resolve. He was confirmed by the Senate on May 13 in a 54-45 vote, the most divisive confirmation in the Fed's history, and took the chair when Powell's term expired two days later.

The irony has not been lost on the market. Since taking office, Warsh has faced his own version of the credibility test he once leveled at Powell. Long-end yields have climbed rather than fallen on his watch, and the July FOMC decision to hold rates unchanged — even as core PCE inflation held near 3.3%, well above the Fed's 2% target — left investors questioning whether his hawkish rhetoric would translate into hawkish action. Testifying before Congress in May, Warsh said policymakers "have no tolerance for persistently elevated inflation." Markets have been waiting since for that line to show up in policy rather than just in prepared remarks.

The Wednesday-Friday sequence

The two-event calendar this week is what makes the current calm feel provisional rather than settled. Wednesday's PCE print will show whether the disinflation trend that brought core PCE down from May's reading has continued into July, or stalled. A hot number would arrive just two days before Warsh speaks, sharpening scrutiny of whatever he says about the inflation path. A soft one would hand him room to sound more measured without appearing to cave.

Friday's speech itself, according to Warsh's own framing after the July meeting, is meant to address "the big questions" — structural issues around the Fed's monetary framework rather than near-term rate guidance. He has convened a panel of 15 outside economists to review that framework, with recommendations due by year-end, and there is speculation the review could ultimately narrow or scrap the average-inflation-targeting regime adopted under Powell. Investors will listen for that regardless of what he intends to emphasize; forward guidance has a way of leaking out of speeches billed as philosophical.

What the range-bound tape is pricing

The market's posture — flat indices, elevated but not spiking yields — suggests investors see both outcomes as live and are unwilling to lean either way until they see actual numbers and actual words. That is a reasonable position given how the past three months have gone: a chair who campaigned on credibility has so far delivered rate stability and rising long yields, an outcome that satisfies neither the hawks who wanted tightening nor the markets that wanted lower borrowing costs.

Friday's speech will not settle the framework review, which does not conclude until later this year. But it will be the first extended public test of whether Warsh's words can move markets the direction he intends, rather than the opposite direction, as has been the pattern since May. Wednesday's PCE data lands first and will frame how skeptically — or generously — the podium remarks two days later get read.
