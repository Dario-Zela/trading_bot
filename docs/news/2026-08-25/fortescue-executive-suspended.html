Fortescue suspends senior executive amid harassment investigation

Andrew Forrest, Fortescue's executive chairman and founder, whose company reversed course on an executive facing harassment allegations. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Fortescue suspended an unnamed executive over harassment claims days after defending its decision to keep them working.

Fortescue has suspended the senior executive at the centre of a sexual harassment and bullying investigation — a reversal that, on its own, tells a more interesting story than the original complaint.

The sequence matters. A female employee lodged a complaint in July alleging she had been sexually harassed and bullied by the unnamed executive. Fortescue hired MinterEllison, an external law firm, to investigate. But rather than stand the executive down immediately, the company kept them working through the inquiry — a position it defended as recently as its full-year earnings last week, when energy chief Gus Pichot told reporters: "We have taken extensive external legal and governance advice so we are quite confident we are following the proper process."

That confidence didn't last. On Monday, Fortescue reversed course, telling the ABC that "the employee who is the subject of the allegations will not undertake any work for Fortescue while the investigation continues," and that the claims are being treated "extremely seriously." No explanation was offered for the about-turn — whether new evidence emerged, whether the earlier stance drew internal pushback, or whether external pressure simply became too loud to ignore.

A pattern, not an incident

The complaint doesn't arrive in isolation. Fortescue is separately defending a Federal Court class action filed in June by law firm JGA Saddler, alleging the company failed to protect women at its Australian sites — including remote Pilbara camps — from sex discrimination, harassment and, in some testimonies, sexual assault and stalking, dating back to 2006. JGA Saddler ran comparable suits against Rio Tinto and BHP in late 2024, part of a wider reckoning with fly-in, fly-out mining culture that both companies have spent years trying to address through independent reviews. In 2023, Fortescue itself paid roughly A$1.4 million to settle a Western Australian regulatory matter after failing to hand WorkSafe internal records on dozens of prior harassment allegations. The company's own disclosures show 98 psychosocial breaches in the past financial year — including 13 cases of inappropriate sexual contact, 10 of sexual harassment and one sexual assault — a 20% improvement on the year before, alongside 11 staff dismissals.

Why the reversal is the story

For a trading desk, an unnamed executive's suspension is not, by itself, price-moving news — Fortescue shares have traded in a narrow band around A$17.80-18.50 through August, moved far more by iron ore prices than HR disclosures. What's worth watching is whether the company's initial instinct — keep the accused at work, cite "proper process" — reflects genuine legal caution or a boardroom underestimating how a hostile-workplace story would land against the backdrop of an active class action. Iron ore miners already operate under Australian regulatory scrutiny over FIFO camp culture; a governance stumble that forces a public reversal invites questions about what other calls management is getting wrong under pressure. The MinterEllison investigation's outcome, and whether the executive is ultimately named, are the next markers to watch.
