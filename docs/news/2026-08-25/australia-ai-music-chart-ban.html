Australia bars AI-generated songs from its official music charts

In one sentence
ARIA will exclude AI-generated tracks from Australia's official charts from Friday, joining a global push that streaming platforms have so far declined to match.

Starting Friday, a song made entirely by artificial intelligence cannot appear on an official Australian music chart, no matter how many times it gets played.

The Australian Recording Industry Association has rewritten its Charts Code of Practice to exclude recordings created wholly or mostly by generative AI. The rule isn't a blanket ban on the technology — AI used in a "supporting role," say for a backing track or a mixing assist, still counts as chart-eligible. What's barred is music where AI generates "the entirety or the primary portion of the creative elements." ARIA can now strip ineligible tracks from the rankings, adjust chart positions retroactively and revoke awards; artists get a right to contest.

The trigger

The policy has an obvious origin story. In July, a cover of Madonna's "Like a Prayer" by Queensland DJ Josh Fawaz became the most-played song on Australian radio and climbed to number one on the ARIA Dance chart and number four on the ARIA singles chart. After an ABC investigation raised questions about how it was made, Fawaz updated the track's Spotify credits to disclose AI-generated vocals and drums. He defended the record — "I use AI as a tool," he said — but the backlash from the local music community was swift, and ARIA's new rule reads like a direct response. Under the updated code, a track produced the way Fawaz's was would likely be ineligible.

ARIA chief executive Annabelle Herd framed the change as a matter of self-preservation for the industry the charts represent. "This gives everyone who releases music in Australia a clear rule to work to," she said, adding that a chart "that rewards unlicensed AI output would undercut the very basis of the recorded music we exist to represent." She's also pushing radio networks and other promotional platforms to adopt matching rules.

A line, not a wall

Australia isn't acting alone, but the global picture is more fractured than a single trend line suggests. In late July, the International Federation of the Phonographic Industry — backed by Universal, Sony, Warner and independents including HYBE and BMG — published eligibility principles now spreading across more than 20 national chart programs. South Korea's copyright body, KOMCA, tried the strictest version of this in March, requiring songwriters to certify zero AI involvement; it reversed course in August after an audit found AI assistance in roughly 61% of a sample of registered songs, replacing the ban with a disclosure framework that still permits AI-assisted work with a substantial human author. India's rights bodies are reviewing the question but have issued no formal rule.

No major streaming service has followed with an outright ban. Spotify's response has been narrower — badging AI-driven artist personas and excluding them from default recommendations, which affects how an act is presented, not whether a specific recording can stream or earn royalties. That gap is the story: prestige chart placement is now a gated space, while distribution, streaming and royalty collection remain open to AI-made music almost everywhere. For labels and music-tech platforms, the practical question shifts from "can this ship" to "can this chart" — a much narrower, and more commercially loaded, distinction.

The next test comes from the streaming platforms themselves. If Spotify, Apple Music or YouTube move beyond artist-persona badging toward chart-style exclusions of their own, the market splits formally into AI and non-AI tiers. If they don't, Australia's rule stays a boundary around one prestige venue rather than the start of a broader wall.
