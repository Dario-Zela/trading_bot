Beijing mounts public defence of economy as slowdown deepens

The People's Daily headquarters in Beijing, where the paper ran unusual back-to-back commentaries defending China's economic outlook. — Morio / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Beijing published rare back-to-back People's Daily commentaries defending its economy after Q2 growth slowed to 4.3% and July retail sales barely moved.

China's government does not usually explain itself in public. When growth disappoints, the customary response from Beijing is silence, or a terse statement from the National Bureau of Statistics noting that the "external environment remains complex." So it was notable when the People's Daily, the Communist Party's flagship paper, ran two commentaries on consecutive days in August under the pen name "Zhong Caiwen" — a byline widely read as speaking for the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Commission, the body Xi Jinping chairs — insisting that "risks and hidden dangers in the economic field are being steadily resolved and contained" and that annual growth targets would still be met.

The commentaries followed a run of data that gave officials good reason to be defensive. Second-quarter GDP growth slowed to 4.3%, the weakest pace since late 2022, and July brought little relief: retail sales rose just 0.6% year-on-year, down from 1% in June and well short of the 1.5% economists had forecast. Industrial output grew 4.5%, also a miss. Property investment fell 27% in July alone, and new home prices notched their 37th straight month of decline.

A defence, not a denial

The commentaries did not dispute the numbers. Instead they argued for looking past them: growth "quality" was improving, they said, pointing to expansion in high-tech and equipment manufacturing, and urging readers to judge the economy on more than headline GDP. They acknowledged, unusually explicitly, that property, local-government debt and risk at smaller financial institutions remained live problems — but framed all three as being actively managed rather than left to fester. The pieces closed with a pledge that performance would "remain within a reasonable range" and that 2026's targets, understood to sit around 5%, would still be hit in the second half.

That combination — conceding the weak print while insisting the trajectory is under control — is itself informative. Beijing rarely spends two days of front-page commentary rebutting a single data release unless it is worried about how the release is being read, at home and abroad.

What the numbers are actually saying

The retail sales stall is the more telling of the two figures for anyone trying to gauge household confidence. Big-ticket spending collapsed in July: car sales down 17%, furniture down 8.8%, building materials down 14.2% — all categories tied to housing, which has been shrinking for more than three years. Smaller discretionary items held up better, with communication equipment up 20.4% and cosmetics up 6.8%, but that pattern looks less like resilient consumption and more like households retreating to cheap purchases while avoiding anything tied to a mortgage or a renovation. Fixed-asset investment fell 6.7% over the first seven months, and youth unemployment sat at 14.9% in June, the highest for that month since Beijing changed how it counts the figure.

None of this is new in kind — China's property slump has been running since 2021 — but the combination of a weak GDP print and stalling retail sales suggests the drag has shifted from an export or construction problem into a demand problem sitting inside Chinese households themselves.

Why traders should watch, not just read

The People's Daily commentaries are words, not policy. The relevant test comes next: whether Beijing follows the rhetoric with fiscal or monetary stimulus large enough to move the numbers, or whether the assurances stand alone. China's property sector still absorbs a large share of the country's steel, copper and cement demand, and a genuine reacceleration in stimulus tends to put a floor under industrial commodity prices and emerging-market risk sentiment more broadly. Its absence — commentary without capital — would tend to confirm the more structural reading of the slowdown: an aging, over-built economy adjusting to slower trend growth rather than one merely waiting out a cyclical air pocket.

The next test comes with August activity data, due in mid-September, alongside any concrete announcements from the Ministry of Finance on consumption subsidies or local-government bond issuance. Until then, the public defence stands as a signal in its own right — an acknowledgment, however indirect, that officials are more concerned about this slowdown than the headline growth figures alone would suggest.
