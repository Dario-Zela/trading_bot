Treasury unleashes sweeping Iran sanctions as Hegseth won't rule out Hormuz force

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who unveiled 'Operation Economic Outcast' and warned a major financial institution will be sanctioned this week. — US Department of the Treasury
In one sentence
Treasury sanctioned ~60 Iran-linked entities and vowed to hit a major bank this week, as Hegseth left military force in the Strait of Hormuz on the table.

The US Treasury put a number and a deadline on its Iran pressure campaign Monday. Nearly 60 entities, individuals and vessels tied to Tehran's nuclear, missile, cyber and oil networks were sanctioned in one sweep, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent promised something sharper still: a major financial institution doing business with Iran will be hit before the week is out.

The announcement, branded "Operation Economic Outcast," lands nearly six months into a shooting war that most of Wall Street has learned to price around. The US and Israel struck Iranian nuclear and military sites on February 28; Iran responded by attempting to shut the Strait of Hormuz, and Washington has run a naval blockade of Iranian ports ever since. Monday's sanctions push, paired with a defense secretary who won't take strikes off the table, is the clearest signal yet that the administration intends to run economic and military pressure on parallel tracks rather than pick one.

A bank in the crosshairs

Bessent's language was deliberately martial. He called the campaign "the single greatest financial offensive ever marshaled against an adversary" and said Washington was giving Iran's trading partners a choice between "prosperity and isolation, peace and terror, America and Iran." The new measures extend secondary-sanctions exposure into five sectors Iran has used to route money and materiel abroad: digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping. Any entity caught laundering money for Tehran, Bessent said, "will be removed from the US dollar system. The clock just started ticking."

He singled out Bank Melli — Iran's largest state bank, with branches scattered across the Gulf — saying it "must be shuttered and dark," though he stopped short of confirming it as the institution due to be named this week. That ambiguity is itself doing work: any bank with Gulf or Chinese counterparty exposure to Iranian trade now has a live reason to review its books. Bessent was explicit that China is not exempt, a pointed line given Beijing remains the largest buyer of Iranian crude and Presidents Trump and Xi are due to meet in Washington in late September. A secondary-sanctions hit on a Chinese lender days before that summit would be difficult for either side to shrug off diplomatically, whatever the market does with it.

Hormuz: the strait nobody fully controls

The military half of the message came from Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, who told reporters the Pentagon is "by no means... foreclosing using kinetic strikes anywhere in the Strait of Hormuz or around Iran." It's a notably open-ended formulation from a department that has spent recent weeks insisting the opposite point — that the US blockade already has the situation in hand. Hegseth said as recently as August 13 that the Navy could sustain the blockade "indefinitely," rotating ships as needed, and has maintained that oil continues to flow through the strait despite it.

Iran's answer to the blockade has been legal rather than kinetic. Its parliament's national security committee approved a measure over the weekend that would charge transit fees — for navigation, environmental services, fuel supply and insurance — to vessels from "permitted" countries passing through the strait, payable in rials or another currency Tehran deems acceptable. It still needs a full parliamentary vote and Guardian Council sign-off before becoming law. The substance matters less than the gesture: a government whose ports are under active US naval blockade is nonetheless asserting the right to toll a waterway that carries something on the order of a fifth of the world's seaborne oil. Iran's finance minister, Ali Madanizadeh, called the country "fully prepared" for the sanctions campaign, saying Tehran has a two-year plan and "knows how to play the game"; chief negotiator Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf added that the US is "not in an economic position" to police everyone else's dealings with Iran.

Markets shrug, for now

The price action so far argues that traders have already absorbed most of this. Brent crude fell roughly 1.5–2.5% on Monday to around $92–93 a barrel, and WTI slipped a comparable amount to the mid-$80s, as investors booked profits after a run-up the previous week rather than braced for fresh disruption. In London, the FTSE 100 spent Monday essentially flat, with mining stocks — Endeavour Mining up 5.3%, Antofagasta up 2%, spot gold near $4,640 an ounce — offsetting a 1.2% drop in BP and a smaller decline at Shell, alongside weakness in AstraZeneca and GSK. Banks and insurers, notably, were among the gainers: Standard Chartered rose 1%, with Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest all up around 0.6%, suggesting UK-listed lenders see limited direct exposure to whatever institution Bessent is preparing to name.

That calm is the tell. Markets have had six months to build in a baseline expectation of US-Iran friction, and a headline count of "60 entities sanctioned" no longer moves much on its own. What would move it is specificity — an actual bank, an actual date, an actual disruption at Hormuz rather than a threatened one. Bessent has now put a name-and-shame deadline on the calendar; Hegseth has left the door open on force; and Iran's parliament is quietly trying to legislate a toll booth on a strait it doesn't control. All three threads point to the same test: whether the next concrete step — the bank named this week, or an incident at Hormuz — turns a policy story into a market one.

What to watch

The financial institution Bessent promised to sanction is expected to be named by the end of this week, which will show whether the threat lands as a routine Iran-linked designation or something with genuine dollar-system counterparty risk attached. Iran's Hormuz fee legislation still needs a full parliamentary vote and Guardian Council review before it has any legal force. And the Trump-Xi summit in late September now sits downstream of a sanctions campaign that explicitly does not exempt China's Iranian oil purchases — a collision point worth watching well before the meeting itself.
