US strikes Syria from list of state sponsors of terrorism

Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa at the COP30 climate summit in Belém, Brazil, November 2025. — Ricardo Stuckert / Presidency of Brazil, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
The US formally removed Syria from its terrorism-sponsor list after 47 years, clearing the last major legal barrier to foreign investment in the post-Assad economy.

For 47 years, Syria sat on a short and unhappy list: countries the United States judged to be state sponsors of terrorism, first drawn up in 1979 alongside Libya, Iraq and South Yemen. All three of those have long since come off it. Syria was the last one left — until Monday, when the State Department formally struck it, closing a chapter of American foreign policy that outlasted the Cold War, the Assad dynasty that earned the designation, and much of the region's postwar order.

The removal took effect after a 45-day congressional review period lapsed without objection, triggered when President Trump notified lawmakers on July 8 of his intent to rescind it. Alongside it, the State Department revoked the terrorist designation for Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham, the former al-Qaeda affiliate whose fighters swept into Damascus in December 2024 and ended more than five decades of Assad family rule. Its leader, Ahmed al-Sharaa — once a wanted militant with a $10 million US bounty on his head — is now Syria's president, and stood in the Oval Office as a head of state for the first time on November 10, 2025.

A list built for the Assads, lifted for their successor

The 1979 designation was never really about geography — it was about behaviour Washington attributed to Hafez al-Assad's government: harbouring Palestinian militant factions, aligning with revolutionary Iran, and funnelling material support to Hezbollah. That behaviour outlived Hafez and continued under his son Bashar, whose government also stood accused of chemical weapons use against civilians during Syria's 13-year civil war. The label carried real teeth — it barred US foreign assistance, blocked defence exports, and required Washington to oppose loans to Syria at the IMF and World Bank.

Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the delisting was made "in recognition of the positive actions taken" by al-Sharaa's government since Assad's fall. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent framed it in blunter economic terms: the move, he said, would "help foster additional investment in Syria to promote political and economic stability." It follows a year of incremental unwinding — a Treasury general license and a Caesar Act waiver in May 2025, removal of al-Sharaa's own name from the terrorist list in November, and Congress's permanent repeal of the Caesar Act in December as part of the annual defence policy bill. Monday's move removes the last major legal scaffolding still standing.

What the delisting actually unlocks

The practical effect is to let Syria trade and borrow like an ordinary country again. The World Bank estimated in October that reconstructing Syria will cost at least $216 billion — and potentially as much as $345 billion — after a war that left roughly half of all damage concentrated in infrastructure and housing. Investors have not waited for Washington's final signature: roughly $28 billion in deals has already been pledged, including $14 billion signed at a single Damascus ceremony last August, aimed at energy, logistics, real estate and digital infrastructure. Syrian Central Bank Governor Safwat Raslan called Monday's move "a historic step that restores the country to its natural place," while US envoy Tom Barrack described it as a "decisive step in Syria's remarkable journey from isolation to partnership."

Targeted sanctions do remain — against Assad himself, Captagon traffickers, and individuals tied to ISIS and al-Qaeda — and banks are expected to move cautiously given how recently Syria was treated as radioactive for compliance purposes. But with only Cuba, Iran and North Korea left on the US terrorism-sponsor list, Syria's removal is as much a statement about where Washington now places the country diplomatically as it is a sanctions technicality.

The regional fault lines this doesn't erase

The diplomatic reset has not settled Syria's security picture. Barely a week before the delisting, Israeli jets struck the Abu al-Duhur airbase in Idlib province, hitting the runway with eight strikes after Israel said it received intelligence that Turkey intended to station a radar and air-defence unit there. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's office said Damascus was "on the verge of breaching" an informal security understanding by allowing Turkish forces onto the base. Barrack called the strikes "an unnecessary escalation," and Qatar, Egypt and Saudi Arabia issued statements condemning them — a reminder that Israel, Turkey and Gulf states are each pursuing their own read of what a post-Assad Syria should look like, sanctions relief notwithstanding.

That tension is the real test ahead. Sanctions relief gives al-Sharaa's government the tools to rebuild an economy that has contracted by more than half since 2010, and gives foreign capital a legal path back in. It does nothing to resolve who controls Syrian airspace, what role Turkey plays on its soil, or how Israel responds to a neighbour it fought a shadow war against for decades and now must recalibrate around. The next marker will be whether the investment pledges convert into disbursed capital before the security picture — Idlib, the Kurdish-held northeast, and Israel's strike posture — forces Washington to recalibrate a policy it has only just finished writing.
