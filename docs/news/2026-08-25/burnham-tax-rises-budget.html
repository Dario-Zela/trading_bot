Burnham refuses to rule out tax rises in autumn Budget

Prime Minister Andy Burnham, whose government faces a narrowing fiscal buffer ahead of the 28 October Budget. — Alecsandra Dragoi / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
PM Andy Burnham declined to rule out tax rises ahead of the 28 October Budget, and gilt yields are already reacting.

Andy Burnham has been prime minister for barely five weeks, and already the arithmetic of the job is catching up with him.

Asked directly, during his first official visit to Kyiv, whether he could rule out tax rises in the autumn Budget, Burnham declined to. "I will do what I can, but I won't be unrealistic and people really need to understand that," he told ITV News. "We are in a challenging position."

It is the closest the new government has come to admitting what markets have suspected since Burnham took office in July: that the fiscal room to fund his early policy blitz — a £340m package to cut rough sleeping, VAT scrapped on domestic electricity bills, a £2 cap on bus fares — is running out, and October's Budget is where the bill comes due.

A shrinking buffer

Chancellor John Healey has set the Budget for 28 October and asked the Office for Budget Responsibility to prepare a fresh forecast alongside it. That forecast is expected to show the government's fiscal headroom — the buffer against its own borrowing rules — thinner than it was in the spring, squeezed further by the cost of Burnham's giveaways and by the wider economic drag from the escalation in the Middle East.

Burnham has said he intends to keep Labour's 2024 manifesto pledge not to raise income tax, National Insurance or VAT, and to stick to the fiscal rule requiring day-to-day spending to be met by revenue by 2029-30. That leaves him reaching for the rules' one obvious lever: more borrowing for investment, dressed up as "flexibility." Bond investors have not been reassured. The 10-year gilt yield has climbed to around 5.04%, the highest among G7 economies, and the 30-year yield touched roughly 5.75%, a two-month high, each time Burnham has floated the idea in public. Sterling has softened alongside the moves.

That combination — a prime minister unwilling to close off tax rises, and a chancellor short of room to avoid them — is precisely the setup that unsettles gilt and currency markets ahead of a Budget. Investors read "won't rule out" as a signal, not a hedge.

For companies and households, the practical question is which taxes move if income tax, NI and VAT are off the table: capital gains, dividends, property, or the freeze on income-tax thresholds dragging more earners into higher bands by stealth. Any of those would weigh on consumer spending and corporate earnings in ways equity investors have started to price in cautiously rather than dramatically — UK indices have not sold off, but gilts have told a clearer story than shares this month.

The next hard data point is 28 October, when the OBR's forecast lands alongside Healey's Budget and the size of the shortfall — and Burnham's choice of how to fill it — becomes public. Until then, expect gilt yields to keep doing the talking.
