Plane may have broken apart before deadly Florida jet crash, investigators say

Flames and smoke at the Greenacres crash site, metres from a busy road, where all nine people on board the Learjet died. — Eduin Cruz/Reuters
In one sentence
The NTSB says a Learjet 60 that crashed in Florida, killing all nine aboard, may have begun breaking up before impact, but the cause is unknown and weather is a major focus.

US investigators say a business jet that crashed in South Florida on Sunday may have started to come apart in the air before it hit the ground. The accident killed all nine people on board. The finding is early and the cause is undetermined. The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) has also said weather will be a major focus of its inquiry.

What investigators have said

The aircraft was a Learjet 60. It crashed in a residential area of Greenacres, in Palm Beach County, about 11km south-west of West Palm Beach. Video posted online shows it striking the ground beside a busy road and exploding. The jet had left Austin, Texas, bound for North Eleuthera in the Bahamas. Two crew and seven passengers were aboard.

Tom Chapman, the NTSB member leading the briefing, said investigators had found evidence that the aircraft began breaking up before impact, and that debris could be widely scattered. He did not say which parts were involved or what the evidence was. He said recovery would be a "daunting challenge" because the impact crater has filled with rainwater.

The wording is cautious, and it matters. "Evidence" of breakup is not a finding of structural failure, and the NTSB has not said what caused the aircraft to come apart, if it did. An airframe can break up because a defect or fatigue weakened it. It can also break up because it was overstressed by something else, such as extreme speed or turbulence.

The flight's final minutes

Air traffic control recordings captured by the LiveATC.net website, as reported by ABC News (Australia), give a partial picture. The pilot reported ice building on the aircraft and turbulence that jolted it by as much as 700 feet. He first described the ice as light and the turbulence as moderate, then asked to climb about 2,000 feet.

The jet then lost height rapidly. The pilot declared an emergency and reported a descent of 10,000 feet a minute. He later reported an overspeed condition at 20,000 feet a minute. Other video shows the aircraft banking sharply to the right before impact.

These details point in two directions. Ice, turbulence and an uncontrolled dive are a plausible route to loading the airframe beyond its limits, which would make the breakup a consequence of the upset rather than its origin. An aviation safety expert quoted by the BBC suggested that key parts of the tail may have been lost in the turbulence or during the dive. The sequence can only be settled by working out which parts separated first, and at what altitude.

Who was on board

The dead included the copilot, Nathalia Gadelha Filgueiras, 20, who earned her pilot's licence in 2023 while still at school. Her licence carried a rating for the Learjet model but not for commanding the flight. Also killed were the technology entrepreneur Adam Robinson, his wife Helen Sharp and their children, Emma, 4, and Charlie, 2. The other victims were Rachael Reed, Charmi Fehr and Anita Maria Cetina, the family's nanny. The operator has not been named in the reports reviewed.

About 600 residents of almost 80 mobile homes near the site have been sheltered. Joel Flores, a Palm Beach County commissioner, said the county did not know when they could return home. One man leaving the area in a vehicle suffered a leg injury.

What to watch

The aircraft was originally fitted with a cockpit voice recorder, and investigators hope to recover it. Its condition, together with the wreckage map, will show whether the breakup came before or after the loss of control. The NTSB usually issues a preliminary report within about 30 days. That report would be the first formal statement of what was found. A probable-cause finding typically takes a year or more. Any inspection orders for the Learjet 60 fleet, or action directed at the operator, would depend on evidence of a structural fault rather than weather damage. No such evidence has been made public.
