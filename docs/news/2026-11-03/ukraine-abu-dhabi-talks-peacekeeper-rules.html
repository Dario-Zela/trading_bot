UKRAINE · CEASEFIRE · By C. Tanaka · 2026-11-03 · 4 min read

Washington plans Abu Dhabi talks with Kyiv and Moscow as Europe drafts rules of engagement and rehearses a Ukraine force

In one sentence
Draft rules reportedly let European peacekeepers fire back at Russian forces as Europe rehearses a deployment and the US plans Abu Dhabi talks, sharpening Moscow's objection.

The argument over a Western force for Ukraine after a ceasefire is shifting. The question is no longer mainly whether European soldiers would deploy, but what they would be allowed to shoot at. Three developments reported in the past few days bear on that: a report that draft rules of engagement would let UK and European troops return fire against Russian forces, a large European exercise rehearsing a deployment, and a US plan for three-way talks in Abu Dhabi.

Some of this rests on single-source reporting. Where that is so, it is flagged below.

The rules of engagement

The force in question is the mission planned by the UK- and France-led Coalition of the Willing. It is designed to deploy only after a ceasefire is in place, so there is as yet no truce for it to monitor. The Guardian reports that draft rules would allow European peacekeepers to fire back at Russian forces. This account comes from one outlet. The precise wording and the conditions attached to it, such as whether the right covers only self-defence of personnel or extends to defending positions and Ukrainian territory, have not been independently confirmed.

The distinction matters. A force that may fire only in narrow self-defence, and can withdraw when pressed, is a tripwire or a reassurance presence. A force with a standing right to return fire, and to hold what it occupies, starts to resemble a deterrent. Its purpose then is to make a Russian breach of the truce costly, and a political signal becomes a military commitment.

That is where Moscow's objection becomes concrete. Russia has consistently rejected any troops from NATO countries on Ukrainian soil. Until now that objection has been argued over deployment as a principle. Rules of engagement are where it turns operational. If an incident occurs along a ceasefire line, the text of those rules decides whether European soldiers return fire at Russian ones.

A rehearsal before there is a peace

The planning is also becoming visible. BBC World reports a large exercise involving thousands of European troops, rehearsing a deployment to Ukraine in the event of peace. Its purpose is to test the reassurance force intended for a post-war Ukraine. The troop numbers, participating countries, exercise name and dates were not confirmed at the time of writing and should be checked against the BBC's report.

Even without those details, the significance is plain. Governments do not usually drill a deployment for a settlement that does not exist unless they want others to see it. The exercise tests logistics, command arrangements and, in all likelihood, the rules discussed above. It is also a message to Kyiv that the guarantee is being built, and to Moscow that it is being built regardless of Russian objections.

Talks in Abu Dhabi

Washington, meanwhile, is working on the diplomatic side. A US official has said trilateral talks between the United States, Ukraine and Russia are planned in Abu Dhabi next week, according to Reuters. The material available names no negotiators, dates or agenda. Bloomberg, as relayed by Benzinga, separately reports a fresh NATO push to keep arms deliveries to Ukraine flowing.

The two tracks pull in different directions. One has Washington convening both warring parties. The other has allies tightening military support for one of them and drafting a force that Moscow has said it will not accept. The security guarantee is likely to be one of the hardest items on any agenda. Europe wants a credible force. Russia wants none. The United States has to find wording that both sides can live with.

What to watch

The first test is whether the Abu Dhabi talks go ahead as announced, and whether Russia's delegation raises the foreign-troops question. The second is whether the draft rules of engagement are confirmed, denied or qualified by London or Paris. A confirmed right to return fire would sharpen Moscow's objection, and a hedge would suggest the mission is being designed to avoid the confrontation it is meant to deter. Detail on the exercise's size and participants would show how far European capitals are committed beyond the planning stage.
