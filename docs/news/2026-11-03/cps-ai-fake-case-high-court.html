CPS prosecutor cited imaginary case in High Court after AI search

In one sentence
The CPS put two non-existent cases before the High Court, probably from generative AI; the judge accepted its apology but recorded the episode and warned of unsupervised AI research.

A lawyer for the Crown Prosecution Service put two court authorities in front of the High Court that do not exist. The CPS now says generative AI was probably where they came from. The errors were caught before the hearing and changed nothing in the outcome. They still got into a judgment, because the judge decided a mistake of this kind needed to be on the record.

The judgment, reported as [2026] EWHC 1720 (Admin), was covered by legal trade outlets in July 2026. The wording is cited from those reports. A single Guardian report was the prompt for this piece, and it could not be located, so its exact phrasing, including the description of the lapse as 'a serious failure', could not be confirmed. That phrase does not appear in the reports checked.

What happened

The case was a pair of extradition appeals brought by Romanian nationals, reported by Law360 under the name Tobosaru v Court of Law Craiova. The CPS acted for the Romanian authorities. Two cited cases appeared in its grounds of opposition, prepared the year before. They were then carried forward into a document headed 'Respondent submissions'.

They got past the permission stage. They were not relied on in the February skeleton argument, but that document did not say they had already been identified as unreliable. The CPS first gave the court an account suggesting the appellants' counsel had spotted the problem. It later corrected that and apologised twice. Both appeals were rejected.

What the CPS and the judge said

The CPS told the court the 'operative cause was human error in the failure to verify the authorities relied upon'. It said generative AI may have been the immediate source, and it stressed that this was not a deliberate attempt to mislead. The tool was not named in the reports.

A Chief Crown Prosecutor told the court that a full internal review had been carried out. Senior managers went through 78 other cases handled by the same lawyer and found no similar problems.

Mr Justice Sweeting accepted the apology. He set out what happened 'given the serious consequences that an error of this nature might have had in other circumstances'. He added that the episode 'highlights the risks of its use without appropriate oversight particularly for legal research'. Legal Futures noted that he did not appear to apply the Ayinde guidance, the earlier High Court warning about fake citations, in deciding whether to take further action.

Why it matters

Fake citations from AI tools have turned up in court filings before, mostly from individual litigants and private practitioners. This one involved the state prosecution service, which handles a large volume of work, and the lawyer's own caseload had to be audited as a result.

The CPS's own explanation is the useful part. It blames a missing check, not the software. A lawyer who files an authority vouches for it, whatever produced it, and a search tool that can invent a case does not change that duty. It does make the check unavoidable: every citation has to be looked up in a primary source before it goes into a filing.

The CPS says it has reminded its lawyers of their duties as officers of the court, reviewed its processes and shared the lessons across the organisation. Whether that is enough is open. The reports show no further sanction, and the next test will be whether another filing, from the CPS or anyone else, brings the same problem back before a court.
