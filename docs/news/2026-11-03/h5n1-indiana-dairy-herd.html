H5N1 confirmed in another Indiana dairy herd

In one sentence
The USDA confirmed H5N1 in an Indiana dairy herd on 2 November, the state's first cattle case, found by routine milk testing, as Washington's outbreak reached 40 herds.

The US Department of Agriculture has confirmed highly pathogenic avian influenza H5N1 in a dairy herd in Indiana. The detection was announced on 2 November and is the state's first in cattle. The headline's "another" is therefore slightly off: this is a new state for the outbreak, not a repeat. Indiana's own animal-health board reported in its August situation update that it had not diagnosed the disease in livestock. Its 2026 cases had been in poultry, mostly commercial duck operations in LaGrange County.

What the USDA has said

The National Veterinary Services Laboratories confirmed the infection by PCR testing. The virus belongs to genotype B3.13, the lineage tied to the cattle outbreak since it was first identified in March 2024. The herd was found through routine sampling under the National Milk Testing Strategy, not through pre-movement testing of animals bound for sale. That matters. It suggests the surveillance programme is catching infections that nobody had flagged by clinical signs, though the release does not say whether the animals were showing illness.

The agency's release leaves several things unstated. It does not name the county or give the herd's size. It does not say how the virus arrived. One aggregator, which cites the release, reports Newton County. The release itself, as retrieved, names no county, so that detail should be treated as unconfirmed. Nor did the USDA say whether other herds are under investigation. The Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service said it is working with the Indiana State Board of Animal Health on on-farm investigations, diagnostic testing and the collection of epidemiological data. It added that the detection does not change its eradication strategy.

Indiana's response plan, published by the state board, calls for a quarantine on lactating cattle on the affected premises and a 10-kilometre surveillance zone around it. Whether those measures have been invoked here has not been reported.

Why this one is more than a routine update

On its own, one herd is a small event. The context is what lifts it. The USDA says dairy cattle in 21 states have now been infected since March 2024, and it notes recent cases in previously unaffected states. Colorado has reported its first infected herd in nearly two years. Washington state, where the virus was confirmed in two Yakima County herds on 8 October, reported six more on 30 October. That brought its total to 40 herds, nearly all in Yakima County.

The Washington outbreak has also had a human cost. The Yakima Health District said a county resident who had been exposed to infected dairy cattle died of avian influenza on 1 November. The person had an underlying health condition, and the district withheld their name, age and sex. Capital Press reported it as the third US death from the virus and the first following exposure on a dairy farm. "While this is a serious and heartbreaking outcome, the risk to the general public remains low," the district's health officer, Dave Atteberry, said.

Nothing published links the Indiana herd to Washington. The two are separated by roughly 2,000 miles, and genotype B3.13 is not specific to either. The Washington article did not name a genotype, so even that comparison cannot be drawn from available reporting. Linking the cases would take sequencing and animal-movement tracing that has not yet been released.

Safety of milk and risk to people

The Food and Drug Administration says pasteurisation inactivates the virus, and the USDA says milk from affected animals is diverted or destroyed so it does not enter the food supply. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention continues to rate the risk to the general public as low. People with occupational or recreational exposure to infected animals face higher risk and are advised to follow CDC precautions, including protective equipment.

What to watch

The next indicators are concrete. The first is whether the USDA's livestock dashboard lists the Indiana herd and any further Indiana herds, and in which counties. The second is whether state testing of neighbouring dairies turns up more positives, which would suggest local spread beyond a single introduction. The third is whether sequencing ties the herd to a known cattle lineage or to a fresh spillover from wild birds, as in the Wisconsin case reported last December. Any of those would change this from a one-herd addition to a sign of wider spread.
