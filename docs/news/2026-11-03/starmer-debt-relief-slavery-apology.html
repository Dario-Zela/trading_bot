Starmer floats debt relief as slavery apology talks begin

In one sentence
Starmer is reported to be floating debt relief as formal apology talks open with affected Commonwealth countries, which would turn a symbolic gesture into a fiscal commitment.

Britain is preparing to open talks on a formal apology for its part in the transatlantic slave trade, and Keir Starmer is reported to be considering a debt reset for affected Commonwealth countries alongside it. Headlines from the BBC and the Guardian describe the plan. The full texts could not be checked for this article, and no dates, debt figures or named countries have been confirmed. Debt relief is being floated, not adopted.

From symbol to balance sheet

An apology costs a government little. Debt relief does cost money. If the two are linked, the apology becomes a policy commitment with a fiscal side, and other governments will read it as a precedent.

The reported plan also changes who drafts the apology. Starmer is said to want affected countries to help shape its wording, and presumably what comes with it. That is a different posture from one in which London announces a statement and the Caribbean and African capitals respond to it.

How the position moved

The shift is large. Before the October 2024 Commonwealth summit in Samoa, Downing Street said reparations were "not on the agenda" and that "we won't be offering an apology". Starmer called slavery "abhorrent" but said the talks would not be "about money".

Caribbean governments pressed anyway. The Caribbean Community's ten-point reparations plan includes a formal apology, debt cancellation, technology transfer and help with public health and illiteracy. The summit ended with Britain agreeing to further discussion of the legacy of slavery. Reporting at the time, citing a source in Starmer's office, said he had opened the door to non-financial measures such as debt relief. The latest reports suggest that idea has moved from briefing to a possible proposal.

What is unsettled

Three questions will decide whether the idea matters.

  • Scale. Nothing published says which debts would be covered, or whether Britain would write off its own claims or help restructure debt owed to multilateral lenders. The first would be a modest line in the accounts. The second would take years of negotiation with institutions London does not control.
  • Terms. Whether relief is framed as reparation or as development policy matters legally and politically. Britain has long avoided the language of reparations, partly out of concern that it would invite claims.
  • Reception. Caribbean governments have asked for an apology and for tangible measures. They may welcome the invitation to help shape the process. They may also regard a debt reset as too narrow, or as a substitute for the wider programme they have sought.

There is a domestic question as well. The Treasury is under pressure, and relief that is not clearly tied to spending already planned would invite scrutiny.

What to watch

The first marker is whether the government publishes terms for the talks: which countries are invited, who leads for London, and whether debt is on the formal agenda. The second is the next Commonwealth leaders' gathering, where governments that pressed Britain in Samoa will test whether the offer is substantive. Other former colonial powers will be watching what Britain puts on the table.
