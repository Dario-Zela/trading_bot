King's former horse wins the Melbourne Cup

In one sentence
Point of Law, sold by King Charles III earlier this year to an Australian syndicate, won the Melbourne Cup by 3.75 lengths in a Ciaron Maher one-two-three.

A three-year-old colt sold out of the royal string earlier this year has won the Melbourne Cup. Point of Law, bred in England and owned until recently by King Charles III and Queen Camilla, took the 3,200-metre handicap at Flemington on Tuesday by 3.75 lengths.

It was a fine day for the Maher stable. Ciaron Maher trained the first three home, with Point of Law ahead of stablemates Zahrann and Juja Kibo. It is his second Melbourne Cup win. Ethan Brown, the winning jockey, claimed his first.

From Windsor to Flemington

The royal link is confirmed by ABC News, which describes Point of Law as "formerly owned by King Charles III and Queen Camilla". Its separate Cup roundup says the King "sold the colt earlier this year" to a consortium of Australians. It names one member: Chris Judd, the former West Coast and Carlton star.

The detail is thinner than the headline. ABC gives no exact sale date, price or method. Other outlets give differing months for the sale, so the timing is best left at "earlier this year". Maher thanked "the owners" after the race without naming them, and the rest of the syndicate has not been identified in the reports checked. No reaction from the King or the Palace had been reported at the time of writing.

The colt is a Northern Hemisphere three-year-old, and the Cup was only his sixth start. A young European import, sold on to Australian buyers and then asked to see out two miles in the country's biggest race, is a well-worn route to the Cup. Winning it with a horse bred and raced for the monarch is less usual.

The race that stops a nation

The Melbourne Cup is run on the first Tuesday of November, a date that closes offices and fills Flemington. Its nickname, "the race that stops a nation", is only a mild exaggeration. For the royal racing operation, which is run with a long-term breeding eye rather than a sales one, the result carries a certain wryness: the prize went to the buyers.

ABC's roundup could not resist the obvious speculation about how the late Queen Elizabeth II, a devoted owner-breeder, might have taken her son selling a horse that went on to win the Cup. Whatever the answer, the sale will be remembered at least as well as the win.

What next

Two things are worth watching. One is the identity of the full ownership group and what Point of Law is now worth, since a Cup winner's value rises sharply. The other is whether the Palace says anything about the sale. Maher's plans for the colt, including whether he stays in Australia or returns to Europe, have not been announced.
