Scientists report signals at the smallest scale yet explored

In one sentence
LHC physicists report sensitivity to physics at about 10⁻²¹ metres, the smallest scale yet probed, but no new particle has been found and the paper's status is unclear.

Physicists at the Large Hadron Collider have reported seeing the effects of physics at a distance of about a billionth of a trillionth of a metre (10⁻²¹ m), roughly 100 billion times smaller than an atom. The finding was first reported by the BBC on 3 November. The scale is the smallest yet probed by experiment, according to the researchers. Nothing new has been found at it, and the claim is more modest than the headline suggests.

What was done

The LHC, at CERN on the Swiss-French border, smashes particles together at very high energy. Researchers then study the debris. This team, led from the University of Cambridge, looked at a beauty quark, an unstable particle that decays after about 1.5 trillionths of a second. Despite that brief life, the team detected it interacting with other tiny particles.

The logic is indirect. Quantum theory lets short-lived, unseen particles influence how a quark decays, even when they are too heavy to be made directly. A tiny shift in a decay rate or pattern can therefore carry information about distances far smaller than any microscope could resolve. Some physicists call this regime the "zeptoverse".

Dr Harry Cliff, who led the data analysis, put it this way: "We're knocking on the door of this zeptoverse." He added that more data is needed to find any new particles. So the report is of sensitivity to this scale, not of a discovery there.

What is not yet clear

The BBC article, as reproduced by Yahoo, does not name a journal or paper, so the result's peer-review status is unclear. It links the work to the LHCb detector's planned upgrade and quotes the LHCb collaboration leader, but it never explicitly names LHCb as the source of the data. Those gaps matter. "Smallest scale ever explored" is a claim about the reach of a measurement, and it can only be judged against the paper's own uncertainties and earlier limits.

The dark matter angle is also a possibility, not a result. Some theories hold that particles at this scale could be more sensitive to dark matter, which makes up much of the universe but is seen only through its gravity. If so, the effects might show up in how quarks interact and decay. No dark matter detection is reported.

Money, and who gets to look

The more concrete news in the story is about funding. The 28 member countries have agreed a £150m upgrade to the LHCb detector, which would raise the rate of these decays tenfold. UK Research and Innovation has said it will withdraw its share, as part of cuts that also affect Jodrell Bank's Lovell telescope.

Prof Tim Gershon, who leads the LHCb collaboration, warned: "The UK is at risk of not being involved and making really major discoveries." Dr Mary Richardson-Slipper, part of the Cambridge team, said she is considering jobs abroad because of better opportunities there. Prof Brian Cox, who was not involved, called the result "important" but criticised the funding cut. UKRI says it remains a fully engaged CERN member and its second-largest funder, at about £175m a year.

What to watch

The first marker is the paper itself: its venue, its statistical significance and how the claimed scale is defined. The second is the upgrade. A tenfold rise in decays would show whether the faint effects hold up or fade, and the UK's decision on its share will determine who is in the room when the data arrive.
