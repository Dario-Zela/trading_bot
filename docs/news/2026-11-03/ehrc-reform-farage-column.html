Equalities watchdog raises concerns with Reform over documentary remarks as Farage's Telegraph column ends

In one sentence
The equalities regulator has written to Reform UK asking how it is meeting the Equality Act after an ITV undercover documentary, a first step that stops short of a formal investigation.

The Equality and Human Rights Commission, the statutory regulator for equality law in England, Scotland and Wales, has written to Reform UK about remarks made in an ITV undercover documentary. The letter asks the party what it is doing to comply with the Equality Act 2010. It is a request for information. The reports seen so far do not describe a formal investigation.

What the regulator has asked

The EHRC said it had received complaints alleging unlawful activity under the Equality Act after the broadcast of Undercover: A Year in Reform. ITV's reporting on the film is dated 14 October. The watchdog's letter asks Reform to set out the steps it is taking to meet its obligations under the Act. The reports describe the concern as being about "racial and sexual remarks".

The programme, as reported by ITV and Reuters, showed Reform councillors and candidates making racist, sexual and violent remarks. One candidate was reported to have called Asian homeowners "parasites" and to have expressed anti-Muslim views. Two councillors who were later elected made sexually explicit comments about female Reform candidates. Reform MP Sarah Pochin was shown describing the party as "chauvinistic" and full of "alpha male shit".

One source dates the broadcast to September. The ITV coverage points to October, and that is the date used here.

Reform's response

A Reform spokesperson said the party was confident it had complied with its Equality Act obligations and that there was "no room for racism in Reform UK". The party has said the individuals filmed are under investigation and have resigned or been suspended. It has also argued that not everyone captured on camera was a member. It reported ITV and the journalists involved to the police and has threatened civil action against the broadcaster. Nigel Farage has said the party's vetting of candidates has improved greatly.

The EHRC letter also sits alongside a separate approach. A group of Labour MPs led by Afzal Khan wrote to the commission alleging Islamophobia within Reform. That letter named several of the party's elected representatives and was not confined to the documentary. Reform's reply to it was: "We stand by our comments. We will not be intimidated." The coverage seen does not say whether the commission's letter relates to this complaint, to those about the film, or to both.

Why a letter matters

The EHRC has powers to open inquiries and investigations, and to issue legally binding agreements or compliance notices. A request for information is the first and mildest step. It puts on record that the regulator has taken the complaints seriously enough to ask for answers. It does not make a finding. Whether the exchange stays at that level will depend on how detailed Reform's reply is, in particular on what it says about candidate vetting, training and complaint handling.

The politics are awkward for the party. Reform has built much of its appeal on being a challenger to established institutions, and its leadership has been quick to attack the broadcaster. A regulator asking about its internal conduct is harder to dismiss as a media story.

The Telegraph column

The Guardian has also reported that Mr Farage's regular Telegraph column is to end. The material available gives no date, no reason, and no statement from the paper or from Mr Farage, so it is not clear whether the move is connected to the documentary row or is a routine change. It is better read as a separate development than as evidence of a breach with the paper.

What to watch

The next marker is Reform's written reply to the EHRC and whether the commission publishes it or moves to a formal stage. Until then, the letter is a question to the party rather than a finding against it.
