Supreme Court rejects Trump's bid to stop California counting disputed votes

In one sentence
The Supreme Court refused, in an unsigned one-line order on 2 November, to make California set aside about 20,000 ballots the Justice Department calls likely noncitizen.

The Supreme Court has turned away the Trump administration's emergency request to make California set aside roughly 20,000 mail ballots that the Justice Department says come from "likely noncitizens." The order came on Monday, 2 November, the eve of the midterm elections. Counting continues as before. The ruling decides no question about who may vote. It settles only that the justices will not rewrite California's count while the case is still being litigated.

What the court did

The order was one line and unsigned. Reports describe no noted dissents and no explanation. The court acted without asking California officials to respond. Reports differ on the mechanics: some say a single justice, Elena Kagan, denied the application without referring it to colleagues, and others describe it as an order of the court. Either way, no reasoning accompanies it, so it should not be read as a ruling on the merits.

That is the point of the emergency docket. The Justice Department did not ask the court to decide whether any particular person is a citizen. It asked for interim relief: segregate the flagged ballots now, sort out eligibility later. Its argument, as reported, was that once an unlawfully cast ballot is counted and commingled with lawful ones, the harm cannot be undone.

How it got there

The Justice Department sued California on 15 October. It has lost at every stage since.

  • District court: Judge David Carter rejected a temporary restraining order last week. He called the government's process for identifying alleged noncitizens "rife with uncertainty and errors," according to CBS News.
  • Ninth Circuit: On 30 October a 2-1 panel declined to intervene. Judges Mark Bennett and Gabriel Sanchez wrote: "We decline the United States's invitation to contravene the Elections Clause by undermining the Constitution's plain text." They also said there is "no feasible way to identify and segregate the ballots of specific individuals," and that the request likely violates the National Voter Registration Act's bar on systematic voter-list purges within 90 days of an election. Judge Patrick Bumatay, dissenting, called the proposal "a modest remedy."

State and county officials had warned that the federal list was unreliable, that eligible citizens could be disenfranchised, and that segregating ballots would push counting past California's 3 December certification deadline. California has more than 23 million registered voters, so 20,000 flagged people is a small fraction of the electorate. The dispute is about method and authority more than margin.

Why the emergency docket is the story

The lower-court rulings leaned heavily on who runs elections: the Constitution's Elections Clause gives states the primary role in setting the manner of voting. The Supreme Court's refusal leaves that reading undisturbed for now, without endorsing it.

Timing also mattered. A change to counting procedure ordered days before an election, on the strength of a contested federal list, would have been a large intervention by a federal court in a state's administration of its own vote. The justices declined, and did so before the polls opened.

What comes next

The underlying case, United States v. State of California, remains live. A hearing is reported for 13 November, according to local coverage; CBS did not mention one, so the date is worth confirming. Counting will largely be done by then, which raises a practical question: whether a court could order any remedy after the fact. The Justice Department's complaint also rests on its claim about the 20,000 names, which a court has yet to test on the evidence. California's certification deadline of 3 December is the next fixed point.
