UK and EU agree draft deal to scrap Brexit red tape on food exports

In one sentence
The UK and EU have agreed a draft SPS deal that would remove most food-export border checks in return for UK alignment with EU rules, but it is unsigned and has no firm start date.

The United Kingdom and the European Union have agreed a draft text that would strip out most of the routine border checks on British food and farm exports. The text is a draft, not a signed treaty, and nothing changes at Dover or Calais until it has been approved, ratified and brought into force. The deal is the most concrete sign yet of how far the two sides will go in unpicking the friction that followed Brexit.

What the draft says

The text is a sanitary and phytosanitary (SPS) agreement. It covers the health rules behind trade in animals, plants and food. Reports describe a document of roughly 200 pages, which is due to be reviewed by EU ambassadors at a Coreper meeting on Wednesday and discussed ahead of a UK-EU summit later this month. The principle was agreed at the May 2025 summit, and the details have taken since then to settle.

The mechanism is what officials call "dynamic alignment". The UK would observe, and keep pace with, EU rules on plant, animal and food standards. In return, the routine certificates and border inspections that have burdened exporters since 2021 would largely go. The government has long described the end state as a shared "SPS zone".

Some products that Brexit effectively shut out would be able to resume trade. Reports name fresh sausages and burgers, shellfish from domestic waters and seed potatoes. The text would also allow passports for UK pets taken into the EU. The government's own estimate is that the UK economy would eventually gain £5.1bn a year. That is a ministerial projection, not an independent forecast, and it assumes the deal is fully in operation.

The size of the problem

The case for the deal rests on how much trade has been lost. The government says UK agrifood exports to the EU fell 21% between 2018 and 2024, while imports fell 7%. The gap shows who carries the cost. British exporters face the paperwork on shipments leaving the country. Many smaller producers, particularly in meat, dairy and shellfish, found the cost of certification higher than the margin on the sale. The British Meat Processors Association has said the deal promises to bring back trade that "simply ceased" after Brexit.

Supermarkets and farm representatives have backed the plan. The British Chambers of Commerce says the current trading relationship is "failing to deliver". The EU is still the largest destination for UK red meat and dairy, so even partial recovery would register in the sector's accounts.

The price of alignment

The trade-off is sovereignty over rules. Under dynamic alignment the UK would accept changes to EU food law as they are made, without a vote on them. Earlier descriptions of the framework also give the European Court of Justice a role in overseeing it, which some farm groups have criticised. Pro-Brexit campaigners object that reapplying EU rules reverses the point of leaving. The government's answer is that the UK would be consulted on rule-making and that the economic return justifies the constraint. Which of those views prevails will shape the ratification debate at Westminster.

The draft sits within a wider package. Separate texts are reported to cover emissions trading, a youth travel and work scheme, defence cooperation and access to law-enforcement databases. Some details, including the numbers on the youth scheme, are still to be settled at political level. That makes it likely the food chapter will be traded against other items before the summit.

What to watch

A draft has three hurdles before it affects a lorry driver. The first is political sign-off: Coreper's review this week and the summit later this month. The second is legal ratification on both sides, which for a text of this size involves parliamentary scrutiny in London and the EU institutions. The third, and the one exporters care about, is implementation. Earlier ministerial messaging told businesses to prepare, but gave no firm date for checks to end, and current controls remain in place in the meantime.

The test, then, is not whether the text exists but whether it is signed and when it takes effect. Final wording on the court's role, on UK influence over future rules and on any transition period will decide how much of the £5.1bn claim is realistic. Exporters will want a date. At the moment the summit is the earliest point at which one might be given.
