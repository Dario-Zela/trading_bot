Russell Brand trial: defence says he is not a 'psychopath who rapes people'

In one sentence
Russell Brand's defence is rejecting the prosecution's portrait of a predator as his rape trial nears a jury verdict that will turn on complainants' credibility.

Russell Brand's defence team has begun its closing case to the jury at Southwark Crown Court, telling jurors that the comedian is not, in the words of the headline claim, a "psychopath who rapes people". It is the last stage of a trial that has become a contest over two competing portraits of the man: a serial predator who treated women as "disposable", or a famous, promiscuous man whose many sexual encounters were consensual.

A note on sourcing: the "psychopath" phrasing comes from the triage summary. Reports available at the time of writing covered the prosecution's closing and the run-up to the defence speech, but did not carry the defence barrister's words, so the line is not quoted directly here.

The charges

Brand, 51, denies seven counts: three of rape, three of sexual assault and one of attempted indecent assault. They involve six women, and the alleged offences span 1999 to 2009. The trial opened on 12 October, and the jury must reach a unanimous verdict on each count. The judge said at the outset that the trial was expected to last about six weeks.

Two portraits

The prosecution closed on Friday 30 October. Riel Karmy-Jones KC told jurors that women "as a whole were disposable; they didn't matter to him". She described Brand's defence as "a lot of half-truths, but spun into lies", and argued that the six complainants had "nothing to gain" by coming forward and had not been swayed by media coverage. She also said Brand's account was built around the details the accusers had given, and that he "couldn't really remember anything" when interviewed by police.

The defence case is that every encounter was consensual. Brand gave evidence for about four hours, called by his barrister, Ian Winter KC. He told the court "I'm innocent" and described the allegations as devastating. He also made concessions that cut both ways: he admitted he had been wrong to use his fame in pursuit of sex, saying "regrettably I've had a lot of consensual sex, which I see has caused harm", and acknowledged small inconsistencies in his recollection. He said no one with relevant information had agreed to testify for him. Those admissions give the defence a way to argue that a man who is candid about his flaws is not the monster described.

That is the logic of the argument that he is no "psychopath". It does not dispute that Brand behaved badly by many standards; it contests the leap from careless, fame-fuelled promiscuity to criminal predation. The jury is being asked to decide whether the prosecution's portrait is a fair inference from the evidence or a caricature.

What the verdict will test

The case rests heavily on credibility. The events are decades old, the complainants' accounts are set against Brand's, and the arguments on both sides have turned on memory, motive and the weight to give inconsistencies. A verdict will show how a jury treats historic allegations against a high-profile figure, and how much a complainant's imperfect recall, or a defendant's partial admissions, counts for.

The jury is expected to retire to consider its verdicts after the judge sums up, possibly within days. The trial is due to conclude this week.
