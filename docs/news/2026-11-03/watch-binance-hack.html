Binance: record $6.3bn hack keeps drawing withdrawals

In one sentence
The largest crypto hack on record, about $6.3bn at Binance, has reportedly drawn heavy withdrawals, while North Korea, a prime suspect, is said to deny involvement.

Binance, the world's largest crypto exchange, says hackers have taken about $6.3bn from it. That would be the largest crypto theft on record, and it is now a test of whether customers still trust the platform with their money.

The scale is easiest to see against the previous record. In February 2025 the Bybit exchange lost about $1.5bn, a hack the FBI attributed to North Korea's TraderTraitor unit. The Binance loss is roughly four times that.

What is known

The blockchain analytics firm TRM Labs said an attacker moved about $6.36bn out of Binance wallets across ten blockchains, most of it within roughly 20 minutes. TRM attributed the attack to TraderTraitor, which it called North Korea's "elite hacking unit", and said it did so "with high confidence". Chainalysis chief executive Jonathan Levin said on X that the hack was "likely done by DPRK group TraderTraitor". TRM pointed to features shared with earlier North Korea-linked heists, Bybit among them.

Binance has confirmed the theft and said its own figure "may continue to increase". It says it worked with blockchain partners to freeze roughly $3bn of the affected assets, including more than $2.3bn through the stablecoin issuers Tether and Circle. That leaves about $3.3bn whose fate Binance has not described.

The exchange has promised to cover all losses from company funds and its Secure Asset Fund for Users (SAFU). It said deposits were unaffected, withdrawals were suspended, and it expected them to resume within a day or two. Founder Changpeng Zhao posted that "User funds are safe!" and that, as a shareholder, he expects customers to be made whole.

The cause is unsettled. Binance first pointed to a compromised laptop, then said a later investigation found further "points of breach". It says it has found no evidence that a wallet private key leaked. Kok Tin Gan of the security firm NyxLab questioned why stronger approval controls were not in place. He suggested a single signature may have yielded a non-expiring, full-access token to a hot wallet. That is speculation, not a finding.

The run on the exchange

The angle that matters for markets is the customer reaction. The reports behind this brief put user withdrawals from Binance at $3.6bn in the aftermath. This desk could not match that figure against Binance's own statements, and the sources it did check describe withdrawals as paused. The number should be read as reported, not confirmed, and it may reflect outflows before the suspension or after service resumed.

Even so, the sums are clear. If the figure holds, customers have withdrawn more than half the amount stolen, on top of the theft itself. An exchange that promises to cover a loss of this size must keep enough liquid assets to do so while its users are also asking for their money back. Binance says it can. The test of that claim is whether withdrawal queues clear without further delay.

Attribution and denial

North Korea is named as a prime suspect and has denied involvement, according to the reporting behind this piece. The AP report this desk reviewed carries no statement from Pyongyang, so the denial is not independently confirmed here. Pyongyang has denied earlier attributions as well, and analysts say crypto theft funds its weapons programme despite UN sanctions. A Binance spokesperson would say only that the company is working with law enforcement "to confirm the identity of the criminals and bring them to justice".

What to watch

Three things will settle the matter. The first is the resumption of withdrawals and how quickly queues clear. The second is Binance's promised technical post-mortem, which should show whether the breach was a stolen credential or a flaw in its wallet controls. The third is the fate of the roughly $3.3bn not yet frozen, since movement through mixers or exchanges would show how much is recoverable. If North Korean authorship is formally confirmed by US authorities, pressure on regulators over state-linked hackers is likely to return.
