HSBC weighs Turkey exit as it joins new Swift payments push

In one sentence
HSBC is in early talks to sell its shrunken Turkish unit to Emirates NBD, even as it joins a new Swift standard for faster, fee-transparent cross-border payments.

HSBC Holdings is exploring an exit from Turkey while simultaneously signing on to a new industry framework meant to make cross-border payments faster and more transparent — two moves, announced within days of each other, that together sketch a bank narrowing its retail footprint in harder markets while investing in the plumbing of global payments.

HSBC is in early talks to sell its Turkish banking operations to Emirates NBD, the Dubai-based lender majority owned by the Investment Corporation of Dubai, according to a Bloomberg report. Both banks have declined to comment beyond the standard line about not discussing market speculation, and people familiar with the matter caution the talks could still fall apart before any deal is signed.

A shrinking Turkish footprint

What's left to sell is a fraction of what HSBC once ran in Turkey. The bank operated more than 300 branches there in 2013; that network had shrunk to roughly 36 by March 2026. HSBC Turkey now ranks 15th among the country's lenders by assets, with about 0.3% of total loans — a rounding error in a banking system dominated by domestic giants and, increasingly, foreign entrants with local scale.

Emirates NBD already has that scale, by way of DenizBank, the roughly 500-branch lender it bought from Russia's Sberbank in 2019 after Sberbank came under Western sanctions pressure. DenizBank's strength is retail, small-business and agricultural lending; HSBC's remaining Turkish book leans toward wholesale banking, trade finance and wealth management for multinational and affluent clients. The fit, on paper, is complementary rather than overlapping — which is presumably the point.

HSBC has tried this before. It explored a Turkish exit in 2015, with ING Groep as the prospective buyer, before that deal collapsed over regulatory hurdles. Turkey's banking regulator has a history of scrutinising foreign ownership changes closely, so the current talks — described as early-stage — carry no guarantee of reaching a signed agreement, let alone regulatory clearance.

The broader pattern is familiar. Chief executive Georges Elhedery has spent the past year and a half trimming HSBC's presence in businesses and geographies that no longer clear the bank's return hurdles, following a similar logic to the retreat from French and Canadian retail banking in prior years. A Turkish exit, if it happens, would be one more subtraction from a bank increasingly organised around Asian wealth management and global transaction banking rather than a broad emerging-markets retail footprint.

The Swift piece

Separately, HSBC is among the first banks — alongside Barclays, Lloyds and NatWest in the UK — to go live on a new Swift framework for cross-border consumer payments. The initiative, backed by more than 60 banks across 25 countries, aims to fix two long-standing gripes with international transfers: unpredictable deductions along the correspondent-banking chain, and unclear fees and exchange rates at the point of sending. Under the new standard, senders see the fee and rate before confirming, and the amount that arrives should match what was quoted, with transfers landing in minutes where local systems support it.

Mark Evans, HSBC's global head of payment products, said the bank was "proud to help pioneer this initiative and set a higher standard for transparency and customer experience." The initial corridors covered include transfers from Australia, China, India and Turkey, and outbound transfers to Australia — a modest starting scope that will presumably widen as more banks connect.

The two stories share a theme more than a mechanism: HSBC retreating from direct retail exposure in a volatile market while doubling down on the infrastructure — payments rails, wealth platforms — that let it serve customers without owning the branch network. Neither move is unusual in isolation; global banks trim emerging-market retail units and join payments standards routinely. What ties them together is the direction of travel.

The next marker is whether the Emirates NBD talks produce a signed agreement — and at what price, given HSBC Turkey's thin asset base makes this more a strategic tidy-up than a material capital event for either bank.
