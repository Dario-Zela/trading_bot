US oil rig count climbs to 445, total rigs up to 580

A land-based drilling rig in the Appalachian basin — natural gas rigs rose to 126 last week, up from a September 2024 low of 96. — Meredithw / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
US oil rigs rose 5 to 445 and total rigs to 580, but the gain trails a price spike from Middle East shipping disruptions, not a durable rebound.

The number of rigs drilling for oil in the United States rose to 445 last week, up five from 440 the week before, according to Baker Hughes data released July 2. Natural-gas-directed rigs added one to reach 126. Combined with a handful of miscellaneous units, the total U.S. rig count climbed to 580 from 573 — a 1.2% increase and the second straight weekly gain after crude oil rigs bottomed at 433 two weeks earlier.

It is a small number moving in the right direction, and in an industry that has spent three years learning to do more with less, small is the operative word.

A rebound, but from a low base

Rig counts are one of the oldest tells in the oil patch — a rough proxy for how confident operators feel about prices holding long enough to justify the cost of putting steel in the ground. By that measure, confidence has been thin. The Lower 48's average monthly rig count fell from 750 in December 2022 to 517 by October 2025, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, even as crude and gas production climbed to records. Longer laterals, better completions and tighter focus on the most productive acreage let producers pump more oil from fewer holes, particularly in the Permian Basin, where rig counts dropped 29% since late 2022 while output rose 18%.

So the two-week bounce to 445 oil rigs doesn't reverse that structural decline so much as interrupt it. The current count sits well below the 1987–2026 average of roughly 498 rigs, and further still from the 1,609-rig peak of October 2014. It is also down sharply from a year ago, when North America's broader rig tally — Baker Hughes's combined U.S. and Canada figure — stood at 707, itself off from 779 in July 2024.

The price backdrop is doing the talking

What makes the timing notable is the crude market operators are drilling into. West Texas Intermediate has traded around $67 to $68 a barrel this week, elevated by the aftermath of the Iran conflict and disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, but well off the triple-digit spikes the standoff produced at its peak. The EIA's own baseline forecast has WTI averaging closer to $51 a barrel across 2026 — a level that would sit under the breakeven price many shale operators need to justify new drilling in marginal acreage, even as the most productive Permian and Appalachian wells stay profitable regardless.

That gap between today's price and the EIA's full-year forecast is the tension running under this week's numbers. A rig count that rises alongside prices propped up by geopolitical risk premium is a different signal than one rising because operators expect $70 oil to be the new normal. Baker Hughes doesn't parse motive, only machines in the field, so the honest read is that operators added five rigs while the geopolitical premium was still in the price — and the real test comes if that premium unwinds toward the EIA's Iran-related producers gradually restoring output and Strait of Hormuz flows normalizing.

Gas drilling tells a quieter version of the same story. The one-rig addition to 126 comes off a September 2024 trough of 96, when gas prices were persistently weak. The EIA expects natural gas output to grow modestly in 2026 as prices firm relative to oil, which would argue for gas rigs holding up better than their crude counterparts if crude weakens.

The next Baker Hughes report, due the second Friday of the month per the holiday-shifted schedule, will show whether last week's gain was the start of a trend or a one-off tied to a price spike that is already fading. Either way, the rig count remains a lagging confirmation of decisions operators made weeks ago — the more forward-looking signal is what WTI does from here.
