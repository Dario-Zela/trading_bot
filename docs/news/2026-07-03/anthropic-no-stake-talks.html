Anthropic says no talks held with US government over equity stake

Dario Amodei (center), Anthropic's CEO, with fellow AI leaders at 10 Downing Street in 2023 — the kind of government meeting now under fresh scrutiny. — Simon Walker / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence)
In one sentence
Anthropic says it has held no talks on a government equity stake, distancing itself from OpenAI's reported 5% stake pitch to Washington.

Anthropic has not held any talks with the Trump administration about the government taking an equity stake in the company, a source familiar with the matter said Thursday — a denial that arrived within hours of a very different report about its biggest rival.

The Financial Times reported the same day that OpenAI has discussed handing Washington a 5% stake in the company, a holding that would be worth roughly $42.6 billion at OpenAI's recent $852 billion valuation. Sam Altman is said to have first raised the idea with the administration in early 2025, with talks reportedly involving Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. The pitch: fold that 5% into a vehicle modeled on the Alaska Permanent Fund, the sovereign fund that pays Alaskans a yearly dividend from the state's oil revenue, so ordinary Americans get a cut of AI's upside. Altman has framed it as the fairest way to spread gains from an industry now valued in the trillions.

That report immediately raised the question of whether other frontier labs were in similar conversations. Anthropic's answer, relayed through a spokesperson to Benzinga and other outlets, was a flat no. Neither the White House nor the Commerce Department added anything on the record.

A tenser backdrop than it looks

The timing matters. Anthropic spent much of June sparring with the same Commerce Department over export controls, not equity. On June 12, the department's Bureau of Industry and Security ordered the company to cut off foreign nationals' access to two of its newest models, Claude Fable 5 and the more restricted Mythos 5, after researchers found a jailbreak that could let users extract cyber-vulnerability information the models were meant to withhold. Anthropic built a new safety filter it says blocks the workaround more than 99% of the time; Washington lifted the restrictions on both models by June 30. So this is a company that has just finished one round of federal scrutiny over its models' safeguards, now fielding a different kind of question about who owns a piece of it.

There is also a louder proposal sitting in the background. Senator Bernie Sanders has introduced a bill that would impose a one-time 50% stock tax on AI companies with over $200 million in annual revenue — Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI among them — funneling the shares into a public sovereign wealth fund he estimates could be worth $7 trillion, paying $1,000 a year to every American. Altman has said he supports some public equity stake in principle but not at 50%. Trump himself said last month he was weighing ways to give the public a stake in leading AI firms, worried that ordinary Americans won't otherwise share in the sector's gains.

Against that noise, Anthropic's denial reads less like news and more like a company drawing a line before it gets pulled into someone else's negotiation. Whether that line holds may depend on what OpenAI actually agrees to — and whether Congress gives Sanders' bill any oxygen when it comes up for consideration later this year.
