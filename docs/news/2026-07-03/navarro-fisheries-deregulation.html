White House eases fishing rules, opens Georges Bank to scallopers

Peter Navarro, White House trade adviser, announced the fisheries deregulation push. — Robert M. Golightly Jr. / The White House (public domain)
In one sentence
The White House reopened 171 square miles of Georges Bank to scallop fishing, but left quotas, timing and supply estimates for the New England council to decide later.

The White House said this week it is loosening federal fishing rules across the Atlantic and Pacific, and the headline move is a narrow one with an outsized symbolic charge: reopening 171 square miles of Georges Bank, off the Massachusetts coast, to scallop dredging for the first time since 1994.

Peter Navarro, the White House trade adviser, framed it as part of a broader deregulatory push. "We're opening the Atlantic and the Pacific, and American consumers are going to benefit from what we're doing now," he said Thursday. A senior administration official was blunter about the goal: "We need to defend our domestic fishing industry, promote productive harvest of our resources and boost the great American fishermen." President Trump marked the occasion by declaring July 2nd "National Scallops Day."

Why the northern edge was closed in the first place

The National Marine Fisheries Service shut the northern edge of Georges Bank in 1994 after finding cod stocks had fallen 40% in four years and that the fishing fleet working the bank was roughly double what the grounds could sustain. The closure survived four administrations of both parties. As recently as 2024, the New England Fishery Management Council — the regional body that actually sets scallop quotas — voted against reopening the area, citing spawning habitat and long-term scallop productivity.

What changed was procedural rather than scientific. The Fisheries Survival Fund, a nonprofit representing Atlantic sea scallop boats, filed a petition in April seeking access to the northern edge under a February executive order directing agencies to identify regulations imposing "undue burdens" on business. The Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy picked the petition as one of 21 priorities out of more than 75,000 submitted nationwide — the only fisheries rule on the list. NOAA and the Commerce Department acted on it Thursday, though officials said implementation will still run through the New England council, the same body that rejected the idea eighteen months ago.

A move without a market yet

That's the gap investors should note: there's a regulatory decision but no operational one. No date has been set for boats to start dredging the northern edge, and NOAA has offered no estimate of how many additional pounds of scallops the area will yield, beyond an administration description of "millions more pounds" a year — a phrase with no backing figure. The U.S. scallop fleet is also concentrated and largely private. New Bedford, Massachusetts, lands more scallops by value than any other American port, but its boats are independently owned, and there is no significant pure-play scallop stock on U.S. exchanges to catch a re-rating. High Liner Foods, the closest public comparable, processes a broad seafood portfolio and would feel any supply shift only at the margins.

The more immediate backdrop is a scallop fishery already under strain — New Bedford's mayor has pointed to falling ex-vessel prices and fewer fishing days this year, pressures a modest supply increase from one new patch of seabed is unlikely to reverse on its own. The New England council is expected to revisit access rules in September, which is the point at which quotas, timing and vessel permits — the details that would actually move a market — get decided.
