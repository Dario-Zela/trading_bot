US refiners are running flat out — and that's why pump prices won't follow crude down

A Houston-area oil refinery — US refiners are running near the top of their operable capacity, leaving little slack to convert cheaper crude into more gasoline. — Antony-22 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Crude has fallen nearly 40% from its wartime peak, but refiners running at 96%+ of capacity are holding gasoline margins wide, keeping pump prices sticky.

Crude oil has given back nearly 40% of its wartime spike since April, yet the average American filling a tank is still paying $3.83 a gallon — down only from $4.29 a month ago, and still well above where prices sat before a shooting war broke out in the Persian Gulf. The arithmetic that used to hold — cheaper crude, cheaper gasoline, roughly in step — has stopped working, and the reason has nothing to do with crude at all.

"U.S. refiners are already running incredibly hard," Helima Croft, head of global commodity strategy at RBC Capital Markets, told CNBC on July 2. Her point was narrow but consequential: refiners are converting crude to gasoline about as fast as the physical plant allows, and that ceiling — not the price of the barrel going in — is what is setting the price of the gallon coming out.

From war premium to working inventory

The backdrop is the 2026 Strait of Hormuz crisis. After the US and Israel struck Iran on February 28, Tehran responded by targeting tankers transiting the strait, and Brent crude went from $72.48 to above $118 a barrel within a month — the International Energy Agency called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the oil market. Refiners with product already in the pipeline, or access to discounted barrels, made extraordinary margins: the 3-2-1 crack spread, the standard measure of profit from turning three barrels of crude into two of gasoline and one of diesel, hit $37.62 a barrel on March 27, the widest in more than two years. Marathon Petroleum's refining margin for the quarter came in at $17.74 a barrel, more than 30% above a year earlier; Valero ran its plants at roughly 95% of capacity and posted record throughput.

A ceasefire and a US-brokered naval stand-down have since let Iran resume exports — more than 40 million barrels since the blockade lifted, according to CNBC reporting, albeit at a 20% premium to pre-war pricing — and Gulf allies have restored flows through Hormuz to more than 10 million barrels a day. Brent has fallen back to around $73. But the crack spread, while off its March peak, has not collapsed with it, because gasoline inventories drawn down during the crisis have not been rebuilt.

The ceiling under the pump

That is where refinery utilization becomes the load-bearing number. Weekly EIA data show US refiners running at 96.6% of operable capacity for the week ending June 26, having sat in a 95%–97% band for the preceding month — near the practical maximum for a system that also needs downtime for maintenance and unplanned outages. At that level, there is very little spare capacity to convert the now-cheaper crude into enough additional gasoline to meaningfully rebuild stocks, let alone do it quickly. Refiners are, in effect, producing exactly as much fuel as they can — which lets them hold the wider margin the war created even as their raw material gets cheaper, because gasoline supply relative to demand hasn't loosened nearly as fast as crude supply has.

Croft's other point was that this isn't purely a domestic capacity story — traffic through Hormuz, while recovering, is still running below the 100-plus vessels a day seen before the crisis, and geopolitical risk premium hasn't fully unwound. Talks in Doha are reportedly progressing, but Croft flagged mid-August, when a nuclear-related negotiating deadline comes due, as the next point at which the risk premium embedded in crude — and by extension the incentive structure facing refiners — could shift again.

What it means for the margin

The practical upshot is that the crude-to-pump pass-through has effectively decoupled for now. Consumers get the benefit of cheaper crude only to the extent refiners choose, or are able, to pass it on — and at 96%-plus utilization, running harder isn't an option, so the incentive is to hold price rather than chase volume. That is a good outcome for refiner equity holders: Valero and Marathon shares are up 128% and 96% respectively over the past year. It is a less good one for drivers who saw crude fall and expected the pump to follow. Whether it does depends less on OPEC+ output decisions than on two narrower things: how fast US gasoline stocks rebuild through the back half of the summer driving season, and whether the mid-August negotiating deadline in the Iran file adds a fresh risk premium back into crude before that rebuild finishes.
