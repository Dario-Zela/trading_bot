Shell's trading chief retires after a Q1 that offset a 10% production hit

Shell Centre, the company's global headquarters in London, where the energy trading business is based. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Shell's trading chief David Wells retires just after his desk's gains offset a war-driven 10% hit to oil and gas output in Q1.

David Wells is stepping down as Shell's head of energy trading, handing over one of the most closely watched desks in the commodities world at the precise moment it proved its worth. Wells, who has run the roughly 800-person trading unit since March 2022, will be succeeded by Bob Kijkuit, currently Shell's energy trading vice president for Europe and Africa, the company confirmed on July 2. Kijkuit's old post goes to Rodrigo Vilanova, who arrives from Galp Energia, where he ran trading for the Portuguese energy group.

A Shell spokesman declined to elaborate on the reasons for the reshuffle beyond calling it a planned succession. But the timing is hard to ignore: Wells leaves having just delivered a quarter that did exactly what a trading desk is supposed to do — cushion the group when the physical business takes a hit.

A quarter that made the case for trading

Shell's chemicals and products segment, where the trading operation sits, swung from a $66 million loss in the fourth quarter of 2025 to a $1.925 billion profit in the first quarter of 2026. Group-wide, adjusted earnings came in at $6.92 billion, ahead of analyst forecasts of $6.1 billion and up 24% from $5.6 billion a year earlier. Shell does not break out trading profit on its own, but investors treat the chemicals and products swing as the closest available proxy — and this one was hard to miss.

CEO Wael Sawan credited "relentless focus on operational performance in a quarter marked by unprecedented disruption in global energy markets." The disruption was real. The war between Iran, Israel and the United States, which escalated in late February, closed off supply routes and hit infrastructure across the Gulf. Shell's own Pearl gas-to-liquids plant in Qatar was struck and is expected to stay offline for roughly a year; Iran also hit Qatar's Ras Laffan LNG complex on March 18, cutting the country's liquefaction capacity by an estimated 17% and prompting QatarEnergy to declare force majeure on some contracts.

Production down, output volatile, profit up

The result was a 10% drop in Shell's oil and gas production, with total output down about 4% once other business lines are included. That is normally the kind of number that drags a quarter down. Instead, the same volatility that disrupted supply pushed prices and margins higher, and Shell's traders — buying and selling not just the company's own barrels and cargoes but third-party volumes across oil, gas, LNG, chemicals and power — captured enough of that swing to more than offset the loss. Sawan has separately warned that the war could leave the global oil market short by nearly a billion barrels this year, a deficit that keeps the volatility, and the trading opportunity, alive.

Kijkuit inherits a desk with a freshly proven case for its own importance, at a moment when Shell's upstream business is under more geopolitical strain than it has faced in years. The next test comes with second-quarter results, expected in early August, when the market will be watching whether the Qatar outage deepens the production drag — and whether trading can cover for it again.
