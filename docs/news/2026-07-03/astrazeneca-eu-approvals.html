AstraZeneca wins tumour-agnostic EU approval for Enhertu, starts new lung trial

AstraZeneca's headquarters and R&D centre on the Cambridge Biomedical Campus, UK. — D Wells / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AstraZeneca's Enhertu won the EU's first tumour-agnostic HER2 approval, while a Datroway breast-cancer nod and a new Abbisko lung trial extend its oncology pipeline.

AstraZeneca has picked up European approval for its cancer drug Enhertu to treat any solid tumour that carries a particular protein marker, rather than one specific type of cancer. It is a small change in wording with a large change in scope — and one that the company followed within days by opening a new lung-cancer trial with a Chinese biotech partner, underlining how much of its growth plan now runs through oncology.

The European Commission approved Enhertu on 29 June as a monotherapy for adults with unresectable or metastatic HER2-positive solid tumours who have already been treated and have no satisfactory options left. Crucially, the label is not restricted by where the tumour started. It is the first tumour-agnostic approval in the EU for a HER2-directed therapy and antibody drug conjugate — the class of medicine, sometimes described as a "biological missile," that pairs a targeting antibody with a chemotherapy payload delivered directly into cancer cells.

What the data actually shows

HER2, a growth-signalling protein, is best known as a breast-cancer marker, but it turns up in excess on tumour cells across a range of other cancers too. The EU approval draws on a subgroup of HER2-positive (IHC 3+) patients from three separate Phase II trials. In DESTINY-PanTumor02, which covered biliary tract, bladder, cervical, endometrial, ovarian and pancreatic cancers, 111 patients had a confirmed objective response rate of 52.3%, with responses lasting a median of 21.1 months. In DESTINY-Lung01, 17 patients with HER2-positive non-small-cell lung cancer had a 52.9% response rate. DESTINY-CRC02, in colorectal cancer, showed a 46.9% response rate across 64 patients. Response rates in the 45–53% range, holding up across six-plus distinct cancer types, is the evidence base regulators leaned on to grant a label that ignores tumour origin altogether.

The drug is not free of downside: grade 3–4 adverse events included neutropenia in 18.5% of patients and anaemia in 9.9%, and interstitial lung disease or pneumonitis — a known risk with this drug class — caused 1.0% of patients to die. Those numbers are why the approval is confined to previously treated patients with no other satisfactory options, not a front-line green light.

Enhertu is co-developed with Japan's Daiichi Sankyo under a 2019 collaboration; AstraZeneca owes its partner a $25 million milestone payment for this indication, with Daiichi Sankyo retaining sales recognition rights across most EU markets. It is Enhertu's sixth EU indication overall. Dr Benedikt Westphalen, an oncologist involved in the trials, put the clinical logic simply: HER2 overexpression "occurs across multiple tumor types," and the approval "opens a new treatment option for patients with HER2 positive cancers regardless of where the tumor originated."

A second nod, and a new trial with Abbisko

The tumour-agnostic approval landed alongside a related but separate move: the EMA's CHMP recommended AstraZeneca and Daiichi Sankyo's other antibody-drug conjugate, Datroway (datopotamab deruxtecan), for first-line use in adults with unresectable or metastatic triple-negative breast cancer who are not candidates for PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy. That recommendation, backed by the Phase III TROPION-Breast02 trial, still needs a formal European Commission decision, but if it clears that step Datroway would become the first TROP2-directed ADC approved in the EU for that patient group — moving the drug earlier into the treatment pathway rather than reserving it for later-line use.

AstraZeneca's pipeline moved again days later. The company struck a strategic collaboration with China's Abbisko Therapeutics to test lumipodlin, an experimental oral PD-L1 inhibitor, in combination with AstraZeneca's lung-cancer drug Tagrisso. The Phase I/II trial targets patients with EGFR-mutated, PD-L1-positive advanced non-small-cell lung cancer, after China's National Medical Products Administration cleared the investigational new drug application on 20 May. Abbisko will lead the study, with the two companies sharing clinical responsibilities — a lower-cost way for AstraZeneca to probe an oral checkpoint inhibitor without building the molecule in-house.

Why the market is watching

None of this happens in isolation from AstraZeneca's stated ambition to reach $80 billion in total revenue by 2030. Enhertu alone generated $3.575 billion in combined sales over the first nine months of 2025, growing 34% at constant exchange rates, and the group's oncology unit overall grew 16%. A tumour-agnostic label widens the pool of patients who can be prescribed the drug without AstraZeneca running a fresh trial for every cancer type — a more capital-efficient way to grow a blockbuster than launching new molecules from scratch.

Investors have registered the news calmly rather than dramatically. JP Morgan's Richard Vosser maintained a buy rating and left his price target unchanged at 16,000 pence following the Enhertu decision; AstraZeneca shares closed at 14,298 pence on 30 June, still below the Street's average 12-month target of roughly 18,452 pence. The next checkpoints are the European Commission's formal decision on Datroway's first-line label and early readouts from the Abbisko combination trial, which will show whether AstraZeneca's oral PD-L1 bet has legs alongside Tagrisso, its best-selling lung-cancer drug.
