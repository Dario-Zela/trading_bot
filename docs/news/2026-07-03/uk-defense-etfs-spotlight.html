UK's $20B military overhaul puts defense ETFs back in focus

HMS Vanguard returns to HM Naval Base Clyde — the submarine fleet at the center of the UK's £63bn nuclear deterrent commitment — CPOA(Phot) Tam McDonald / UK Ministry of Defence, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The UK's $20B Defence Investment Plan lifted BAE Systems, Rolls-Royce and defense ETF holdings, but rising gilt yields and a leadership transition mean markets are pricing execution risk as much as the headline number.

The number in the headline is £15 billion — call it $20 billion — spread across four years. That is the size of the increase Keir Starmer's government confirmed on June 30th in its long-delayed Defence Investment Plan, lifting annual UK defense spending from roughly £54 billion today to £79.1 billion by 2029, or 2.7% of GDP. For an industry that spent the past decade absorbing budget cuts, it is real money. The question investors are actually pricing is whether it survives contact with the UK's fiscal position and, now, a change of prime minister.

What's actually being bought

The plan's biggest single line is nuclear: more than £63 billion over four years for the Defence Nuclear Enterprise, over £20 billion of that an increase versus the prior four-year period. It funds the Dreadnought-class ballistic missile submarines and SSN-AUKUS attack boats under construction at Barrow-in-Furness, a new sovereign warhead codenamed Astraea, and 12 Lockheed Martin F-35A jets that return the RAF to NATO's nuclear-sharing mission for the first time since 1998 — meaning US B61-12 bombs based on British soil, deliverable by British aircraft. The Tempest sixth-generation fighter programme, run jointly with Italy and Japan as GCAP, gets £8.6 billion. A further £5 billion goes to drones and autonomous systems.

The market reaction was immediate and legible: the FTSE 350 Aerospace & Defence index rose almost 5% in the days after the announcement. BAE Systems, already sitting on a record £83.6 billion order backlog after adding £36.8 billion in new orders last year, was the clearest beneficiary given its work across submarines, Typhoon, Tempest and armored vehicles. Rolls-Royce, up more than 20% year-to-date on the strength of its defense arm's £17.4 billion backlog, and Babcock, which maintains the submarine fleet, also moved higher. Funds with concentrated exposure to those names — the iShares US Aerospace & Defense ETF (ITA), BlackRock's newer iShares Defense Industrials Active ETF (IDEF), and thematic vehicles like Global X's Defense Tech ETF (SHLD) — carry all three as top holdings, which is the mechanical reason a UK budget line moves a fund priced in dollars.

The financing problem

Here is where the story gets less clean. Of the £15 billion, roughly £4 billion comes from cutting public investment elsewhere and £2.8 billion from trimming transport and energy projects; a further £4.7 billion is simply marked "to be funded at Budget 2026" — not yet identified. Defence chiefs reportedly asked for £28 billion and got just over half. Gilt yields moved higher across maturities in the days following the announcement even as Starmer insisted the increase would be funded through departmental cuts rather than new borrowing. The UK already pays a higher borrowing premium than its G7 peers, and S&P Global has flagged that further defense spending could pressure the sovereign rating if it is not credibly offset. The Institute for Fiscal Studies, in its initial response, noted the plan adds £3.8 billion a year in practice and left the harder question — how Britain reaches the government's own 3.5%-of-GDP target by 2035 — unanswered.

Retired Royal Navy commander Tom Sharpe was blunter, describing parts of the plan as "cost-cutting by another name" and arguing the surface fleet remains "chronically underfunded" even after the announcement.

A plan without the prime minister who wrote it

The timing complicates the signal further. Starmer resigned as Labour leader on June 22nd, remaining in Downing Street only as caretaker while the party selects a successor — widely expected to be Andy Burnham — before Parliament returns in September. Starmer himself called the Defence Investment Plan "most likely" his last major policy announcement and said he intends to present it at the NATO summit on July 7th–8th, days after nominations open in the leadership contest that will replace him. A four-year spending plan announced by an outgoing prime minister, funded in part by budget lines still to be specified at a Budget that hasn't happened yet, is a different proposition for contractors and index funds than one backed by a government with a full term ahead of it.

That is the real test for defense-ETF holders here: not whether the headline number is large enough to matter, but whether it survives a new Labour leader, an autumn Budget, and a fiscal position that was already stretched before the announcement. BAE's and Rolls-Royce's backlogs are contracted and multi-year regardless of who sits in Number 10, which is why both stocks held their gains even as gilts sold off — existing orders don't get cancelled by a leadership change. The £4.7 billion still unfunded is the piece to watch.

What's next

The next real test lands at the UK's Budget 2026, where the government has to show where the unfunded £4.7 billion actually comes from. Before that, Starmer presents the plan at the NATO summit on July 7th–8th, and Labour's leadership contest — nominations open July 9th — will determine who owns this budget by autumn. Contractors report next: BAE Systems and Rolls-Royce half-year results in late July will show whether order intake is already reflecting the new plan or still running on prior commitments.
