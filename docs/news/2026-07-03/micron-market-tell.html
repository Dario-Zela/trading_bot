Micron is the new market 'tell,' says Benzinga's Jay Woods

A Micron Technology fabrication facility in Taichung, Taiwan. — Thingreenline4546 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Benzinga's Jay Woods says Micron is the market's new bellwether stock, succeeding Nvidia, Apple and Microsoft — but offers no data to back the pattern.

Jay Woods has a habit of picking a stock and telling clients: watch this one, it will tell you where the market is headed. For years that stock was Nvidia. Before that, Apple. Before that, Microsoft. Now, says the chief market strategist at Freedom Capital Markets, the baton has passed to Micron.

"It's been Nvidia forever. It was Apple for years. There was Microsoft for years," Woods told Benzinga in an interview published July 2. "Micron is now the new tell."

The case starts with the numbers. Micron's fiscal third-quarter results, reported June 24, showed revenue up 346% year over year to $41.46 billion, comfortably ahead of consensus, with non-GAAP earnings of $25.11 a share against estimates of $20.28. Management guided to $50 billion in revenue and roughly $31 a share for the current quarter. Shares hit an all-time high on the back of it, closing above $1,050 — before sinking more than 6% days later on fears of a memory supply glut, then giving back another 4% as the broader chip sector sold off.

That whipsaw is Woods' actual evidence. He points to a bearish divergence between Micron's price and its momentum indicators — earnings "growing hand over fist," he says, while "price action's starting to slow." He calls it the same setup he saw in Nvidia in the third quarter of 2023, when a record quarter failed to move the stock and its run lost steam soon after.

The gap in the argument

What Woods does not offer is any accounting of how well the Nvidia-Apple-Microsoft "tell" actually forecast the S&P 500 in real time, as opposed to in hindsight. The claim rests on a pattern-match — three mega-cap tech names took turns leading the market's mood, therefore a fourth must be doing the same now — without data showing the S&P moved in step with those stocks' momentum shifts, or that Micron's $200 billion market cap carries comparable index weight to Nvidia's multi-trillion-dollar heft. Micron is roughly a fortieth the size of Nvidia. Whether a stock that small can still function as a market-wide barometer is an assumption, not a finding.

Woods pairs the Micron call with a broader S&P 500 forecast: a possible 10% drawdown to below 7,000 within three to four months, followed by a rally once November's midterm elections are behind the market, landing the index around 7,650 to 7,700 by year-end. He calls a finish in the 7,200-7,300 range "a successful year" regardless. Longer term, he thinks Micron itself could reach a $2.5 trillion valuation, but expects consolidation first.

The next test of the theory is close at hand: Micron's fiscal fourth-quarter results, due in late September, will show whether the stock's momentum divergence resolves upward, downward, or not at all — and whether the S&P 500 moves with it, or simply moves.
