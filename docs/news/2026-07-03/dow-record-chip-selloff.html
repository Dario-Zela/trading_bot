Dow hits a record as weak jobs data eases Fed fears; a chip-stock rout sinks the Nasdaq 100

The Dow closed at a record Thursday even as chip stocks led the Nasdaq 100 sharply lower. — Scott Beale / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
A weak June jobs report pushed the Dow to a record by easing Fed rate-hike bets, but a semiconductor rout dragged the Nasdaq 100 down and spread overnight to Asian chipmakers.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average closed at a record on Thursday, its 30 stocks climbing in near-lockstep as traders concluded that a weak June jobs report would keep the Federal Reserve from raising rates anytime soon. One floor down, in the Nasdaq 100, the mood was closer to panic: a semiconductor selloff wiped out nearly 2% of the index's value in a single session, and the damage in individual chip names ran into double digits.

Both things happened because of the same data point. That is the story worth sitting with — not which index won the day, but why a single jobs report could be read simultaneously as gasoline for blue chips and as confirmation of the market's worst fears about tech.

A soft print, a hard rally

The Labor Department reported that US employers added just 57,000 jobs in June, well short of the 115,000 economists polled by Dow Jones had expected, and the weakest pace of hiring since February. May's already-soft number was revised down again, to 129,000. The unemployment rate ticked down to 4.2% from 4.3%, but for a discouraging reason: labor force participation fell three-tenths of a point to 61.5%, the lowest reading since March 2021. Fewer people working, and fewer people even looking.

For equities, weak used to mean weak. On Thursday it meant something else: less ammunition for a Federal Reserve that has spent the year fighting inflation with higher rates. The CME FedWatch Tool showed the odds of a hike at the Fed's July meeting collapsing to roughly 20%, with traders now pricing an October move as the more likely next step, at a 45.9% probability. "As we are learning how the Fed reaction function will form under [Chairman Kevin] Warsh, this print takes some of the pressure off of the inflation-fighting institution to hike near term," said Bradford Smith, a portfolio manager at Janus Henderson Investors. "With oil price inflation moderating, some softness on the jobs front likely keeps the Fed on hold at least for the next meeting."

The Dow responded by adding 594.83 points, or 1.14%, to close at 52,900.07, a fresh all-time high after touching an intraday record of 52,903.85.

Where the money actually went

The rally wasn't a rising tide so much as a redirection. Strategists have taken to calling it a rotation out of this year's AI-and-chip momentum trade and into the kind of stock a rate-sensitive, slower-growth economy is supposed to reward: industrials, financials, healthcare, materials — the traditional Dow crowd. The Dow's tracking ETF is up 8.77% year to date, a hair ahead of the S&P 500's own ETF.

That doesn't mean mega-cap tech had a bad week, in fairness. After June's rout — the worst month for Microsoft since 2000 — the so-called Magnificent Seven actually bounced: Microsoft gained 6.7% over the week, Meta Platforms 5.8%, Apple 4.7%. The distinction is that those gains came after a hole had already been dug, while the Dow's industrials and consumer names were setting records outright.

Nike offered the week's cleanest read on where consumer demand and the broader economy actually stand. The company reported a headline profit of $0.72 a share against a consensus of $0.13, but that number was inflated by a one-time $986 million tariff refund worth $0.52 a share; strip that out and adjusted earnings were $0.20. Revenue of $10.97 billion beat estimates regardless, and the stock rose 7% on the week even as sales in China kept softening — evidence, for those looking for it, that American consumers are still spending even as the global picture wobbles.

The chip reckoning

What the rotation story leaves out is how violent the exit from semiconductors became. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH) was down 4.4% by midday Thursday; the sector's other major benchmark, the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX), finished the week off more than 5%. The Nasdaq 100 — stuffed with chip names — fell 1.9% to 29,287.

Some of the individual moves were brutal. Micron Technology, this year's best-performing large chipmaker, was down nearly 6% at midday and finished the session off more than 10%. Further down the semiconductor supply chain, the damage was worse still: Applied Optoelectronics tumbled 15.8%, Onto Innovation fell 14.1%, FormFactor lost 13.4%, and both Vicor and SiTime dropped 12.6%. The selling didn't stay contained to Wall Street — by Thursday evening, Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix were both down more than 9% in Seoul as the rout spread through the global chip supply chain overnight.

There is no jobs-report logic that explains a semiconductor stock falling 15% in a day. What connects it to the broader story is timing and psychology: after eighteen months of AI capital-expenditure enthusiasm carrying chip valuations far above the rest of the market, a soft economic print was enough of an excuse for investors already nervous about stretched multiples to take profits, and to take them fast.

The two stories don't cancel out

The honest read on Thursday is not that Wall Street decided the economy is fine, nor that it decided the economy is in trouble — it did both, in different corners of the market, at the same time. The jobs number bought the Fed room to hold rates steady, and rate-sensitive, economically-broad names took the win. But that same weakness in hiring is also a data point about demand, and investors holding the most richly-valued growth story of the cycle — AI-linked semiconductors — used it as the trigger to lock in gains rather than wait to find out whether the softening labor market is a blip or the start of something slower.

The next test comes quickly: Micron reports fiscal earnings in late September, but before that, the July jobs report — due the first Friday of August — will show whether June's weak print was a one-off or the start of a trend. If payrolls stay soft, the Fed's October decision becomes the next flashpoint for both halves of this market at once.
