HSBC says the market's AI discount on Adobe is a mistake

Adobe's headquarters complex in downtown San Jose, California. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC upgraded Adobe to Buy, arguing its AI-fear discount ignores 13% revenue growth and a valuation gap versus software peers.

Adobe has spent 2026 as one of software's more conspicuous casualties. Shares are down roughly 23% this year even as the S&P 500 has climbed about 12%, a gap that has made the Creative Cloud maker a stand-in for a broader market anxiety: that generative AI is coming for the companies that used to sell tools to creative professionals, not just the professionals themselves. HSBC thinks the market has gotten carried away.

The bank's analyst Renato Neves upgraded Adobe to Buy from Hold on Thursday, raising his price target to $308 from $282 — roughly 46% above where the stock trades now. His argument is not that AI poses no threat to Adobe. It's that investors have priced in a version of that threat which the company's own numbers don't support.

"Investor concerns about generative AI disrupting Adobe's core business have become excessive," HSBC wrote.

In one sentence: HSBC's upgrade argues Wall Street has confused a leadership vacuum and AI anxiety for a broken business, leaving Adobe undervalued against software peers with similar growth.

The discount, in numbers

HSBC's case rests on a valuation gap that it says doesn't square with the fundamentals. Adobe trades at about 8.5 times its projected 2026 non-GAAP earnings — a discount to the broader software sector despite delivering earnings growth that is broadly comparable to peers trading at richer multiples. In the bank's telling, that gap is the market's AI tax, applied to a company that hasn't actually shown the revenue erosion the tax implies.

The most recent quarter is the evidence HSBC leans on. Adobe posted record revenue of about $6.62 billion, up 13% year-on-year, and beat consensus. Remaining performance obligations — the backlog of contracted revenue not yet recognized — rose 13.1%, a proxy for demand that hasn't yet shown up in the income statement. The company also bought back $2.2 billion of its own stock in the quarter, and HSBC nudged its earnings-per-share estimates for 2026 through 2028 up by 3% to 8%, even while trimming its non-GAAP operating margin forecast slightly, to 45% from 46.2%. Management, for its part, raised full-year revenue guidance to $26.5 billion–$26.6 billion.

The read HSBC draws from this is narrow but specific: customers are adopting Adobe's own AI-enabled tools inside Photoshop, Firefly and its Creative Cloud suite rather than defecting wholesale to rivals like Canva or AI-native upstarts built around OpenAI's models. There is, the bank says, little evidence yet of AI-native competitors taking meaningful share.

The part the spreadsheet doesn't capture

What HSBC's model can't easily price in is Adobe's leadership vacuum. CEO Shantanu Narayen announced in March that he would step down after 18 years once a successor is named — a decision that followed a lackluster sales forecast and came amid investor impatience with the pace of Adobe's AI pivot. He remains in place, and will move to board chairman once a replacement is found, but the search is ongoing. Then, on June 15th, chief financial officer Dan Durn left to become CFO at Marvell Technology, with 20-year Adobe finance veteran Steve Day stepping in as interim CFO. Adobe shares fell by the most in 15 months on the CFO news, even though it landed alongside the strong quarterly results.

That double vacancy — a company hunting for both a CEO and, until Durn's replacement is permanent, effectively a CFO — is arguably the more concrete risk in this story, separate from the abstract question of whether AI erodes Adobe's moat. HSBC's upgrade is a bet that the fundamentals outrun the leadership uncertainty; it is not a claim that the uncertainty doesn't exist.

What it tests

Adobe has become something of a proxy question for the software sector generally: is the AI-driven derating of names like Adobe rational repricing of a genuine competitive threat, or a panic discount applied indiscriminately to anything that touches creative or knowledge work? HSBC's answer, for now, is panic — at least for Adobe specifically. If the thesis holds and the stock re-rates toward peer multiples, it will be read as evidence that other software names carrying similar AI-fear discounts are also mispriced. If Adobe keeps sliding regardless of the earnings beat, that says the market is pricing something other than quarterly numbers — durability of the moat, or simply confidence in who is going to run the company. The next test comes whenever Adobe names its new chief executive; until then, the AI-discount debate is likely to keep trading on sentiment as much as spreadsheets.
