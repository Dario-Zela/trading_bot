National Grid pours $1.75B into the US power buildout

High-voltage transmission towers — the kind of large-scale grid infrastructure at the center of National Grid's US buildout. — Stefan Andrej Shambora (St_A_Sh) / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0
In one sentence
National Grid is putting $1.75B into a 35% stake in Joulent to build a Chevron-backed, Microsoft-contracted Texas power project for AI data centers.

National Grid Ventures, the commercial investment arm of the UK-listed utility, is putting $1.75 billion into Joulent LLC, a Texas-based energy developer, in exchange for a 35% stake. The deal is the clearest signal yet that a major transmission and distribution utility is willing to underwrite the power infrastructure that data centers and artificial intelligence workloads now require — not just wait for regulators to approve rate cases to pay for it.

The money funds Joulent's first project, called Kilby: a 2.67-gigawatt co-located power facility in West Texas, built in a 50/50 arrangement with Chevron and anchored by a 20-year power purchase agreement with Microsoft, which will draw on the output for a data center campus. GE Vernova has already been lined up to supply turbines, and the partners are targeting first power in 2028. National Grid says it expects to connect more than 10 gigawatts of data-center demand across the UK and US combined within five years — Kilby alone accounts for roughly a quarter of that.

"Our investment in Joulent is a disciplined, partner-led investment in contracted critical infrastructure for the AI-driven large load economy," said Zoë Yujnovich, National Grid's chief executive. The framing matters: this is not National Grid building merchant generation and hoping for demand to show up. The Microsoft contract and Chevron's gas supply are locked in before a shovel goes into the West Texas ground.

A cheap stock, or just a fairly priced one?

Coverage of the deal has leaned on the idea that National Grid shares look inexpensive relative to the growth on offer. The numbers are more mixed than that framing suggests. On one reading, the stock trades near 14 times forward earnings with a PEG ratio of 1.15 — a level that would flatter a growth-adjacent utility. On another, its trailing price-to-earnings sits closer to 22 times, roughly in line with peers and below the 29 times analysts calculate as fair value given National Grid's growth trajectory. Wall Street's consensus view is closer to "fairly valued" than "cheap": ratings run to a mix of buy and hold, with price targets clustered between $85 and $91, implying upside in the high single digits to low double digits rather than a mispriced bargain. Shares rose roughly 3% on the announcement to around $83.

The $1.75 billion is incremental to National Grid's existing five-year capital program of at least £70 billion (about $90 billion) through 2031, funded off the group's own balance sheet headroom rather than new equity or debt raises disclosed alongside the deal. A final investment decision on Kilby itself is still pending, expected later in 2026, which is the next checkpoint for whether the Chevron and Microsoft commitments convert into steel in the ground.
