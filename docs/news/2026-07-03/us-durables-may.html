US durable goods orders ex-defense stay flat at -4.6% in May

Aircraft fuselages at Boeing's Everett factory — swings in civilian aircraft bookings drove nearly all of May's durable goods decline. — SounderBruce / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Durable goods orders fell 4.6% ex-defense in May, but the drop was mostly aircraft-order volatility — core capital-goods orders rose for a third straight month.

Durable goods orders fell 4.6 percent in May, excluding defense — a headline number that, taken on its own, reads like a fresh sign of manufacturing trouble. It isn't. The decline is almost entirely a giveback of April's aircraft-driven spike, and the parts of the report that actually track business investment moved the other way.

The Census Bureau's advance report, released June 25, put total new orders for durable goods at $332.1 billion in May, down 4.5 percent overall and 4.6 percent excluding defense. That followed an April reading that was revised up to an 8.5 percent surge — the sharpest monthly gain in a year — so May's drop largely unwinds a month that was itself an outlier. Economists had penciled in a decline near 5 percent; the actual figure came in a touch better than that.

The plane problem

The swing traces almost entirely to one volatile category: civilian aircraft. Orders for nondefense aircraft and parts collapsed 51.8 percent in May, after jumping 167 percent in April. Boeing bookings alone fell from 136 planes to 27 — the kind of lumpy, order-by-order volatility that has always made the transportation component a poor guide to underlying industrial demand. Strip transportation out entirely, and orders rose 1.3 percent in May, more than double the 0.6 percent economists had expected.

What was actually growing

The cleanest read on business investment sits in nondefense capital goods orders excluding aircraft — the Federal Reserve's preferred proxy for capex intentions. That measure rose 1.6 percent in May, its third gain in four months, and is now running more than 10 percent above year-ago levels for a third consecutive month. Motor vehicles and parts added 1.1 percent, a sixth straight monthly increase despite tariff-related cost pressure on automakers. Computers and electronic products rose 0.3 percent, extending gains in eight of the past nine months. Unfilled orders — the backlog manufacturers are still working through — rose 0.6 percent to $1.58 trillion, and inventories ticked up 0.2 percent to $600 billion, neither suggesting demand is drying up.

None of this erases the cost pressures manufacturers are navigating. Benjamin Shoesmith, an economist at KPMG, noted that shipping disruption tied to the Strait of Hormuz "will take a few quarters to return to normal" even with the recent de-escalation, a reminder that input costs and logistics remain a live risk to the sector even as order books hold up.

The headline ex-defense figure will keep making economic calendars look grim as long as aircraft orders swing wildly from month to month — Boeing's bookings are famously lumpy, and a slow month can subtract several points from the topline print almost by itself. The more useful number is the capital-goods series, which has now posted double-digit annual growth for three straight months. The next test comes with June's durable goods report, due in late July, when a repeat aircraft swing either confirms this month's move as noise or starts to look like a pattern.
