Intel's foundry turnaround becomes 'too good to ignore,' says HSBC

Intel CEO Lip-Bu Tan, whose turnaround push has centered on reviving the company's foundry business. — U.S. Department of Commerce / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
HSBC doubled its Intel price target to $200 on foundry optimism, even as the stock — up 233% this year — sold off 6% the same week.

Frank Lee's team at HSBC did something Wall Street doesn't do lightly this week: they doubled a price target. Intel's, to be exact, from $100 to $200, while keeping the stock rated Buy. The bank now reckons there's another 60% of upside left in a stock that has already surged 233% since January — and the reason, for the first time, isn't really about chips going into laptops or servers. It's about the factory business Intel almost gave up on.

HSBC's note credits two things: Intel's server CPU shipments, which the bank now sees growing 25% year-over-year in 2026 (up from a 20% estimate), and — the newer piece — Intel Foundry, the contract manufacturing arm CEO Lip-Bu Tan has been trying to rebuild around the 18A process node. HSBC has started folding foundry into its sum-of-the-parts valuation for the first time, calling the business "too good to ignore now" as capacity bottlenecks ease and outside customers start kicking the tires.

The turnaround story, so far

Tan took over in March 2025 promising discipline after Pat Gelsinger's ouster — cutting 15,000 jobs, killing side projects, chasing roughly $10 billion in annual savings. The bet paid off on the stock chart: Intel shares have run from around $22 to well over $100 in the past year, a 462% gain that has made the company one of the most talked-about turnaround trades in the market. Panther Lake, the first laptop chip built on the 18A node, is now shipping, and Intel says yields have been climbing 7-8% a month. Nvidia and SoftBank have both taken stakes. Reports have floated engagements with Tesla, Apple, SpaceX and Google.

What hasn't happened yet is the one thing that would settle the argument: a committed anchor customer for Intel's next node, 14A. Management has said publicly it won't build 14A capacity without firm external orders in hand — a scar from years of overbuilding capacity nobody used — and as of Intel's most recent earnings call, it had none. The company is pointing to the second half of 2026 as the window when test-chip customers are expected to decide. HSBC's upgrade is, in effect, a bet that they will.

A market already ahead of the story

The timing is the awkward part. HSBC published its note on a day Intel shares fell about 6% to $119.83, part of a broader semiconductor pullback — AMD dropped 5%, the SOXX chip ETF fell 6% — that traders characterized as profit-taking rather than anything company-specific. Cantor Fitzgerald also raised its Intel target that week, to $150 from $90, so HSBC wasn't alone in reaching for a bigger number even as the stock gave some back.

That's the tension in the HSBC call: it isn't wrong that foundry has stopped being a pure liability, but a 233% year-to-date rally means most of that improvement is arguably already in the price. Intel Foundry still posted roughly $2.4 billion in operating losses in the first quarter. The bull case rests on customer commitments that, by Intel's own account, don't exist yet. HSBC is pricing in the second half of 2026 playing out as hoped; nothing in the note describes what happens to the stock if it doesn't.

What comes next

The test is concrete and dated: Intel has pointed to the back half of 2026 for the first firm 14A customer decisions, and that announcement — or its absence — will matter more to the foundry narrative than any single analyst note. Until then, HSBC's $200 target reads less like new information about Intel's factories than a reminder of how far sentiment has already run ahead of them.
