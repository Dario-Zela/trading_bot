German dronemaker Quantum Systems raises $1.2B at an $8B valuation

Quantum Systems' Vector and Scorpion unmanned aircraft share a common airframe and can be reconfigured between fixed-wing reconnaissance and multicopter roles. — Quantum Systems / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Quantum Systems raised $1.2 billion at an $8 billion valuation, more than doubling its price tag in eight months as Blackstone and Airbus bet on European drone autonomy.

Quantum Systems, a Bavarian maker of surveillance and reconnaissance drones, said it has raised $1.2 billion in a Series D round that values the company at roughly $8 billion — more than double the €3 billion-plus valuation it commanded just eight months ago. The round, co-led by Blackstone, Airbus, the European growth investor Noteus and private equity firm Advent, ranks among the largest single financings in European defense technology and adds to a run of megadeals that has made autonomous systems one of the more crowded corners of venture capital this year.

Existing backers Bond, Fidelity Management and Research, Balderton Capital, HV Capital and Elephant Lake Ventures also participated. The company, founded in 2015 by former Bundeswehr helicopter pilot Florian Seibel, said the capital will fund expanded production capacity, deeper supply-chain resilience, faster delivery across allied markets and continued investment in the AI software that increasingly differentiates one drone maker from another.

From battlefield sensor to software platform

Quantum Systems built its name on the Vector, a portable, vertical-takeoff reconnaissance drone that converts into a multicopter called the Scorpion without swapping hardware. Ukraine has been the proving ground: the company opened a production facility there in April 2024, and a network of Ukrainian sites now turns out 40 to 80 Vectors a month, feeding a fleet that racked up more than 19,000 missions in the country in 2025 alone. NATO militaries in Europe, along with forces in the US, Australia and New Zealand, have also bought in.

The newer Vector AI variant carries dual AI chips for target identification and swarm coordination, and can operate without GPS — a necessity on a front line where jamming is routine. That shift, from a sensor platform toward an autonomy and software company, is the story Quantum Systems is now selling to investors, and it is a large part of why the valuation has moved as fast as it has. Seibel has said the company is on track to roughly double 2025 revenue, to around €300 million, while running profitably with double-digit EBITDA margins — an unusual claim in a venture-funded sector still dominated by cash-burning growth stories.

Money chasing European autonomy

The round's structure says as much as its size. Airbus, already a partner with Quantum Systems on a "networked, autonomous and sovereign ecosystem" for unmanned aircraft, is putting capital behind a company whose products it will also help field. Mike Schoellhorn, chief executive of Airbus Defence and Space, framed the tie-up as being "about ensuring Europe's sovereignty in AI-supported mission systems and crewed-uncrewed teaming." Blackstone's David Kaden put it more bluntly for the investor side: "A structural shift in the European defence market has created significant demand for capital to support the sector's development."

That demand has been building for a year. Quantum Systems now sits in a crowded second tier of European defense-tech unicorns that includes Stark, ICEYE and Portugal's Tekever — all racing to convert battlefield data from Ukraine into recurring software revenue. Above them sits Helsing, the Munich-based defense AI firm backed by Spotify founder Daniel Ek, which is close to closing its own $1.2 billion round at an $18 billion valuation, roughly 30% above where it stood less than a year ago. Both rounds trail the scale of Anduril Industries in the US, which raised at a $61 billion valuation in May. The gap illustrates where European capital still lands relative to American defense tech, even as it grows faster in percentage terms.

What the money buys, and what it signals

Seibel has floated ambitions beyond drones — mentioning humanoid robotics as an area "we are probably going to look into" — and has left open the possibility of eventually combining Quantum Systems with Stark, the strike-drone maker he also co-founded, though both companies say no merger is currently planned. An IPO has been publicly ruled out as premature, with the company instead pointing to a formal readiness review.

For markets, the more durable signal is what investors are willing to pay for now: hardware makers that have proven they can ship at volume into an active conflict, paired with the software layer that turns individual drones into a coordinated, AI-directed system. Blackstone and Advent are traditional buyout shops, not early-stage venture funds — their presence in a Series D round suggests a bet that European defense manufacturing capacity, long treated as a policy problem, has become a financeable one. Airbus's participation adds a strategic-buyer dimension that could eventually shape consolidation across the sector, much as UK defense primes have been doing domestically.

The next marker to watch is Helsing's round closing at its reported $18 billion figure, which would cement a two-tier structure in European defense tech — one dominant AI-and-hardware platform, and a cluster of well-capitalized specialists like Quantum Systems below it. Whether that capital converts into NATO and EU procurement contracts at a pace that justifies these valuations remains the open question for the sector through the rest of 2026.
