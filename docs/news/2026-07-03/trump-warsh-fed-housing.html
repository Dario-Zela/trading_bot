Trump says Fed's Warsh faces a 'little bit hostile' board

Kevin Warsh, sworn in as Federal Reserve chair in May 2026, pictured in an earlier official Fed portrait. — Federal Reserve Board of Governors / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
Trump called Fed chair Warsh's board 'a little bit hostile' but offered no policy specifics, and separately shrugged off a housing bill he once refused to sign.

President Trump has spent the better part of a year needling the Federal Reserve to cut interest rates faster. On Thursday, in a CNBC interview with Joe Kernen, he found a new target: not the man he installed as Fed chair in May, but the committee surrounding him.

"He's got a board that maybe is a little bit hostile, and you know, unfortunately, and maybe a board that wants to do the wrong thing," Trump said of Kevin Warsh, the Fed chair he nominated and who was confirmed by the Senate in a bitterly divided 54-45 vote on May 13 before being sworn in at the White House ten days later. It was, notably, the first time in decades a Fed chair had taken the oath there rather than at the central bank's own building — Alan Greenspan was the last, in 1987.

A softer line than the one he took with Powell

What's striking is the tone. Trump paired the "hostile" remark with something close to sympathy: Warsh, he said, "has to do what he has to do," and is "a great guy and a great pro." That's a markedly gentler register than the one Trump used for months against Jerome Powell, whom he needled relentlessly and at one point openly discussed firing before Powell's term expired. Here, the frustration is aimed sideways — at the rest of the Federal Open Market Committee, whose composition Trump doesn't fully control, rather than at the chair himself.

It's worth noting the remark came with no specifics attached. No named governor, no vote count, no rate decision under dispute. Warsh's Fed has not yet delivered a policy shift that would explain the complaint — the swipe reads more as scene-setting for future friction than a reaction to anything that happened this week.

The housing bill, and the fight he actually cares about

On housing, Trump was almost breezy. Asked whether he'd sign the bipartisan housing bill that passed the House on June 23 — the most substantial federal housing policy overhaul pushed through Congress in years, aimed at boosting supply by making it cheaper and easier to build — he said: "There's a lot of Democrat points in there that I don't even think are good, but it's fine."

That's a reversal of sorts. A week earlier, Trump had abruptly canceled a planned signing ceremony on Capitol Hill, hours before it was due to start, in a dispute with Senate Republicans. His actual condition for putting pen to paper hasn't changed: "I've made the case I'd rather not sign anything until we sign the SAVE America Act," the voter-ID bill he has tied to his broader legislative wish list. The housing bill, in other words, is fine — it's just not the priority.

What to watch

None of this moved markets, and there's no indication it was meant to. Trump offered no rate view, no timeline, no target for his irritation. The next real test comes at the Fed's next FOMC meeting, when investors will find out whether Warsh's board is actually as resistant to the White House's preferred path as Trump suggests — or whether Thursday's comment was simply Trump thinking out loud.
