EU and UK tighten AI oversight as Brussels launches inspections and ASA bans ads

The Berlaymont building in Brussels, headquarters of the European Commission, whose AI Office is coordinating the first wave of AI Act compliance inspections. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Brussels has begun inspecting hiring, credit and healthcare AI under the EU AI Act's new penalties, while UK regulators keep banning AI ads over the sexualisation of women.

For three years, Europe's Artificial Intelligence Act has existed mostly as a compliance exercise: a stack of obligations for companies to document, a timetable for lawyers to track, a set of risk tiers to classify systems against. That changed this month. The European AI Office in Brussels, working with national regulators including France's CNIL and Germany's BfDI, has begun the first scheduled wave of on-the-ground compliance inspections — the moment the AI Act stops being a rulebook and starts being enforced.

The inspections, which began in September and are coordinated across 24 national market surveillance authorities, are targeting three sectors specifically: automated CV-screening tools used in hiring, algorithmic credit-assessment systems at retail banks, and AI triage tools in private healthcare clinics. All three fall under Annex III of the AI Act, the list of "high-risk" applications that carry the heaviest compliance burden — and, now, the heaviest scrutiny.

What the inspectors are actually checking

The AI Act's high-risk provisions became fully applicable on 2 August 2026, and regulators wasted little time turning theory into practice. Under Article 11 and Annex IV of the Act, providers of high-risk systems must keep a live technical documentation file — architecture diagrams, training-data provenance, human-oversight mechanisms, cybersecurity testing — ready for inspection before a system ever reaches the market. CNIL, BfDI and Spain's AESIA are now requesting exactly that: version-controlled model weights, pre-processing scripts, and evidence that a human can actually override the machine when it matters.

For hiring platforms, the requests centre on bias testing across protected groups and whether rejected candidates were properly notified that an algorithm was involved in the decision. For lenders, regulators want to see how credit-denial decisions can be explained and where the training data came from. For healthcare triage tools, the question is whether clinical staff retain meaningful control over which patients get seen first, rather than deferring to a scoring system.

The penalties attached are tiered by severity. The headline figure — up to €35 million or 7% of global turnover, whichever is greater — applies to the most serious violations, such as deploying an outright prohibited AI practice. Failures to maintain proper technical documentation under Article 11 sit a rung down, at up to €15 million or 3% of turnover. Neither number is trivial for a mid-sized HR-tech or fintech vendor, and unlike the fines that GDPR made familiar, the AI Act's ceiling is designed to exceed them: 7% comfortably tops the 4% cap that has anchored European privacy enforcement for eight years.

Finland has moved fastest on the domestic side, activating its national AI Act supervision law in January and becoming the first member state with fully operational enforcement powers. The pattern regulators describe — employment, credit and biometric systems drawing the earliest attention — tracks closely with where the Act's drafters judged the risk of discriminatory or opaque decision-making to be highest.

The advertising watchdog is moving too

Across the Channel, a parallel but distinct crackdown has been building at the UK's Advertising Standards Authority, and it points to the same underlying anxiety about AI systems operating with too little friction between capability and consequence.

In March, the ASA banned a YouTube advert for Saeta Tech's PixVideo — AI Video Maker after eight complaints. The ad showed a before-and-after image of a young woman: a red scribble over her midriff in the "before" frame, bare skin revealed in the "after" one, captioned "Erase anything" with a heart-eyes emoji. The ASA ruled that the ad reduced the woman to a sexual object and implied the app could be used to remove clothing without consent — regardless of whether the product's actual safeguards would have allowed that. Saeta Tech pulled the campaign, suspended its marketing, and said it had tightened its ad-approval process.

That wasn't an isolated case. In August, the ASA banned an ad for Candy AI, a companion app, over a script that told viewers they could "control her" — language the regulator found promoted harmful ideas about how women, real or synthetic, should be treated. Taken together with the PixVideo ruling, the two decisions read less like scattered rulings than a pattern: image and video-generation tools marketed on the promise of undressing or dominating a digital woman are now a standing target for UK ad enforcement, and the European Parliament has separately proposed banning "nudification" tools outright under revised AI Act provisions.

The ASA's capacity to catch these ads has also grown sharply. Its AI-based Active Ad Monitoring system processed more than 60 million ads in 2025, up from 28 million the year before, and now generates the large majority of the watchdog's enforcement actions proactively rather than through public complaints. An AI-generated advert for an AI product is, increasingly, being caught by another AI system before anyone has to complain about it at all.

The shared thread

What links Brussels' inspectors and London's ad regulator is not the subject matter — hiring algorithms and undressing apps have little in common on the surface — but the shift from writing rules to applying them. Both regimes spent years building frameworks that companies could treat as a future problem: something to prepare for, staff up for, eventually comply with. That runway has ended. The AI Office is now inside company technical files rather than just publishing guidance about them, and the ASA is banning specific named products rather than issuing general warnings about AI marketing.

For companies operating high-risk or AI-marketed products in Europe, the practical effect is that documentation gaps and careless creative decisions now carry the same kind of exposure that used to be reserved for data-protection breaches. A credit-scoring vendor that cannot produce its Article 11 file, or an app developer whose ad team ships a suggestive before-and-after clip, is no longer betting against a hypothetical fine — it is betting against regulators who have shown, this year, that they will act.

The next marker to watch is how the first wave of AI Office inspections resolves: whether any findings escalate into the formal penalty process, and whether Finland's early enforcement experience becomes the template other member states follow as their own supervisory laws come online through the rest of 2026.
