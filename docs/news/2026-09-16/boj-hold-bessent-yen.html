BoJ expected to hold as Bessent comments fuel yen-hike speculation

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda, who has signaled the board will weigh "upside price risks" at this week's meeting. — Cabinet Public Relations Office of the Cabinet Secretariat / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
Economists now overwhelmingly expect the BoJ to hike to 1.25% on Thursday, a reversal traders partly credit to Bessent's yen-strength campaign.

Six weeks ago, economists surveyed by Reuters were split on whether the Bank of Japan would even move this year. Now 66 of 68 expect it to raise its policy rate to 1.25% on Thursday — the highest level in 31 years — and futures markets are pricing the move at close to 99%. The swing from hold to hike has a name attached to it: Scott Bessent.

The US Treasury secretary has spent September publicly pushing Japan toward a stronger yen, an unusual position for a US Treasury chief, whose predecessors have generally tolerated — if not quietly welcomed — a weak yen that keeps Japanese exports cheap. "I have information that the market doesn't have," Bessent told CNBC in early September, "and it's my belief that the Japanese government and the BOJ will do the things that will lead to a stronger yen." Days later, at a Southern Methodist University event, he went further: "I am the house now, so when we intervene with the Japanese yen, I have pretty good insight into what the Bank of Japan is going to do."

From jawboning to policy

The rhetoric hasn't operated in a vacuum. Washington and Tokyo carried out a rare joint intervention to support the yen in late July, and the currency has since slipped back above 160 per dollar, reviving intervention chatter and, with it, pressure on the BOJ to act through rates rather than the spot market. In a Reuters poll this month, 82% of economists said the intervention and Bessent's comments had "significantly" or "somewhat" lowered the political barriers to a September hike.

The inflation backdrop has done its part too. July's national CPI ran at 1.9% year-on-year, with the core-core reading — the BOJ's preferred gauge, stripping out fresh food and energy — also at 1.9%, both above the figures the board had in hand when it last met. Governor Kazuo Ueda, speaking after a G20 finance leaders' meeting in Asheville, North Carolina, said the board would weigh "upside price risks" from Middle East tensions, AI-driven demand and the inflationary drag of a weak currency — language markets read as a clear hike signal.

What's actually at stake

A quarter-point move to 1.25% would be the BOJ's third hike this year, following increases that took the rate from near-zero to 1.0% by June. It would also land against a global backdrop of rising long-end yields — Japan's own 10-year bond touched 3% this month for the first time since 1996 — meaning the BOJ is tightening into an already-jumpy bond market, not a calm one.

The more delicate question is whether a hike driven partly by US political pressure, rather than domestic data alone, sets a precedent the BOJ can live with. Nearly all economists in the Reuters poll expect a follow-up move by January, a third of them betting on December; a growing minority now see the rate reaching 1.50% before year-end. If Bessent's campaign has indeed pulled the timeline forward, the test comes fast: whether yen strength holds once the announcement is priced in, or whether traders decide the move was more theatre than fundamentals and sell the currency right back down.

The decision lands Thursday, September 18, Tokyo time. A hold — still the tail scenario — would likely hit the yen hard given how thoroughly a hike is now priced in; the bigger swing risk may be a smaller-than-hoped move or a downbeat statement, especially if Ueda hedges the pace of hikes to come.
