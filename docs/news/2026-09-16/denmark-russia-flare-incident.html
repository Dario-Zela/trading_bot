Denmark accuses Russian warship of firing flares at NATO helicopter

A Royal Danish Air Force AS 550 Fennec helicopter, the same type fired on by a Russian frigate near Gedser. — Pajx / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
A Russian frigate fired two unannounced flares at a Danish NATO helicopter near Gedser, prompting Denmark to summon Moscow's ambassador and call the act reckless.

In the grey waters off Gedser, Denmark's southernmost point, a Danish Air Force Fennec helicopter was doing what NATO aircraft in the Baltic have done routinely for years: photographing a Russian warship passing through international waters. On Monday, the routine broke. The frigate fired two emergency flares toward the helicopter. One passed close enough to alarm the crew. No one was hurt, and the aircraft was not hit — but Copenhagen is treating the episode as a marker of how far Russia is now willing to push.

"Reckless action from Russia towards the Danish military," Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said of the incident, adding that it was meant to intimidate and would not succeed. Foreign Minister Lars Løkke Rasmussen went further, calling it "completely unacceptable" and warning it "could have endangered human lives." Defence Minister Jeppe Bruus described the frigate crew's conduct as "highly unprofessional seamanship" and said the episode was "serious and without precedent." Denmark's foreign ministry summoned Russia's ambassador in Copenhagen, Vladimir Barbin, to explain.

A flare is not a warning shot — until it is

Military flares are not weapons in the conventional sense; navies use them for signalling, illumination and, in narrower circumstances, as a blunt warning to aircraft or vessels judged to be operating too close. That is precisely the dispute here. Danish officials describe the helicopter's photographic run as standard NATO surveillance of a Russian warship transiting a strait it has every legal right to use. Barbin offered a different account, blaming Danish pilots for "provocative actions" and "dangerous manoeuvres," and claiming Danish aircraft had carried out low-altitude hovering near Russian ships before, in episodes Copenhagen had allegedly not acted on.

Both versions cannot be squared, and Denmark has offered no evidence to rebut the Russian counter-claim beyond its own account of the encounter. What is not in dispute is the outcome: a NATO member state's military aircraft was fired at, without warning, by a warship it did not consider itself in conflict with. The gap between the two narratives is itself part of the story — it is a textbook feature of Baltic "grey zone" incidents, where each side frames a close encounter as the other's provocation, leaving outside observers with an escalation and no independent referee.

A crowded corridor gets more crowded

The flare incident did not happen in isolation. On the same day, NATO fighter jets shot down a drone that had crossed from Belarus into Lithuanian airspace, with Lithuanian President Gitanas Nauseda calling the intercept evidence of why regional readiness matters. European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen linked the two episodes directly, telling reporters that "these are not isolated incidents" but "part of a broader pattern of Russian aggression" against Europe that was "reckless and dangerous."

That pattern has been building for nearly two years. NATO launched its Baltic Sentry patrol mission in January 2025 after a string of undersea cable and pipeline damage incidents blamed on vessels in Russia's so-called shadow fleet — commercial tankers, often flagged in third countries, that move sanctioned Russian oil and, allies suspect, occasionally double as instruments of sabotage. Since late 2023, at least eleven cables in the Baltic have been cut or damaged, some by anchors dragged for hundreds of kilometres along the seabed — distances NATO assessors say are too long to be accidental. Denmark has separately reported repeated drone sightings near its military sites over the past year, and Sweden jammed a suspected Russian reconnaissance drone over the Øresund Strait in February.

The Baltic is not a peripheral waterway. It carries a large share of the energy and container traffic moving between Scandinavia, the Baltic states, Germany and Poland, and it is bounded almost entirely by NATO members since Sweden and Finland joined the alliance — leaving Kaliningrad and a short stretch of Russian coast near St Petersburg as Moscow's remaining access. Every additional close call raises the operating risk for military patrols and, over time, filters into insurance and compliance costs for the shipping that still has to pass through those same waters.

What Copenhagen does next

Denmark's response so far has been diplomatic rather than military: a summons, a public condemnation, and appeals to allied solidarity. Danish political parties across the spectrum backed the government's handling of the episode, and officials framed it explicitly as a test of NATO unity rather than a bilateral spat. Whether that holds depends on what happens the next time a NATO aircraft photographs a Russian hull in the Baltic — an encounter that, on current form, is a matter of when rather than if. The immediate marker to watch is what, if anything, Russia's foreign ministry says in response to the summons, and whether Baltic Sentry's naval-drone and patrol posture is quietly reinforced in the weeks ahead.
