Altman says the world is 'right to be afraid' of AI power

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman, who says concentrated AI power could produce "dystopian" outcomes. — Steve Jurvetson / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Altman says the world is right to fear concentrated AI power, then asks for trust in labs — including his own, still closely held and delaying its IPO.

Sam Altman has a habit of saying the quiet part out loud. This week the OpenAI chief executive told an interviewer that the world is "right to be afraid" of the power concentrated in AI companies — his own included — even as he asked the public to trust frontier labs to wield that power responsibly. It is not the first time he has said something similar, and the repetition is starting to look less like candour and more like a pattern OpenAI can't talk its way out of.

Altman has been explicit about what specifically worries him. "If an extraordinarily powerful AI is used by one person or company to impress their worldview onto everyone else, the results could be extremely dystopian," he said in one recent appearance, naming concentration of power alongside loss of control as the two threats he considers unacceptable. He has put a number on the second one too, telling an interviewer it would be "unacceptable to be taking a 10% chance of killing everybody by the end of the decade."

The trust he's asking for

What makes the remarks awkward is who is doing the concentrating. OpenAI is the dominant player in the industry it is warning about, backed by a roughly 27% stake from Microsoft, and reportedly delaying a public listing rather than opening its books to markets this year. Altman told Fortune in an interview published Tuesday that going public in 2026 would be "ill-advised" given the safety work still ahead — "I would say not 2026" — while separately floating the idea that leading labs might strike a pact to slow development and coordinate on risk. That combination — acknowledge the fear, ask for patience, keep control in-house — is precisely the move Altman himself has criticised elsewhere. He has said he is "deeply afraid" of a world where fear is used to justify the argument that "only this small group can own it, because it's too dangerous and only they understand it," followed by a promise that they'll make the right decisions for everyone. He calls that outcome "fundamentally anti-human." Critics note it also describes OpenAI's own governance structure reasonably well.

Regulators are listening

None of this is happening in a vacuum. The Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission have both signalled interest in AI market concentration, and OpenAI's scale and Microsoft's stake are the kind of facts that antitrust lawyers underline in red pen. In Europe and the UK, regulators are already tightening oversight of frontier AI providers rather than waiting for firms to self-police. For a company that has spent two years asking investors and governments to take its word on safety, admitting the fear is justified doesn't buy goodwill — it hands sceptics a quote to put in their next filing.

The next test comes if and when OpenAI actually files for a public listing, whenever that lands after 2026: prospectus disclosures will force the numbers behind the rhetoric — revenue, governance, control rights — into public view for the first time.
