Employers to face duty to meet staff requesting flexible working

Louise Haigh, First Secretary of State, announced the new flexible working duty at the TUC conference in Brighton. — Cabinet Office / OGL v3.0
In one sentence
Ministers will force UK employers to hold a meeting with any staff member requesting flexible work before they can refuse it, tightening 2025's employment law overhaul.

There is a particular kind of workplace conversation that, for years, employers in Britain have been free to skip. An employee asks to shift to four days a week, or to start at ten instead of nine, or to work from home on Fridays. The employer reads the request, decides it is inconvenient, and replies with a letter. No meeting, no discussion, just a decision. From next year, that shortcut disappears.

Speaking at the TUC conference in Brighton on Tuesday, Louise Haigh — First Secretary of State and Chancellor of the Duchy of Lancaster — announced that the government will require employers to sit down with any employee who requests flexible working before they can turn it down.

"If an employer wants to turn down a request they will have to sit down with their employee, properly consider it and explain why."

From box-ticking to a real conversation

Under the current regime, employers must merely consider a flexible working request "in a reasonable manner" and respond within two months — a standard that in practice let many requests be waved off by email, provided one of eight permitted business reasons (extra cost, inability to reorganise work among existing staff, and so on) was cited. Haigh's announcement builds directly on the Employment Rights Act 2025, which received royal assent in December and is already being rolled out in phases through 2026 and 2027.

A government consultation on the mechanics — which closed at the end of April — set out what the meeting would need to cover: exploring alternative arrangements, giving the employee advance notice to prepare, ensuring a manager with actual authority to approve the change attends, and producing a written summary of what was discussed and why. Separately, the Act introduces a tougher "reasonableness test" for refusals, due to land in 2027, meaning citing one of the eight grounds will no longer be enough on its own — the refusal itself will have to be reasonable, and defensible if challenged at tribunal.

Why bosses are wary

Employer groups have generally accepted the principle of flexible working — the CBI's own research suggests roughly nine in ten requests are already granted in some form, formally or informally — but have pushed back on the administrative load of the wider Employment Rights Act, warning it risks overburdening businesses, particularly smaller ones with thin HR teams. A mandatory meeting for every request, backed by a written record, adds a procedural layer that larger firms with dedicated HR functions will absorb far more easily than a five-person shop needing to free up a manager's time and paper the file to avoid a tribunal claim later.

Haigh framed the flexible working change as part of a broader package — including day-one rights to paternity and unpaid parental leave, the removal of the lower earnings limit and waiting period for statutory sick pay, and new limits on zero-hours contracts — that she called "the biggest uplift" in worker rights in a generation.

What's still missing

What the announcement does not yet answer is precisely how the meeting requirement will be enforced, what recourse an employee has if a manager treats the sit-down as a formality, or how much it will add to the cost of running a small business — details expected to emerge once the government publishes its response to the spring consultation and drafts the secondary legislation needed to bring the reasonableness test into force. Employers should expect that response, along with a firmer implementation timetable, before the wider Employment Rights Act reforms complete their rollout in 2027.
