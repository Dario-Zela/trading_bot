'One in, one out' migrant return scheme costs £56,000 per person

Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood, who disclosed the £56,000 per-person cost to the Home Affairs Select Committee. — Lauren Hurley / No 10 Downing Street (Open Government Licence v3.0)
In one sentence
Shabana Mahmood confirmed the UK's France return scheme costs £56,000 per migrant sent back, reviving questions about its value for money.

It is not the number that will trouble a home secretary in her first year in the job — it is the arithmetic underneath it. Shabana Mahmood, giving evidence to the Home Affairs Select Committee this week, was asked a straightforward question: how much does it cost to send one migrant back across the Channel under the government's flagship 'one in, one out' returns deal with France? Her answer — "about £56,000" per person — is now doing more political work than any small-boat statistic in months.

"I think it's per person for the numbers that have been returned so far, it's about £56,000," Mahmood told MPs, adding that the sum was "still cheaper, or just about the same as supporting someone in a hotel for a year."

A pilot scheme, reciprocally priced

The scheme, agreed with Paris and launched in August 2025, works on a strict one-for-one basis: for every migrant who arrives on a small boat and is returned to France, the UK accepts one asylum seeker from France through a legal route instead. It was sold as a way to break the smugglers' business model by removing the certainty that a Channel crossing, once made, would stick.

On the government's own figures, roughly 1,400 people have been sent back to France since launch, though the pace has been slow and uneven — independent tallies put confirmed returns under the formal one-in-one-out mechanism at closer to 377, against 380 arrivals accepted from France in exchange, an average of around 12 removals a week. Some of those returned have simply re-crossed, by lorry or dinghy, undercutting the deterrent logic the policy was built on.

Why £56,000 is the number that sticks

A single removal costing £56,000 is easy to turn into a headline, and harder to defend line by line: charter flights, legal challenges, detention costs and administrative overhead all sit inside the figure, spread across a caseload that is still small. Mahmood's comparison to hotel accommodation — official estimates have long put the cost of housing an asylum seeker in a hotel at somewhere near £40,000-£45,000 a year — is meant to reframe the number as a saving rather than an extravagance. But it invites the obvious retort from critics on both flanks: if removals are barely cheaper than the hotels they are meant to replace, and are running at a fraction of small-boat arrival numbers, what is the £56,000 actually buying?

The politics are complicated further by Paris. Laurent Nuñez, France's interior minister, has told a French parliamentary inquiry that the pilot was never intended to run beyond 1 October 2026, and that he wants the bilateral arrangement folded into a broader EU-UK migration framework rather than renewed as a standalone deal. That leaves the Home Office negotiating the scheme's future at the same time as defending its price tag.

What it signals

For a government that has staked considerable credibility on demonstrating fiscal discipline, a six-figure-adjacent per-head cost on its most visible migration policy is an awkward data point to hand to opponents — both a Conservative opposition demanding tougher enforcement and a Reform UK riding high in the polls on the same issue. It does not, on its own, threaten the scheme's survival. But it sharpens a question that will follow the government into the next spending review: whether 'one in, one out' is a genuine deterrent worth its cost, or a pilot being kept alive mainly because scrapping it would look like defeat.

The next marker is 1 October 2026, when the current pilot period lapses and Britain and France must decide — separately, and not entirely in agreement — what comes next.
