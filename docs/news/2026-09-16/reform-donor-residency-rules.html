Government moves to close donor loophole after Reform's £72m row

Nigel Farage, whose party received £72m in two days from British citizens based overseas — donations now at the centre of a government rule change. — Laurie Noble / UK Parliament, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ministers are fast-tracking a 12-month residency test for political donors, backdated to March 2026, aimed squarely at the £72m Reform UK took from two Britain-born crypto financiers based abroad.

Nigel Farage does not, as a rule, sound defensive. But asked directly whether Reform UK's record-breaking £72m windfall would survive contact with the law as ministers now intend to rewrite it, the party leader could only manage that the donations were "100% compliant with the law today." Today, it turns out, is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

The money arrived in two identical instalments of £36m, a day apart, from two British-born cryptocurrency financiers who have spent years building their fortunes a long way from Westminster. Ben Delo, a co-founder of the crypto exchange BitMEX who was pardoned by President Trump in 2025 after a Bank Secrecy Act conviction, gave £36m from what had been a Hong Kong base. Christopher Harborne, a Tether investor who lives in Thailand and is already Reform's biggest backer, matched it — reportedly after Delo mentioned the gift to him and a competitive streak took over. Combined, it is the largest sum ever given to a British political party in such a short window, and roughly ten times what Reform raised in the whole of the previous year.

A loophole hiding in plain sight

Under UK electoral law, foreign donations to political parties are banned outright. But the rules draw a sharp line at nationality, not location: any British citizen registered to vote, however long they have lived abroad and however loosely they maintain ties to the country, can give as much as they like. Reform's deputy leader, Richard Tice, has leaned on exactly that distinction, saying the party is "not responsible for the donors' personal schedules" and that its compliance checks confirmed both gifts met the applicable rules. Reform has not said when Delo returned from Hong Kong, nor how much time Harborne — still registered to vote in Britain despite living in Thailand — actually spends in the country.

That ambiguity is precisely what the government now wants to close off. The vehicle is the Representation of the People Bill, already working its way through the House of Lords, into which ministers plan to insert amendments requiring donors to have been continuously UK-resident for electoral purposes for a full 12 months before they can give above a new £100,000 annual cap. Sir Philip Rycroft, the former senior civil servant who led a government-commissioned review into foreign interference risk, put the standard bluntly: returning Britons would need "a full calendar year" back in the country before being treated as ordinary domestic donors.

Retrospective, and deliberately so

The detail that has sharpened tempers in Westminster is timing. The cap and residency test are due to apply retrospectively from 25 March 2026 — months before Delo and Harborne wrote their cheques, and well before the Bill itself becomes law. Housing and Communities Secretary Steve Reed, who commissioned the Rycroft review in December 2025, framed the measure as a security response rather than a partisan one: "Foreign interference and dirty money are menacing the integrity of our elections," he said, adding separately that the Bill would "protect our democracy against foreign interference" and stop "hostile foreign states" using "dirty money to buy our elections." Schools minister Georgia Gould told the BBC's Today programme ministers were "really worried about the big money that is coming into politics," and defended backdating the rule as the only way to stop a rush of donations timed to beat the new law.

Tice has pushed back hard on the retrospective element specifically, arguing Reform followed the regulations that were actually in force when the money changed hands, and that rewriting the rules after the fact to catch a specific gift sets an uncomfortable precedent regardless of what any party's opponents might feel about it. The Bill also folds in a separate, ban on cryptoasset donations altogether — Reform is currently the only UK party that uses a third-party provider to accept crypto gifts — on the grounds that the true ownership behind a wallet address is often impossible to verify. Parties found to be holding money that becomes unlawful under the new rules will reportedly have 30 days to return it once the law takes effect.

What happens to the £72m

None of this makes Reform's windfall illegal today, and Farage has been careful to say only that he cannot promise the same money would be permissible under the proposed rules — not that it is currently unlawful. That distinction will matter enormously once the amendments reach a vote: the government wants the legislation through both Houses before the next general election, which puts Reform in the position of fighting a law explicitly reverse-engineered around its own biggest donation, while insisting throughout that its conduct was blameless. The next marker is the Lords' consideration of the amendments in the coming weeks, where the retrospective date — and the question of what happens to £72m sitting in a party's accounts if Parliament decides it should never have arrived — will be argued in detail rather than in soundbites.
