UK rates squeeze: gilts near 19-year highs, BoE decides Thursday, CPI due

The Bank of England on Threadneedle Street, where the MPC meets Thursday to set Bank Rate. — George Rex / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0)
In one sentence
Gilt yields climbed toward 5.4%, their highest since 2007, as investors price a chance of further Bank of England tightening ahead of Thursday's MPC decision.

UK government bond yields pushed toward 5.4% this week, their highest since August 2007, as investors bet the Bank of England has further tightening in it yet — or at least a longer wait before any relief. The move lands two days before Thursday's Monetary Policy Committee decision and hours after the morning's inflation print, squeezing the Bank from both directions at once: a bond market losing patience and a price index refusing to cooperate.

A selloff with a familiar culprit

The 10-year gilt yield touched roughly 5.3–5.4% this week, a level last seen when Tony Blair was still prime minister. Thirty-year gilts, sold at syndication, priced at their highest yield since at least 1998. Two-year and five-year yields have also climbed to multi-year highs, which tells its own story: this isn't a slow drift at the long end from fiscal worries alone, but a broad repricing across the curve that implicates monetary policy itself.

The immediate trigger is energy. Oil has traded above $100 a barrel for the first time in six weeks amid renewed Middle East disruption — the same crude spike rattling markets from Riyadh to Washington this week. For an economy as exposed to imported energy costs as Britain's, a sustained move in oil prices feeds almost mechanically into the inflation outlook, and bond investors have adjusted accordingly, dialling back bets on near-term cuts and nudging up the odds of a hike instead.

Thursday's vote, and the split behind it

The MPC announces its decision on Thursday 17 September at noon. Market pricing puts the odds of a hold at Bank Rate's current 3.75% at around 73%, versus roughly 27% for a hike — a live possibility, not a rounding error, for a central bank that has held rates for five consecutive meetings.

The committee is not of one mind. At the July meeting, the vote split 6-3, with Huw Pill, Megan Greene and Catherine Mann pushing to raise Bank Rate to 4% on concerns that higher energy prices could entrench inflation rather than pass through it. That a third of the committee was already voting to tighten before this week's oil spike suggests Thursday's discussion will be more contentious than the headline consensus implies. Governor Andrew Bailey has previously played down expectations of an imminent hike, but the data since has not made his job easier.

Pay growth is doing its part to keep the committee uneasy. Regular earnings rose 3.5% in the three months to July, comfortably above the roughly 2% pace the Bank has judged consistent with its inflation target once productivity is accounted for. Wage growth at that clip, layered onto an energy shock, is precisely the combination the MPC's hawks have been warning could keep price growth sticky through next year.

The number that lands first

Before the Bank even meets, the Office for National Statistics published August's inflation figures this morning. The measure investors watch most closely, CPIH, was already running at 3.1% as of July — nearly a point and a half above the Bank's 2% target — and economists had flagged a real chance today's reading pushes the headline CPI figure above 3% for the first time in months, up from 2.9% in July.

The timing is not incidental. This morning's release, followed by Thursday's rate decision, and Chancellor Rachel Reeves's autumn Budget in the weeks ahead, form a single sequence rather than three separate events. Sticky inflation narrows the Chancellor's room for manoeuvre on tax and spending even as households absorb elevated borrowing costs; a hawkish central bank reading of the data would tighten that vice further, at precisely the moment gilt investors are already demanding a higher premium to hold UK debt.

What it means for the cost of borrowing

Higher gilt yields are not an abstraction confined to trading floors. They set the marginal cost of government borrowing, feed into fixed-rate mortgage pricing, and shape how UK pension funds and insurers value long-dated liabilities — all channels through which Thursday's decision, and this morning's inflation print, will be felt well beyond the bond market. A Bank of England caught between a fragile gilt market and a still-restless inflation number has few comfortable options: hold and risk looking behind the curve, or move and validate the market's more hawkish read. Thursday's vote, and the minutes that accompany it, will show which risk the committee decided to take.

What to watch next: the 17 September rate decision and MPC minutes at noon, for both the vote split and any shift in forward guidance; and the 30-year gilt auction results in the weeks following, as a market-based referendum on how much fiscal and inflation risk investors are willing to underwrite before the Chancellor's autumn Budget.
