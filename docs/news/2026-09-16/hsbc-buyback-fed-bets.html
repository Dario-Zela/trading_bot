HSBC shares swing as buyback offsets Fed-driven bank jitters

HSBC's global headquarters at 8 Canada Square, Canary Wharf, London — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
HSBC's ~$1bn buyback is propping up its stock this week even as Fed rate-hike bets weigh on UK bank valuations.

HSBC's stock has spent the week caught in a tug-of-war between its own chequebook and the bond market. Shares closed at 1,528p in London on September 15th, down 0.54% on the day and roughly 4.8% over five sessions, even as the bank continues to buy back its own stock at a pace of tens of millions of pounds a day. Zoom out, and the shares are still up over 28% for the year — a gap between the weekly wobble and the annual trend that tells its own story about what's holding the price up.

That support is a US$1 billion buyback HSBC launched on August 5th after first-half profit jumped 23%, with completion targeted around the third-quarter results. By September 14th, the bank had repurchased roughly 29.5 million shares — split between London and Hong Kong listings — for a combined US$609.6 million, according to filings tracked by Stocktitan. The daily rhythm is mechanical: on September 14th alone HSBC bought back 800,000 UK shares and 314,400 Hong Kong shares; three days earlier it was 10,000 and 321,600 respectively. Buybacks reduce the share count outstanding — now just over 17.15 billion ordinary shares — which mechanically lifts earnings per share and gives the stock a standing bid regardless of what the news cycle is doing.

The Fed overhang

What the news cycle is doing is a Federal Reserve decision landing Wednesday, with markets pricing a hike from the current 3.5–3.75% target range. HSBC's own economists are among those forecasting 50 basis points of increases split between September and December, taking rates to a 4.00–4.25% range. Higher-for-longer policy cuts both ways for a bank: it can widen net interest margins on new lending, but it also squeezes loan demand, raises deposit costs, and tightens credit conditions — the kind of macro static that has kept UK-listed banks broadly under pressure this week as sterling and rate-sensitive valuations react to every Fed signal.

HSBC added to the crosscurrents itself on September 11th, pricing £750 million of 6.061% fixed-to-floating senior notes due 2035 — a reminder that even as it hands cash back to shareholders with one arm, it's still terming out debt at levels well above where it borrowed a few years ago.

Why the buyback matters more than usual

For anyone trying to read HSBC's share price as a signal about the bank's health, the buyback complicates the picture. It is, by design, a floor: a standing daily buyer that dampens downside moves independent of what earnings, credit quality or rate expectations are actually doing. That's useful support during a week when the macro backdrop is genuinely uncertain, but it also means the stock's resilience says less about HSBC's fundamentals than about how much dry powder remains in the programme. When it completes — expected around the Q3 results — that daily buyer disappears, and the shares will be left to trade on rate expectations and credit conditions alone.

The next real test comes Wednesday afternoon, when the Fed's decision and dot plot either validate the hawkish case HSBC's own analysts have been making or hand rate-sensitive bank stocks a reason to rally.
