Valve's four-figure Steam Frame headset tests VR pricing limits

Valve's Steam Frame headset, shown with its two handheld controllers. — Valve Corporation / Wikipedia
In one sentence
Valve's Steam Frame headset draws praise for its engineering but costs £889-£1,089 and lasts under an hour on battery, reviving the debate over whether VR can ever leave its high-end niche.

Valve's new virtual reality headset arrived this week to a familiar verdict: superb engineering, and a price that undercuts its own case for existing.

The Steam Frame, successor to the seven-year-old Valve Index, comes in two configurations — £889 for 256GB of storage, £1,089 for 1TB. That's before a £29 power supply, sold separately. Reviewers who got early units were, almost unanimously, impressed by the hardware itself: a Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 chip, a 2160×2160-per-eye display running up to 144Hz, eye tracking, and pancake lenses in a shell light enough that several described it as closer to wearing a large pair of ski goggles than a headset. It ships with a copy of Half-Life: Alyx, Valve's flagship VR title, playable either standalone or streamed from a PC.

"VR today is ultimately a high-end niche."

That's gaming writer Christopher Dring, summing up the reaction that has followed every VR launch for the better part of a decade. Excellent hardware, tiny addressable market.

What's new, and what isn't

The headset's most genuinely novel feature is foveated streaming: two eye-tracking cameras watch where the user is looking and the system sends four video feeds — two low-resolution ones covering the full field of view, two high-resolution ones aimed precisely at the eye's focal point. It's a clever way to squeeze high-fidelity wireless VR through ordinary bandwidth, and Valve says the technique will work with any eye-tracking headset that supports Steam Link, not just its own.

What hasn't improved much is battery life. In standalone mode at 144Hz, reviewers clocked roughly 55 minutes running Half-Life: Alyx before the headset died. Streaming from a PC — which offloads the heavy graphics work — stretched that to about an hour and a half. For a device costing four figures, an hour of play before needing a charge is a hard number to explain away.

Asked why the headset wasn't cheaper, Valve developer Jeremy Selan pointed to component costs rather than margin: "We set out to come out with a device that would have been much more affordable," he said, but "the global RAM market has impacted us." Memory prices have indeed climbed sharply through 2026 on AI-driven demand, squeezing margins across consumer electronics — a real constraint, if not one that will register with a buyer staring at a £1,089 receipt.

The pricing problem, again

Line the Steam Frame up against other living-room hardware and the gap is stark. Meta's Quest 3 launched in 2023 at £480 with 128GB of storage — less than half the Steam Frame's price for a comparable capacity tier. The budget Quest 3S undercuts it further, at £320 list and often discounted to £250. A Nintendo Switch 2 is £420. A PlayStation 5 is around £470. Even Sony's PSVR2, not cheap by any measure, comes in below Valve's flagship configuration. Only Apple's Vision Pro, at roughly £4,000, makes the Steam Frame look like a bargain — and that device targets a different market entirely.

This is the pattern VR has repeated at nearly every hardware cycle since the first consumer Oculus Rift shipped in 2016: a new generation promises the display quality, comfort or wireless freedom that will finally pull the format out of enthusiast circles, and then arrives priced for enthusiasts anyway. Facebook subsidised the original Quest line to buy market share; Valve, without an advertising business to cross-subsidise hardware, appears to be pricing the Steam Frame closer to its actual cost.

UploadVR's David Heaney offered the more forgiving read, noting that £420-£470 game consoles aren't obviously cheap either, and that VR hardware carries display and sensor costs a flat-screen console doesn't. It's a fair point on unit economics. It doesn't change the fact that a customer choosing between a Switch 2 and a Steam Frame is choosing between one device that plays games their friends already own and another that costs more than twice as much and needs recharging every hour.

What's next

Valve has not disclosed sell-through numbers, and won't need to for some time — the Steam Frame is available to order now, with wider retail stock expected through the autumn. The number worth watching is Steam's own hardware survey, which tracks what headsets are actually plugged into PCs each month; if the Steam Frame fails to move VR's install-base share, which has sat in the low single digits for years, that will say more about the pricing paradox than any review score.
