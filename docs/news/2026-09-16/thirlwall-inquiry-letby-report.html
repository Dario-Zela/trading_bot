Thirlwall Inquiry finds 'complete failure' of safeguarding in Letby hospital deaths

Countess of Chester Hospital, where the Thirlwall Inquiry found a 'complete failure' of safeguarding between 2015 and 2016. — Geograph.org.uk / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The £18.5m Thirlwall Inquiry found a 'complete failure' of safeguarding at Countess of Chester Hospital, concluding some Letby victims' deaths were preventable.

A public inquiry into the deaths of babies at the Countess of Chester Hospital has concluded that the institution failed, completely and repeatedly, to protect the infants in its care — and that some of the deaths could have been prevented.

The Thirlwall Inquiry, chaired by Lady Justice Kathryn Thirlwall, published its final report in Liverpool on Monday after six months of evidence, 60 days of public hearings and a cost to the taxpayer of more than £18.5m. Its subject was the hospital where neonatal nurse Lucy Letby murdered seven babies and attempted to murder others between 2015 and 2016. But the report's real subject, in the end, is not Letby herself — it is everyone around her who failed to act.

'A gulf between leadership and clinicians'

Thirlwall's report describes "dysfunctional" management and governance at the trust, and a wide gulf between hospital leadership and the consultants raising the alarm. Senior nurses and managers, it finds, did not understand — or chose not to act on — a basic principle of safeguarding: that suspicion of deliberate harm by a staff member requires immediate protective action, not certainty of guilt first.

Consultants at the hospital raised concerns about Letby as early as October 2015, after a cluster of unexplained collapses on the neonatal unit. Those concerns were, in the inquiry's account, dismissed, deflected or actively obstructed by senior figures more concerned with reputational risk than patient safety. Letby was moved off clinical duties only in mid-2016, by which point the pattern of deaths and near-deaths had continued for months.

"Some babies would have been saved and some attacks would have been prevented if action was taken earlier," Thirlwall found.

Recommendations, and a caveat

The report sets out a raft of recommendations: mandatory safeguarding training for NHS staff, a new escalation protocol when a colleague is suspected of harm, reform of the Care Quality Commission's inspection regime, and — perhaps its most striking practical proposal — livestreamed cameras over every cot and incubator in neonatal units, so parents can watch their babies remotely. Ministers have said they will respond formally and are setting up a single government tracker to monitor progress across this and other maternity-safety inquiries, after previous reviews were criticised for recommendations that were made and then quietly shelved.

The report lands against the backdrop of a live legal controversy: Letby's lawyers have continued to press new evidence they say undermines her convictions, and an independent panel has been reviewing the medical evidence separately from the inquiry, which was explicitly barred from revisiting guilt or innocence. Thirlwall's findings do not touch that question. They are about the institution, not the individual — about a hospital that, by the inquiry's own words, failed completely.

What happens next is administrative rather than dramatic: the Department of Health and Social Care is expected to publish its formal response in the coming weeks, and NHS England's chief nursing officer has already said she will review the national safeguarding framework in light of the report. Neither carries a market angle. This is a story about accountability, not equities.
