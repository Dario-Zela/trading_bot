UK in talks to join Canada-led global defence bank

Chancellor John Healey, who as defence secretary first pushed for UK membership of the Canada-led defence bank and now negotiates it from the Treasury. — Richard Townshend / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 3.0)
In one sentence
Chancellor John Healey, once overruled as defence secretary, is now negotiating UK membership of Canada's new defence-financing bank himself.

John Healey has spent this month in a negotiation with echoes of one he lost only weeks earlier — except now he is the one holding the Treasury's pen.

Britain's chancellor is in talks with Ottawa about the UK becoming a founding member of the Defence, Security and Resilience Bank, a Canada-led institution designed to help NATO allies borrow cheaply to rearm against Russia. Healey is expected to meet his Canadian counterpart, finance minister François-Philippe Champagne, this week, having already sat down with him twice at a G20 finance ministers' gathering in North Carolina last month. Canada is understood to have formally invited the UK to join, alongside eight other founding backers including Ukraine and Turkey.

The twist is that Healey argued for exactly this position a year ago — and lost, to the very department he now runs.

A reversal Healey engineered from both sides

Until June, Healey was defence secretary, and by most accounts an unusually insistent one. He pushed hard for the UK to sign up to the Canadian bank, framing it as a cheaper, faster route to funding the munitions plants, air defences and industrial capacity Britain needs to rearm. He lost that argument to the Treasury, then run by Rachel Reeves, and quit the defence brief in June in what was described at the time as a clash over delaying the government's military spending timetable.

Reeves's objection to the Canadian bank was substantive rather than reflexive. She judged it too focused on channelling loans to smaller defence firms in lower-rated economies to serve Britain's own procurement needs, and opted instead to help launch a rival vehicle — the Multilateral Defence Mechanism, unveiled with the Netherlands, Finland and Poland at NATO's Ankara summit in July, backed by an initial UK commitment of £600 million. Britain has since floated merging that mechanism with the Canadian bank rather than simply joining it outright.

What has changed is not the merits of the Canadian bank but who occupies 11 Downing Street's chair. Healey is now chancellor. The reshuffle that moved him out of defence and into the Treasury handed him the authority to revisit a decision he never accepted in the first place.

What the bank actually does

The Defence, Security and Resilience Bank was declared into being on the second day of the Ankara summit in July, when Canadian prime minister Mark Carney — a former governor of both the Bank of Canada and the Bank of England — announced nine founding backers: Canada, Albania, Belgium, Greece, Latvia, Luxembourg, Romania, Turkey and Ukraine. Combined, their economies are worth roughly $5.75 trillion, nearly matching Germany's GDP on their own, though Germany itself has stayed on the sidelines.

The ambition is to mobilise $134 billion in capital and begin lending as early as 2027, extending affordable, long-term financing to governments for defence procurement while also drawing private money into the industrial base — the small and mid-sized manufacturers that supply shells, drones and armour but often struggle to raise capital against defence contracts alone. Twelve financial institutions, including JPMorgan Chase, Deutsche Bank and three of Canada's largest banks, have signed on in support. Headquarters will sit in Canada, city still to be decided; a European hub is planned for Luxembourg.

The institutional logic behind rearmament

What makes the bank notable is less its balance sheet than its permanence. Since 2022, Western support for the frontline against Russia has largely run through bilateral aid packages and ad hoc coalitions — arrangements renegotiated, and sometimes reversed, with each change of government. A multilateral bank with a treaty, a board and a capital base is a different kind of commitment: it outlasts the ministers who created it, which is precisely why Healey's about-turn matters more than a single funding line would.

That logic sits alongside NATO's broader shift toward treating defence spending as sustained industrial policy rather than emergency outlay — a shift visible too in Denmark's recent stand-off with a Russian warship accused of firing flares at a NATO helicopter, a reminder of why allies are formalising the financing in the first place.

For the UK, the practical question is no longer whether Canada's bank is a good idea — Healey answered that as defence secretary — but what membership costs, and how it sits alongside the Multilateral Defence Mechanism Britain already helped build. Officials expect a decision in the coming weeks, timed to land before the DSRB firms up its 2027 lending targets. The chancellor best placed to explain why Britain should join is the same man the Treasury once overruled.
