Trading house Radiant World sues Glencore for $2bn over Singapore fraud claims

Glencore's headquarters in Baar, Switzerland, at the centre of a $2bn Singapore lawsuit over its dealings with trader Radiant World. — Paradise Chronicle / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Radiant World and Sapphire Minmetals are suing Glencore for over $2bn, while Glencore counters that Radiant sent lenders forged invoices and fake emails.

Radiant World and Sapphire Minmetals, two commodity trading houses already fighting off fraud claims from three banks and a Jefferies-linked fund, have turned the guns on their biggest counterparty. The pair filed suit against Glencore in Singapore this week seeking more than $2 billion in damages, arguing the Swiss mining and trading giant was not merely a customer but the "senior partner" behind their iron ore business — and structured the relationship deliberately off the books.

"The relationship between Glencore Plc and the network of companies linked to Radiant World was one of 'senior partner-junior partner,'" Rakesh Sethi, Sapphire Minmetals' chairman, said in comments reported by Bloomberg. A legal letter sent on Radiant World's behalf goes further: Glencore "organised and structured the arrangements and at all material times acted as the senior partner in the relationship."

The claim leans on a November 2024 WhatsApp message from Peter Hill, Glencore's head of steelmaking raw materials, who told the smaller trader that Glencore had "basically bank rolled your entire existence for the last few years." Radiant World says Glencore pushed transactions through face-to-face meetings and WhatsApp rather than paper trails — a preference it attributes to compliance scrutiny Glencore was already facing from the US Department of Justice, which has separately contacted Glencore about Radiant World's dealings, alongside the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and Singapore police.

Glencore's counter-fire

Glencore calls the suit "meritless" and says it will contest it "vigorously." Its own account, laid out in filings and statements this month, is considerably less flattering to Radiant: the miner says it has found evidence that Radiant World and associated companies sent several banks and lenders falsified invoices and contracts, plus fabricated emails purportedly written by Glencore staff, to raise financing against trades that were smaller or non-existent.

The specifics are granular. Mizuho, exposed to the tune of $95.5 million after buying five iron-ore receivables from Radiant in June, says it received a forged reply supposedly from Glencore confirming payment — only to be told directly by Glencore that it had never sent the email and had no record of the receivables at all. Glencore's own analysis concluded the correspondence was "generated fraudulently." Separately, Incomlend, a Singapore invoice-financing platform, alleges Radiant reused Glencore invoices that had already been paid, backed by fake contracts, to raise $31.7 million, and is suing for more than $34 million. A Jefferies-managed fund has gone further still, winning a worldwide freezing order in London's High Court against Radiant, Sapphire, founder Pinkesh Nahar and Sethi over more than $500 million in alleged losses.

Glencore has booked a $480 million provision against its Radiant exposure — one of the largest trading losses in its history as a listed company — while Sapphire puts its own unrecovered claims against Glencore at over $900 million and Radiant estimates its total losses at more than $1.4 billion. A Singapore court has already frozen up to $499 million in assets tied to Radiant and capped Nahar's personal spending at £4,000 a week pending the fraud investigation.

The documentation problem

What both sides agree on, in effect, is that paperwork moving through this corner of the iron-ore trade could not be trusted at face value. Whether that is because Radiant fabricated it, as Glencore, Mizuho and Incomlend allege, or because Glencore itself preferred informal channels that left no clean audit trail, as Radiant claims, the practical result is the same: banks financed hundreds of millions of dollars against invoices and emails that turned out — by multiple accounts — to be forgeable enough to fool trade-finance desks at a Japanese megabank and a fintech lender. Radiant World, founded by Nahar, is estimated to have traded around 75 million tonnes of iron ore a year, worth more than $7 billion, with roughly $12 billion in annual revenue — a scale that made its financing paper look routine rather than suspect.

What's next

Glencore says it is now reviewing its historic dealings with Radiant, Sapphire and related entities, a process likely to shape both the Singapore suit and the parallel DOJ, CFTC and Singapore police inquiries. The freezing orders in London and Singapore stay in force in the meantime, and further creditor claims look likely as banks that financed Radiant paper comb through their own files for forgeries they missed.
