UK water complaints jump 84% as Ofwat fines sector £158m

Haslemere Sewage Treatment Works in Surrey, operated by Thames Water, one of the companies facing the largest share of Ofwat's £158m penalty. — Simon Burchell / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Water complaints hit a nine-year high as Ofwat claws back £158m from suppliers for missing sewage and leakage targets.

Water customers in England and Wales are complaining in greater numbers than at any point in almost a decade, just as the industry regulator confirms it is clawing back £158m from suppliers for missing pollution and leakage targets. The two developments, arriving in the same stretch of the regulatory calendar, describe a sector caught between rising bills, ageing pipework and a customer base that has stopped giving it the benefit of the doubt.

Complaints climb even as trust falls

Household complaints escalated to the Consumer Council for Water (CCW), the industry's statutory watchdog, rose to 8,235 in the year to March — the highest total in nine years — even as complaints made directly to the water companies themselves fell 8% to 205,853. That divergence is itself telling: customers are increasingly bypassing company complaint desks and going straight to the referee. Within CCW's own figures, complaints about the scale of bill increases jumped 138% and those citing affordability leapt 110%, reflecting average household bills that crossed £600 a year after the steepest round of price rises in memory. Satisfaction with sewerage services, meanwhile, slumped to a record-low 60%, and just 35% of customers say they trust companies to protect the environment — the CCW's broader Water Matters survey puts overall trust at 6.07 out of 10, its lowest reading on record and the third consecutive annual decline.

The £158m bill

Ofwat's parallel reckoning covers the sector's performance against targets set for the 2020-25 period: an 30% cut in sewage pollution incidents delivered only 2%, a 16% leakage reduction came in at 6%, and a 41% cut in sewer flooding managed just 10%. Nine of the eleven wastewater companies saw pollution incidents rise rather than fall. The resulting £158m penalty is returned to customers as a deduction on future bills rather than paid to the regulator directly — Thames Water and Southern Water account for the largest shares, at roughly £56.8m and £31.9m respectively — and adds to more than £400m in similar performance penalties levied since 2020. Ofwat's own report called the shortfall "stark evidence that money alone will not bring the sustained improvements that customers rightly expect," pointing instead to weaknesses in company culture and leadership.

For investors, the arithmetic is uncomfortable on both sides: penalties eat into regulated returns just as Ofwat has approved roughly 21% in average bill increases through 2030 to fund the infrastructure spending the targets assume. The government's broader response — folding Ofwat into a single integrated water regulator recommended by last year's Cunliffe review, alongside bonus bans for polluting executives — is still working through Parliament. Ofwat's next annual performance report, due in October, will show whether this year's investment surge has narrowed the gap or simply bought the sector more time to explain it away.
