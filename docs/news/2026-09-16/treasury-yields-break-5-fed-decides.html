BOND YIELDS · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-16 · 6 min read

10-year Treasury yield tops 5% for first time since 2007 as Fed decides today

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C., where the FOMC meets to set interest rates. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The 10-year Treasury yield hit 5.04%, its highest since 2007, on a fifth day of bond selling driven by oil-price inflation fears and fiscal jitters, hours before the Fed's rate decision.

The 10-year Treasury note traded above 5% on Tuesday for the first time since July 2007, capping a fifth consecutive session of selling in long-dated government debt and handing markets a number that has not mattered in this way for nineteen years. The yield touched an intraday high of 5.04%; the 30-year bond, more exposed to inflation and fiscal risk, pushed above 5.4%. Neither move was caused by the Federal Reserve, which has not yet acted. That is the uncomfortable part. The bond market moved first, and it moved on its own.

The Federal Open Market Committee announces its rate decision Wednesday at 2 p.m. Eastern, with Chair Kevin Warsh due to face reporters half an hour later. CME FedWatch pricing has hovered near 90% probability of a quarter-point increase, to a target range of 3.75%–4.00% — the Fed's first hike since July 2023, after more than two years spent either cutting or holding. That a rate increase is now the consensus, rather than a stray forecast, says something about how far inflation expectations have travelled since the summer.

Five days, nineteen years

What makes this week different from ordinary rate-cycle repricing is sequencing. Long yields did not rise because the Fed signaled hikes were coming; they rose first, forcing the Fed's hand into a meeting where a hike is now close to fully priced. The 10-year note closed at 4.96% just five sessions ago and has climbed in a straight line since, a pace of movement more associated with a shock than a drift. Thirty-year yields at 5.4% have not been seen since before the 2008 financial crisis, when Treasuries still carried a meaningfully different fiscal backdrop — a federal deficit a fraction of today's, and none of the current interest-payment arithmetic.

That arithmetic is now unfavorable. The federal deficit is on pace to reach roughly $2 trillion this fiscal year, and net interest payments on the national debt are projected to exceed $1 trillion for the first time. Every basis point of yield increase compounds directly against that number, which is part of why this episode reads less like a normal rate-cycle move and more like what analysts have taken to calling, half-seriously, the return of the bond vigilante — investors selling government debt not because they doubt near-term Fed policy, but because they doubt the government's long-run fiscal discipline.

What's pushing the number

Two forces are doing the work. The first is energy. Brent crude has traded above $105 a barrel after Saudi Arabia shut its East-West pipeline — the principal overland route that lets Saudi crude bypass the Strait of Hormuz — following attacks in the region, and a tanker crew went missing after an attack in the same waters. Energy costs feed directly into headline inflation, and a market already nervous about sticky prices does not need much persuading that a Hormuz-adjacent supply shock will show up in the CPI print within a month or two.

The second is fiscal, and it is proving harder to manage. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has tried to talk yields down, at one point declaring he was now "the house" in the bond market and expanding Treasury's buyback program for longer-dated debt in a bid to cap borrowing costs. It has not worked. "I'm nervous, because Bessent failed to cap long-term Treasury yields," Tracy Chen, a portfolio manager at Brandywine Global, said this week. "The bond-market behavior shows that the bond vigilantes still don't believe him." Bessent has also argued investors are trading on "bad information" about the deficit picture — an assertion that, notably, has not moved the yield curve.

Warsh's test

Inside the Fed, the debate has been less unified than the futures pricing suggests. Governor Christopher Waller argued as recently as September 3 for patience, telling reporters, in a nod to John Lennon, to "give disinflation a chance," and noting the Fed "can wait one meeting" if incoming data cooperated. It didn't: the August CPI report ran hot enough to flip the consensus toward a hike, and TD Securities has revised its forecast to call for three increases in this cycle, with the next two expected in October and January.

Chair Warsh, five months into the job after taking office in May, has staked his early tenure on inflation-fighting credibility over accommodation. At a central-bank gathering earlier this year he was blunt about where the line sits: if markets believed the Fed would tolerate inflation running persistently above 2%, he said, "I guess they'd be disappointed. We're going to deliver price stability." Wednesday's dot plot — the anonymized grid of each policymaker's rate projections — will be read as much for what it implies about October and December as for today's decision itself. In June, nine of nineteen committee participants had penciled in at least one hike for 2026; six saw more than one. A hawkish revision of that grid would tell markets Warsh intends to keep pressing even after today's move.

The valuation reset

The practical transmission is already visible away from Wall Street. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate has jumped to 7.17%, its highest level since January 2025 and up 23 basis points in barely a week — on a $400,000 loan, roughly $185 more a month than borrowers faced in early July. Equities have followed a more muted but consistent path lower: the S&P 500 shed 0.58% and the Nasdaq 0.65% in Tuesday's session, with futures pointing to further declines ahead of the Fed decision.

The deeper issue for stocks is structural rather than a single day's move. The 10-year yield functions as the risk-free rate against which every other asset is priced, and a rate above 5% raises the discount applied to future corporate earnings — a mechanism that hits hardest the growth and technology names whose valuations depend most on cash flows many years out. Bonds, meanwhile, become more competitive with equities simply by offering a higher guaranteed return, a dynamic that has already begun showing up in relative fund flows this month.

What comes next

Wednesday's 2 p.m. decision is now close to a formality; the dot plot and Warsh's press conference are where the real signal sits. If the Fed hikes and the projections show a pause thereafter, long yields have room to stabilize. If the committee signals October and December moves to match TD Securities' three-hike call, the 5% threshold may prove a waypoint rather than a ceiling — with mortgage borrowers, growth-stock investors and, not least, the Treasury's own interest bill absorbing the difference.
