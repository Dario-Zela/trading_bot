Heathrow's third runway should hinge on airlines funding carbon removal, advisers say

A British Airways Airbus A380 lands at Heathrow, where a proposed third runway is at the centre of a clash between growth plans and climate law. — Krzysztof Kaczala / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
UK climate advisers say Heathrow's third runway can only proceed if airlines are legally required to fund carbon removal for their added emissions.

Britain's official climate advisers have told the government it cannot expand Heathrow Airport and still meet its legal climate targets — unless airlines are made to pay, in full, for the emissions a third runway would add.

The Climate Change Committee, the independent body that monitors UK progress toward net zero, said on Tuesday there is "no credible pathway" under current policy for Heathrow to grow while Britain holds to its 2050 net-zero target. Its chair, Nigel Topping, put it bluntly: "The government cannot expand Heathrow Airport without requiring the aviation industry to clean up its emissions."

That is not a rejection of the runway outright — it is a price tag. The committee wants legislation forcing airlines to cover the full cost of decarbonising their own growth, including buying credits for engineered carbon dioxide removal once cheaper options such as sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) run out of road. It is also calling for a 25-year national aviation decarbonisation strategy and contingency plans in case the underlying technology fails to scale.

Why the numbers don't add up yet

The problem, as the committee and outside researchers see it, is that the maths behind Heathrow expansion currently leans on technologies that barely exist. SAF still makes up under 1% of global aviation fuel. Engineered carbon removal — machines that pull CO2 out of the air for permanent storage — has produced fewer than 2 million tonnes worldwide to date, a rounding error against the tens of millions of tonnes a bigger Heathrow would need offset by mid-century. A separate analysis by the Tyndall Centre for Climate Change Research at the University of Manchester found UK aviation is already on course to exceed its share of the 2038-42 carbon budget by 21-50%, before a single extra runway is built. With a third runway, Heathrow's share of London's total 2050 emissions would rise from roughly 17% to around 30%, the Tyndall researchers estimated.

Dr Lois Pennington, the study's lead author, said sustainable fuels and carbon removal "can contribute to limiting aviation emissions, but there is currently no evidence that these technologies can support the scale of aviation expansion" the government envisages.

A government already committed

The advice lands awkwardly for Downing Street. Chancellor John Healey confirmed on 8 September that ministers had "given the go-ahead" to the £33bn project, which includes £1.5bn to reroute the M25 and would lift Heathrow's capacity to 756,000 flights and 150 million passengers a year. "After years of delay, years of indecision, years of often vexatious challenge, we have given the go-ahead to this project quite simply because the economic case stacks up," Healey said. Prime Minister Andy Burnham had previously criticised the scheme for pulling investment toward London and away from the north of England — a reversal that has already drawn accusations of a U-turn from opponents, including London mayor Sadiq Khan.

The committee's intervention effectively hands ministers a second decision to make on top of the planning one: whether to write airline liability for full decarbonisation into law, which would functionally set a price floor under aviation growth, or approve the runway on the current voluntary footing and risk breaching the UK's binding carbon budgets outright.

What happens next

Heathrow is due to submit its formal Development Consent Order in 2027, with a final planning decision possible by 2029 — the same year Healey has pledged to have "spades in the ground." Whether the aviation legislation the CCC wants materialises before then, and how it prices carbon removal liability, will determine whether airlines treat net zero as a real constraint on capacity or a cost to be negotiated down later.
