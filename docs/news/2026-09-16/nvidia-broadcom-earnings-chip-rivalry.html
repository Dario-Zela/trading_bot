Broadcom's custom-chip surge sharpens challenge to Nvidia as both post blowout results

Broadcom CEO Hock Tan, whose company's custom-silicon business now drives more than half its revenue. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Broadcom's AI chip revenue jumped 221% on custom hyperscaler silicon, even as Nvidia and AMD posted their own record quarters.

Three of the world's biggest chipmakers posted blockbuster earnings within weeks of each other this quarter, and the market read all three the same way: fine, but Broadcom's numbers said something the others didn't. Buried in an otherwise familiar beat-and-raise report was a growth rate that has no real precedent in the semiconductor industry's history — and it wasn't coming from the company most investors associate with the AI boom.

Broadcom's AI semiconductor revenue hit $16.7 billion in its fiscal third quarter, up 221% from a year earlier and 54% from the prior quarter alone. That business — chips designed to order for a handful of the largest technology companies on earth — now accounts for 56% of everything Broadcom sells. CEO Hock Tan, never a man given to hedging, told analysts on the September 9 call that "Q3 demand was simply hot and we're just getting started," then backed it up with numbers: Broadcom now expects roughly $115 billion in AI revenue for fiscal 2027 and $230 billion by fiscal 2028.

A different kind of chip business

What Broadcom sells isn't a GPU. Nvidia builds general-purpose accelerators that any customer can buy off a price list and deploy for almost any AI workload. Broadcom instead designs application-specific integrated circuits — XPUs, in the industry's shorthand — tailored to a single customer's own model architecture, built in collaboration with that customer's engineers, and sold to essentially nobody else. It is a slower, more bespoke business, but one with a structural advantage once it's won: switching costs that run into the billions of dollars and years of joint engineering.

Six hyperscale customers anchor that book today, including Google, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. The Google relationship alone is described internally as worth "multi-tens of billions of dollars of TPUs annually." In June, OpenAI and Broadcom unveiled Jalapeño, an inference-optimized chip taken from design to manufacturing tape-out in nine months — a deliberate move by OpenAI to reduce its dependence on Nvidia GPUs for the workloads it runs constantly, at enormous scale, in production. Meta has gone further still, signing a multi-year, multi-generation agreement with Broadcom to co-develop what would be the industry's first 2-nanometer AI accelerator, extending its existing MTIA chip line through 2029. Anthropic, meanwhite, has been in talks with Samsung's foundry business about building a chip of its own from the ground up.

None of this is speculative anymore. It is now sitting inside Broadcom's income statement, and it explains why the company briefly crossed a $2 trillion market capitalization this month, overtaking Tesla in the process.

Nvidia and AMD aren't standing still

The custom-silicon story only matters because it's unfolding while Nvidia is also having its best quarter in memory. The company reported $96.2 billion in revenue for its fiscal second quarter, up 106% year-over-year, with data-center revenue alone reaching a record $89 billion, up 117%, driven by the ramp of its Blackwell Ultra platform. Nvidia guided to roughly $108 billion in revenue for the current quarter. Those are not the numbers of a company losing its footing.

AMD's turnaround has been just as real, if smaller in scale. Its data-center segment brought in $6.7 billion in the June quarter, more than doubling year-over-year and flipping from a $155 million operating loss to $2.1 billion in operating income, powered by its Instinct MI350 GPUs. Management is guiding for the second half of the year to accelerate further.

The market's reaction to all three reports in the same week was telling. When Broadcom released its own results on September 2, its stock fell roughly 6% — not because the AI number disappointed, but because fourth-quarter revenue guidance of $34.8 billion came in just under the $35 billion Wall Street wanted, and gross margin compressed to 73% from 78% a year earlier as the custom-chip mix, which carries thinner margins than Broadcom's networking and software businesses, grew heavier. Nvidia's stock actually rose about 1% that same day, with traders reading Broadcom's disclosure of a $350 billion two-year hyperscaler order book as confirmation that AI infrastructure spending broadly is not slowing — a rising tide, not a zero-sum fight. AMD slipped roughly 2% on read-across concerns about margin compression across the sector.

What the split verdict means

That divergence is the real story here, more than any single earnings beat. Investors are no longer treating "AI chips" as a single trade. They are pricing Nvidia for what it still overwhelmingly is — the default, general-purpose supplier that captures the largest share of AI capital expenditure by a wide margin — while pricing Broadcom for a narrower but faster-growing role as the contractor of choice for the handful of companies large enough to justify building silicon around their own workloads. AMD sits in between: a credible second source of merchant GPUs, gaining share, but not yet reshaping the buying pattern the way custom silicon is.

The deeper shift is in who is doing the buying. Google, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic are not hedging against Nvidia because Nvidia's chips are inferior — they are hedging against the economics of running trillion-parameter models at planetary scale on hardware they don't control and can't optimize for their own architectures. That calculus doesn't require Nvidia to falter. It only requires the biggest customers to have both the balance sheets and the incentive to build alternatives, which they increasingly do.

The next test comes when Broadcom next updates the durability of that $350 billion order book, and when Nvidia reports its fiscal third quarter in late November — the first full quarter in which Wall Street will be watching not just Nvidia's own growth rate, but whether the pace of hyperscaler custom-silicon deployment is starting to show up as a drag on it.
