Oil tops $105 as Saudi Arabia shuts Hormuz-bypass pipeline and tanker crew go missing

A very large crude carrier (VLCC) of the type that plies the Strait of Hormuz and Red Sea export routes. — Eric Gao / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Saudi Arabia's shutdown of its Hormuz-bypass pipeline, hit by drone strikes, has erased the market's last spare export route just as tanker attacks in the Strait intensify.

In one sentence: Saudi Arabia's shutdown of its Hormuz-bypass pipeline, after drone strikes on its pumping stations, has stripped out the last spare capacity in a market already rattled by tanker attacks and a missing crew.

Brent crude traded above $107 a barrel and West Texas Intermediate above $103 on Tuesday, levels not seen since the spring, after Saudi Arabia confirmed it had shut the East-West pipeline — the one route that lets its crude reach world markets without passing through the Strait of Hormuz. The closure followed multiple drone strikes, launched from Iraq, that hit pumping stations along the line on September 10 and 11. Riyadh called the shutdown "precautionary." Markets read it as something closer to structural: the safety valve that has quietly underpinned oil-supply planning since the 1980s is now gone, at the exact moment the primary route it was built to bypass is also under fire.

A pipeline built for exactly this moment

The East-West pipeline, known within the industry as the Petroline, has carried Saudi crude 1,200 kilometres from the Abqaiq processing hub to the Red Sea terminal at Yanbu since 1981, built for the specific purpose of keeping exports flowing if the Strait of Hormuz ever became too dangerous to use. Its timing could not be worse. Satellite imagery released by Vantor and distributed via Getty shows extensive fire damage and blackened ground around at least one pumping station — Saudi officials say repairs could take months. At full capacity the line can move 7 million barrels a day; it had been running at 4 to 5 million bpd, doing the exact job it was designed for since the Strait of Hormuz crisis began.

That crisis dates to February 28, when the United States and Israel launched an air campaign against Iran. Tehran's response was to effectively close the Strait — a waterway that in normal times carries roughly a fifth of the world's crude and LNG — laying mines, boarding vessels and threatening any ship that tried to pass. A ceasefire in April and a further understanding in June restored some traffic, but flows have stayed well below pre-war norms: the strait was moving 6 to 9 million barrels a day as of this month, against more than 20 million before the war. The East-West pipeline was the pressure valve that let Saudi Arabia keep exporting anyway. With that valve closed, Aramco has already begun notifying European customers that a number of September cargoes will not be delivered.

The tanker still missing two of its crew

The pipeline news landed three days after a separate attack that has kept the Strait itself in headlines. The Panama-flagged tanker El Gaia caught fire in its engine room roughly 103 nautical miles northeast of Musandam on September 13, prompting an Omani naval vessel to evacuate the bulk of its crew. Oman's Maritime Security Centre says two crew members remain unaccounted for as the tanker is towed toward an Omani port. The cause is disputed: Iran's Revolutionary Guard says the ship struck a naval mine after attempting to cross a stretch of the strait it had declared forbidden; U.S. Central Command rejects that account, saying El Gaia was hit by an Iranian missile last month and then a drone over the weekend. It is at least the second tanker attacked in the waterway since Saturday.

Whichever version holds up, the incident underscores what traders have been pricing for months — that Hormuz is not a route so much as a running casualty count, one that governments on both sides continue to dispute even as ships burn. Goldman Sachs has already flagged the scenario now unfolding: the bank's commodities team warned this month that Brent could climb above $120 in 2027 if Gulf output stays roughly 4 million barrels a day below pre-war levels, calling "more intense shipping attacks in Hormuz and the Red Sea" the most likely path to that outcome.

One chokepoint, not two

The two stories are really one story told from opposite ends of the same 1,200 kilometres of pipe. For seven months, Saudi Arabia's ability to route crude around the Strait of Hormuz has been the reason global supply hasn't fully seized up despite the war next door — the reason Brent traded in the $80s and $90s rather than the triple digits investors feared in March. That redundancy is now gone, not by policy choice but because drones reached the pipeline itself. The practical effect is the same regardless of intent: the world's spare capacity for routing around Hormuz has collapsed to whatever the strait itself can carry, and the strait is where ships are currently catching fire.

Crude eased slightly Tuesday, down about 1% intraday as WTI slipped to roughly $104.68, but that read as noise against a four-month high rather than a turn in direction. The next catalysts are concrete: how long Aramco needs to restore the East-West line, whether Oman's search for El Gaia's missing crew resolves before the story fades from front pages, and whether the ceasefire architecture from April and June has enough left in it to stop a second escalation. Until one of those breaks decisively, the market has one route left, and it's the one currently under attack.
