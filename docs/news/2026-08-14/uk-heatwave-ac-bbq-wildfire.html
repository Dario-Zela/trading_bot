UK heatwave drives air-conditioning search boom and calls to ban disposable BBQs

A wall-mounted air conditioning unit — a fixture becoming a selling point in UK property listings — Kskhh / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
AC-home searches on Rightmove have doubled this summer as buyers price cooling into UK property, while wildfires match last year's record and Burnham pushes a voluntary BBQ retail ban.

On the final scorching afternoon of the UK's fifth heatwave of the summer, the property portal Rightmove has a number that says more about where British housing is heading than any temperature reading: searches for homes with air conditioning have risen 104% between May and July compared with the same period last year — effectively doubling.

It is a small, precise signal of a much larger shift. In a Rightmove survey of just over a thousand adults, 87% said a home's ability to stay cool in summer now matters when choosing where to live next; only 11% described their current home as very comfortable in hot weather, while 45% said it struggles to cope at all. Supply has not caught up with that appetite — homes for sale or rent with air conditioning still make up less than 1% of UK listings — which is precisely the kind of scarcity that tends to show up in asking prices once buyers start treating cooling as a baseline feature rather than a luxury import from warmer climates.

Friday brought the heatwave's last gasp — up to 33C in the southeast, with thundery breakdowns already moving through Wales and northern England. The Met Office expects a sharper cooldown by the weekend, with London highs falling to around 28C on Saturday and fresher air reaching the southeast by Sunday. But this is the fifth heatwave of the year, not the first, and estates agents report that buyers are no longer pricing homes for the weather they remember — they are pricing them for the weather they now expect every summer.

Retailers asked to stand down

The same heat has revived a familiar argument about disposable barbecues. England and Wales have recorded 1,017 wildfires so far in 2026, matching the whole of last year's record total, according to the National Fire Chiefs Council. Andy Burnham, who chaired an emergency Cobra meeting on the crisis earlier this month, has renewed his call for retailers to voluntarily pull single-use grills from shelves: "We would say to retailers do not put disposable barbecues on sale at this moment in time."

That request leans on a voluntary framework agreed between fire chiefs and the British Retail Consortium back in 2023, under which major chains suspend sales once an extreme heat event is declared imminent — not a statutory ban. Burnham has said ministers will look at a permanent, legally binding restriction during summer months, but for now enforcement rests on retailers choosing to comply, and a public petition for a UK-wide ban stays open for signatures until 15 December.

Both threads point the same direction. Where markets can price climate risk — through a home's listing, its air conditioning, its resale value — they are already doing so, fast and visibly. Where the response depends on public bodies asking companies nicely to change their stock, it moves slower and less certainly. The next test of that gap comes with the weekend cooldown: whether wildfire numbers plateau once the heat breaks, or whether disposable barbecues are still on supermarket shelves the next time a heatwave — the sixth of the year, or the first of 2027 — arrives.
