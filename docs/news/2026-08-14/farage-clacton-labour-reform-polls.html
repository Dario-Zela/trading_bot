Farage holds Clacton on low turnout as fresh polling shows Labour edging ahead of Reform

Nigel Farage retained Clacton on 13 August 2026 with 63.3% of the vote, but turnout fell to 44.4% from 58.0% in 2024. — UK Parliament / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Farage held Clacton on a turnout down 13.6 points from 2024, even as Ipsos, Opinium and JL Partners put Labour narrowly back ahead of Reform UK nationally — though YouGov only flipped this week.

Nigel Farage spent Thursday doing what he has done in Clacton since 2024: winning comfortably. He took 22,239 votes to 63.3% of the poll, more than double the 9,455 cast for Count Binface, the satirical bin-costumed candidate who ran him closest. But the number that mattered most to analysts by Friday morning was not the margin — it was the turnout. Just 44.4% of registered voters in the Essex seaside seat bothered to cast a ballot, down from 58.0% at the 2024 general election, a 13.6-point collapse that undercuts any claim that Thursday's result carries a fresh mandate.

The by-election landed the same week that three separate pollsters — Ipsos, Opinium and JL Partners — put Labour narrowly back ahead of Reform UK nationally for the first time in more than a year. A fourth, YouGov, had Reform still in front as recently as 2–3 August, then flipped to Labour by 9–10 August. Taken together, the two stories describe the same underlying fact from different angles: Reform's momentum, which looked unstoppable a year ago, is no longer moving in one direction — and nobody, including the pollsters, agrees on exactly where it now stands.

A landslide with a shrinking electorate

Farage triggered the contest himself, resigning his seat in July after questions surfaced over a £5m cryptocurrency donation to Reform UK, which he dismissed as an "establishment smear" before inviting Clacton voters to render their own verdict. Labour and the Conservatives declined to contest it at all, treating the whole exercise as a stunt designed to distract from the standards inquiry rather than a genuine electoral test. That left Count Binface — a novelty candidate who has previously stood against Boris Johnson and Theresa May — as the de facto opposition, among a record 34 names on the ballot, the most in any UK parliamentary contest since 2008.

Clacton is fertile ground for Farage regardless of the day's turnout: a retiree-heavy, 96%-White Tendring peninsula seat where Jaywick has repeatedly ranked as England's most deprived neighbourhood and economic inactivity runs at more than double the national rate. It backed Leave overwhelmingly in 2016 and swung to Reform by 45 points in 2024. None of that changed on Thursday. What did change is the read on his standing outside it. "The UK's politics have never been so fragmented, so there's no precedent for any of what we're seeing now," the polling expert Sir John Curtice said ahead of the vote — a comment aimed as much at the national picture as at Clacton itself.

The Burnham effect

That national picture has moved fast. Keir Starmer quit as Labour leader and prime minister on 22 June, four days after his party lost the Makerfield by-election, itself won by Andy Burnham — the former Greater Manchester mayor who was acclaimed unopposed as Labour leader on 17 July with the backing of 94% of the parliamentary party, and sworn in as prime minister on 20 July. His government's opening act was a VAT cut on energy bills aimed squarely at the cost-of-living pressure that had fuelled Reform's rise.

The effect shows up consistently, if not identically, across the polls. Ipsos's fieldwork of 30 July–4 August put Labour on 28% (up four points on June) against Reform's 25%, the party's best showing in that pollster's series for over a year. "These findings confirm Labour are benefiting from changing their leader," said Gideon Skinner, Ipsos's senior director of politics — though he cautioned that "the Labour party itself is still less liked than it has been for most of the last 20 years." Opinium (5 August) had it tighter, 27–25, as did JL Partners, which described its own result as Labour's strongest position since November 2024. Reform, which was polling as high as 35% roughly a year ago and once led the chasing pack by 12 points, is now within striking distance of a different indignity: More in Common's early-August survey put the Conservatives just two points behind Reform for third place.

No consensus, no clean trade

Yet the same fortnight that produced those figures also produced YouGov's reversal — Reform 23%, Labour 22% on 2–3 August; Labour 24%, Reform 22% a week later, a two-point gap YouGov's own elections team called "not statistically conclusive." With Ipsos's Labour lead sitting within a stated four-point margin of error, and pollsters disagreeing on whether Reform or Labour is even in first place, the underlying signal is genuinely noisy rather than merely close.

That matters beyond Westminster. Sterling has traded around $1.35 this week, near the top of its 30-day range, but currency desks attribute the move chiefly to this month's UK growth and inflation data rather than to politics — a sign that FX and political-risk models have, for now, simply set the polling volatility aside rather than resolved it. That is a temporary fix, not a stable one. A political-risk premium needs a baseline to price against, and when three parties sit within a handful of points of each other and pollsters can't agree on their order, hedging models are left holding scenarios rather than a forecast.

The next test comes fast: further voting-intention releases from YouGov, Ipsos and Opinium through late August, and then the Autumn Budget, Burnham's first serious fiscal set-piece as prime minister, which will show whether the VAT cut and the wider cost-of-living push can convert a polling bounce into something more durable — or whether, once by-election season's convenient boycotts and 44% turnouts give way to a full national contest, Reform's underlying floor turns out to be sturdier than August's numbers suggest.
