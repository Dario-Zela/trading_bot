Houthis claim second drone strike on Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery in a week

In one sentence
Houthis hit Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery for a third time in three weeks, undercutting a defence pact Riyadh, Ankara and Islamabad signed days earlier.

Yemen's Houthi movement said its fighters struck Saudi Aramco's Jazan refinery with drones for a second time in less than a week, an escalation that lands squarely on the doorstep of a defence pact Saudi Arabia signed with Turkey and Pakistan barely six days earlier. The Houthi military spokesman, Brigadier General Yahya Saree, said the strike on Thursday was retaliation for what he called Saudi drone incursions into the airspace of Saada and Hajjah, two Houthi-held provinces near the Yemeni-Saudi border. Saudi authorities had not confirmed the claim by the time this article was filed.

The target matters as much as the timing. Jazan is a 400,000-barrel-a-day refinery on Saudi Arabia's Red Sea coast — a piece of the kingdom's energy infrastructure that had gone untouched by direct Houthi fire for four years until late July. That it has now been hit three times in three weeks, with the two most recent strikes arriving within days of Riyadh, Ankara and Islamabad putting their names to a mutual-defence treaty, is the kind of coincidence that reads like a message.

A refinery that won't stay online

The pattern began on July 25, when Houthi forces combined ballistic missiles and drones in a coordinated assault on Aramco facilities at Jazan and Yanbu — the first direct hit on Saudi energy infrastructure since 2022. That strike damaged Jazan's Integrated Gasification Combined Cycle complex, which supplies the plant's power, steam and hydrogen, along with part of its tank farm; roughly a tenth of stored fuel at the site was affected. The refinery has been offline since July 27.

Aramco had hoped to restart the plant by August 15. A second drone strike on August 9 — two days after the Mecca signing ceremony — forced that date back to August 30, according to an alert from industry monitor IIR cited by Reuters. Thursday's attack, described by Houthi-aligned Sabaa News Agency as retaliation for "Saudi violations of Yemeni airspace and sovereignty," threatens to push the timeline further still. Each additional week offline removes a meaningful slice of diesel and gasoline supply from a market that customers in East Africa and South Asia lean on, at a moment when Middle East refining capacity is already strained by the aftermath of the Iran war and Ukrainian strikes on Russian refineries.

An alliance's first test

The strikes' proximity to the Mecca Joint Defence Agreement is not incidental to why this story matters beyond Yemen's border. On August 7, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif signed a NATO-style pact in the holy city, declaring that "any armed attack against any one of the three states shall be regarded as an attack against them all." It built on a bilateral Saudi-Pakistan defence agreement from September 2025 and drew Turkey — NATO's second-largest military — into the fold alongside Pakistan's nuclear arsenal and Saudi Arabia's oil wealth. Combined, the three countries field roughly 1.4 million active troops, 3,400 aircraft and more than 340 naval vessels, backed by a combined defence budget near $124bn a year.

The pact was framed, explicitly, as deterrence against exactly this kind of threat — a message aimed as much at Iran, the Houthis' principal patron, as at any single attacker. Instead, the ink was barely dry before Jazan was hit again, and then again. Nothing in the agreement's text commits its signatories to intervene against sub-state actors like the Houthis, and Riyadh has so far responded with the same toolkit it used before the pact existed: limited strikes on Houthi positions, diplomatic protests, and repair crews. That gap between what the alliance promises in principle and what it delivers against a low-cost drone campaign is precisely the credibility question now hanging over it.

Markets already pricing the risk

Oil prices had been climbing through the second week of August on a mix of factors — lingering uncertainty over whether the Strait of Hormuz would reopen fully, and Iran's list of conditions for a peace settlement with Washington — with Brent crude touching nearly $89 a barrel on August 12 before easing back toward $87 as demand concerns crept back in. The Jazan strikes are a secondary but real contributor to that risk premium: they demonstrate that Houthi forces can reach a major Saudi refinery repeatedly despite the kingdom's air defences, expanding the list of assets traders have to worry about beyond the Strait of Hormuz chokepoint itself.

Jazan alone is not large enough to move global crude benchmarks on its own — 400,000 barrels a day is a rounding error against roughly 100 million barrels of daily global consumption. But refined-product markets are tighter and more regional than crude markets, and repeated outages at a single plant compound faster than one-off shocks. Every week Jazan stays down adds pressure to diesel and gasoline cracks already elevated by refinery losses elsewhere in the region.

What comes next

The immediate marker is August 30, Aramco's latest stated restart date for Jazan — a date that has already slipped once and may slip again if strikes continue. Beyond the refinery itself, the sharper question is whether Riyadh, Ankara and Islamabad translate their new pact into a visible joint response, or whether it remains, for now, a paper commitment tested and found wanting in its first three weeks. Iran's ongoing negotiations with Washington over a wider settlement will also shape whether the Houthis have reason to de-escalate — or further incentive to keep proving that Saudi energy infrastructure remains reachable.
