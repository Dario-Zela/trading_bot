White House tariff crackdown widens as court backs power to end de minimis exemption

Stacked shipping containers at port — the physical layer beneath the tariff-evasion allegations and the de minimis ruling. — Unsplash / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A White House report alleges a $75bn scheme let China dodge tariffs via 40+ countries, as a court cleared the way to end duty-free treatment on small imports.

Two developments landed within a day of each other this week, and together they sketch the real shape of the White House's tariff campaign: aggressive on paper, leaky in practice. On Wednesday, the administration's trade office published a report accusing more than 40 countries of helping China route roughly $75 billion in goods around US tariffs. On Thursday, a federal trade court removed one of the last legal obstacles to a separate piece of the enforcement push — the end of duty-free treatment for low-value packages. Read side by side, they say less about policy direction, which has been clear for months, than about how much of that policy is actually collecting at the border.

The $75 billion workaround

The report, titled "The Great Transshipment Scam" and produced by the White House Office of Trade and Manufacturing Policy, alleges that Chinese exporters have spent the past year relabeling and rerouting goods through third countries to strip out their Chinese origin before the final leg into the United States. A component made in Guangdong gets shipped to a plant in Vietnam, Mexico or elsewhere, undergoes minimal processing, and re-emerges with a new country-of-origin stamp — and a tariff bill that reflects the new label, not the old one.

The administration puts the value of goods moved this way between February 2025 and February 2026 at $75 billion, translating into an estimated $19 billion to $34 billion in tariff revenue that never reached the Treasury. The list of alleged enablers is long — Mexico, Canada and the European Union feature alongside India, Japan and South Korea — which is itself notable, since several of those economies are nominal US trade partners rather than adversaries. "For years the 'great transshipment scam' has let communist China launder its exports to more than 40 countries, rob our treasury of tens of billions of dollars and steal the paychecks of American workers," said Peter Navarro, the White House's senior trade counselor. He was careful to redirect blame outward: "This isn't about China … This is about the 40-plus countries that are enabling the transshipping." The report's fix is a proposed AI-driven customs screening system, dubbed a "detective border," meant to flag suspicious routing patterns before goods clear entry — an acknowledgment, in itself, that current screening isn't catching the volume the administration says is moving.

A court clears the way, but not the fight

The second thread runs through a Manhattan courtroom. The Court of International Trade ruled Thursday that the president lawfully ended the de minimis exemption — the rule that let shipments under $800 enter duty-free — ahead of the July 2027 date Congress had set when it separately voted to repeal the exemption. The challenge came from Detroit Axle, a Michigan auto-parts distributor that runs a distribution center in Juárez, Mexico, sourcing parts from Chinese manufacturers and shipping them to US customers in small, previously tariff-free batches. The company argued that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, the statute the administration invoked, gave the president authority to impose new tariffs — not to unilaterally rescind an exemption Congress had already scheduled on its own timeline.

A three-judge panel disagreed, drawing a distinction between imposing a tariff and revoking what it called a trade-related "privilege." That distinction matters because it survives the Supreme Court's ruling in February that struck down IEEPA-based tariffs outright — the de minimis case threads a narrower needle, and the panel found room for the White House to keep this particular tool. Detroit Axle can still appeal to the Federal Circuit, so the legal question isn't fully closed. But for now, low-value shipments — the volume trade that fed fast-fashion platforms, small parts distributors and direct-to-consumer importers — face duties they didn't carry a year ago.

Where enforcement and evasion meet

The throughline is that the administration is simultaneously widening its legal authority to tax imports and admitting, in its own report, that a meaningful share of trade is slipping past the tariffs already in place. For traders, that gap matters more than the headline rate. A tariff schedule that isn't fully enforced doesn't translate cleanly into higher landed costs for trade-sensitive sectors — retail, autos, consumer electronics — because the effective rate paid by importers running transshipment routes is lower than the posted one. That's a live variable for anyone pricing in tariff pass-through to margins.

The near-term catalysts are concrete. Detroit Axle's Federal Circuit appeal will determine whether the de minimis ruling holds, with a decision unlikely before next year. Separately, watch whether Customs and Border Protection moves from report to rulemaking — the "detective border" system remains a proposal, not a deployed tool, and its funding and legal authority to flag origin fraud at scale are both unresolved. Until enforcement catches up with the stated policy, the gap between the two is where the actual tariff rate — and the actual revenue — gets decided.
