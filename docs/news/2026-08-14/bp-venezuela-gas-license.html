BP secures Venezuela offshore gas license alongside ADNOC's XRG and UCC

A BP-operated offshore gas platform in the North Sea; BP will operate the new Loran Phase 2 license off Venezuela in similar fashion, with two equal partners. — Alnitak3 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
BP will operate a new Venezuelan gas license with ADNOC's XRG and Qatar's UCC, a deal made possible only by January's ouster of Maduro and April's US sanctions relief.

BP has been awarded a license to operate the second phase of the Loran offshore gas field in Venezuelan waters, splitting the project equally with two partners making their first entrance into the country: XRG, the international investment arm of Abu Dhabi's ADNOC, and UCC Oil and Gas Holding, a unit of Qatar's UCC Holding conglomerate.

The license, awarded August 13, covers gas resources estimated at more than 4 trillion cubic feet. BP will operate the project, with each of the three companies holding an equal working interest. It is a modest transaction by the standards of global energy deals — but it lands at a moment when Venezuela's oil and gas sector is being reshaped faster than at any point in two decades, which is the part of the story worth dwelling on.

Why now

BP spent the 2000s and 2010s selling down its Venezuelan positions — offloading upstream and midstream assets to TNK-BP in 2011, then more recently agreeing to hand a stake in the cross-border Cocuina-Manakin gas field to Trinidad's National Gas Company. Western majors broadly retreated as U.S. sanctions tightened around the Maduro government and expropriation risk made Venezuelan assets difficult to finance or insure.

That calculus changed abruptly this year. U.S. forces captured Nicolás Maduro in Caracas on January 3 and flew him to New York to face narco-terrorism charges. The Trump administration opted to work with Maduro's former vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, who became acting president and has spent the months since courting international capital. Washington removed her from the sanctions list in April, and the Treasury Department issued a broad authorization in March allowing state oil company PDVSA to sell crude directly to U.S. buyers. BP signed a memorandum of understanding with Rodríguez's government in April covering the Plataforma Deltana area, of which Loran is part; last week's license is the first concrete output of that agreement.

The Trinidad angle

Loran is not a standalone Venezuelan project — it is one flank of the Loran-Manatee accumulation, which straddles the maritime boundary with Trinidad and Tobago and holds an estimated 10 Tcf combined, with roughly 7.3 Tcf on the Venezuelan side. Shell is separately developing the Trinidadian portion, Manatee, and has upgraded the export pipeline to carry gas to Trinidad's Beachfield facility ahead of a planned 2027 start.

The reason that matters commercially: Trinidad's Atlantic LNG plant, the largest liquefaction facility in Latin America, has been running below capacity for years because feedstock gas has dried up, and downstream fertiliser and petrochemical plants on the island have faced periodic rationing. New Venezuelan supply from Loran Phase 2, developed alongside the already-sanctioned Phase 1, would help refill that pipeline — one of the more concrete demand signals behind the deal, rather than a purely speculative bet on Venezuela's reopening.

What BP and its partners said

Mohamed Al Aryani, XRG's international gas president, framed the move as part of a broader push into transatlantic gas positions that already include the U.S., Egypt, Azerbaijan and Mozambique: "Venezuela holds significant gas resources and has the potential to play a greater role in meeting regional and international energy demand... our entry through Loran reflects XRG's strategy to invest with partners in advantaged resources with access to established infrastructure and clear routes to market."

Meg O'Neill, who took over as BP chief executive on April 1 after nine years running Australia's Woodside Energy, has made the Venezuelan re-entry an early marker of her tenure — a notable choice for an executive who has otherwise signalled a pullback from BP's prior low-carbon commitments in favour of hydrocarbons. BP's regional head, David Campbell, is also overseeing a separate exploration-stage agreement covering the Carúpano East block.

What's still unresolved

BP itself flagged the caveat that matters most: the transaction remains subject to "definitive license and development arrangements, government and regulatory approvals, and applicable international sanctions and compliance requirements." Rodríguez's government is not a democratically elected one, and Washington's decision to lift sanctions on her personally is a policy choice that could be reversed as easily as it was made. The equal three-way split across a UAE state-linked investor, a Qatari conglomerate and a UK major also reads as deliberate risk diversification — spreading exposure to a jurisdiction where the rules could still change.

The next marker is Phase 1 development progress alongside this new license, and whether Shell's 2027 Manatee start-up on the Trinidadian side actually materialises — the clearest test of whether Venezuelan offshore gas becomes a reliable regional supply source or remains a bet on a political transition that is still less than a year old.
