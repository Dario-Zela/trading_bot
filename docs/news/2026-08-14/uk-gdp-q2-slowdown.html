UK GDP growth slows to 0.4% in second quarter

The City of London and Canary Wharf, financial heart of an economy now leaning almost entirely on services for growth. — Mewiki / Wikimedia Commons (public domain)
In one sentence
UK GDP grew 0.4% in Q2, down from 0.6%, as services carried the economy while production stalled — manufacturing gains wiped out by falling utilities output.

Britain's economy grew 0.4% in the second quarter, the Office for National Statistics said Thursday, a step down from the 0.6% pace recorded in the first three months of the year. Economists had expected exactly this number, so the headline surprised no one. What's underneath it is the more interesting story.

One sector did almost all the work. Services output rose 0.5% in the quarter — slower than its 0.8% pace in Q1, but still enough to carry the entire economy on its back. Production, by contrast, flatlined: zero growth, after a 0.2% rise in the prior quarter. Construction added 0.3%, a bright spot, though the sector remains 2% smaller than it was a year ago.

"Growth slowed in the second quarter of the year, following a strong start to 2026, but remained relatively robust," said Liz McKeown, the ONS's director of economic statistics. "Services were once again the main driver of growth, while production was broadly unchanged and construction also grew."

A one-sector economy

Strip out services and the UK's growth engine barely turns over. Information and communication output jumped 2.7% in the quarter; professional, scientific and technical activities rose 1.7%. Consumer-facing services — restaurants, retail, leisure — grew a more modest 0.3%, a gain economists partly credit to an unusually warm quarter and the football World Cup, which pulled forward hospitality and retail spending that may not repeat. Suren Thiru, chief economist at the ICAEW, said much of the quarter's strength reflected "temporary factors, including Iran war-driven stockpiling by businesses, unusually warm weather and the World Cup, rather than genuine momentum," and warned that a more painful deceleration is likely in the third quarter as higher inflation and energy costs squeeze household incomes.

George Brown, senior economist at Schroders, offered a similar read: seasonal quirks, he suspects, have flattered activity through the first half of the year, and growth is likely to lose steam as 2026 wears on.

The month-by-month data back up the case for caution. April contracted 0.1%, May was revised down to flat from an initial 0.1% gain, and only June's 0.3% jump kept the quarter in positive territory — a pattern of a soft start rescued by a single strong month, not a steadily accelerating economy.

Where production actually broke down

The flat headline production number conceals a sharper divide. Manufacturing output rose a solid 1.0% and mining and quarrying added 0.2% — but those gains were wiped out by a 2.3% fall in electricity, gas, steam and air-conditioning supply and a 3.7% drop in water supply and waste management. In other words, factories built more, but utilities produced and delivered less. That points toward energy-cost and infrastructure strain rather than a simple loss of industrial demand, and it is the kind of drag that doesn't show up cleanly in a single GDP print but tends to persist quarter after quarter.

The Bank's dilemma

The slowdown lands at an awkward moment for the Bank of England. Its Monetary Policy Committee left Bank Rate unchanged at 3.75% when it met in early July, but the vote was split — three of nine members wanted to raise it, a sign that inflation concerns have not gone away even as growth cools. A weaker GDP trajectory would normally argue for patience or cuts; persistent price pressure argues the opposite. That tension is likely to define the MPC's next several meetings.

Sterling's reaction on the day was muted — GBP/USD barely moved, trading around 1.3495 — suggesting markets had already priced in a headline figure in line with consensus and are waiting for clearer signals on where the Bank leans next. Gilt yields ticked up modestly, a small tell that fixed-income traders are weighing the inflation side of the ledger more heavily than the growth slowdown for now.

The next test comes with the monthly GDP release for July, plus revised industrial production figures that should clarify whether the utilities-driven production drag was a one-quarter anomaly or the start of a trend. Treasury modelling has already flagged a bleaker scenario: if disruption tied to the Iran war continues to weigh on the Strait of Hormuz through the end of 2026, growth next year could slow to as little as 0.3%. For now, the UK economy is still expanding — but on one engine, not two, and the engine that stalled is the one that keeps the lights and factories running.
