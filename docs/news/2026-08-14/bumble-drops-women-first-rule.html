Bumble scraps signature 'women-first' chat rule, dividing users

Bumble founder and CEO Whitney Wolfe Herd, whose company has now abandoned the messaging rule it was built on. — Steve Jennings / TechCrunch, via Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Bumble has scrapped the women-message-first rule that defined it since 2014, letting anyone open a chat as revenue and paying users slide.

Bumble spent twelve years selling a single idea: on this dating app, women message first. It was the rule that separated Bumble from Tinder, the pitch that took founder Whitney Wolfe Herd from a Tinder co-founder with a harassment lawsuit behind her to the world's youngest self-made female billionaire when Bumble went public in 2021. On 11 August, the company switched it off. Anyone — man or woman, in a straight match — can now send the first message.

The company frames this as evolution rather than retreat. "Being women-first was never about prescribing just one way to connect," Wolfe Herd said in the announcement. "It was about designing an experience with women's needs in mind." The immediate justification is survey data: Bumble says 66% of women polled would rather men make the first move, and 62% of members felt more confident with greater choice over how a conversation starts. Alongside the change, the company stretched the window to reply to an opener from 24 hours to 72.

A brand identity meets a balance sheet

The timing is hard to separate from Bumble's numbers. Second-quarter revenue came in at $210.5 million, down 15.2% year-on-year, and paying users fell 16.4% to roughly 3.2 million — a loss of about 620,000 subscribers in a single year. Full-year 2025 revenue had already dropped nearly 10%, to around $966 million, and the stock has shed close to half its value over the past twelve months. Reuters reported in June that Bumble was working with Morgan Stanley to explore strategic alternatives, though no deal was confirmed. Against that backdrop, the founding rule that once generated headlines and app-store downloads had become, by the company's own account, a friction point: fewer messages sent, fewer conversations started, fewer reasons for women to open the app at all.

Bumble had already been loosening the model. Its 2024 "Opening Moves" feature let women post a question men could answer to break the ice without composing a line from scratch — a soft acknowledgment that the original mechanic asked a lot of users on a bad-dating-app day. Executives have also floated swipe-free formats, in-person events and AI matching tools as they try to arrest what the industry broadly calls swipe fatigue, a problem hitting Match Group's Tinder and Hinge just as hard.

The reaction split roughly along the line Bumble's own data predicted. Some users, especially those citing the mental load of always messaging first, welcomed the change. Others said it erases the one feature that made the app worth choosing over Tinder, and some women flagged a familiar worry: that the 72-hour window and open messaging simply reopen the door to the unsolicited-message problem Bumble was built to close.

Bumble reports full third-quarter results in November, the first window to see whether scrapping its founding rule moves the user and revenue numbers it was designed to fix — or merely confirms that the identity it built the company on had already stopped being the reason people used it.
