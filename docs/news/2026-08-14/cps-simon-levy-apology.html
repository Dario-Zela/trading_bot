CPS director apologises for failings that let Simon Levy go on to rape and murder

The Old Bailey, where Simon Levy was sentenced to a whole-life order on 12 August 2026. — Acabashi / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
The CPS chief admitted prosecutors kept a bail court in the dark about Simon Levy's rape and murder investigations before he killed a second woman.

Stephen Parkinson does not use the word "apology" lightly. As Director of Public Prosecutions, the head of the Crown Prosecution Service for England and Wales, he has spent his career inside a system built on the presumption that it gets these calls right. This week he said, in terms, that it did not.

Simon Levy, 40, was handed a whole-life order at the Old Bailey on 12 August for murdering two women — Carmenza Valencia-Trujillo, 53, and Sheryl Wilkins, 39 — and raping a third. Both killings happened while Levy was on bail, and Parkinson has now admitted the court that granted him that bail was never told what prosecutors already knew: that Levy was under investigation for rape and for a previous murder.

"The CPS did not meet the standard the public should expect and we have been clear that this was a serious failing," Parkinson said. Asked whether Wilkins might still be alive had Levy been remanded in custody, he did not equivocate: "Clearly, if he had been remanded in custody, then he would not have had the opportunity to commit the crime that he did."

A chain of missed warnings

Levy's offending stretches back to 2018 — indecent assaults at Camden Market and Notting Hill Carnival, a 2021 conviction, and, after his release, a string of sexual assaults on London Underground and rail passengers between 2023 and 2025 that eventually drew 11 separate convictions. In January 2025 he raped and strangled a woman in Haringey. Two months later Valencia-Trujillo was found dead on the Aylesbury Estate; Levy was arrested on suspicion of her murder on 1 April and bailed, because her cause of death could not immediately be established. Parkinson said the two bail hearings that followed her killing both fell on a Saturday, when prosecutors were working through "an incredibly busy list"; details of the rape and murder investigations were, in his words, "in the system" but the lawyers in court that day were not "aware of that information" and so never raised it. Five months later, Wilkins was killed near Tottenham High Road. A Metropolitan Police deputy assistant commissioner, Kevin Southworth, went further than Parkinson, saying Wilkins would probably be alive today were it not for "collective system failures" spanning the Met, British Transport Police and the CPS. Two Met officers are now under Independent Office for Police Conduct investigation over their handling of Levy after his 2023 prison release.

Staff versus structure

Parkinson's fix is more people: additional support for prosecutors covering busy remand courts, stronger training on bail and risk decisions, and closer coordination between police and prosecutors on suspects showing a pattern of escalating offending. Campaigners are not convinced that is the whole answer. The End Violence Against Women coalition noted that the CPS withheld relevant information at three separate hearings, not one, and questioned whether "rape myths, discrimination and stereotypes" shaped how officers treated the January rape complainant — who was herself arrested for breaching a community order after reporting the attack. Their argument is that a staffing pledge addresses capacity, not the judgment and information-sharing failures that let a known, escalating offender pass through three different agencies undetected.

The IOPC's inquiry into the two Met officers continues, and the CPS has yet to say who, if anyone, will be held individually accountable for the missed bail disclosures. Whether Parkinson's promised reforms survive contact with the next overstretched Saturday court list is the test that will actually matter.
