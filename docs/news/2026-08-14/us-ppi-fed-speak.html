US producer prices hold flat as Fed officials flag inflation persistence

The Marriner S. Eccles Federal Reserve Board Building in Washington, D.C. — AgnosticPreachersKid / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
July producer prices were flat, but sticky services and core costs — plus Fed officials openly doubting current rates are restrictive — have markets pricing hike risk over cut hope.

Producer prices went nowhere in July — the Bureau of Labor Statistics' final-demand PPI came in flat against a Wall Street consensus for a 0.2% rise — and a day earlier the consumer price index showed the same grinding, unglamorous pattern: inflation cooling, but not by much, and not evenly.

Taken together, the two reports haven't settled the argument at the Federal Reserve so much as sharpened it. Some officials still think the disinflation trend has months to run. Others, including two who spoke publicly this week, are no longer sure the current level of interest rates is doing enough work.

The numbers, unpacked

July's PPI headline flattered the picture. Beneath it, goods prices fell 0.7%, dragged down by a 3.1% drop in energy costs and a 5.7% plunge in gasoline — the kind of move that has more to do with crude markets than monetary policy. Services prices rose 0.2%, and construction costs jumped 2.2%. Year-over-year, producer price growth slowed to 4.7% from 5.5% in June, a meaningful deceleration on its face.

But the core measure — which strips out food, energy and trade services, and is the one Fed staff tend to watch most closely for signal — surged 0.4%, four times June's pace, driven in part by a 6.5% spike in portfolio management fees. That is not the kind of category that responds to interest rates or supply chains; it moves with asset prices. Still, its presence in the core print is a reminder that "underlying" inflation is doing more than goods deflation can offset.

Wednesday's CPI told a similar story from the consumer side: headline prices rose 0.1% in July, with the annual rate easing to 3.4% from 3.5%. Core CPI rose 0.2% and sits at 2.5% year-over-year. Shelter, still the largest single driver, added just 0.1% — a sign that the rent-disinflation story, however slowly, is still playing out. None of it surprised forecasters; all of it came in roughly at consensus.

Officials aren't reassured

What makes the week's data awkward for the Fed is the timing. Two regional presidents used it to argue, in effect, that the job isn't done. Cleveland's Beth Hammack told an audience in Kettering, Ohio, on Wednesday that she isn't convinced the recent cooling will hold: "I love to see that those numbers are coming in lower — that's a good thing — but I don't have confidence that we're going to continue to see that, or that we're going to see them low enough that it's going to bring us back down to that 2% number." She does not believe the federal funds rate, currently 3.50%–3.75%, is "meaningfully restricting" the economy, and has been the most vocal advocate on the committee for raising rates rather than waiting.

Richmond's Tom Barkin was more circumspect but landed close to the same place. Speaking to the Greenville Chamber of Commerce, he noted that inflation has now run above the Fed's 2% target for five straight years — long enough, he warned, to risk "an upward shift in the price expectations of firms and consumers," which is central-bank language for the moment psychology stops cooperating with policy. He also pointed to a subtler wrinkle: surging demand for the labor and equipment behind the AI buildout, which he expects to ease over time but which is adding real, if temporary, price pressure now. Both officials pushed back on the idea that AI is primarily a story about job losses — Barkin said use cases remain too immature to drive large headcount cuts — while agreeing the technology's capital and energy demands are inflationary in the near term.

What it means for September

The combination has shifted the market conversation. Rate-cut bets, which dominated pricing earlier this year, have all but disappeared: prediction markets now put the odds of a 25-basis-point September hike near 29%, against roughly 71% for a hold and almost none for a cut. That is a striking reversal from the standard post-CPI playbook, and it reflects a committee that, five years into an inflation overshoot, is no longer treating "transitory" as an adequate explanation for what services and asset-linked prices keep doing.

The next scheduled test comes August 26, when the Fed's preferred gauge — the PCE price index — is released, followed by the September 16 FOMC meeting where Hammack, Barkin and their colleagues will have to decide whether five years of missed targets calls for patience or for action.
