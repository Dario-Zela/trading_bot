Citi to acquire rewards platform Kard for its 70 million cardholders

In one sentence
Citi's U.S. cards unit is buying fintech Kard to bring AI-driven, personalized rewards to its 70 million cardholders.

Citigroup's U.S. consumer cards division has agreed to buy Kard Financial, a nine-year-old New York fintech that builds personalized, data-driven rewards programs for banks and merchants. The deal, announced Thursday, gives Citi's roughly 70 million U.S. cardmembers access to a platform that uses machine learning and verified transaction data to match individual spending habits with merchant-funded offers — the kind of real-time, tailored rewards that legacy card issuers have historically struggled to build in-house.

Terms were not disclosed. Citi called the transaction immaterial to its financial results, which tells its own story: this is not a balance-sheet event, it's a capability purchase. The deal is subject to customary closing conditions and regulatory approval.

"We're focused on helping customers get more value from their everyday spending," said Abhinav Anand, Citi's head of value cards, lending and commerce. "Kard has built advanced capabilities that complement our vision for the future of commerce and loyalty." Kard founder and chief executive Ben Mackinnon framed it as scale: "Now being able to do that for Citi's 70 million cardmembers accelerates that original vision."

Buying the data layer, not building it

Kard, founded in 2015 and backed by Tiger Global, Fin Capital and Underscore Ventures, describes itself as an "independent commerce media network" — infrastructure that connects card issuers, fintechs and merchants through first-party transaction data rather than the blunt, static discount codes that have defined card rewards for decades. It has raised outside capital as recently as this year, including growth funding from Trinity Capital, and counts a roster of banks and neobanks as clients.

The acquisition fits a pattern industry researchers have flagged repeatedly this year: card issuers collectively spend an estimated $100 billion annually on rewards programs that exert little influence at the actual moment a customer decides which card to tap, and the competitive battleground is shifting toward who controls that decision in real time. Surveys of issuers this year found a majority planning to expand AI-driven transaction categorization and personalized offer targeting over the next twelve months — components Kard already sells as a packaged product.

Rather than build that stack internally, Citi is doing what Capital One did at far larger scale with its Discover acquisition: buying distribution and technology instead of engineering it from scratch. For an issuer with Citi's cardholder base, the calculus is straightforward — Kard's client list already includes banks and fintechs it will now, in effect, compete against from the inside.

Keefe, Bruyette & Woods advised Kard on the deal; Sullivan & Cromwell represented Citi and Latham & Watkins represented Kard. No closing date has been announced, and the terms of Citi's continued relationship with Kard's existing bank and fintech clients — some of whom are Citi rivals — remain unclear.
