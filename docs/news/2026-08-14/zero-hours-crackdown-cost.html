Zero-hours crackdown could cost UK employers up to £2.9bn a year

Retail is among the sectors most exposed to the guaranteed-hours reforms, alongside hospitality and logistics. — Velela / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Government impact assessments show zero-hours reform could cost UK employers as little as £350m or as much as £2.9bn a year, and nobody yet knows which end of that range is real.

Britain's zero-hours contracts are heading for extinction in their current form, and the government has now put a price on it. Impact assessments published alongside a Department for Business and Trade consultation put the direct annual cost to employers of the Employment Rights Act's guaranteed-hours reforms at between £350m and £2.9bn, with a central estimate of £1.1bn — a range wide enough to unsettle anyone trying to model margin impact across retail, hospitality and logistics.

What the reforms actually do

The Employment Rights Act, which received royal assent in December, obliges employers to offer zero- and low-hours workers a contract with guaranteed weekly hours reflecting what they actually work over a reference period. Firms will also have to give reasonable notice of shifts and pay compensation when shifts are cancelled, cut short or moved at late notice. Workers who like the flexibility can turn the guaranteed offer down and stay as they are.

The consultation now running is about where to draw the lines: the threshold for what counts as a "low hours" contract is up for debate, with options spanning eight to 48 hours a week and the government's own preference sitting between eight and 20. That single parameter does a lot of the work in determining whether the eventual bill lands near £350m or near £2.9bn — it decides how many workers get pulled into the new regime and how many firms have to rebuild scheduling systems around it. The consultation closes on 25 August, with the reforms expected to take effect sometime in 2027.

Where the exposure sits

Officials flagged retail, hospitality, entertainment, support services, education and health and social care as the sectors most exposed, reflecting how heavily each leans on variable staffing and short-notice rotas. The British Retail Consortium has been the loudest objector so far. Its chief executive, Helen Dickinson, said the range of estimates raised "serious questions" about whether the reforms would deliver benefits proportionate to the cost, and argued the official numbers understate the true bill — retailers alone could spend "hundreds of millions of pounds" reworking HR and payroll systems, a cost the assessment does not appear to capture. She also pointed out that retailers have already absorbed roughly £6.5bn in extra employment costs over the past two years from higher National Insurance contributions and successive minimum-wage increases.

That context matters for anyone pricing sector risk: the £2.9bn figure is incremental on top of a cost base that has already moved sharply. For thinner-margin employers in hospitality and retail, a second wave of labour-cost inflation — however uncertain its final size — compounds pressure that hasn't yet fully worked through pricing or headcount.

What's unresolved

The government's own range spans a factor of eight, which is unusually wide for a policy this far into the legislative process. The gap reflects genuine uncertainty about employer behaviour as much as about the rules themselves — how many firms restructure rotas to minimise guaranteed-hours exposure, how many simply absorb the cost, and how many reduce headcount at the margin. Business groups have separately warned the changes could make employers more cautious about hiring younger and more casual workers, the group most likely to be on zero-hours arrangements in the first place.

None of this is priced with any precision yet, and it won't be until the consultation closes and the government settles the low-hours threshold. Until then, the honest position for anyone modelling UK consumer-facing and logistics stocks is that labour-cost risk from this reform is real, plausibly material at the sector level, and not yet quantifiable at the firm level. The next marker is 25 August, when the consultation window shuts and the government moves toward drafting the regulations that will decide which end of the £350m-to-£2.9bn range the country lands on.
