Nearly 100 children wrongly detained as adults under UK-France migration deal

A UK Border Force vessel at Ramsgate, chartered to patrol the Channel during the small-boat crossings crisis. — Hsq7278 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Home Office data show 94 of 304 age-disputed cases under the UK-France returns deal were later confirmed to be children, despite minors being explicitly excluded from the scheme.

Nearly one in three people the Home Office flagged as adults under the UK-France migration returns deal turned out to be children, according to figures disclosed to the independent body that inspects British detention centres — a gap between policy and practice that campaigners say has left vulnerable minors locked up, and in some cases deported, in breach of the scheme's own rules.

The "one in, one out" arrangement, which began on 15 August 2025, lets the UK return people who cross the Channel by small boat to France in exchange for accepting an equivalent number of asylum seekers with pending claims. Ministers built an explicit safeguard into the deal: unaccompanied children were to be excluded entirely. In practice, that exclusion has depended on an initial Home Office age assessment — often a rushed screening interview — getting the answer right the first time.

It frequently has not. In a letter to Home Office minister Anna Turley, Jane Leech, interim chair of the Independent Monitoring Boards (IMBs), disclosed that officials had recorded 304 age disputes between 15 August 2025 and 1 May 2026. Of those, 94 — 31% — were subsequently assessed to be children.

"Your officials have informed the IMB that 304 age disputes have taken place between 15 August 2025 and 1 May 2026, of which 94 (31%) were assessed to be children," Leech wrote, adding that "the IMB is therefore concerned that child safeguarding mechanisms are ineffective."

How the errors happen

A parallel account from the Jesuit Refugee Service UK (JRS UK) and Humans for Rights Network, published on the Oxford Law Blogs' Border Criminologies site in July, traces the errors to the screening process itself. Initial interviews are sometimes conducted by phone, without any visual assessment of the person being questioned. Arrivals are frequently exhausted, hungry and disoriented after a Channel crossing. Interpretation is described as poor or absent, and researchers say Home Office staff have shown little grasp of non-Gregorian calendar systems — in one documented case, a child was unable to convert his date of birth from the Eritrean calendar, and the date the Home Office recorded placed him wrongly over 18.

Sophie Cartwright of JRS UK, who wrote the blog post, describes a wider "culture of disbelief" among assessors, in which the onus falls on the young person to prove their age rather than on officials to establish it reliably. Some children, coached by smugglers to lie about their age to avoid restrictive placements, have had that initial false claim held against them even after they later told the truth. The JRS/HFRN briefing puts the number of age-disputed detentions at 174 by late June, with at least 25 young people forcibly removed to France despite disputed assessments — a higher, more recent count than the IMB's nine-month snapshot, reflecting the different cut-off dates.

Government policy limits immigration detention of unaccompanied children to 24 hours. The IMB found some age-disputed detainees held far longer than that — one for 27 weeks — with cases where 40 days or more elapsed between arrival at a removal centre and a formal age assessment. Researchers also documented a decline in detainees' mental health, including self-harm and withdrawal, and warned that children removed to France face risks of destitution and exploitation, with some losing contact with support services altogether.

Reaction and response

Imran Hussain of the Refugee Council said the pattern reflects a systemic failure rather than isolated error: "Too often refugee children are being treated as adults and put into unsafe situations." The Home Office has said it will "strengthen and modernise" its age-assessment processes and plans to introduce what it calls "cutting-edge AI technology" for the purpose from next year — a proposal that has itself drawn criticism, with officials cited in the JRS/HFRN briefing acknowledging that current AI age-estimation tools are "inaccurate and containing racial bias."

The episode lands awkwardly for a scheme designed, in part, to demonstrate that Britain and France could manage Channel migration through orderly bilateral cooperation rather than unilateral crackdowns. A safeguard written into the text of an agreement between two governments is only as good as the screening interview that applies it on the ground — and nine months of data suggest that interview has been getting nearly a third of contested cases wrong. The scheme, extended from its original June 2026 end date to October 2026, remains under review; the next test will be whether the promised overhaul of age assessment — AI-assisted or otherwise — narrows that error rate before more children pass through it.
