Twitch admits 'nobody would opt in' as it defends default AI training on streams

Twitch, the Amazon-owned livestreaming platform, now trains generative AI on streamer content by default. — Twitch Interactive / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Twitch's product chief admitted an opt-in AI-training setting would get no takers, so the company enrolled every streamer by default instead.

Twitch has never pretended its streamers are fond of generative AI. Now it has admitted, in public, that they hate it enough to reject it outright — if anyone actually asked them first.

On August 12, the Amazon-owned livestreaming platform rolled out a new privacy setting letting creators stop their broadcasts, archived videos, clips and chat logs from being used to train generative AI models. The catch: the setting is opt-out, not opt-in. Every streamer was automatically enrolled the moment it launched, and disabling it takes a deliberate trip through Settings, then Security and Privacy, then a toggle marked "Training for Generative AI."

Within hours, a post on Twitch's own support forum demanding an opt-in model instead had drawn close to 14,000 upvotes. During a livestream Twitch held to address the anger, chief product officer Mike Minton was asked, plainly, why the company hadn't just made consent the default. He didn't dress it up.

"If it was opt-in, nobody would opt in. That's honestly the answer."

An admission, not a defence

It's a rare moment of corporate candour, and it lands badly precisely because it is candid. Minton wasn't claiming streamers were simply unaware of the benefits of AI training, or that a friction-free opt-in flow would surely win people over given time. He was saying the company already knows the answer and chose the setting that overrides it anyway. Mary Kish, Twitch's head of community, added that the opt-out numbers would help the company keep telling itself what it apparently already knows: "our community is not receptive of AI in any form."

What exactly happens to the harvested footage is murkier. Minton told the livestream audience that "Twitch has not been training models," while declining to say what Amazon, the parent company, does with the data once it leaves Twitch's systems. That ambiguity — a subsidiary disclaiming responsibility for what its owner does with the pipe it built — is itself becoming a familiar shape in these disputes. The opt-out also has a gap: it protects a streamer's own broadcasts, but not comments they leave in someone else's chat.

Why it's more than a UI complaint

Twitch's framing is that this is merely a new setting, not a new practice — implying the data collection was arguably happening already. That's a common move in platform-consent disputes: present the toggle as a gift rather than acknowledge the years without one. Meta and Google have made versions of the same argument, treating anything posted publicly as fair game for training absent an explicit objection.

The reaction suggests that argument no longer buys much goodwill. Streamers make a living from the platform and have limited exit options, which makes the opt-out design feel less like an oversight and more like a bet that inertia will do the enrolling that persuasion couldn't. Twitch's own product chief has now confirmed that bet in his own words.

For Amazon, the reputational cost is a rounding error next to the data value, at least for now. But the pattern — quietly default users into AI training, then defend it by admitting persuasion would fail — is exactly the kind of practice regulators in the UK and EU have been circling on data-consent grounds. Twitch says it will keep the setting as is. The next thing to watch is whether that forum post, and the ones like it, start showing up in a regulator's inbox rather than just the company's.
