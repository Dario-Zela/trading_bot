Ackman's Pershing Square returns to Netflix with $2.1bn stake

Bill Ackman, whose Pershing Square Capital Management has re-entered Netflix four years after exiting at a $400m loss. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Ackman's Pershing Square built a new $2.1bn Netflix stake four years after a $400m loss, part of a six-stock bet that AI-disruption fears are overblown across streaming, payments and market infrastructure.

Bill Ackman is buying Netflix again. Four years after Pershing Square unwound a Netflix position at a $400m loss — one of the more visible black eyes of his career — the activist investor's fund disclosed a new 3.15 million-share stake worth roughly $2.1bn, according to its interim report for the six months to June 30. It is not a small trade: at 4.9% of the portfolio, Netflix is now one of Pershing Square's largest holdings, and it arrives alongside five other new positions — Visa, Mastercard, S&P Global, Intercontinental Exchange and Alcon — in what several analysts are calling the fund's broadest portfolio overhaul in years.

Netflix shares rose about 4% on the disclosure. The harder question is what, precisely, Ackman is betting on this time.

The last time this happened, it went badly

The history is worth restating because Ackman himself has made a point of restating it. In January 2022, Pershing Square built a roughly $1.1bn Netflix position at prices near $400 a share, only for the company to report its first subscriber decline in a decade weeks later. The stock fell 35% in a single session. Ackman sold the entire stake in April 2022 at around $225, crystallising a loss north of $400m, and said at the time he had "lost confidence in our ability to predict the company's future prospects with a sufficient degree of certainty" — a rare public admission of a conviction call gone wrong.

That backdrop is doing a lot of work in the coverage of this week's filing, and Pershing Square has leaned into it rather than away from it. The fund's letter to investors states flatly that "Netflix has since effectively won the streaming wars." The entry point is different this time, too: Pershing built the new stake after Netflix shares fell roughly 50% from their June 2025 high of $134, a decline that took the stock's valuation from more than 40 times forward earnings to about 21 times.

A moat thesis, not just a comeback story

What differentiates this disclosure from a simple re-entry is the company it's keeping. Visa, Mastercard, S&P Global and Intercontinental Exchange are not streaming names — they are payments-rail and market-infrastructure businesses that have, for different reasons, all been marked down this year on fears that artificial intelligence changes their competitive position. Visa and Mastercard sold off on worries that agentic AI checkout tools and stablecoins could route payments around card networks; Netflix has faced its own version of the same anxiety, with investors asking whether AI-driven short-form video pulls viewing time away from subscription streaming. Pershing Square's argument, in each case, is that the disruption narrative is overstated relative to the moat.

On S&P Global, Ackman and chief investment officer Ryan Israel described "a high-margin, IP licensing business with a formidable competitive moat inside an oligopolistic market structure" — language that could apply equally to how the fund frames Netflix's scale advantage. Pershing's own numbers for Netflix back the argument up: cash content spending has grown only about 2% a year since 2021 even as the subscriber base passed 325 million, operating margins expanded from 21% to roughly 31.5%, and the company now converts about 90% of earnings into free cash flow. The fund is projecting double-digit revenue growth and earnings compounding close to 20% annually.

Alcon, the eye-care device maker, sits somewhat apart from that AI-disruption thread and looks more like a straightforward quality-compounder bet, added — like Intercontinental Exchange — after the June 30 reporting cutoff, meaning it will technically show up as a third-quarter position when the fuller 13F filing lands.

What the thesis doesn't yet answer

The skeptics' case against Netflix specifically is not hard to find. Revenue growth slowed to 13.4% in the second quarter from 17.2% a year earlier, and the company's own third-quarter guidance of 11.7% would mark its weakest growth in three years. YouTube, not Netflix, now takes the largest share of US television viewing time — 12.6% versus Netflix's 8.3%, per recent industry data — which is an awkward fact for a fund arguing the streaming wars are settled. Pershing's rebuttal, that time lost to short-form video comes disproportionately from linear television rather than from Netflix, is plausible but is an assertion, not yet a demonstrated trend.

That is the gap in this story as it stands: Pershing Square has disclosed the position and offered a rationale in its investor letter, but the full 13F — with exact share counts, cost basis and the precise sizing of the Alcon and ICE additions — was still pending as of this filing, expected around August 14. Until then, what's known is directional: a fund with a large, painful memory of Netflix has decided the arithmetic looks different now, and has made the same bet, in size, on five other companies facing a similar disruption narrative. Whether that reads as a coherent thesis on AI-resistant moats or as a value investor buying beaten-down mega-caps and building the story afterward will become clearer as the full portfolio disclosure — and Netflix's next earnings print — arrive.

The next test is concrete: Netflix reports third-quarter results in October, against guidance of 11.7% revenue growth that the company itself has called its softest quarter in three years. If that number holds or improves, Pershing's re-entry looks prescient. If it slips further, the comparisons to 2022 will write themselves.
