Trump orders Navy to revert aircraft carriers to steam catapults

USS Gerald R. Ford, the first carrier built with the electromagnetic catapult system now facing reversal, underway during builder's sea trials in 2017. — U.S. Navy photo by Mass Communication Specialist 2nd Class Ridge Leoni
In one sentence
Trump has ordered the Navy to rip EMALS electromagnetic catapults out of future carriers and revert to steam, a reversal contractors say could cost billions.

President Trump has ordered the Navy to rip the electromagnetic catapult system out of future aircraft carrier construction and go back to steam — reversing nearly two decades and multiple administrations' worth of naval modernization in a single memorandum, and putting billions of dollars of contractor investment on notice.

The national security memorandum, signed this week, directs the Secretary of War, in consultation with the Secretary of the Navy, to deliver a plan within 60 days for replacing the Electromagnetic Aircraft Launch System (EMALS) and Advanced Weapons Elevators with steam and hydraulic systems on the future USS Doris Miller (CVN-81), the fourth Ford-class carrier. The first three ships in the class — Gerald R. Ford, John F. Kennedy and the still-building Enterprise — are exempted and will keep EMALS.

A decade-old grievance becomes policy

Trump has been complaining about EMALS since his first term, when he told naval officers he wanted them "going to goddamned steam" instead of what he called a "digital" system that cost "hundreds of millions of dollars more" and was "no good." That 2017 riff stayed just talk. This time it's a signed memo with a deadline attached.

EMALS was selected by the Navy in 2009, under the Obama administration, on the promise of lower lifecycle costs, less maintenance and gentler treatment of carrier airframes than the steam catapults used since the 1950s. The system had a rocky start — reliability problems dogged early testing — but the Navy says it has since logged thousands of successful launches aboard the Ford. Defense officials under Trump's own first term and later the Biden administration continued funding it regardless of the president's public grumbling.

Contractors on the hook

General Atomics, the privately held San Diego firm that builds EMALS, said production on Doris Miller's launch system is already close to half complete. "Changing course now would introduce significant cost, schedule, and integration risks," the company said, warning the reversal "will have broader implications for carrier readiness, national security, and U.S. technological superiority." Bryan Clark of the Hudson Institute noted a further wrinkle: the Navy hasn't bought a steam catapult in more than 20 years, meaning the supplier base for the old technology has largely atrophied and would need to be rebuilt from scratch.

Huntington Ingalls Industries (NYSE: HII), which builds the Ford class at its Newport News, Virginia yard, is the publicly traded name most directly exposed — its shares ticked up only modestly in the hours after the memo emerged, suggesting investors are still digesting how a mid-construction redesign gets priced. The Navy has previously estimated the total switch could run into the billions once re-engineering, workforce retraining and supply-chain rebuilding are included; official estimates put the near-term hit at "hundreds of millions of dollars," though naval analysts expect that figure to climb as the scope of the redesign firms up over the 60-day review window.

What else is in the memo

The same order loosens rules on building certain Navy vessels abroad and calls for a fifth public shipyard dedicated to submarine maintenance — both signals that the administration is willing to unwind recent shipbuilding orthodoxy wholesale, not just on carriers. Navy leadership has previously flagged workforce constraints as an obstacle to standing up new public yards.

The next checkpoint is the 60-day plan due from the Secretary of War's office, which should put a firmer number on the redesign cost and clarify whether General Atomics' EMALS contracts on Doris Miller get renegotiated, cancelled, or absorbed as sunk cost. Contractor earnings calls this quarter are the more immediate tell — watch for Huntington Ingalls management addressing schedule and cost risk on CVN-81 directly.
