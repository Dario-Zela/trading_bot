South Korean stock market's wild swings leave retail investors nursing steep losses

Yeouido, Seoul's financial district and home to the Korea Exchange, where retail-driven volatility has upended small investors' savings. — S h y numis / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 4.0)
In one sentence
South Korean retail investors have lost tens of billions of dollars in leveraged chip-stock ETFs after a violent Kospi reversal exposed a retail-dominated market's fragility.

For months, South Korea's stock market looked like the trade of the decade. The Kospi index roughly doubled between January and June, riding the same AI and semiconductor enthusiasm that lifted Nvidia and its peers, with Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix as the local proxies. Then, in June, it broke. What followed has left ordinary Koreans nursing losses that, for some, wiped out years of savings in a matter of weeks.

"I got completely swept up in the frenzy," said Kim Han-kyung, a Seoul resident in her late thirties who started trading in May. "Now, I'm honestly scared." She has since set herself two rules, she told local media: never invest in the Korean stock market again, and follow the first rule. Lee Jung-min, 40, took out a 50 million won loan against his apartment to trade — and lost heavily. "The government put fuel into the fire with those leveraged ETFs," he said. "I think it's wrong how they turned the stock market into a casino."

The products he means are single-stock leveraged ETFs tracking Samsung and SK Hynix, launched on 27 May to give retail traders the kind of geared exposure previously available only offshore. Domestic investors poured in 14 trillion won ($9.7bn), seven times what foreigners bought, and came to hold 92 percent of the positions. When chip stocks reversed, the KODEX SK Hynix leveraged fund fell more than 80 percent from its June peak. The Kospi itself dropped from above 8,800 points to 6,820 by 16 July — a decline of more than 25 percent — logging its worst monthly fall since the 2008 financial crisis and tripping circuit-breaker halts 19 times since the ETFs debuted.

A market built on retail money

The damage was concentrated because Korea's market is unusually retail-driven: individuals accounted for roughly 64 percent of annual trading value, against about 30 percent in the US or Japan. Regulators say margin calls hit 1.2 million accounts — about one in 30 adults — and between 320,000 and 360,000 were forcibly liquidated, wiping out principal entirely for many. Estimated cumulative losses on the leveraged products alone run near $38bn. Late last month, protesters laid roughly 30 funeral wreaths outside the National Assembly, reading "abolish single-stock leveraged ETFs" and "we'll get even with our votes." Finance minister Koo Yun-cheol apologised in parliament: "We sincerely apologise for not thoroughly considering how these products would perform after their launch." Authorities have since raised the minimum deposit for leveraged ETF trading to 30 million won ($21,000) and set up a debt-counselling hotline.

The irony is that the market has since turned again. The Kospi reclaimed the 7,000-point mark on Thursday, up some 22 percent from its 30 July low and back in technical bull territory as chip stocks rallied once more. But the retail money that drove the mania hasn't followed: individuals' share of trading value has slid from 48 percent in January to about 31 percent in July, the sharpest such retreat on record. Whether that caution holds — or whether the next AI-fuelled rally pulls Korean households back in — is the question regulators are now watching closest.
