Chip stocks power S&P 500 to a record as Applied Materials and AMD ride the AI capex wave

A 450mm silicon wafer of the kind produced using tools from equipment makers like Applied Materials. — Intel Free Press / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Chip stocks pushed the S&P 500 to a record as SanDisk jumped 15% and Applied Materials raised guidance, even as AMD borrowed $4.75bn to fund the AI buildout.

The S&P 500 closed at a fresh all-time high on Friday, its fourth record close in under two weeks, as semiconductor stocks did most of the heavy lifting. The Nasdaq Composite gained alongside it. The proximate cause was a one-two punch from the chip sector: SanDisk shares jumped 15% after laying out an ambitious five-year financial plan, and Applied Materials — the industry's biggest maker of chipmaking equipment — beat earnings estimates and raised its growth outlook the night before.

Both events point in the same direction: demand for AI-related computing hardware, particularly memory and the tools used to fabricate it, is still accelerating rather than plateauing. But a third data point from the same 48 hours — AMD moving to raise up to $5 billion in new debt — is a reminder that the buildout is also being financed with leverage, not just retained earnings. For anyone allocating around this sector, the distinction between "demand is genuinely widening" and "a small number of AI-exposed names are getting more expensive and more indebted" matters a great deal.

Memory chips lead the record

SanDisk's rally was the standout. At its investor day on Thursday, the company targeted non-GAAP gross margins of roughly 80% and free-cash-flow margins near 50% through fiscal 2030, alongside mid-to-high-teens annual revenue growth from fiscal 2028 onward. Management said it already has "New Business Model" supply agreements in place with eight customers covering two-thirds of the bits it expects to ship by fiscal 2028 — in effect, pre-committed demand that reduces the usual boom-bust risk in memory pricing. The company is betting on enterprise flash storage tied to AI inference workloads, projecting the addressable market will reach 1.2 zettabytes by 2030.

The market reaction rippled across the memory complex: Western Digital rose roughly 10%, Micron gained about 6%, and South Korea's SK Hynix added roughly 8% in its own session. The Philadelphia semiconductor index has now climbed more than 7% over the past two weeks, a pace that outstrips the broader market by a wide margin — a sign this is sector-specific enthusiasm, not simply a rising tide lifting all boats.

Equipment makers say the demand is real

Applied Materials gave that enthusiasm a harder foundation. The company reported record fiscal third-quarter revenue of $9.12 billion, up 25% year over year, with adjusted earnings of $3.50 a share against a $3.40 estimate. More significant than the beat was the guidance revision: Applied Materials raised its outlook for 2026 semiconductor-equipment revenue growth to more than 30%, up from roughly 20% just a quarter ago. Advanced packaging — the technology used to stack and connect chips inside AI accelerators — is now expected to grow around 50% this year, with additional demand coming from gate-all-around transistor architectures that require more deposition, etch and inspection steps per wafer.

"As the rapid global adoption of AI drives unprecedented demand for our materials engineering solutions, we are further raising our Semiconductor Systems revenue expectations for calendar 2026," chief executive Gary Dickerson told analysts.

That matters for the "is this real" question because equipment orders lead chip production by several quarters — fabs don't buy tools speculatively. A guidance raise from the sector's largest tool supplier, delivered alongside a genuine earnings beat rather than mere sentiment, is closer to a leading indicator of sustained AI capital spending than a stock-specific pop. The company's own Q4 guidance — revenue of $10.25 billion, plus or minus $500 million, and adjusted earnings of $4.02 a share — implies the growth is expected to continue into the next quarter, not fade.

Financing the buildout

The same week produced a less celebratory data point. AMD moved to raise as much as $5 billion through a new bond offering — its largest-ever dollar-denominated debt sale — ultimately pricing $4.75 billion across four tranches with maturities from three to ten years. Pricing on the longest tranche tightened to about 0.9 percentage points over Treasuries, roughly a quarter-point inside initial guidance, reflecting strong investor demand for the paper. AMD said proceeds would go toward general corporate purposes, including refinancing $875 million of bonds maturing next month, as it broadens supply agreements with customers including Anthropic and Microsoft.

AMD is not alone. The bond sale is part of a wider pattern this year of AI-exposed technology companies tapping debt markets rather than funding data-center and chip capacity purely from cash flow — a shift from the balance-sheet conservatism that characterized the sector for most of the past decade. Debt financing isn't inherently a warning sign; it can simply reflect confidence that AI infrastructure investment will pay off faster than it costs to service. But it does change the risk profile. A company funding capacity growth with borrowed money is more exposed if AI spending decelerates, than one funding it from free cash flow.

What it adds up to

Taken together, the week's evidence leans toward genuine, broadening chip demand rather than a narrow re-rating of two or three mega-cap names. Applied Materials sells tools to a wide swath of the industry, not just to one customer, and its order book is a harder signal than a single stock's earnings beat. SanDisk's contracted supply agreements suggest customers are locking in memory capacity years out, not just chasing a rally. But the AMD debt raise is worth watching precisely because it's the piece that could turn a demand story into a leverage story if the growth assumptions underlying it don't hold. For portfolios weighing sector rotation into semiconductors against concentration risk in AI-exposed tech, the next test comes with Nvidia's own earnings later this month, and with how the bond market prices the next round of AI-linked debt issuance — a cheaper spread than AMD's would confirm confidence is still building, not fraying.
