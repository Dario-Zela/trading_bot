Ofwat provisionally clears £3.4bn extra water company spending, bills to rise

A Thames Water sewage treatment works in Haslemere, Surrey — one of the companies provisionally cleared for extra investment spending. — Simon Burchell / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Ofwat provisionally clears £3.4bn of extra water company spending, adding up to £80 to some household bills by 2030, with a final call due in December.

Ofwat has provisionally cleared £3.4bn of extra spending by five English water companies, extending a spending programme that has already pushed household bills up 36% since 2025 — and confirming that the regulator, faced with a choice between infrastructure and affordability, has again come down on the side of infrastructure.

The draft determination, published Thursday, covers Thames Water, Severn Trent, Southern Water, Wessex Water and South East Water. All five will be permitted to raise customer charges between 2027 and 2030 to fund the work, with increases weighted heavily toward Southern Water — up to £80 over the period — against roughly £8 apiece for Thames Water and Severn Trent, £11 for Wessex Water and just £1 for South East Water.

A mechanism built for this moment

The money flows through Ofwat's Cost Change Process, a facility created after the 2024 price review to let companies bid for extra funding on costs that were uncertain or unknowable when that review was finalised in December 2024 — new housing demand, data centre connections, PFAS contamination. Thirteen companies applied for £4.3bn combined; Ofwat rejected £752m as out of scope and approved £3.39bn, or 96% of what it judged eligible. Only five of the thirteen will actually charge customers more, because the rest are recovering costs from other levers in their existing settlements.

Of the total, £1.2bn goes to safeguarding existing assets and services, £477m to growth-related projects tied to housebuilding and data centres, and £34m specifically to removing PFAS — so-called forever chemicals — from drinking water supplies. United Utilities' allocation, for instance, funds new infrastructure to enable data centre development in East Manchester, a reminder that water capacity has become as much a constraint on Britain's AI buildout as electricity grid connections.

The politics of passing it on

The reaction has been pointed. Prime Minister Andy Burnham said he understood public anger over the increases: "Customers have been asked to pay more for years, yet serious pollution incidents are at record levels and the pipes are still leaking… Customers cannot be treated as a blank cheque." Environment Secretary Angela Eagle struck a similar note, blaming "years of underinvestment and toothless regulation." The Consumer Council for Water was more measured but no less pointed, saying Ofwat needs to demonstrate "every pound of this additional £3.4 billion is necessary."

Ofwat's own defence rests on outcomes rather than optics. Helen Campbell, the regulator's executive director for delivery, said the funding would "unlock much-needed new housing development and boost business growth," while noting that spending tied to specific performance targets can be clawed back if companies fail to deliver. A Thames Water spokesperson welcomed the decision as support for "vital water security projects, which will keep the taps flowing for millions of customers across London and the South East" — a company for which any incremental revenue certainty matters given its long-running solvency troubles.

What it means for the sector

For investors in regulated water, the draft determination is a signal that Ofwat will fund legitimate cost pressure even amid intense political scrutiny of bills — supportive for the earnings and dividend trajectories embedded in current price controls, and a precedent other utilities facing their own AI-driven demand spikes will be watching closely. For everyone else, it is another instalment in a bill trajectory that shows little sign of flattening: this year's rise already averaged 5.4%, on top of last year's steeper jump.

Ofwat will consult on the draft determination until 24 September, with a final decision due in December — the same month it will also finalise the broader price review recalibration. David Black, Ofwat's chief executive since 2022, is due to step down at the end of August, leaving his successor to defend the final numbers.
