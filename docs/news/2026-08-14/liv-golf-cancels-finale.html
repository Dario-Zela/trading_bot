LIV Golf cancels $40m season finale as Saudi PIF funding wavers

Patrons at a LIV Golf tournament — the league has cancelled two 2026 events as its Saudi PIF backing winds down. — Western Shadow / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
LIV Golf axed its $40m Michigan finale — its second cancelled event this year — as Saudi PIF prepares to end funding after 2026.

LIV Golf has scrapped the $40 million Team Championship it had scheduled for Michigan later this month, the second event the Saudi-backed league has cancelled in 2026 and the clearest sign yet that its financial backer is tightening the taps.

The tournament, set for August 27-30 at The Cardinal at St. John's Resort in Plymouth, was supposed to close out the season with the richest prize purse on the calendar. Instead, LIV confirmed the cancellation on August 12, after tickets quietly vanished from its own website days earlier — an operational stumble that had fans and local media speculating before the league said anything official. Golf.com and CBS Sports both reported that LIV Golf Indianapolis, August 20-23, will now stand in as the season's final event.

The money behind the cancellation

The proximate cause is Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, which told LIV in April that it would end funding once the current season wraps — having already put more than $5 billion into the four-year-old league. Reporting since then has filled in just how tight things have become: LIV is said to be burning roughly $100 million a month, and PIF has delivered only about a third of the $600 million the league needed to finish out 2026. A New Orleans event worth $30 million was pulled in June for the same reason, the first tournament LIV had ever cancelled. Michigan is the second.

There have been leadership signals too. PIF chairman Yasir Al-Rumayyan has stepped down from LIV's board, and the league says it is now chasing $250-300 million in outside investment to keep operating past this season. CEO Scott O'Neil has pointed to progress — "an agreement in place with a lead investor, signed by the investor and approved by the board" — and floated a restructuring in which players would become majority equity holders, a first for a major sports league if it happens. Terms haven't been disclosed, and it isn't yet clear whether the $40 million in team prize money tied to the cancelled Michigan event will still be paid out.

A smaller league by design

Whatever emerges next season looks leaner by construction, not accident. LIV's 2027 calendar is expected to shrink from 14 events to 10, with purses dropping below the current $30 million ceiling. Players would regain the right to compete on rival tours and to control their own name, image and likeness commercially — concessions that only make sense if LIV can no longer promise the exclusivity and money that lured its roster away from the PGA Tour in the first place. The Asian Tour, previously aligned with LIV, has already switched allegiance to a partnership with the PGA Tour and DP World Tour.

None of this moves markets in the conventional sense. But it is a data point in a pattern trading desks have watched across PIF's sports portfolio: headline-grabbing commitments — golf, football club stakes, Formula One-adjacent ventures — followed by quieter retrenchment once the fund's own capital allocation priorities shift, whether toward Vision Fund 2030 obligations or domestic Saudi projects competing for the same money. A sovereign fund's willingness to bankroll a loss-making sports league indefinitely was always a political choice more than an economic one, and political choices can be revisited.

The next marker is Indianapolis, August 20-23 — the tournament now carrying the weight of being LIV's de facto finale. Whether it delivers a functioning prize structure, and whether O'Neil's "lead investor" actually signs, will say more about the league's 2027 survival than anything from Michigan.
