Mount Etna eruption enters seventh day, Catania airport still shut

Mount Etna erupting at night, seen from the Catania waterfront during a previous eruptive episode. — Auregann / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0
In one sentence
Etna's longest eruption since the 1970s has kept Catania airport shut into a seventh day, cancelling 700 flights and costing Sicily's tourism sector up to €24m.

Mount Etna has been erupting continuously for seven days — not the intermittent lava fountains that usually punctuate a Sicilian summer, but a sustained outpouring that Italian volcanologists say has no real precedent since the 1970s. Catania's Fontanarossa airport, Sicily's principal gateway and Italy's fifth-busiest by passenger traffic, has kept its runways closed through the morning of 14 August, the latest extension of a shutdown that began on 6 August.

"It is not the volcano that is in the wrong place, but the airport."

So said Sicily's regional civil protection chief Nello Musumeci, reviving a debate that has simmered since 1999 over whether Catania's airport — built a few kilometres from an active volcano's summit — should ever have gone there at all. Nobody moved it then, and nobody will move it now, but the eruption has made the argument freshly literal.

What makes this eruption different

Etna is Europe's most active volcano and rarely goes long without some activity. What has changed is duration. Salvo Gambino, director of the INGV's Etnean Observatory, told reporters that the current phase shows nothing volcanologically novel — lava fountains, ash columns, effusive vents are all familiar Etna behaviour — but that in recent decades those episodes have typically burned themselves out within hours, half a day at most. This one has run for a week. Gambino's working theory is that magma rising from greater depth, and richer in dissolved gas, is sustaining the activity longer than the shallower, quicker-draining systems that have driven most 21st-century paroxysms.

Italy's National Institute of Geophysics and Volcanology has tracked the activity to vents at roughly 2,750, 2,360, 2,090 and 1,900 metres altitude, with the Voragine crater producing sustained lava fountaining and steady ash emission while a separate set of vents feeds a lava field into the Valle del Bove, the volcano's great eastern amphitheatre. INGV assesses the flows as posing no threat to inhabited areas — Etna's slopes are steep and its lava, for now, is going where nobody lives. The ash is a different matter: southerly winds have carried it over Malta, roughly 220 kilometres to the south, and as far as northern Libya, prompting Malta to postpone fireworks for a religious festival and reroute aircraft of its own.

The airport keeps losing the argument

Catania airport operator SAC has extended arrival and departure suspensions repeatedly since 6 August, most recently pushing the reopening to 8am local time on 14 August. Comiso airport, one of the alternates absorbing diverted traffic, has itself had to halt operations during the worst ash episodes. SAC has called the disruption the most severe hit to the airport's infrastructure in 20 years — not in physical damage, but in the sheer duration of a single closure event during the year's highest-traffic weeks.

The numbers give some sense of scale. Between 6 and 12 August, roughly 700 flights were cancelled outright and around 630 more were diverted, according to SAC — about 36% of Catania's scheduled flights over that stretch. Palermo absorbed some 330 of the redirected flights, Comiso more than 100, Trapani 22, with a handful pushed as far as Lamezia Terme on the Italian mainland. Sicily's regional tourism estimates put the economic hit at somewhere between €12 million and €24 million, spread across airlines rebooking passengers, hotels absorbing cancellations, and tour operators losing bookings at the peak of the season.

A hub with no real backup

What the past week has exposed is less about volcanology than about redundancy — or the lack of it. Catania handles the bulk of Sicily's air traffic; Palermo, Comiso and Trapani were never sized to absorb a sustained diversion of Catania's volume, and the strain has shown in delays and rebookings stacking up across all three. Airlines have had little choice but to eat the disruption: Etna's ash plumes are not predictable weeks in advance, and unlike a scheduled strike or a known seasonal risk, there's no contingency capacity built into Sicily's aviation network for a week-long closure at its main hub. The result has been an improvised, day-by-day reshuffling of flights that airport authorities themselves describe as unprecedented in two decades.

Oddly, the eruption has also been good business for one part of Sicily's tourism sector. Guides on Etna's slopes report crowds rivalling a Saturday in Taormina, with visitors pushing dangerously close to the unstable rim of the Valle del Bove to see the lava up close — enough to trigger multiple alpine rescue call-outs. The same event that is emptying arrivals halls is filling hiking trails.

What's next

INGV's daily bulletins are the immediate thing to watch: any drop-off in vent activity would let SAC start rebuilding Catania's schedule, while a shift in wind direction could just as easily extend the ash risk to Reggio Calabria or further afield. Longer term, Musumeci's question about the airport's location will likely resurface once the ash settles, as it has after past closures — with the same likely outcome of no action, given the cost and disruption of relocating a hub that serves millions of summer travellers to a volcano that, historically, erupts and subsides within days rather than weeks. This time it hasn't, and Catania is finding out what that costs.
