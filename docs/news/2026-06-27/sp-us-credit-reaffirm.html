S&P reaffirms US AA+/A-1+ sovereign rating with stable outlook

The US Treasury Building in Washington, D.C. — the fiscal anchor behind America's AA+ sovereign credit. — Rchuon24 / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
S&P held the US at AA+/A-1+ with a stable outlook, citing resilient growth and tariff revenues as offsets to deficits forecast to average 6% of GDP through 2029.

S&P Global Ratings affirmed the United States sovereign credit rating at AA+/A-1+ with a stable outlook on Friday, June 26, holding its assessment steady against a backdrop of elevated fiscal deficits, polarized politics, and a tax-cut package that could widen the debt path further.

The agency's anchor was straightforward: the US economy keeps growing, and growing economies generate tax receipts. S&P forecast real GDP expansion of 2.1% in 2026 and 1.9% in 2027, supported partly by AI-related capital investment and business spending incentivised by recent tax provisions. Tariff collections — up roughly 242% year-over-year as of mid-2025 — were also cited as a partial fiscal offset, with the agency expecting "meaningful tariff revenue to generally offset weaker fiscal outcomes" from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's tax-cut provisions.

The stable outlook is not a clean bill of health. S&P projects the general government deficit will average 6.0% of GDP between 2026 and 2029, lower than 2025's 6.9% but still wide by historical standards. Net government debt is expected to clear 100% of GDP by 2029, up from 96% in 2025. Interest costs are set to remain near 11.6% of government revenues — a figure that compounds uncomfortably as rates stay elevated.

The floor, not the ceiling

Friday's affirmation matters less as a validation than as a floor. Moody's stripped the US of its last AAA in May 2025, cutting to Aa1 — the equivalent of AA+. With all three major agencies now at the same effective tier, S&P's decision to hold removes the tail risk of a sudden single-agency gap forming beneath current valuations. That keeps Treasury collateral unchanged for most institutional portfolios and forestalls any mechanical repricing in credit spreads tied to sovereign floors.

For traders, the stable outlook also closes off the near-term downgrade scenario that had lingered as a vol-amplifying event risk, particularly given the One Big Beautiful Bill's uncertain fiscal arithmetic. S&P was explicit that the rating could come under pressure "if fiscal deficits widen further because lawmakers fail to curb spending or offset revenue losses" from the legislation — language that maps closely onto the deficit-hawk critique of the bill.

The agency acknowledged the political backdrop directly, noting "greater political polarization and sharper policy swings" than seen in many other highly rated sovereigns, and flagged that midterm election dynamics could constrain the administration's fiscal manoeuvring room heading into 2027.

What to watch

S&P's next scheduled review will be the next meaningful checkpoint. Between now and then, the variables are fiscal: whether tariff revenues hold at elevated levels as trade negotiations develop — see the DST tariff ultimatum playing out in parallel — whether OBBBA spending cuts actually materialise in Medicaid and discretionary lines, and whether the deficit trajectory for 2026 prints closer to the agency's 6.0% forecast or slips toward the 2025 pace. A sustained undershoot on growth, currently assumed at 2.1%, would tighten all three simultaneously. Inflation easing toward the 1.9% S&P expects by 2028 would ease the interest-cost pressure — but that path runs through a Federal Reserve that is, as of this week, contemplating rate hikes rather than cuts.
