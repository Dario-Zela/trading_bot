CENTCOM strikes Iran over Hormuz drone attack as crude slides below $69 despite the escalation

The Strait of Hormuz photographed from orbit during Space Shuttle mission STS-4, with ship wakes visible in the Persian Gulf. The waterway carries roughly 20% of global seaborne oil trade. — NASA / STS-4 crew
In one sentence
Iranian drones struck the Ever Lovely in the Hormuz Strait and CENTCOM retaliated — yet WTI crude settled near $69 as tanker flows resumed and markets bet on containment.

On the same day that U.S. warplanes struck targets in Iran, the price of crude oil fell. Not rose — fell. West Texas Intermediate futures settled near $69 a barrel on Friday, close to their lowest level since the war between the United States, Israel, and Iran began in late February. The contrast between the military exchange and the market's reaction captures something important about how sophisticated traders are reading the Strait of Hormuz crisis: not as a return to full conflict, but as a managed, if volatile, de-escalation.

Attack on the Ever Lovely

At 14:10 UTC on June 25, the Singapore-flagged containership Ever Lovely, operated by Taiwan's Evergreen Marine Corporation, was transiting the southern exit of the Strait of Hormuz when an Iranian drone struck her starboard side. The ship was approximately 7.5 nautical miles southeast of Dahit, Oman — just beyond the chokepoint through which roughly 20% of the world's seaborne oil trade passes. Bridge windows were shattered. There were no injuries. The main engine kept running, and the vessel continued on its way.

Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps had fired at least four one-way attack drones at ships in the strait, according to U.S. officials. Three were intercepted; the fourth found its mark. The attack came days after the International Maritime Organization activated a voluntary evacuation framework to help clear the hundreds of vessels that had been stranded in the Persian Gulf since hostilities began — a sign that the ceasefire agreed on April 8 was slowly, unevenly, reopening the waterway.

The following morning, Trump posted on Truth Social that the IRGC had committed "a foolish violation of our Ceasefire Agreement," citing Iran's use of "at least four One Way Attack Drones at Ships transversing the Strait of Hormuz." CENTCOM moved quickly: six land-based U.S. aircraft struck four Iranian military targets, including missile and drone storage facilities and coastal radar sites on Qeshm Island and along the strait's northern shore. It was the first direct American strike on Iran since the ceasefire extension was agreed the previous week.

The Paradox in the Price

What the market did with all of this was, on its face, counterintuitive. WTI futures for August settled at $69.23 a barrel, down 3.74% on the day. Brent crude fell 4.34% to $71.99. Both benchmarks were trading near their lowest levels since February 27 — the session before the conflict began and oil surged 10–13% to above $80.

The explanation lies in the data running alongside the headlines. Even as the Ever Lovely was absorbing a drone strike in the Gulf of Oman, ship-tracking services were logging a surge in tanker departures through the southern transit corridor. More than 20 oil tankers carrying approximately 35 million barrels of crude had passed through the strait in the preceding days as the ceasefire framework took effect. Persian Gulf exports had recovered to roughly 75% of pre-war levels. OPEC+ had pushed through nearly 600,000 barrels per day of additional quota increases across four rounds of hikes since the Hormuz closure, and some of that supply was finally reaching the market.

The IRGC drone attack, in this reading, was the act of an institution that has consistently resisted the ceasefire rather than a signal of renewed state-level hostilities. Market participants appear to have drawn that distinction sharply. The structural supply story — resuming tanker flows, rising OPEC+ quotas, recovering Gulf exports — outweighed an incident that damaged bridge windows on a container ship. When the IMO paused its evacuation operation immediately after the attack, prices barely flinched.

That calculus is defensible. It is also not without risk.

What the Market Is Betting On

The floor under this trade is the assumption that neither Washington nor Tehran wants a return to full hostilities. The evidence is the April ceasefire itself, the diplomatic architecture around it, and the fact that CENTCOM's Friday strikes — precise, limited, targeting military infrastructure — were calibrated not to escalate. Trump's tone was sharp but his public comments were non-committal. Iran issued no immediate retaliatory threat.

Yet the Strait of Hormuz is not yet free in any commercial sense. The IRGC has insisted on controlling designated transit routes; vessels are moving, but on Tehran's terms. For buyers of physical crude and the companies that carry it, war-risk insurance premiums remain elevated. For Shell and BP — both already squeezed at these price levels — any renewed disruption to flows would land at a particularly uncomfortable moment. The Baker Hughes rig count has been edging higher, with seven additional U.S. rigs active this week bringing the total to 440, but domestic production gains take months to offset a chokepoint closure.

The test will come not in how markets priced Friday's session, but in whether the diplomatic architecture holds through the next provocation. Washington and Tehran have now exchanged fire once since the ceasefire was extended. The drone that hit the Ever Lovely was a single strike from an institution that has every incentive to sabotage the reopening. The question the forward curve does not yet answer is how many such strikes the framework can absorb before one side concludes the ceasefire has already failed.
