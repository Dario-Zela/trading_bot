Shell and BP face free-cash-flow squeeze as Brent breaks below $69

A drilling platform in the North Sea — the region where both Shell and BP hold major upstream assets. — Erik Christensen / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Brent's dip below $69 puts Shell and BP below the $70/bbl planning threshold underpinning both companies' FCF guidance and shareholder-return commitments.

Brent crude broke through the $69 per barrel mark on Friday, dropping below the $70 planning benchmark that both Shell and BP anchor their free-cash-flow guidance to. For Shell, still running an active buyback tranche through to late July, the move tightens return metrics without threatening the dividend. For BP, whose strategic reset pledges were already tested by the oil-price whipsaw of the past six months, the timing is sharper.

Below the benchmark

Neither company has set $69 as a contractual trigger. What matters is that $70/bbl Brent is the shared reference point for forward guidance at both majors. Shell's March 2025 Capital Markets Day normalised return on average capital employed to $70 per barrel and anchored its long-range cash-flow projections on the same figure. BP was more explicit: two of its headline "reset" pledges — to lift cash flow by more than 20 per cent annually through to 2027, and to push returns on average capital employed above 16 per cent — required Brent above $70. Friday's close through $69 means both companies are operating below those stated planning assumptions.

The proximate cause was supply, not demand. Shipping transits through the Strait of Hormuz have accelerated as progress toward a US–Iran diplomatic settlement allowed Persian Gulf exports to resume at scale. Saudi Aramco has restarted loading at Ras Tanura as OPEC+ quotas rise. Brent has shed more than 10 per cent over the past week — a move that has stripped away the war-risk premium that briefly pushed prices above $100 in the first quarter.

That premium had been good while it lasted. Brent averaged roughly $81/bbl in the January-to-March period, lifting Shell's free cash flow to $2.9 billion for the quarter and helping BP more than double its underlying replacement-cost profit to $3.2 billion. The second-quarter read, due from both companies in late July, will reflect a sharply different environment.

Asymmetric exposure

The two majors are not equally positioned. Shell's dividend breakeven sits well below current prices — analysts put it near $40/bbl — and the company stated at its Capital Markets Day that buybacks would remain substantial down to $60. Shell repurchased shares as recently as 11 June; the current tranche continues through 24 July. At $69, the question is one of pace, not programme survival.

BP is more exposed. The company suspended its buyback entirely at fourth-quarter 2025 results in February before reinstating limited repurchases in Q1 2026. Net debt climbed to $25.3 billion by end-March, up from $22.2 billion at the end of 2024; management targets $14–18 billion by end-2027. That trajectory assumed commodity prices closer to its $70 planning floor. BP reaffirmed $13–13.5 billion in 2026 capital expenditure and $9–10 billion in divestment proceeds — commitments that look credible but leave little room for the kind of price softness now materialising.

The Q2 earnings reports in late July will be the first formal occasion to revisit guidance. If Brent holds below $70 through June — which the current trajectory suggests — investors should expect conservative language on buyback volumes from BP and tighter capital-return framing from Shell. BP's 2027 free-cash-flow and ROCE targets, already stretched, will face pointed questioning.
