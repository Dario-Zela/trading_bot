AI rout exposes Wall Street's $270 billion leverage machine as chip stocks drag indices globally

The New York Stock Exchange trading floor — where a week of AI and semiconductor pressure closed with defensive rotation in the final hour — Eduard Hueber / Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 3.0)
In one sentence
Bloomberg counts $270 billion in leveraged AI-equity products globally — amplifiers that turn a sector downturn into forced selling — as chip stocks drag Nasdaq to its fifth straight losing session.

The week ended where it began: under pressure. By Friday's close in New York, the Nasdaq Composite had posted its fifth consecutive losing session, dipping 0.24% on the day and off more than 4% on a week in which the artificial intelligence trade — the engine of equity market gains for the better part of eighteen months — showed sustained signs of exhaustion. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, the closest proxy for the AI infrastructure boom, sat near correction territory, roughly 10% below its June peak.

That headline number barely captures what was unfolding beneath the surface. Bloomberg, drawing on global fund data published Thursday, reported that the sector's wobble had exposed a $270 billion machine of leveraged and speculative products built almost entirely around AI equities. This was not passive money rotating out of a crowded trade. In many cases, it was structured capital compelled to sell.

The Amplifier

The $270 billion refers to assets inside leveraged exchange-traded funds globally — products that use derivatives to deliver daily multiples of an underlying index's return. The US market accounts for more than $200 billion of that total; Asia, primarily South Korea, contributes a further $45 billion or more. These funds were engineered for short-term tactical bets. Retail adoption has turned many of them into persistent, semi-permanent positions in the AI ecosystem.

The mechanism is what makes the figure consequential. Every down day in the underlying requires these products to rebalance at the session's end, mechanically selling assets that have already fallen. Barclays estimated that rebalancing by US leveraged ETFs has recently run to "several times its long-term average" — a pace potentially large enough to be market-moving on its own. The clearest illustration came on June 23, when two sets of 2x leveraged Korean ETFs tracking Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix sold roughly $6 billion in shares in a single afternoon. Not because managers chose to; their mandates required it.

James St. Aubin of Ocean Park Asset Management put it plainly: "These products bring a casino element into the market." The structural concern is narrower and more specific: a $270 billion asset base now sits as a mechanical amplifier capable of converting an AI sentiment shift into a forced-liquidation cascade — one that does not require a fundamental deterioration to trigger.

Atlantic Transmission

The contagion did not respect time zones. European markets closed lower on Friday, with Germany's DAX shedding 1.25%, the CAC 40 in Paris off 0.55%, and the UK's FTSE 100 dipping 0.21%. Tech-weighted European names bore the worst of it. ASML Holding, the Dutch maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography equipment and the single most critical bottleneck in global chipmaking, fell 3.08% on Friday alone — and that followed a 7.60% decline the Monday prior, when South Korea's KOSPI plunged 10% and triggered a circuit-breaker halt.

ARM Holdings, listed in both London and New York, joined the declines. Neither company had company-specific catalysts on Friday. Both were transmission nodes for a momentum reversal that originated in US semiconductor positioning.

For investors who had treated European chip names as partial insulation from US AI volatility, the week served as a corrective. The AI infrastructure narrative is sufficiently global — and European chipmakers sufficiently integrated into its supply chain — that sector shocks in US semiconductors route through Amsterdam and Frankfurt with minimal delay.

Breadth, and the Question of What Comes Next

The more durable concern among strategists is not the daily close but the shape of what lies beneath it. Market breadth has been contracting even as headline indices held elevated levels. By mid-week, just 21 stocks in the S&P 500 were registering new 52-week highs — a reading that implies an index sustained by a thin layer of mega-cap weight while the broader market quietly stalled. The Nasdaq 100's relative strength indicator fell to 48%, down from readings above 80% in late May, marking the clearest momentum break in the AI trade since the April 2025 correction.

Friday's final trading hour saw flows shift visibly toward consumer staples and other defensive names — the kind of late-session, end-of-week repositioning that reads more like portfolio hedging than directional conviction. Three signals have now aligned in a pattern some strategists have called a potential regime shift: the semiconductor sector's break in outperformance; narrowing breadth; and growing positioning in rate-hedging instruments as the Federal Reserve signals a credibly hawkish posture.

With US inflation running above 4% and Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari signalling the possibility of rate increases before year-end, the cost of carry on leveraged AI positions is rising precisely as the underlying assets correct. Reports this week that OpenAI may delay its IPO to 2027 — citing SpaceX's post-debut volatility as a cautionary signal for market conditions — drained additional speculative air from the trade. OpenAI's listing had been one of the more concrete near-term catalysts priced into AI-linked valuations through the first half.

The next hard data points are Q2 semiconductor earnings, beginning in mid-July. Guidance from Nvidia and Broadcom will either confirm the infrastructure spending narrative or add fundamental weight to what has so far been a structural and momentum-driven unwind. Until then, the $270 billion leverage machine and its daily rebalancing mechanics remain a floor risk that the broader market has not fully priced.
