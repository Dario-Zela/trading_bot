US, Israel and Lebanon sign trilateral framework after four-day Washington talks

In one sentence
Israel, Lebanon and the US signed a performance-based trilateral framework in Washington, making Hezbollah disarmament a precondition for any Israeli withdrawal.

At the State Department in Washington on Friday, three diplomats — Israeli Ambassador Yechiel Leiter, Lebanese Ambassador Nada Hamadeh Moawad and State Department Counselor Daniel Holler — picked up pens and signed a document that their countries have spent most of the past four decades failing to produce: a shared framework for peace. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, looking on, described it as "the beginning of the beginning." That careful phrasing was deliberate.

The Israel-Lebanon Trilateral Framework, completed after four days of intensive US-mediated talks, is not a peace treaty. It is not a ceasefire in the conventional sense. It is a conditional road map — a sequenced set of obligations that each party must fulfil before the next step unlocks. Whether that architecture holds is the only question that matters.

"Today is a good day in that we are happy to announce a framework agreement that begins to put in place a framework for lasting peace and security." — Secretary of State Marco Rubio

A sequenced, conditional architecture

The agreement's central logic is performance-based. Lebanon's army must first restore "effective sovereign authority over all Lebanese territory, pending the verified disarmament of non-state armed groups" — the framework's careful circumlocution for Hezbollah. Only once that disarmament is verified will Israel "progressively redeploy" its forces. There is no fixed calendar: Israeli Ambassador Leiter made clear the withdrawal will not be scheduled in advance but triggered by compliance.

To manage the process, the three countries have established a Trilateral Military Coordination Group for Lebanon — the MCG4L — facilitated by Washington. The group will oversee the framework's first concrete tests: two "pilot zones," one north and one south of the Litani River, where Lebanon's army will begin gradually assuming full security responsibility. Once Hezbollah's disarmament is confirmed in those areas, reconstruction efforts begin.

The financial architecture backs the ambition. The United States has committed $100 million in immediate humanitarian assistance through the UN, along with more than $30 million in reimbursements to the Lebanese Armed Forces to support their expanded security role.

The missing party

The framework's structural problem is that its primary target had no seat at the table. Hezbollah was absent from the Washington talks and has responded with characteristic hostility: the group demands unconditional Israeli withdrawal, and one of its parliamentary members warned that forcing the Lebanese army to enforce disarmament could trigger civil war.

Israel currently occupies roughly one-fifth of Lebanese territory. Netanyahu, while welcoming the deal as "a severe blow to Iran," was explicit that Israeli forces will remain deployed "as long as Hezbollah is not disarmed." That is a condition the other signatories cannot themselves deliver. Israeli Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, meanwhile, signalled ambitions that go beyond what the framework envisages, describing the need for "defendable borders." Israeli air strikes continued in Lebanon on the day the agreement was signed.

The timing sharpens the regional stakes. The same week that Leiter and Hamadeh Moawad signed in Washington, US CENTCOM carried out strikes against Iran following a drone attack near the Strait of Hormuz. The framework's anti-Iran subtext — Ambassador Leiter declared "Iran is out, Hezbollah is out" at the ceremony — lands in a context where Tehran is simultaneously absorbing direct US military pressure.

Analysts are divided on what that means for durability. Hanin Ghaddar of the Washington Institute for Near East Policy called it "absolutely a breakthrough," but warned that Iran "will try everything to make sure this one fails as well." Walid Phares was blunter: this is "just a ceasefire, not a conduit to a larger peace deal." The last comparable US-brokered accord — the May 17, 1983 agreement between Israel and Lebanon — was abrogated by Beirut within a year, under Syrian and Iranian pressure, without ever being implemented. That precedent hangs over every cautious formulation in Friday's text.

Washington's calculation — and Riyadh's silence

The Trump administration has been pursuing an expanded Abraham Accords framework since early 2025, with Saudi Arabia as the strategic prize. Trump suggested in May that Israel's neighbours should be required to recognise the Jewish state as part of any broader regional settlement. Friday's agreement is unlikely to accelerate that directly.

Saudi Arabia made no statement on the signing and has given no signal of imminent engagement. Riyadh's strategic calculus runs through Palestinian statehood, not Lebanese stability, and the kingdom has held to that position despite sustained American pressure. The 2020 Abraham Accords, which normalised relations between Israel and the UAE and Bahrain, did not trigger the cascade analysts initially expected; no third Gulf state followed in the years since.

What the Lebanon framework may offer is a more modest signal: that US-brokered, performance-based architecture can produce signed agreements even between countries technically still at war. Whether markets price that as durable depends on what happens next in two small zones south of the Litani River.

The first operational test will come when the MCG4L convenes and Lebanese armed forces begin their initial deployment into the pilot zones. That is the moment when process becomes performance — and when regional capitals, investors watching Middle East equities, and Hezbollah itself will form their first real read on whether this framework has any traction at all.
