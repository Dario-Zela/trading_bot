Unilever in $4 billion talks for Thorne Health as UBS questions the acquisition logic

Unilever House on the Victoria Embankment in London, the company's global headquarters. — Wikimedia Commons / Cmglee (CC BY-SA 4.0)
In one sentence
Unilever is exploring a $4B bid for supplements brand Thorne, a six-fold markup on L Catterton's 2023 buy-in, as investors question the logic of the group's parallel $45B McCormick food deal.

Unilever is in active talks to acquire Thorne, a US-based dietary supplements company, in a deal that could value the brand at up to $4 billion — roughly six times what private equity firm L Catterton paid to take it private in October 2023. The Financial Times first reported the discussions on June 26. Unilever, Thorne, and L Catterton all declined to comment.

The bid, if completed, would be the largest single acquisition of CEO Fernando Fernandez's tenure and one of the clearest expressions yet of where Unilever thinks the next decade of consumer spending goes.

A six-fold premium and a Gen Z following

Thorne was founded in 1984 in Summerville, South Carolina, and spent most of its life as a practitioner-facing supplement brand trusted by physicians and athletes for strict manufacturing standards. L Catterton paid $680 million for it three years ago; today L Catterton is running a formal sale process with a four-billion-dollar ask. That arithmetic — a near-600% markup in 36 months — tells most of the story.

The business has justified it. Revenue more than doubled from $229 million in 2022 and the brand is now on pace for $650 million in 2026, sustained by a compound annual growth rate of roughly 30% since the L Catterton acquisition. More than 60% of Thorne's seven million customers are under 40, a demographic that is spending approximately 1.5 times what their parents did on wellness products. The brand has expanded into AI-powered personalisation tools and has been named to Time's 100 Most Influential Companies list for 2026.

Unilever is not the only bidder. Haleon — the consumer healthcare group that sells Advil and Centrum vitamins — has also submitted a bid, according to reports, making this a genuine competitive process rather than a negotiation with a single counterpart. The US supplements market was valued at $68.7 billion in 2025 and is projected to roughly double by 2033; the category is drawing capital from multiple directions simultaneously.

Portfolio in motion

The Thorne bid is not an isolated move. It arrives as Unilever executes one of the more radical portfolio reshapes in the company's recent history.

On the exit side: Unilever agreed to merge its foods division — which includes Hellmann's, Knorr, and several smaller brands — with McCormick & Company in a deal valued at roughly $45 billion, announced in March 2026. The ice cream unit has been spun off. Brands including Graze and The Vegetarian Butcher have been divested.

On the acquisition side: Unilever spent April adding Grüns, a US greens-supplement brand with roughly $500 million in annualised revenue. The Thorne bid, if concluded, would dwarf that. The group already holds Nutrafol, SmartyPants Vitamins, Olly Nutrition, and Liquid I.V. — a wellness portfolio built through acquisition over the past five years.

Fernandez, who took over as CEO in March 2025, has set a specific target: lifting the share of Unilever's sales coming from beauty, wellbeing, and personal care from approximately 52% today to 66%. Thorne, at $650 million in revenue and growing at 30%, would move that needle materially.

The market's doubts

None of this has played smoothly with investors.

Unilever's shares dropped roughly 13% in the weeks around the McCormick announcement. Terry Smith, one of the company's most prominent long-term shareholders, sold his entire position — a stake worth hundreds of millions of pounds — characterising the McCormick deal as evidence that management had "abandoned its promised operational focus in favour of activist-driven break-ups." Morningstar analyst Svetlana Menshchikova questioned the transaction on similar grounds: the Foods division carried a 22% operating margin in 2024-25, matching Unilever's personal care segment and outperforming others, so the strategic case for shedding it required more than the generic argument that focused companies trade at higher multiples.

UBS's note on McCormick, published ahead of the spice maker's second-quarter earnings, adds texture to the unease. Analyst Peter Grom maintained a Neutral rating, flagged that "demand trends have slowed for MKC," and warned that "the overhang from the pending Unilever transaction is likely to remain until deal close is in sight." Grom acknowledged the valuation looked interesting at current levels but said greater fundamental visibility was needed before becoming constructive — code, in analyst language, for a deal thesis that hasn't resolved its uncertainties.

The tension sits at the centre of Unilever's current position. Consumer spending in the US and Europe is under genuine pressure; the company guided its 2026 full-year organic growth to the bottom of its 4%-to-6% multi-year range, citing sluggish household finances. Against that backdrop, Unilever is proposing to pay a premium for premium brands — betting that consumers who are tightening elsewhere will protect their wellness budgets. That is a coherent view of how consumer preference hierarchies have shifted since 2020, but it is a view that runs directly into the macro environment the company is simultaneously citing as a risk.

At $4 billion and six times the acquisition price, Thorne is not a test of the thesis. It is a full commitment to it.

What to watch

The sale process for Thorne is competitive, with Haleon still in the picture. A deal announcement, if one comes, is likely to arrive within weeks given the public reporting. The McCormick merger remains subject to regulatory review and shareholder votes; its completion — or any complications — will define the shape of the new Unilever more clearly than any single acquisition. Investors will also watch Unilever's second-half 2026 trading results to see whether premium wellness margins are holding as the consumer environment softens. If they are, the $4 billion for Thorne will look prescient. If household spending continues to weaken across income segments, the question of why Unilever exited a profitable foods business to chase a crowded premium category will grow louder.
