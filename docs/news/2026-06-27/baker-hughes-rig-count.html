Baker Hughes US oil rig count rises seven to 440 as gas rigs also gain

A rotary drilling rig in the Williston Basin oil field — the type of onshore equipment tracked in the Baker Hughes weekly count — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Baker Hughes data shows US oil rigs rose 7 to 440 and gas rigs gained 3 to 125 in the week of June 26, defying a ~28% crude price slide from May highs.

Baker Hughes released its weekly North America rig count on Friday, showing that US oil operators added seven rotary rigs in the week ending June 26, bringing the total oil rig count to 440 — the highest in several months. Natural gas rigs also climbed, rising three to 125. The combined gain of ten rigs across both categories signals that upstream capital activity is edging higher even as the crude market has moved in the opposite direction.

The timing is striking. Brent crude has fallen roughly 28% from its early-May peak above $105 per barrel, settling near $75 as of late last week. WTI, the US benchmark, has slid toward the high $60s, a level that sits at or below the breakeven threshold many shale operators cite for new well economics. By the usual logic of upstream capital allocation, a price drop of that magnitude should prompt producers to defer new drilling commitments, not add rigs.

The Price-Rig Gap

There are two plausible explanations for the divergence, and they carry different implications.

The first is timing. Decisions to deploy rigs are typically made weeks or months in advance, with contracts, crew schedules and pad preparation locked in before a drill bit touches rock. The rig additions reported Friday may reflect commitments made in April or May, when WTI was trading well above $80 and producers were responding to what looked like a sustained geopolitical supply shock driven by tensions in the Strait of Hormuz. Prices have since retreated sharply as ceasefire progress restored Gulf shipping flows and Saudi Arabia signalled a supply ramp-up, but the rigs were already in motion.

The second explanation is producer confidence in a price recovery. Some operators — particularly those with low-cost Permian Basin acreage concentrated through the wave of 2024-25 consolidation among majors — can tolerate prolonged softness at current levels. If their internal price decks assume a return toward $80 over a six-to-twelve-month horizon, adding incremental capacity now looks rational even against a weak spot price.

The natural gas side of the ledger is somewhat cleaner. LNG export demand has remained firm, and Henry Hub futures have held above the $3.50 level that underpins most domestic gas drilling economics. A three-rig gain in gas-directed activity is consistent with producers following through on export-linked supply commitments, largely uncorrelated with crude.

What to Watch

If oil prices stabilize near current levels — Brent in the mid-$70s, WTI in the upper $60s — the more sensitive question is whether this week's additions prove to be the last gasp of the May investment cycle or the start of a renewed push. Baker Hughes data for the week of July 4 will be released a day late due to the holiday; market participants will watch it as the first clean signal of whether the price-rig gap is closing from the bottom up or the top down.

The broader pressure on free cash flow facing integrated majors — Shell and BP in particular have flagged tightening margins as Brent tests the $69 floor — could eventually constrain even disciplined operators. For now, the rig count tells a story of momentum not yet broken.
