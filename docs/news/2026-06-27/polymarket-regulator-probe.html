US financial regulator investigating prediction market platform Polymarket

Polymarket, the world's largest prediction market platform, has logged more than $33 billion in trading volume so far in 2026. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
The CFTC is probing Polymarket over an influencer fake-bet campaign targeting banned US users, raising precedent-setting questions for the entire prediction market sector.

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission is conducting an extensive investigation into Polymarket, the prediction market platform that has logged more than $33 billion in trading volume in 2026, according to multiple people familiar with the matter — the latest escalation in a regulatory saga that has followed the company since its founding.

The CFTC probe comes roughly a week after a Wall Street Journal investigation documented a sprawling fake-bet campaign that appears to have been designed specifically to recruit American users to a platform that is prohibited from serving them. Journalists reviewed 1,105 promotional videos published between December 2025 and May 2026 and found that roughly 70 percent depicted creators placing trades not on Polymarket's actual site but on near-identical dummy websites, including one built on the lookalike domain "poiymarket.com." The 118 videos that displayed winnings showed approximately $900,000 in fabricated profits; the same trades, had they been placed on the real platform, would have lost more than $166,000.

The influencer campaign, run through a marketing agency called Virality, paid mostly college-aged content creators $2,000–$3,000 a month to film the fake trades. Creators were explicitly selected for having at least 60 percent American viewership — a significant tension given that Polymarket formally bars US users following a 2022 CFTC settlement in which the company paid a $1.4 million penalty for operating an unregistered derivatives exchange and agreed to wind down domestic operations.

Polymarket said it is "conducting a comprehensive audit of active promotional content to ensure it complies with our standards, as well as applicable regulatory and legal disclosure requirements." That statement has not addressed the central allegation: that the campaign was targeting an audience the company is legally prohibited from serving.

A sector-wide stress test

The investigation carries implications well beyond Polymarket itself. Prediction markets have navigated a persistent regulatory ambiguity in the United States, sitting uncomfortably between CFTC-regulated derivatives trading and state-administered gambling law. Platforms like Kalshi have spent years seeking formal CFTC designation as legitimate exchanges; enforcement action against Polymarket adds pressure to that entire framework.

Bipartisan concern is mounting. Senators John Curtis (R-UT) and Adam Schiff (D-CA) sent a letter to CFTC Chair Michael Selig this week pressing the agency on whether it is investigating the conduct described in the Journal report and demanding a written response by July 10. The senators also questioned whether the CFTC has adequate authority, expertise, and resources to oversee prediction markets compared with state gaming regulators — a challenge that strikes at the sector's fundamental regulatory positioning.

The scale of the market makes the stakes concrete. Combined global prediction market volume hit roughly $24 billion a month in April 2026, overtaking US legal sportsbook wagering of approximately $14 billion a month. Enforcement action that restricts access or liquidity on major platforms would remove a data source that quantitative strategies in adjacent markets have come to treat as a real-time sentiment signal.

The CFTC's July 10 congressional deadline is the nearest visible catalyst. Formal charges, if they materialise, would likely raise the harder question: whether prediction markets can be shoehorned into the existing US derivatives framework at all, or whether Congress needs to settle the matter explicitly.
