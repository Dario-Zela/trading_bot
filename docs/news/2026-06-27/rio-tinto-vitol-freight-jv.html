Rio Tinto in talks with Vitol over bulk-commodity freight joint venture

A Handymax bulk carrier at sea — the class of dry bulk vessel central to Rio Tinto's iron ore freight programme — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Rio Tinto is in early talks with Vitol to create a freight joint venture, seeking to hedge shipping costs on the 250 million tonnes of iron ore it sends to China each year.

Rio Tinto's hunt for cheaper freight has led the mining giant to Vitol Group, the world's largest independent commodity trader. The two are in early-stage discussions over a joint venture to manage shipping costs across Rio's bulk-commodity supply chain — a move that, if completed, would give the miner access to freight infrastructure and risk management tools it has conspicuously lacked.

Details are scarce. Both companies declined to comment. No timeline, deal terms or scope have been disclosed, and it remains unclear what any venture would ultimately look like. What is clear is the direction: Rio's chief executive Simon Trott, who took over last August, has staked his tenure on wringing cost from the business. He reorganised Rio into three operating divisions, is targeting $5–10 billion from asset disposals and had already delivered a first $650 million tranche of annualised productivity savings by Q1. A freight partnership fits neatly into that agenda.

The shipping problem

Rio ships roughly 250 million tonnes of iron ore to China every year, making it one of the largest participants in the global dry bulk market. For a company working on thin per-tonne margins in a down commodity cycle, unhedged exposure to spot freight rates is a material risk — a single percentage-point move in average shipping costs across that programme can swing earnings by hundreds of millions of dollars annually.

Vitol, already a supplier of fuel to Rio's Simandou project in Guinea and its Australian operations, brings formidable credentials: some 350 million tonnes of crude moved each year, a dry bulk desk that traded around 25 million tonnes of coal and 15 million tonnes of metals last year, and the derivatives infrastructure to hedge around all of it. One area under active discussion is precisely that — freight risk management, potentially including derivative trading within Rio's logistics operations.

What the venture is not: a commodities marketing alliance. Vitol would not be selling Rio's iron ore or copper. The scope, as currently conceived, is confined to logistics and risk.

The talks arrive five months after Rio walked away from merger discussions with Glencore, a deal that collapsed in February over valuation and governance disagreements. That failed bid was an attempt to acquire a full commodity trading platform wholesale; the Vitol approach is narrower — closer to renting capability than buying it outright. Whether that more modest ambition proves easier to execute depends on what Vitol wants from the arrangement and how far Rio is prepared to open its supply chain to an outside operator.

The next marker: a memorandum of understanding, or any public acknowledgement from either company that talks are live. For now, silence from both sides.
