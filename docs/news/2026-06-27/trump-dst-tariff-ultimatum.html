DIGITAL SERVICES TAX · By M. Halloway · 2026-06-27 · 6 min read

Trump threatens 100% tariffs on every nation that imposes a digital services tax on US firms

President Trump displays a tariff chart at the White House — the same confrontational trade posture now directed at digital services tax regimes across Europe. — White House / Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
Trump threatened immediate 100% tariffs on all goods from any country that taxes US tech firms, voiding existing trade deals — putting the EU and UK squarely in the crosshairs.

President Donald Trump escalated his running confrontation with European fiscal policy on Friday, posting to Truth Social that any country which taxes American digital companies will face an immediate 100 percent levy on everything it ships to the United States. The message was blunt and absolute: "Please let this statement serve to represent that any Country that imposes such a Tax will immediately be met with a 100% TARIFF on any and all Goods sent to the United States of America."

That sentence carries a clause most trade negotiators will read twice: "This TARIFF will supersede Trade Deals made with the Country, whether implemented, signed, or not." In practice, Trump is threatening to tear up any existing bilateral arrangement the moment a trading partner collects revenue from Google, Amazon, Meta, or Apple.

Any Country that imposes such a Tax will immediately be met with a 100% TARIFF on any and all Goods sent to the United States of America. This TARIFF will supersede Trade Deals made with the Country, whether implemented, signed, or not.
— Donald Trump, Truth Social, June 26, 2026

Eight Years of Friction, One Post

Digital services taxes have been a transatlantic irritant since France first mooted a 3 percent levy on large-platform revenues in 2018. The logic was intuitive: companies like Meta and Alphabet generate hundreds of millions in local ad revenue without a taxable physical presence under traditional corporate frameworks. The politics were always more complicated — because the companies most affected happen to be American.

Today roughly half of all European OECD members have announced, proposed, or implemented a DST. France collects 3 percent and raised €680 million in 2023, an increase of more than 80 percent from its 2020 debut. Italy (3 percent, €434 million in 2023) and Spain (3 percent, €345 million) have similar structures. Beyond Europe, Austria, Hungary, Turkey and others have joined the cohort. The firms in the crosshairs — Apple, Amazon, Meta, Google — are not marginal contributors to those treasuries.

Trump has never disguised his view of these levies. An executive order signed in February 2025 designated digital service taxes by Austria, Canada, France, Italy, Spain, Turkey, and the United Kingdom as "extraterritorial" and "unfair" and directed formal investigation under trade law. Friday's Truth Social post transforms that bureaucratic finding into an explicit ultimatum.

Brussels Defends Its Tax Base

The European Commission's response arrived within hours and was diplomatically firm without offering any exit ramp. A Commission spokesperson declared that member states retain "full sovereign rights to tax economic activity on their territory regardless of a company's national origin," framing existing DSTs as non-discriminatory measures applied equally to all large platforms. "Unilateral measures targeting such legitimate policies are unjustified," the statement read. "If pursued, the EU will respond swiftly and decisively to defend its rights and regulatory autonomy."

Brussels also gestured toward the multilateral process it would prefer: "The EU has consistently supported a global solution to the fair taxation of the digital economy, in line with the G7 finance ministers' conclusions. That remains our preferred path."

The timing is particularly charged. The EU and the United States finalized a trade deal last month that caps tariffs on most EU exports at 15 percent — a fragile détente that explicitly excluded digital taxation from its scope. Trump has set July 4 as a deadline for EU compliance with that arrangement. The DST threat, arriving eight days before that deadline, adds a second pressure front to negotiations that are already delicate.

The OECD's "Pillar One" framework — designed to replace national digital taxes with a coordinated global system — remains stalled. A parallel "side-by-side" package released in January 2026 addressed US concerns about the global minimum tax (Pillar Two) but did not resolve the DST impasse. Without a multilateral landing zone, Europe's unilateral levies and Washington's unilateral tariff threats continue to fill the vacuum.

Starmer Caught in the Crossfire

Of all the countries named in Trump's February 2025 executive order, Britain's situation is the most exposed — not because its DST is the largest, but because the UK is simultaneously trying to rebuild a post-Brexit trade relationship with Washington.

The UK has levied a 2 percent digital services tax since 2020, a narrower rate than France or Italy but one that raised more than £800 million in fiscal year 2024-25, up from £678 million the year before. The Treasury describes it as a business-agnostic revenue measure that "applies to companies, not countries" — the kind of formulation that satisfies domestic legal scrutiny but cuts little ice in the White House.

Chancellor Rachel Reeves has so far resisted dropping the tax under American pressure, and she has a population behind her: a Fair Tax Foundation survey found 67 percent of UK respondents believe the government should levy higher digital taxes on multinationals, not lower ones. But Keir Starmer's government is also hunting for a trade deal with Washington that would ease post-Brexit friction, and the DST has emerged as a bargaining chip neither side can easily ignore. Previous administrations accepted a truce on the issue; the Trump White House is disinclined to extend that courtesy.

Legal Fog Over the Tariff Cannon

For markets, the central question is whether the threat is executable. On that score, Friday's post raises more questions than it answers.

In February 2026, the Supreme Court ruled that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act — the statute Trump used for his sweeping reciprocal tariffs — does not authorize presidential tariff imposition. That decision invalidated the 15 percent EU baseline tariff and the "fentanyl" levies on Canada and Mexico. A 100 percent DST tariff applied globally would face the same constitutional scrutiny.

The administration is exploring Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 as a fallback authority. Section 301 allows the USTR to investigate and respond to foreign government practices that burden or discriminate against US commerce — a description that arguably fits a tax levied disproportionately on American platforms. But Section 301 actions require investigation periods and formal findings; they are not the "immediate" instrument the Truth Social post describes.

What the post accomplishes, regardless of legal enforceability, is to inject DST risk into portfolio positions that had treated tech-sector taxation as a slow-moving regulatory story rather than a live tariff threat. EUR/USD cross trades, European consumer-tech equity exposure, and trade-sensitive manufacturing sectors in Germany and France all carry new headline risk heading into July 4.

The next concrete data point is that deadline itself. If the EU balks at implementing the May trade deal's 15 percent cap — or if any member state moves to expand its digital levy in the interim — the question of whether Trump's tariff authority can survive judicial review will move from academic to urgent.
