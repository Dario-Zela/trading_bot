Ministers float taxpayer funding for net zero and a state-backed body to invest in the grid

In one sentence
Ministers are weighing moving green levies from bills to taxes and creating a state-owned Great British Grid, raising questions over household bills, the Exchequer and listed network returns.

The UK government is weighing two changes that both bear on who pays for the electricity system. The first would move some of the cost of the green transition off household bills and onto general taxation. The second is a state-owned body, Great British Grid, that would invest in the power network for the first time since privatisation. Neither has been set out with a price tag. Together they point to a rethink of a settlement that has run for roughly four decades: private companies own the wires, and customers repay the capital through regulated bills.

The taxpayer question

Miatta Fahnbulleh, the Secretary for Energy Security and Net Zero, has said the cost of the green transition could be spread among taxpayers. Reporting describes her as examining how green projects are funded, including varying levies between customers and paying for them entirely from general taxation.

The status of the idea matters more than its detail. The reporting describes an exploration, not legislation or a white-paper commitment. It gives no percentage of costs to be moved and no annual Exchequer figure. Any figure quoted at this stage is therefore an assumption.

The scale of the problem is better documented. Officials have put the cost of the grid upgrade at no less than £70bn over the next five years. Ofgem has said roughly that sum is needed between 2025 and 2031, about four times the current rate of investment. Levies added to the average annual bill are set to climb from £44 to £105. Moving them to the Treasury would lower the bill but not the cost. It would relocate the cost to income tax, borrowing or both, at a time when fiscal headroom is already a constraint.

The distribution effects run in a known direction. Bill levies fall on households in proportion to their electricity use, which is regressive because energy takes a larger share of a low income. Income tax is progressive, so a switch would probably help lower earners and leave higher earners worse off. Fahnbulleh has not accepted that net zero policy raises bills. That line is hard to square with a review of how to take levies off them.

A state-owned competitor

The second thread came from Prime Minister Andy Burnham, who announced Great British Grid. It will sit within Great British Energy and use money previously allocated to that body. It is described as the first publicly owned player in electricity networks since privatisation. It would compete with privately owned operators such as National Grid, and it is meant to speed up connections and renewables rollout. The stated ambition is to bring UK energy costs down to European levels within a decade. Fahnbulleh said it would "help speed up connections, unlock investment and boost economies across our nation."

The funding is not new money. It is a redirection of existing Great British Energy resources, so the initial scale looks modest against a £70bn requirement. The signal is larger than the sum. Listed network companies, chiefly National Grid, along with Scottish Power and SSE in northern Scotland, earn regulated returns on an asset base that grows as they invest. The regulator's allowed return depends partly on the perceived stability of the regime. A public competitor for some of that investment, from a government that speaks of rolling back privatisation, raises the risk that the growth in the asset base is shared or redirected.

The coverage reviewed here does not report any share price move in the listed networks. Nothing there shows that the market has priced either proposal.

What the two ideas share

Both proposals move the burden from customers to the state. One does so on the funding side, the other on the ownership side. Both also weaken the link on which regulated utilities depend, in which the bill payer's money is the revenue and the regulator sets the return.

If levies go to taxation, network revenue would still come from bills, but the political pressure to hold those bills down would ease. That could make allowed returns easier to defend. A state investor sitting beside the regulated companies would cut the other way. The two proposals could therefore move network valuations in opposite directions, which may explain why they are not yet priced.

There is a fiscal limit on both. Critics of Labour's net zero plans have argued that the programme will raise costs in the short to medium term and that lower bills are not guaranteed. Shifting the cost to the Exchequer does not change that arithmetic.

What to watch

The first test is whether the energy secretary's review produces a costed proposal. The next is whether it reaches the Budget, which is where any change to levy funding would need to appear. On the ownership side, the questions are how much Great British Grid is allowed to invest and whether Ofgem's next price-control decisions treat it as a rival to National Grid. Until those answers arrive, network valuations rest on what the government has not yet decided.
