More UK homes face becoming uninsurable as flood risk rises

In one sentence
Insurers, lenders and charities warn that rising flood risk could leave hundreds of thousands of UK homes hard to insure and mortgage, even as Flood Re is reformed.

Flood insurance in Britain is still available to almost everyone who wants it. The concern raised by insurers, lenders and flood charities is that this may not hold for much longer, and that the strain will show up first in mortgages and house prices rather than in insurance offices.

About one in ten UK homes sits on a floodplain, according to the House of Commons Library. Rising claims have pushed up premiums and excesses in those areas for two decades, and some streets are gradually being priced or excluded out of affordable cover. Research from the think tank Public First, reported this year, put the number of mortgaged properties at the highest flood risk at about 430,000. Those homes could become uninsurable, or prohibitively expensive to insure, and so hard to mortgage or sell. That figure is an estimate of exposure. It is not a count of policies that have been withdrawn.

Why insurance matters to lenders

A lender will not normally advance a mortgage on a property it cannot see insured against fire and flood. Cover therefore acts as a gate on the whole property market. A home that loses affordable cover loses buyers who need a mortgage, and its price falls. The lender's collateral is then worth less than it was when the loan was made.

That chain explains why the warnings now come from lenders as well as insurers. In March 2026, seventeen insurers, lenders and flood charities wrote jointly to ministers. They said weaker flood safeguards for new-build housing could leave some new homes uninsurable, and therefore unmortgageable and hard to sell.

The backstop is being reshaped

The main protection today is Flood Re, a reinsurance scheme funded by a levy on all home insurers. It is meant to keep premiums for older homes in flood-prone areas affordable. It was designed as a transitional 25-year arrangement and is due to end in 2039. By then the market is supposed to price risk more accurately, helped by better defences and property-level resilience.

In July the government announced changes to the scheme. Ministers say the money has flowed disproportionately to expensive homes. Band H properties, the top council tax band, make up under 4% of UK homes, yet in three of the past four years Flood Re spent more on repairs to them than on Bands A and B combined. Those two bands make up roughly 45% of housing stock. One 2025 claim exceeded £3 million.

Flood minister Emma Hardy said:

"We now have a perverse system where money collected from all insurance customers, from all parts of the country, from all income brackets, is flowing to the richest households."

The reforms include caps on claim values and premium cuts of more than 50% from next year for contents-only cover on lower-value homes. The government also plans Flood Performance Certificates, so that owners who fit protection measures can be offered cheaper insurance.

These steps make the scheme fairer, and they may extend how long it lasts. They do not address what happens after 2039, when the subsidy ends and premiums for the most exposed homes have to reflect the real risk. Nor do they cover homes built since 2009, which sit outside Flood Re.

What to watch

The evidence so far points to a slow squeeze rather than a sudden withdrawal of cover. Two data points should show whether that changes. The first is the detail of the Flood Re reforms and the certificate scheme as they take effect next year. The second is whether lenders begin to write flood-insurance conditions into their own lending criteria for at-risk postcodes. If lenders do that, the insurance problem becomes a housing-finance problem. Governments have usually stepped in with a backstop once that happens.
