Man City chief stays defiant over the 115 charges

In one sentence
Man City, found guilty on most of the 115 charges, say they will appeal as chair Al Mubarak vows to prove innocence and Joe Hart backs him.

Manchester City have been found guilty of the majority of the 115 breaches of Premier League financial rules they were accused of. The club's leadership is not conceding an inch. Chair Khaldoon Al Mubarak says he remains committed to "proving the club's innocence" and that the "process still has a long way to run". City are set to appeal.

The outcome of the case is widely seen as central to Premier League governance. It arrives with no sanction publicly specified in the reporting available, and with an appeal still to come. A verdict with no penalty attached, and a defendant promising to contest it, leaves the league where it has been for years: with a rulebook whose enforcement is still untested.

A public show of loyalty

The most visible support has come from Joe Hart, who made 348 appearances for City between 2006 and 2016. Speaking on BBC Radio 5 Live's Monday Night Club, he said he had no reason to doubt the chair.

"My honest opinion is I've got no reason not to trust Khaldoon. What he said is enough for me."

Hart also praised a letter from Al Mubarak, saying: "I loved how he spoke. I loved [the letter] because that was from someone who cared about the club."

Former players speaking up is not evidence, and nothing in Hart's remarks addresses the substance of the charges. It does show how the club's message is being carried: not through argument about the accounts, but through trust in the man running it. That is a reasonable stance for an alumnus. It is a thinner one for a defendant found guilty on most counts.

Why the money world is watching

The interest goes beyond the trophy cabinet. The case is the first real test of whether the Premier League can police its own financial rules against a club with owners of almost unlimited means. How it ends will inform how much weight investors give the league's governance.

Three things follow from that:

  • Valuations. Club owners and buyers price in regulatory risk. Uncertainty about penalties, and about whether they will stick, makes that harder.
  • Broadcast rights. Media partners negotiating long-term deals want a league whose rules are settled and applied evenly.
  • Betting markets. Prolonged legal uncertainty complicates pricing on points deductions and league outcomes.

These are inferences about incentives, not reported market moves. Nothing in the coverage so far points to a measurable reaction.

What comes next

The next marker is the appeal. Its timetable and any penalty are not yet public, and both will decide whether the league's enforcement is seen as credible. City's line is that the process has a long way to run. On that point, at least, few would disagree.
