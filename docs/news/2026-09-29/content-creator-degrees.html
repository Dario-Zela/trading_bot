Are £30,000 content-creation degrees worth it?

In one sentence
UK universities are selling influencer-economy degrees at about £30,000 in tuition, with no published graduate outcomes to show what buyers get for it.

British universities have found a new product to put in the prospectus: the degree in making content for the internet. Nottingham Trent and Brighton are among the institutions offering three-year courses in content creation and the wider influencer economy. At the current UK fee cap of £9,790 a year, the bill comes to roughly £30,000 in tuition alone, before living costs. The BBC has asked the obvious question: is it worth it?

What the courses promise

The curriculum is less glamorous than the label suggests. Students learn to plan and produce video, build campaigns, understand audiences, analyse performance data and adjust strategy in response. That is a marketing and communications toolkit with a ring light attached. The case for the courses rests on that breadth. Organisations of every size now need people who can make and measure online content, whether in-house, at an agency or as a freelancer serving several clients. Framed that way, this is a vocational digital-marketing degree with a fashionable name.

The framing matters, because the fashionable name is also the selling point. A course billed as a route to becoming an influencer invites a different judgment from one billed as a route to a marketing job.

The missing number

The core measure of any degree's value is what happens to its graduates, and the reporting offers no placement rates, employment outcomes or starting salaries for these programmes. That is partly because the courses are new and have few or no graduates to count. It also means buyers are being asked to pay full fees on the strength of a market trend rather than a record.

The sceptics are not hard to find. One full-time UK creator, with 65,000 followers on Twitch and more than 100,000 on TikTok, told the BBC: "I don't think I would have taken the course." The difficulty is structural. Success on social platforms depends on algorithms that no syllabus controls, and trained and untrained creators alike can go months without traction. A degree can teach craft. It cannot supply an audience.

Why universities are doing this

The motive is easier to read than the outcome. Many English universities are under financial strain. The fee cap has lost real value to inflation, and the pool of 18-year-olds is set to shrink in the coming years, so institutions are competing harder for each applicant. A course that speaks to what teenagers already watch is an efficient way to fill lecture halls. That is an interpretation rather than a stated strategy, but the pattern is consistent: courses are being built around visible cultural demand rather than proven career pathways.

There is a wider unease behind it. Graduate premiums are under scrutiny, student debt is large, and a degree sold on hype is a stress test of whether the qualification still works as a general-purpose route to earnings. If these courses fail to deliver, the damage extends beyond a few cohorts to public confidence in the fee-and-loan model itself.

What to watch

The first real evidence will be the graduate-outcome survey data, published by the Higher Education Statistics Agency, once the first cohorts have been out long enough to be counted. Until then, applicants have the syllabus, the fee and the testimony of people who make content for a living, and at least one of them would not have enrolled.
