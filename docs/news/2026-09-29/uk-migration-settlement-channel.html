Mahmood reworks settlement rules as Channel crossings shift to longer, riskier routes

In one sentence
Home Secretary Shabana Mahmood is reopening refugee resettlement while tightening the terms of permanent settlement, as Channel crossings fall but move to longer routes.

The Home Office is pulling two levers at once. Shabana Mahmood, the home secretary, is reopening the UK's refugee resettlement scheme and, in the same breath, signalling a tougher line on who earns the right to stay permanently. Ministers hope the pairing will show Labour can be both firm and humane on immigration. The test is whether that message holds on both flanks.

A two-track system

The UK Resettlement Scheme (UKRS) reopens immediately. It is separate from the asylum system. It brings in refugees selected before they travel, rather than people who claim protection after arriving. Afghan women and girls and Palestinians are expected to make up about a quarter of arrivals, according to reports of the announcement.

The terms are the point. Refugees who arrive by these routes will be placed on a five-year path to permanent settlement. Those who arrive irregularly and are granted asylum will have their status reviewed every 30 months, for up to 20 years. Mahmood has insisted the routes "must be capped and controlled", and an annual cap stays in place. The government expects hundreds of arrivals at first, perhaps rising to the low thousands if illegal migration keeps falling.

The design is deliberate. Legal routes are being offered as a reward for the numbers coming down, and the price of arriving without permission is a long period of insecurity.

The crossings behind the policy

The numbers give ministers something to point to. Between 1 January and 27 September, 19,135 people crossed the Channel in small boats, about 42% fewer than in the same period of 2025. Arrivals in the year to 9 September, around 27,900, were the lowest since 2021, according to figures compiled by the Migration Observatory.

The fall has come with a change in how people cross. French authorities say departures are moving from the Calais coast to beaches in Normandy and around Cherbourg. That lengthens the sea journey from roughly 20 miles to nearly 80. One boat that left Normandy reportedly spent more than 30 hours at sea before Border Force intercepted it near Dover. In Seine-Maritime, next to the Calais area, authorities logged 58 attempts this year, against 30 a year earlier.

Fewer boats are also carrying more people. The number of boats fell by a third in the year to June, while the average load stayed high at about 65. Tighter enforcement on the busiest stretches of coast appears to be displacing crossings rather than ending them. This account rests on French official reporting and secondary summaries. I found no published mortality or rescue figures for the new routes, so whether they are deadlier is inferred from distance and time at sea, not measured.

The political balance

The two announcements are aimed at different audiences. The tighter settlement rules and the 20-year review cycle speak to voters worried about the pace of change, and to the Reform UK challenge on Labour's right. The resettlement reopening speaks to Labour's own members and MPs, who have been uneasy about hardline rhetoric and who have long pressed for safe routes.

The fall in crossings gives Mahmood cover. If it continues, the case for legal pathways is easier to make. If the longer routes produce a high-profile drowning, or arrivals start to climb again, the government would be attacked from the right for failing to stop the boats and from the left over the routes it has pushed people onto.

The next checks are the weekly Home Office crossing counts, the detail of the settlement changes when they are formally published, and how Labour backbenchers react once they see them.
