HORMUZ · By M. Halloway · 2026-09-29 · 4 min read

Trump rejects Iran's Hormuz plan as oil holds near $100 and tech leads a broad sell-off

In one sentence
Trump's rejection of Iran's Hormuz proposal kept oil near $100 and pushed a broad equity sell-off, with tech hit hardest and defensives holding up better.

President Donald Trump has rejected a proposal from Iran on the management of the Strait of Hormuz. Oil, which was already close to $100 a barrel, held that level. Equities sold off on Monday, with technology shares leading the decline. Eight of the 11 sectors in the S&P 500 fell, and defensive and value stocks did better than growth names. That is the pattern investors expect when the risk is a supply shock and not a slowdown in demand.

The proposal and the rejection

The strait is a narrow passage between Iran and Oman. Roughly a fifth of the world's oil consumption passes through it, and triage material for this piece puts the share of seaborne oil trade higher still. Any proposal that touches who controls or manages traffic there is therefore a market matter as much as a diplomatic one. Tehran's plan was rejected by Trump. The reporting available for this piece does not set out the plan's terms, and this article does not guess at them.

The market reaction was quick, but its size is less clear. The Nasdaq 100 fell after the rejection, and crude rallied on renewed concern about energy security. The precise percentage moves and their timing are not established in the source material, so they are left out. The direction is enough for the argument below. The move was not confined to one corner of the market: the fall in eight of 11 S&P sectors points to broad selling and not a single-sector event.

Why tech takes the hit

The mechanism is simple enough. Growth stocks are valued mainly on earnings expected far into the future. Those earnings are discounted at a rate tied to bond yields. Oil that stays near $100 keeps inflation expectations high. It also makes it harder for central banks to cut rates. A higher discount rate lowers the present value of distant profits, and the effect is largest for the most richly valued companies. Energy producers gain from the same price move, and defensive sectors are assumed to hold up better whatever the cost of crude.

The evidence for this reading is the pattern of winners and losers, not any single price. If Monday's selling had been a pure growth scare, oil would probably have fallen with it. Oil held near $100 while equities fell, and that combination is the one associated with stagflation worries: prices stay high while growth is expected to slow. Oil is resisting downward pressure at a level that reinforces the view that inflation will persist. Investors were not choosing between stocks and oil. They were moving from assets that suffer under a supply shock to assets that tolerate one.

Mixed messages from Washington

The rejection comes with a good deal of noise around it. Trump has said the Iran war will be won "very soon" and that gasoline prices will fall as a result. Bloomberg reports that Iranian officials do not expect an agreement before the November 2026 midterm elections. The two positions do not fit together. If Tehran's expectations are accurate, the relief at the pump that the White House is promising may come later than markets are pricing.

The administration's signals on sanctions have also been inconsistent. The White House indicated it was willing to ease sanctions on Iran, and possibly unfreeze assets, in exchange for "concrete progress" on nuclear issues. Trump then denied making any such offer, contradicting his own administration's stated position. An Iranian official separately denied reports that Tehran had softened its nuclear stance. Each side has publicly disowned the flexibility attributed to it. That leaves traders with no clear reading of how close either party is to a deal, and options and futures markets tend to price that kind of uncertainty as higher volatility.

Domestic fuel relief

The White House is also considering steps at home. It is weighing a tax exemption on red-dyed diesel, the untaxed-for-road-use fuel used mainly in agriculture, construction and mining. The fuel currently carries a federal excise tax, and an exemption would cut the burden for a narrow group of users. The proposal is a response to higher operating costs in farming and logistics.

The measure is small next to the price of crude. Its significance is what it implies: officials are looking for targeted relief because they do not expect the underlying price to fall soon. A broad intervention would be harder to arrange. A narrow tax break lets the administration be seen to act while the diplomacy plays out.

What to watch

Three things will settle whether Monday was a one-day reaction or the start of a longer rotation. The first is whether Tehran tables a revised proposal or hardens its position in reply to the rejection. The second is whether Brent and WTI stay above $100 or slip back as talk of a quick settlement returns. The third is how the White House squares its public optimism on gasoline prices with Iranian officials' expectation of talks running past November. Until one of those moves, the market is likely to keep pricing growth shares as the assets most exposed to a prolonged standoff, and energy and defensives as the ones best placed to benefit from it.
