Houthis battle for control of a key Yemeni city

In one sentence
The Houthis are fighting for Taiz after seizing Mokha and Yemen's Red Sea coast, leaving them holding ground beside Bab el-Mandeb as war-risk premiums and oil prices climb.

The Houthis are fighting for Taiz, Yemen's third-largest city, and the battle has become the central test of a war that reopened this month. Taiz sits 85km inland from Mokha, the Red Sea port the Houthis took on 10 September. Together with the coastline they have since seized, it puts the movement's guns beside one of the world's busiest shipping lanes.

This is not a continuation of old coverage. A ceasefire that had largely held for four years came apart this month. According to the Wikipedia account of the 2026 Yemen offensives, the Houthis opened an offensive on 3 September against the Saudi-backed Presidential Leadership Council, with clashes beginning in Taiz governorate. Displacement figures suggest fighting had begun in July.

What has changed

Within about a week the picture on the coast had shifted. NBC News reported that by 11 September the Houthis had taken Mokha and Perim (Mayun) island in the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, and had moved on the coastal town of Dhubab. That left them holding effectively the whole of Yemen's western coastline except the Midi district, according to the Wikipedia summary.

Bab el-Mandeb is the gateway between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden, and so between Europe and Asia. Holding its northern shore does not by itself close it. But it gives an armed group with a record of attacking ships a permanent vantage point over every vessel that passes.

The fighting has since turned inland. Government forces have claimed positions including Jabal Bazel in Taiz, and the Houthis have pushed towards the Kahboub Mountains. On 27 September, Al Jazeera reported that the government's military media centre described dozens of Houthi casualties in 24 hours across five locations in the governorate. Those are claims by one side and have not been independently verified.

The Saudi dimension

Riyadh is directly involved. The Saudi military has carried out hundreds of air strikes on Houthi positions along the coast, per BBC reporting. The Houthis have replied with drones and missiles aimed at south-western Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia intercepted two ballistic missiles and two drones fired towards Riyadh and Khamis Mushait, according to Al Jazeera.

NBC reported that Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline was shut after drone strikes. The line matters because it lets Saudi crude reach the Red Sea while the Strait of Hormuz is disrupted, a problem that has persisted since late February. Andreas Krieg of King's College London told NBC the Houthis were now hitting "both pillars of Saudi security simultaneously": southern Saudi territory and the kingdom's Red Sea strategic depth. Brent has moved towards $100 a barrel, which links this story to the Hormuz standoff elsewhere in today's edition.

Washington has kept its distance. NBC reported that President Trump has declined direct strikes but agreed to share intelligence and provide targeting assistance to Saudi Arabia.

What it means for shipping

Insurers moved before the territory did. Reuters-sourced reports carried by Insurance Journal said war-risk premiums for Red Sea transits rose from about 0.3% of a ship's value to about 0.75% after the Houthis announced a naval blockade in July. They then passed 1% for southern Red Sea voyages after attacks on tankers. Saudi-linked ships calling at southern ports such as Jizan and Al Shuqaiq were quoted as high as 3%. Northern ports such as Jeddah and Yanbu stayed near 0.1%.

Those percentages are charged per voyage. Even a rise of a few tenths of a point adds hundreds of thousands of dollars to a single transit of a large tanker. I found no published figure for tonnage rerouted or transit delays since Mokha fell, so the premium data above predates the coastal advance. Any post-Mokha repricing remains to be measured.

What to watch

The immediate question is whether Taiz holds. If the city stays in government hands, the Houthi coastal gains are cut off from the highlands behind them. If it falls, the front moves closer to Aden and the Gulf of Aden approaches. The next markers are the war-risk quotes for Bab el-Mandeb transits, the restart date for the Saudi pipeline, and any Houthi attack on a merchant vessel from the newly held coast. The UN says more than 146,000 people have been displaced in three weeks, and the humanitarian toll is likely to keep growing whichever side prevails.
