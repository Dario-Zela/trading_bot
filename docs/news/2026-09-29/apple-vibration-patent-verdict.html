Apple ordered to pay $5.7bn in vibration patent case

In one sentence
A San Diego jury ordered Apple to pay Taction $5.7bn over haptic patents, reportedly a record, but post-trial motions and appeals make the final sum uncertain.

A federal jury in San Diego has told Apple to pay Taction Technology $5,721,961,750 for infringing patents on the haptic engine behind the vibrations in iPhones and Apple Watches. Apple says it will appeal. Reports describe the figure as the largest US patent damages award on record. It is not a bill Apple has to settle now, and the size of the verdict says little about what Apple will end up paying.

What the jury decided

The verdict came on Friday, 25 September, in the US District Court for the Southern District of California. Jurors found that Apple's Taptic Engine infringed claims in two Taction patents, US Nos. 10,659,885 and 10,820,117. The Taptic Engine is the component that produces the tap or buzz on an iPhone or Watch, such as a button press or a notification.

Taction is a San Diego company. It develops haptic technology used in products including headphones and gaming headsets. It sued Apple in 2021. The case has not run in a straight line. A San Diego judge ruled in 2023 that Apple did not infringe. A federal appeals court then took up Taction's appeal, and the case went back for the September jury trial, which Taction won.

That history is a reminder that the case has already been decided against Taction once, and that a court can reverse a result. The jury's answer is the latest step in a five-year fight, not the last.

Apple's response

Apple disputes both infringement and validity. It says the Taptic Engine was developed independently and is fundamentally different from Taction's technology. Both arguments are standard in patent defence, and both can be pursued after trial.

A jury award is the start of the process for a large patent case. The trial judge can throw out the verdict as a matter of law, order a new trial, or cut the damages if the evidence for the number looks thin. Then comes an appeal to the Federal Circuit, which reviews patent cases. Damages models are the usual weak point in awards this size, because a jury has to put a value on one component inside a device that sells for many times more than that component costs. Awards of this scale have often shrunk or disappeared at that stage, though the outcome of this one is not predictable.

Why it matters beyond Apple

The wider interest is in what the number says about litigation risk. Apple is among the best-resourced defendants in the world, with a large legal department and a practised appeals strategy. It still lost a jury trial to a much smaller company on a component it says it built itself. That will be noted by other large device makers and by the small patent holders who sue them.

There is a reasonable argument that a record award raises the value of a patent portfolio. It gives plaintiffs and litigation funders a bigger reference point when they decide whether to sue and when they negotiate a settlement. It also gives defendants more reason to settle. An award of this size sitting on the docket, even one likely to be cut, changes the arithmetic of that negotiation.

The reverse argument is also plausible. If the figure is cut sharply, or vanishes, on post-trial motions or appeal, the episode will look like another case of a jury award that did not survive review. Investors should not price patent risk on the headline figure alone. What matters is how much of it survives.

This piece has not established that the award has any effect on Apple's finances in the near term. Nothing is payable until the appeals run their course, and the company has not indicated it is setting money aside.

What to watch

The next dates that matter are the post-trial motions before the trial judge, where Apple will ask for the verdict to be overturned or the damages reduced. The judge's ruling on how the $5.7bn was calculated will say more about the eventual payout than the jury's number did. After that, the case goes to the Federal Circuit, which can take a year or more to rule.
