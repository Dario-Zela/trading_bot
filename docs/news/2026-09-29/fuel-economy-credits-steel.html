Trump to end fuel economy credit trading in 2028 and pushes domestic steel

In one sentence
The administration finalised a fuel economy rollback that ends CAFE credit trading from model year 2028, while Trump credited steel tariffs for reviving domestic production.

The Trump administration has finalised a rollback of US fuel economy rules that also removes a tool automakers have used for years to manage compliance costs. From the 2028 model year, carmakers will no longer be able to trade fuel economy credits. Credits earned or bought through model year 2027 stay usable. Credits generated from 2028 on can only cover shortfalls at the company that earned them.

The headline change is the standard itself. The Department of Transportation and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration set the 2031 fleet average at roughly 34.9 miles per gallon, against a Biden-era target of 50.4 mpg. The Daily Caller, relaying the administration's framing, reports a claim that new cars will cost about $1,300 less as a result. That figure is the administration's own, and no independent estimate accompanied it.

What the credit market did

Under the corporate average fuel economy (CAFE) system, a manufacturer that beats its target earns credits, and one that misses can buy them from a rival. In practice, makers of electric vehicles were the main sellers, and makers with large truck and SUV ranges were the buyers. The credits worked as a cost-mitigation valve: a shortfall could be settled with a purchase rather than a redesign or a penalty.

The administration describes the market differently. It says the arrangement "artificially propped up the EV industry at the expense of traditional automakers." That is a political reading of a mechanism that also cut compliance costs for the traditional automakers themselves.

The cost arithmetic

The two changes pull in opposite directions, and the net effect on any one company depends on where it sits.

  • Lower targets reduce the gap most manufacturers would have had to close, so the need for credits shrinks.
  • Ending trading means any shortfall after 2027 must be closed in-house, and surplus credits lose their resale value.

Companies that would have sold credits lose a revenue line. Companies close to the new, lower target lose little. Suppliers face a less certain demand picture for efficiency and electrification content. The 2028 start date leaves a runway of about a model year and a half. Automakers backed the broad rollback in earlier comments but sought changes to the proposal, according to Yahoo Finance reporting on the earlier draft.

Steel as industrial policy

The same day, President Trump credited his tariffs for a revival in domestic steelmaking. "Our steel industry is roaring back to life," he said. "Everyone is building their plants here because they don't want to pay tariffs." Search coverage tied the remarks to a reported $15 billion Iowa steel plant backed by an Indian conglomerate.

The sources reviewed do not show a formal defence-necessity designation for steel in the fuel economy rule. The framing here rests on the administration's rhetoric and on triage notes. The pairing is still coherent. Relaxing an environmental compliance pathway for carmakers while supporting domestic metal production treats both as questions of industrial capacity rather than emissions.

What to watch

The first test is whether the rule draws legal challenges from states and environmental groups, as earlier rollbacks did. The second is how the 2028 credit cut-off shows up in automaker guidance: companies that sold credits will have to say how much revenue they expect to lose. On steel, the next signal is whether further plant announcements follow the tariff regime, or whether tariff-driven costs start showing up in vehicle pricing.
