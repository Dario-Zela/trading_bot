Rubio blames a foreign actor for the RAF Fairford bomb plot as five UK men are bailed

In one sentence
Marco Rubio says a foreign actor was 'clearly' behind the alleged RAF Fairford bomb plot, but he offered no evidence and the five British suspects are on bail, uncharged.

Five young British men were released on police bail on Monday, a day after they were detained near RAF Fairford, the Gloucestershire airbase that American bombers have used for decades. That same evening the US Secretary of State, Marco Rubio, said the episode "clearly involves the hands of a foreign actor". He offered no evidence. What began as a counter-terrorism arrest in the English countryside has become an argument about who, if anyone, stood behind it.

What is known

The five were detained early on Sunday after three vans were spotted in the countryside near the base. Fairford is owned by the UK and has been used by US bombers, including in this year's war with Iran. Police say the men are British nationals aged between 23 and 25 from the London area.

They were first held under laws on the possession of explosives. They were later additionally arrested "on suspicion of preparation of a terrorist act", according to counter-terrorism police. On Monday they were bailed, and detectives said they were pursuing "multiple lines of inquiry".

Bail is not an exoneration, and it is not a charge. It usually means investigators need more time, whether for forensic work, for analysis of phones and devices, or because the evidence does not yet meet the threshold for prosecution. Nothing published so far shows which of those applies here, and police have not said what the vans contained or what the men intended.

Rubio's attribution

Speaking to Fox News on Monday evening, Rubio went further than any British official has. A senior US diplomat naming a foreign hand behind an alleged plot at a shared military site is a different kind of statement from a police update. It moves the case from criminal justice into diplomacy and raises the possibility of state-directed action against infrastructure that serves a NATO ally.

Rubio did not say which actor he meant, and the public record contains no evidence for the claim. The context, though, points one way. Fairford's role in the Iran war makes Tehran the obvious suspect in the public mind. Iran's embassy in London said it "categorically rejects and strongly condemns" what it called "unfounded and malicious speculations" about its involvement.

There is also a gap between the two accounts. British police describe an investigation with open questions. The American account already treats the foreign dimension as settled. Washington may hold intelligence that has not been made public, and allies often share such material in private long before either side speaks. But the more precise the accusation, the more it needs support, and none has yet been offered.

The Westminster row

At home, the case was quickly drawn into domestic politics. Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, said he had warned for months about "Iranian agents" entering Britain on small boats. He posted a video claiming to have received intelligence that the Iranian regime was using the Channel route "to get their operatives into Britain".

The arrests did not fit that account. If the five are British nationals from London, they are not Iranian nationals, and it is very unlikely they arrived by small boat. Lisa Nandy, the culture secretary, told the Guardian at Labour's conference on Monday that Farage was a "master of the conspiracy theory". She said he was "good at using the very real sentiment that a lot of people feel, of anxiety and distrust" and "very agile at exploiting that for political gain". Reform has since demanded that the public be told "the truth" about the suspects' identities.

The two disputes are separate but they overlap. A foreign hand could be real even if Farage's Channel theory is wrong. British nationals can be recruited, paid or directed from abroad, as several recent European sabotage cases have shown. Rubio's claim and Farage's do not have to stand or fall together, and neither rests on evidence in the public domain.

What to watch

The next marker is the end of the bail period, when police must either charge, extend or release. Any charge would set out what prosecutors say the men planned and whether they say anyone directed them. A statement from the UK government or security services on whether they share Rubio's assessment would also be telling. Silence would suggest caution in London. A matching statement would turn a Fox News interview into an allied position, with consequences for how Britain and the US handle their bases and their dealings with Iran.
