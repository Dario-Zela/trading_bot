Nvidia passes $5.4tn as $44bn floods back into equity funds and the AI trade spreads to CPU makers

In one sentence
Nvidia's $5.4tn valuation now exceeds the whole Russell 2000, and with $44bn flowing into equity funds, Burry's concentration warning is testing an AI trade that is still broadening.

Nvidia's market value has passed $5.4tn, more than the combined worth of the roughly 2,000 companies in the Russell 2000 small-cap index. Over the same period investors moved about $44bn into equity funds in a single week, with AI-themed exchange-traded funds among the beneficiaries. The two facts describe one market from different sides. Money is returning to equities, and much of it is being directed at the same handful of names.

One company, two thousand

The comparison is a blunt one, but it is useful. The Russell 2000 is the standard yardstick for small and mid-sized American companies: regional banks, biotechs, industrial suppliers, retailers. It covers a large slice of the domestic economy by headcount and by number of listings. A single chip designer is now valued above the lot.

The top of the market has tightened in step. Nvidia, Microsoft and the other AI leaders make up an unprecedented share of total US market capitalisation, according to the triage data behind this piece. Index funds are cap-weighted, so anyone holding a broad US tracker owns those companies in proportion to their size. A saver who thinks they are diversified across 500 or 3,000 stocks is in practice carrying a large, concentrated position in AI spending.

Michael Burry, the investor who became known for betting against the US housing market before 2008, has publicly warned about concentration and possible overvaluation in AI stocks. Burry's record is mixed as well as celebrated: he has called several downturns early, and at times wrongly. But the warning is now about market structure rather than a single stock. The argument is that when a few companies carry so much of an index, a disappointment in any of them, on spending, margins or demand, reaches every portfolio that tracks it.

The money keeps coming

The fund-flow data offers little sign that investors are heeding the caution. The $44bn inflow to equity funds came while oil remained elevated, a backdrop that would ordinarily argue for defensiveness. Higher energy costs feed inflation and squeeze consumers, and the Federal Reserve has less room to cut. Our Fed coverage notes that governor Lisa Cook sees only modest disinflation from AI and that a new stress gauge sits at the 65th percentile.

The reading of the flows most consistent with the evidence is that investors are treating AI's long-term earnings potential as more durable than the near-term energy drag. That is a judgment about the future, and it is being made with the same confidence by a very large number of people. Inflows into AI-themed ETFs reinforce the concentration, because those funds buy the same leaders whose weights are already elevated. Flows follow price, price follows flows, and the loop tightens until something interrupts it.

On the day, the loop was not intact everywhere: technology led a broad sell-off as oil held near $100 after the Trump administration rejected an Iranian proposal on the Strait of Hormuz, as reported on the front page. A week of inflows does not settle the direction.

The trade spreads to CPU makers

A third thread is the reach of the AI trade beyond GPUs. Benzinga reported that shares of Intel, AMD, Arm and Qualcomm were lifted by a so-called 'Muse' trade tied to Meta. The evidence for this is thin and should be read that way. The available reporting does not establish what Muse is, whether Meta has announced or deployed it, or how much processor demand it would create. It also gives no move sizes and no guidance changes from Meta or the chipmakers. What can be said is that traders are looking for the next beneficiary of AI capex, and central processors, long overshadowed by accelerators, are being tried as the answer.

That matters for the concentration argument in two ways. If demand for AI compute is broadening, the risk is spread across more companies, which is mildly reassuring. If the rally is instead a rotation on an unconfirmed story, it suggests the appetite for AI exposure is running ahead of the information available. Both readings should be tested against Meta's next capital-spending disclosure and against the chipmakers' own commentary on orders.

What to watch

The next hard data will come from company reports rather than fund flows. Nvidia's next earnings release will show whether the revenue and margins that support a $5.4tn valuation are still rising. Hyperscaler capital-spending guidance will show whether the customers are still willing to pay. The White House meeting of Mark Zuckerberg, Dario Amodei, Jensen Huang, Elon Musk and Greg Brockman, covered in our technology pages, is a reminder that policy and industry are closely entangled. Export controls are another exposure; ASML has warned that pressure on China could backfire.

Burry's point does not require a crash. It requires only that a market this dependent on a few companies has less margin for error than its index-fund holders assume. The flows suggest they have not priced that in. The next earnings season will begin to show whether they should.
