Tagging delays are putting domestic abuse victims at risk, inspectors find

In one sentence
An inspection report finds only 55% of GPS and radio-frequency tags in England and Wales are fitted on time, leaving domestic abuse victims exposed for weeks or months.

Electronic tags are meant to be a quiet guarantee: an offender is ordered to stay away from a victim, or to observe a curfew, and a device on the ankle makes the order enforceable. An inspection report now says that in England and Wales the guarantee often arrives late. Nearly half of the tags ordered by courts and prisons are not fitted on time, and domestic abuse victims are among those left exposed while they wait.

What inspectors found

According to reporting on the findings, only 55% of GPS and radio-frequency tags were fitted by the required deadline. Alcohol-monitoring tags did slightly better, at 60%. The averages hide a long tail. Some people waited more than two weeks for equipment. One in ten offenders stayed untagged for as long as five months beyond the mandated time.

The delays matter because a tagging requirement often sits at the centre of a protective package. A restriction that exists on paper but not on the offender's ankle gives a victim, and the agencies around them, a false sense of security. A curfew or exclusion zone with no device behind it cannot be checked, and a breach may go unnoticed.

Data that goes unused

Fitting the tag is only half of the job. The report also found the information the tags produce is often not put to work. Major violations were examined in only 46% of relevant cases. So the failure has two stages: devices arrive late, and once they are on, the alerts they generate are not consistently followed up.

This suggests the trouble is not one bottleneck but a chain of them, from allocating the order to fitting the device to acting on the data. The pattern points to capacity, not to a flaw in the policy itself. The policy intent is not much disputed, and the shortfall sits in delivery.

The government's response

A Ministry of Justice spokesperson said tag installation rates had risen by nearly 50% since 2024, and that the government was delivering the "biggest expansion of tagging in British history", backed by £100 million in funding.

Both claims can be true alongside the inspectors' findings. A sharp rise in fittings from a low base can still leave a large share of orders late, particularly if ministers are also asking tagging to do more. Expansion adds to demand at the same moment it adds to supply.

What to watch

The test is whether the on-time rate moves, not whether the total number of tags does. The share fitted by deadline, currently 55%, and the share of serious violations investigated, currently 46%, are the figures to track when the Ministry of Justice and the inspectorate next report. Further pressure on tagging capacity is likely if the government widens the use of community sentences, and any such expansion will be judged by whether victims are protected in the days after an order is made.
