ASML warns that export pressure on China could create the rival the US wants to prevent

In one sentence
ASML warns that heavy US export pressure could push China to build its own chipmaking tools, creating the rival Washington wants to prevent.

ASML, the Dutch company that builds the machines used to print the world's most advanced chips, has told Washington that leaning too hard on China could backfire. Its argument, as reported by Benzinga and other outlets, is that broad export restrictions may push Beijing to build its own chipmaking tools, and so produce the rival the restrictions are meant to prevent. The warning arrives while ASML's place in the AI supply chain is under close scrutiny.

A single point of dependence

ASML makes the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography systems needed to etch the finest circuits on advanced AI and logic chips. It has no real competitor in that niche. Nvidia's designs, and the data-centre build-out that has lifted its market value past $5tn, ultimately depend on a Dutch factory continuing to ship.

That gives the company an unusual position. It is a supplier to the US technology industry, and it is also the chokepoint through which Washington tries to limit China's chip progress. EUV machines are already barred from China. The argument now is over the older deep ultraviolet (DUV) tools, which ASML still sells there.

The China numbers

China made up about 33% of ASML's revenue in 2025, according to reports of the company's guidance. Its chief financial officer has said the share should fall to around 20% in 2026. Sales to China were €2.9bn (about $3.3bn) in the first half of the year, roughly 16% of the total.

The pressure to cut further is building. A bipartisan bill introduced in Congress in April, the MATCH Act, would effectively bar DUV exports to China. In June, Bloomberg reported that US officials had told ASML they were worried China might have obtained its top chip tool. TechCrunch reported that ASML said it had not. In April, ASML's shares fell after proposed US curbs targeted what CNBC described as an already fragile China market.

The paradox

The company's case rests on how technology gets built. A customer that is cut off has a strong reason to develop its own supply, and a large, state-funded one has the means. Chinese firms have been working on domestic lithography for years. In July, CNBC covered a reported Chinese DUV breakthrough and stressed the caveats: a working prototype is a long way from tools that can produce chips at volume and at acceptable yields.

That gap is the core of the argument on both sides. Hawks say it justifies tightening now, before it closes. ASML's warning implies that tightening is what shortens the time it takes to close. The two positions cannot easily be tested against each other. Nobody can observe the counterfactual in which China kept buying Dutch machines and never tried to replace them.

The risk to markets is asymmetric. Valuations across the US technology sector assume that the lead in AI hardware is durable. Export controls that work in the short run may still wear that lead down over a longer horizon. A capable Chinese toolmaker would not only cost ASML a customer. It would also weaken the premium attached to the whole Western supply chain. That is a slow-moving risk, and the evidence that it is priced in is thin.

What to watch

The nearest test is the MATCH Act's progress through Congress, and whether the Netherlands is asked to go along with its restrictions. After that, ASML's next quarterly results will show how far China's share of orders has fallen, and whether the company's warning is being borne out or contradicted by what Chinese buyers actually do.
