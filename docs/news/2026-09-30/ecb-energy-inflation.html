ECB officials blame Middle East energy costs as euro-area inflation stays above 3%

In one sentence
With euro-area inflation at 3.3% and energy up 14.3%, the ECB has raised rates twice this year and blames the Middle East conflict, with further hikes possible.

The European Central Bank is raising interest rates, not holding them, and it has a ready explanation for why. Euro-area inflation reached 3.3% in August, according to Eurostat's flash estimate published on 1 September. That is up from 2.9% in July and the highest reading in almost three years. ECB officials, Christine Lagarde among them, attribute most of the overshoot to the war involving Iran and the disruption around the Strait of Hormuz. That framing matters because it defines what would have to change before the bank declares the inflation fight over.

The premise that the ECB is resisting market pressure for faster cuts does not match the record. Traders were pricing a September hike at close to 99% before the meeting. The bank is being pushed to tighten further, not to ease.

The numbers

Eurostat's flash estimate put annual HICP inflation at 3.2–3.3% for August, depending on the release vintage. Energy inflation accelerated to 14.3% from 10.3% a month earlier. Energy carries a smaller weight in the basket than services or food. Even so, a double-digit energy rise adds materially to the headline, and it feeds into transport, manufacturing and heating costs later on.

Europe is unusually exposed. The blockage at Hormuz has lifted crude and refined products, and the natural gas market has been disrupted as well. The euro area imports most of its energy, so the shock arrives as a terms-of-trade loss as well as a price rise.

The ECB's own projections see inflation averaging 3.0% in 2026 and easing to 2.1% in 2028. That path depends on energy prices falling back, and on the pass-through to non-energy prices staying contained.

The ECB's response

The ECB raised its deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.50% on 10 September. It was the second hike this year, after the June 11 move from 2.00% to 2.25%. Lagarde called the decision a "no brainer". She said it was unanimous and "robust" across the three scenarios the bank had mapped out for the economy. She also warned that the Middle East conflict, together with recent developments in Russia's war in Ukraine, would keep headline inflation "well above target" for an extended period.

Bloomberg, citing people familiar with the discussions, reported that officials expect further tightening, with October in play. Other coverage suggested the September move might be the last. The bank has not committed either way, which is consistent with its stated meeting-by-meeting approach.

A geopolitical defence

The external-shock explanation does two jobs. It is largely accurate: the August acceleration came from energy, and no domestic demand surge explains a jump of that size. It is also convenient. A supply-driven price rise is one the central bank cannot fix directly, so it can be cited as a reason for caution without any admission that policy was too loose.

The risk the ECB is guarding against is second-round effects. If higher energy costs spill into wages and services prices, inflation could stay high after oil and gas retreat. The bank's language about inflation remaining "well above target" for an extended period reads as a warning against reading a single soft month as a turning point. The same logic applies in Britain. Bank of England policymakers, including Ramsden, have tied energy pass-through to a willingness to back hikes if inflation persists.

The cost is on the growth side. Higher rates and a large energy shock land on economies that were already soft. The ECB is tightening into an energy-driven slowdown, and it has chosen to accept that trade-off for now.

What to watch

The next tests are Eurostat's flash estimate for September, due in early October, and the ECB's meeting later that month. Two readings matter. One is whether energy inflation stays near 14%. The other is whether core and services inflation start to rise, since that would undercut the argument that the problem is purely external. A fall in oil and gas prices would let the bank pause. A broadening of price pressure would make further rises likely.
