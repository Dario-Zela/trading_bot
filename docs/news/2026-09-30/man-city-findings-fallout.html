How Man City's financial arrangements unravelled, and how football is reacting

In one sentence
An independent commission found Manchester City broke Premier League financial rules, but sanctions and appeals are pending, and the case tests whether self-regulation can police its strongest members.

Manchester City have been found to have broken the Premier League's financial rules on a scale that has no precedent in English football. An independent commission has upheld 114 of the 115 charges brought against the club. The findings cover the nine seasons from 2009-10 to 2017-18, when City rose from mid-table ambition to domestic dominance under Abu Dhabi ownership. No punishment has been set, and City say they will appeal.

The verdict concerns football, but it also tests a wider question. Can an industry that writes its own rules, relies on its members to report their own numbers and then polices itself do the job credibly?

What the commission found

The commission found that City's revenues were inflated, and their costs understated, by more than £900m across the period. The aim was to make the club look compliant with the league's spending limits. The mechanism at the centre of the case was sponsorship. The commission found that City arranged "sham" commercial deals in which sponsors paid only part of the stated fee. The remainder was funded by Abu Dhabi United Group (ADUG), the club's owner. On paper that looked like arm's-length commercial income. In substance it was the owner's money routed through a third party.

The ruling also covers other breaches:

  • Accounts that were misstated and concealed the club's finances from auditors.
  • Hidden payments that supplemented manager and player salaries.
  • Failure to cooperate with the investigation. Most of the non-cooperation charges, covering 2018 to 2023, were also upheld.

The charges are therefore not only about whether a sponsor's price was too high. Related-party deals only work as a loophole if the club reports them honestly, and the commission found that City did not. Much of the league's regime rests on clubs' own declarations, and the findings suggest those declarations can be gamed for years.

Legal status

The position is incomplete. The commission has ruled on liability, and sanctions will follow in a separate process. Possible penalties reported include a points deduction or, at the extreme, expulsion from the league. City have until Friday 2 October to appeal, and the club has said it was "disappointed and surprised" and is "innocent of the accusations made by the Premier League". It has promised to be "relentless" in pursuing appeals. Reports differ on whether the club was found guilty of "all" charges or of 114 of 115. The count of 114 is the one used by NBC and CBS, and it is used here.

An appeal could take months, and the club is currently leading the league. Sporting consequences may therefore lag the legal ones by some distance.

How football is reacting

The reaction has been bitter, and not only from rivals. Roy Keane, speaking on ITV during England's Nations League win over the Czech Republic, said the club had cheated. Some coverage has described him as a former City captain, but that is wrong. Keane captained Manchester United, City's neighbours and rivals, so his words carry a rival's edge. He directed them at the players who collected medals in the period, which included three Premier League titles.

"They knew it would hurt a lot of people, but they still done it... if I was a player with the success of the last few years – I'd 100 per cent be sticking those medals in the bin."

That is a pundit's rhetoric, not a ruling. It does show that the argument is moving from the boardroom to the dressing room. It now concerns whether the trophies themselves have value. Other commentary has predicted a lasting stain on the era. The league has so far not said whether any titles would be affected, and nothing in the findings reported so far addresses that.

The self-regulation problem

The case took years to reach a verdict, and the conduct it examined ended in 2018. A regulator that finds a breach eight years after the last season it covers has a problem with deterrence, however strong the eventual finding. During that time City won the league repeatedly, and any penalty will fall on a squad and a staff that may look quite different from the ones who benefited.

The design weakness is in how the system works. Clubs report their own sponsorships, and the league then has to prove that the valuations were false. The ownership can fund the "sponsor". Non-cooperation charges show what happens when a club declines to hand over the evidence.

The same structure exists in many industries that supervise themselves, including trade bodies, professional standards boards and other sporting federations. They all depend on members to disclose related-party dealings accurately. The City ruling shows that such a system can uncover abuse. It took a long time to do so, and it needed a formal process with the power to compel evidence.

Two things will show whether it works. The first is the appeal outcome. If the findings survive, the league will have established that self-regulation can hold a dominant member to account. The second is the sanction. A penalty seen as token would confirm the suspicion that the rules bind only those who lack the resources to fight them. The sanctions hearing follows the 2 October appeal deadline, and both will be watched well beyond football.
