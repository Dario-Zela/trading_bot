Fed's Williams sees 'no urgency' after September hike, with one more increase possible if data hold

In one sentence
NY Fed's Williams says there is 'no urgency' after September's hike, though one more increase this year is possible if inflation, growth and jobs data stay on his forecast path.

The Federal Reserve has raised rates in September. One of its most senior officials is now telling markets not to expect the pace to continue. John Williams, president of the New York Fed, said there is "no urgency" for further increases. He also said one more hike this year remains possible if the data come in as expected. Read together, the two statements describe a central bank that has slowed its tightening and is leaving the next move to the numbers.

What Williams said

The outline of Williams's forecast is clear. He sees inflation at 3.5% this year, easing to 2% by 2028. He expects GDP growth of about 2.25% and unemployment near 4%. That is a soft-landing forecast: growth close to trend, a labour market that is not deteriorating, and a return to target that takes more than two years.

The forecast also explains the caution. Inflation at 3.5% is well above target, so the Fed cannot declare the job finished. A path that returns to 2% only in 2028 leaves room to wait and see whether September's increase does the work. "No urgency" is a statement about timing, not about direction. The projected additional hike is conditional, and the condition is that the data hold as expected.

Williams also addressed the bond market. He said higher yields do not signal a shift in long-run inflation expectations. In his reading they reflect tighter financial conditions. That matters for how the Fed reads the market. If long-dated yields were pricing a de-anchoring of inflation expectations, policymakers would have a reason to move faster. Williams's framing says they are not.

A K-shaped economy, and an AI tailwind

Williams described the economy as K-shaped, with material divergence in outcomes across segments. He also tied AI-driven gains in asset prices to consumption through wealth effects. Households holding equities feel richer and spend more. Those without that exposure do not.

The point matters for the forecast. Headline GDP of 2.25% can sit alongside strong spending at the top and strain elsewhere. The Fed's tools work on the aggregate, not on either arm of the K.

The demand-side counterpoint

St. Louis Fed president Alberto Musalem offered a harder-edged view of the same backdrop. He attributed roughly half of current inflation to persistent demand pressure rather than supply shocks. He argued that the AI capital-spending boom is generating demand without supply-side relief yet. Data centres and chips add to orders before they add to capacity.

Musalem also suggested anonymously linking the Fed's rate-dot forecasts to policy announcements. The aim would be clearer communication about the likely path.

The two officials do not contradict each other, but they stress different risks. Williams emphasises that conditions have tightened and expectations are anchored. Musalem emphasises that demand is running hot. If Musalem's reading is right, the conditional extra hike is more likely to be needed.

What it means for markets

The reaction in US equity futures was described in pre-market commentary as a tug between restrained policy and AI enthusiasm. The reports gave no specific levels. The reasoning is straightforward. A Fed that has slowed its pace narrows the range of outcomes for the end of this cycle. Any discussion of rate cuts in 2027 depends on that. Yet a fourth-quarter hike would still cap the appetite for risk.

The practical thresholds are the ones Williams named. Inflation has to fall from 3.5% on a trajectory consistent with 2% by 2028. Unemployment has to stay near 4%. Growth has to stay near 2.25%. A miss on inflation would revive the extra hike. A miss on jobs would strengthen the case for holding at the current level.

Other central banks face the same trade-off. The Bank of England and the ECB are also weighing energy-driven inflation against slowing growth.

The next inflation and payrolls reports ahead of the Fed's remaining meetings this year are the data points that will decide whether "no urgency" becomes "no more" or gives way to a further increase.
