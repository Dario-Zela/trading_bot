Healthy Babies programme to be expanded across England

In one sentence
The government will extend Healthy Babies services to every English local authority through 1,000 family hubs, backed by £500m, to be fully realised by April 2027.

The government is extending its Healthy Babies programme to every local authority in England. The programme bundles infant feeding advice, mental health support for new parents, bonding help, health visiting and midwifery care. Ministers say it will reach a national network of 1,000 family hubs, backed by a £500m commitment that runs to April 2027.

Health and Social Care Secretary Yvette Cooper announced the move at the Labour Party conference in Liverpool. Her framing was of a gap to be closed. "Something is missing in half of communities … the Healthy Babies services – mental health support for mums and dad, the feeding and bonding support for your new baby," she said. "That changes now." She added: "We will bring back the Sure Start health services into every family centre."

What is changing

According to the reporting, more than half of English local authorities already have family hubs but do not offer the Healthy Babies services alongside them. The expansion is meant to close that gap. The programme is a renaming and widening of the earlier Start for Life offer, and government implementation guidance describes it as combining the strongest elements of Sure Start with the Family Hubs approach. The focus is on what is often called the first 1,001 days, from conception to a child's second birthday.

The Sure Start comparison is deliberate. The scheme began in 1998 under the last Labour government. One study found it generated longer-term savings worth twice its cost. Much of it was dismantled during the Conservative-era spending cuts. Ministers are now presenting the new offer as its successor, although the two are not identical in design.

Questions the announcement leaves open

The reporting available so far leaves several details unclear. It does not say how much of the £500m is new money and how much is reallocated. It also does not set out how the cost will be divided between central and local government, or how the services will be staffed. A separate announcement from Bridget Phillipson's education department promised qualified early years teachers in the most deprived areas. It is not yet clear how that will fit with the health workforce.

The evidence base also deserves care. The Sure Start finding is from a single study, and results from earlier family-hub programmes have varied by area. Claims of long-run savings are projections, not guarantees.

What to watch

The deadline that matters is April 2027, the date by which the funding is due to be fully realised. Local authority allocations and the health visitor and midwifery recruitment plans will show whether the rollout is on track. The government's own implementation guidance for 2026 is the document to check them against.

The announcement is a domestic health-policy item. It is not a fiscal surprise. A £500m commitment is small against total public spending, and the reporting names no private contractors or market-facing consequences.
