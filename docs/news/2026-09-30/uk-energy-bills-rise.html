Household energy bills forecast to post biggest rise in four years

In one sentence
Cornwall Insight forecasts a 16% January rise in the UK price cap to about £1,999, the biggest since 2022, complicating the Bank of England's rate-cut path.

Household energy bills are forecast to jump by 16% in January, the sharpest quarterly rise since the 2022 crisis, according to the consultancy Cornwall Insight. The projection, published on 30 September, arrives as the Bank of England is trying to judge whether inflation is falling far enough to justify cutting rates. A rise of this size in a regulated price complicates that judgement.

The numbers

Cornwall Insight expects the typical annual bill under Ofgem's price cap to rise to about £1,999 in January. That is £276 more than the £1,723 cap that took effect in October. The forecast is a large step up from the consultancy's late-August projection, which pointed to a further 9% rise, or roughly £149, to £1,872. Ofgem is due to confirm the actual January cap in late November, so the figure can still move.

The drivers are on the wholesale side, which accounts for more than 40% of the cap. Cornwall Insight has pointed to gas supply disruption linked to Middle East conflict, a hot European summer, supply problems in Norway and strong Asian demand. Together these have left continental gas storage low going into winter. Dr Craig Lowrey, the consultancy's principal consultant, said the prices would "hit households hard", adding that January is "already a difficult month for many".

EDF's chief executive, Simone Rossi, went further, saying the industry is "walking into a second significant energy crisis after the one we experienced just four years ago". Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the government is "looking at any measure that can give people breathing space". No measure has been announced.

Why the Bank of England cares

Energy is a direct and highly visible component of the consumer price index. A step change in the cap feeds through to headline inflation mechanically, and in a single month, when the cap is reset. The larger worry for policymakers is second-round effects: whether households and firms, having lived through 2022, treat another spike as a reason to raise their own inflation expectations and wage demands. Energy price rises of this kind also tend to be quickly visible in surveys of expectations.

Markets are already positioned for a less dovish central bank. Gilt yields are hovering near 19-year highs, and Bank of England policymaker Ramsden has said he would back hikes if inflation persists. The same pattern is visible across the Channel, where ECB officials are blaming Middle East energy costs for euro-area inflation staying above 3%. A bill forecast that adds to UK headline inflation strengthens the case for those who want rates held, or raised, and weakens the case for further cuts.

The effect on the real economy runs the other way. Bills of about £2,000 a year take money from discretionary spending. The result is weaker consumer demand at the same time as higher measured inflation, which is the awkward combination a rate-setter least wants.

What to watch

The next fixed points are Ofgem's January cap announcement in late November and the Bank's next rate decision and inflation projections. Before then, the direction of wholesale gas prices, and of the Middle East supply situation behind them, will determine whether the 16% forecast holds, shrinks or grows. Any government support package would blunt the hit to households, though it would not change the arithmetic of the CPI measure unless it alters the price cap itself.
