South Africa to clean up high-risk areas after 12 women killed

In one sentence
Ramaphosa has ordered a 60-day national audit of unsafe areas after 12 women's bodies were found east of Johannesburg, but funding, scope and follow-through remain unspecified.

South Africa's president has ordered a national clean-up of places that put women at risk, after the bodies of 12 women were found in and around Ekurhuleni, east of Johannesburg, in a little over two months. The announcement is broad in ambition and thin on mechanics. It sets a 60-day audit, but it gives no budget, no list of places and no account of who will do the work.

Cyril Ramaphosa described the killings as "a stain on our national conscience" and announced what officials call a multidisciplinary, national-level emergency response. Local authorities across the country are to identify dangerous and high-risk areas. The examples given are the ordinary furniture of urban neglect: broken streetlights, abandoned buildings, poorly secured parks. The audit of women's safety is to be completed within 60 days.

A cluster, not a pattern

The bodies were found between mid-July and 28 September, mostly around Kempton Park. One more was found in Soweto on 20 September. Some of the women have been named, among them Busisiwe Mahlangu, 37, Itumeleng Kekana, 32, Gracious Nkomo, 28, and Boitumelo Gift Mashita, 23. Others remain unidentified.

There have been four arrests. In the Mahlangu case a suspect, believed to be her partner, was detained. Two women have been charged with premeditated murder over the death of Jabulile Ntimba. A man has been charged over Ntombifuthi Nxumalo. Mashita's estranged husband was found dead. For most of the others, no one has been arrested.

Police have said they cannot rule out a serial killer, but they have found no evidence tying the deaths to one perpetrator, and most appear unconnected. That matters for the policy response. A clean-up of unsafe public spaces addresses one kind of risk. Several of these cases, on the police's own account, involve partners or acquaintances, for whom lighting and park fencing are largely beside the point.

The wider backdrop

The 12 deaths are a small share of a much larger toll. South Africa has one of the world's highest homicide rates, with 5,427 people murdered between April and June this year. The South African Human Rights Commission said 569 women were killed in that quarter and 1,052 others survived attempted murder. That is roughly six women killed a day. Public anger over the killings therefore reflects the clustering and the publicity of these cases as much as their scale.

What is missing

The announcement does not say which agency leads the audit or what happens once it is finished. It gives no funding and no deadline for repairs, and it does not say how municipalities that already struggle to maintain basic services will fix what they find. Gender-violence plans and emergency declarations have been announced before, and campaigners have often pointed to the distance between those pledges and delivery. Nothing in this announcement shows whether it differs in kind.

The first marker is the end of the 60-day audit window, in late November. By then the government should be able to say how many sites were identified and what it has committed to do about them. Court progress on the four arrests will show whether investigators can link, or separate, the cases.
