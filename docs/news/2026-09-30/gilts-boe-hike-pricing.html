Gilt yields hover near 19-year highs as BoE's Ramsden says he would back hikes if inflation persists

In one sentence
The 10-year gilt yield is about 5.4%, near a 19-year high, as Ramsden says he would back a hike if inflation pressures build and markets price an 80% chance of a November rise.

The Bank of England has left interest rates alone. The gilt market has not. The yield on the 10-year UK government bond rose to about 5.4% this week, its highest in nearly two decades, after Deputy Governor Dave Ramsden said he would support a rate rise if inflationary pressures keep building. The 10-year yield stood at 5.42% on 29 September, according to Trading Economics.

The gap between a central bank on hold and a bond market leaning toward a hike is the story. Bank Rate is 3.75%. Investors are betting it will not stay there.

What Ramsden said

Ramsden is one of six Monetary Policy Committee members who voted to hold rates at the 17 September meeting. That makes his remarks more notable, not less. Speaking on 28 September, he said that "were upside pressures on the inflation outlook to continue to build, there could be a case for increasing Bank Rate." Reports of the speech also carried a separate message on the Bank's balance-sheet reduction. Ramsden defended the quantitative tightening strategy and said he was content with how markets had reacted to it.

The condition matters. He did not call for a hike. He said he would back one if pressures persist. Markets have treated that as a shift in tone, and the yield moved accordingly.

The September vote was 6–3 to hold at 3.75%. Huw Pill, Catherine Mann and Megan Greene voted for a quarter-point rise to 4%. Andrew Bailey, Sarah Breeden, Swati Dhingra, Clare Lombardelli, Alan Taylor and Ramsden voted to hold. A committee in which three of nine already want to raise rates needs to move only a few votes to change course. Ramsden's comments suggest at least one of the six is listening for that.

Why the inflation outlook is moving

CPI inflation rose to 3.1% in August. The Bank now expects it to reach around 3.75% in the final quarter of 2026 and slightly above 4% in early 2027. That forecast is based on energy prices prevailing in mid-September, so it is sensitive to the Middle East energy costs also weighing on the euro area. Household energy bills are forecast to post their biggest rise in four years.

Bailey set out the conditional logic in the September statement: "So far, higher global energy costs have had a limited effect on price and wage setting in the UK. But the longer this volatility persists, the bigger the impact it will have on inflation, and the more likely it is we will need to raise Bank rate to ensure that inflation falls back to our 2% target."

The Bank has not yet seen second-round effects in wages and prices. The market is pricing that they will come.

What the market is pricing

The pricing is firmer than the official language. Reports put the probability of a 25-basis-point rise at the November meeting near 80%. They also show roughly four increases priced in by the middle of next year. Four quarter-point rises from 3.75% would take Bank Rate to 4.75%.

That is well beyond anything the MPC has signalled. The committee's own guidance is conditional, and the majority voted to hold less than two weeks ago. Either the market is running ahead of the Bank, or the Bank will need to catch up. Both readings are consistent with a 10-year yield at 5.4%.

The figures come from secondary reports of market pricing and move daily. They should be read as a snapshot, not a settled forecast.

The cost of a high yield

The 10-year gilt yield is a reference rate for much of the UK economy. It feeds into fixed-rate mortgage pricing, corporate borrowing and the government's own debt-servicing costs. A yield near a 19-year high raises all three. It also shows that investors want more compensation for holding UK debt than they have in years. Some of that is expected policy rates. Some is an inflation-risk premium.

The Fed's recent September hike, and Fed official John Williams's comment that there is "no urgency" for another, show the UK is not alone in a tightening bias. The BoE's dilemma is sharper because inflation is forecast to keep rising while the committee remains split.

What to watch

The next tests are the September CPI print, due in October, and the MPC's November decision. If inflation comes in at or above the Bank's path, the hawkish minority is likely to grow and the market pricing will look prescient. If energy prices ease and inflation undershoots, the 10-year yield has room to fall back and the bet on four hikes will unwind. Until one of those happens, the gilt market is holding its position ahead of the committee.
