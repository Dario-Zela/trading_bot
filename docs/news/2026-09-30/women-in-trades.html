Women take up DIY and plumbing trades as shortages bite

In one sentence
A slow rise in women entering plumbing and other trades reflects acute UK skills shortages, with pay and inflation implications, though hard data on the shift is thin.

Britain has a plumber problem, and part of the fix is turning up in an unexpected place: the adult-education college workshop, where more women are learning to sweat a copper joint. The change is small in absolute terms. As a signal of how tight the labour market is for skilled hands, it is worth reading closely.

The starting point is a low one. Industry estimates put women at only around 1% of UK plumbers, and the shortfall of plumbers and heating engineers is put at about 59,000, according to trade-press analysis of the 2026 market. Recruitment difficulty in plumbing and heating and ventilation work is reported at roughly 23%. Those are secondary figures from industry sources, not official statistics, and should be read as indicative.

Why the doors are opening

The demand side is not subtle. Heat-pump and decarbonisation programmes are expanding faster than the workforce that must install them. A trade that once recruited from a narrow pool of school-leavers, overwhelmingly male, now has more work than pairs of hands. When a firm cannot fill a van, the question of who is in the driving seat matters less.

Trade bodies and industry commentators have made the argument bluntly: women are an under-used pool of labour. The share of women in skilled trades has been growing in most sectors over the past decade, though from very small bases, and the pace is slow. No reliable national series yet tracks year-on-year intake by trade, which is why the anecdotes outrun the data.

The barriers that remain

The hiring convention may be cracking, but the training pipeline has not caught up. A traditional apprenticeship can run for several years on pay at or near the apprentice minimum, which shuts out career-changers with rent to pay. Women who want to retrain in their thirties or forties, often the most motivated entrants, usually need savings or a partner's income to bridge it. Employers offering higher starting pay, or shorter conversion routes, would find a ready audience.

That is the piece the evidence does not yet show. There is no clear data that firms are paying a premium specifically to attract women, nor that hiring standards have been loosened. The more likely story is a broader one: pay for qualified tradespeople is being bid up regardless of who fills the role, and openness to new recruits is a by-product.

What it means for pay and prices

For economists, trades are a useful barometer. Repairs, boiler installations and home improvement feed directly into household costs, and skilled-labour scarcity tends to show up first in day rates. With the Bank of England's Ramsden saying he would back rate rises if inflation persists, and gilt yields near 19-year highs, persistent wage pressure in labour-intensive services is exactly the kind of stickiness policymakers watch.

Next to watch: the Office for National Statistics' labour market release for evidence on construction vacancies and earnings, and whether government skills funding for adult retraining is widened. Without shorter routes into the trades, the shortage will keep pushing up wages, whoever holds the wrench.
