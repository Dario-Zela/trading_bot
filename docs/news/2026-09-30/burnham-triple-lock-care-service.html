Burnham pledges to scrap the triple lock in 2030 to fund national care service, and puts EU rejoining on the table

In one sentence
Burnham pledged to end the triple lock's earnings link from 2030 to fund a national care service, but savings arrive mostly by 2040, so near-term gilt supply is unaffected.

Andy Burnham, who succeeded Sir Keir Starmer as prime minister in July, used his Labour conference speech in Liverpool on Tuesday to commit to ending the pension triple lock in its current form from 2030. The money saved is to go towards a national care service. For gilt investors the commitment is informative but not actionable. The policy signals where social care sits in Labour's spending priorities. It changes no borrowing number before the end of this parliament.

The brief for this piece framed the speech as opposition positioning. The reporting shows Burnham is the sitting prime minister, so the 2030 date is a pledge about a government's own term, not a manifesto wish.

What was announced

The triple lock raises the state pension each April by the highest of price inflation, average earnings growth or 2.5%. Burnham said it would stay in place until the end of this parliament. From 2030 the pension would rise by inflation or 2.5%, whichever is higher, which cuts the automatic link to earnings.

Officials expect the change to save about £15bn a year by 2040, according to the reporting of the speech. The savings would help fund an NHS-style national care service, including home care free at the point of use, which Burnham has championed for years. No overall cost for the service was given in the material available.

The pledge is narrower than the headline suggests. The lock is not abolished, and pensions would still rise with prices. Its effect would show up only when earnings growth outpaces both inflation and 2.5%, which is the gap that compounds over time.

The arithmetic of delay

The Institute for Fiscal Studies cautioned that the savings "are likely to be relatively small in the first few years, but rise substantially over time". It warned against expecting the proceeds to fully fund universal social care in the next parliament.

That shapes the gilt read. The £15bn figure refers to 2040. In the early 2030s the gain would be a small fraction of it, while a care service would need spending to start well before then. Any gap would be covered by borrowing or other taxes. The plan therefore gives no near-term reduction in gilt issuance, and could add to it during the build-out if the service is phased in faster than the savings arrive.

Gilt yields are hovering near 19-year highs, with the Bank of England's Ramsden saying he would back rate rises if inflation persists. In that setting a pledge with no cash effect before 2030 is unlikely to move pricing much. The one market-relevant element is that a prime minister has put a politically costly pensioner saving on the record. That is some evidence that the government accepts it must show how new spending is paid for.

The distributional question is still open. Pensioners would lose relative to an earnings-linked path, while the recipients of care, who are often elderly or disabled, and their families, who currently provide much of it, would gain. The speech did not set out which cohorts would bear the cost of any shortfall.

Europe and electoral reform

The speech also moved on Europe. Burnham said that "Brexit has done more harm than good" and promised to set out options for the UK's relationship with Brussels ahead of a major UK-EU summit later this year. Those options could include closer alignment, and reports of the speech say rejoining is among them. He did not commit to a model. Some accounts say he is seeking closer ties without currently proposing to rejoin the EU, the single market or the customs union. The distinction matters for sterling and for trade-exposed sectors, and it remains unresolved.

He also announced a national commission on electoral reform. "We will all have failed in our public duties if we carry on as we are," he said. A commission defers the decision, in the same way that the 2030 date defers the pension change.

What to watch

The speech made no commitments with a cost before 2030. The next market-relevant data points are therefore the UK-EU summit later this year, where Burnham's Brussels options will face scrutiny, and the next fiscal event. At that event the Office for Budget Responsibility's costings will show how much of the care service the triple-lock change can carry, and how much borrowing is needed to bridge the gap. The IFS has already indicated that the gap will be large in the early years.
