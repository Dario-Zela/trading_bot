Oura pulls $15bn listing days after announcing it

In one sentence
Oura postponed its Nasdaq listing at a roughly $15bn valuation, and the evidence points to a pricing and deal-structure dispute, not a shut market.

Oura, the Finnish maker of the smart ring, has postponed its Nasdaq listing days after setting terms. The company had marketed shares at $40–$44, an implied valuation of about $15bn. On Tuesday it said it would wait, citing "uncertainty in the IPO market". Headlines have called this a pull. The company's word is postponement, and it has given no new date.

The deal that was

The offering was for roughly $2.2bn of stock. Most of that was not new money for the company. According to reports, about 13.5 million of the shares were new, and around 36.5 million were to be sold by existing holders. Forerunner Ventures planned to sell its entire 9.3% stake, worth about $1.2bn at the $42 midpoint, according to TechCrunch. Reports differ slightly on the share count, at 50 million or 55 million. The size of the secondary component is what matters here: a deal that mostly pays out early holders gives new buyers less reason to accept a full price.

The operating numbers are not those of a struggling business. Revenue was $907.9m in 2025, and Oura said it expects growth of about 90% in 2026. It reported 5.7 million paying members, up from 5 million in June. Membership gross margin was 89%. Cash stood at $372m at the end of June.

On those figures, the triage description of Oura as an unproven hardware play is too harsh. This is a subscription-heavy business with fast growth. The open question is what multiple that growth deserves, since the ring itself is hardware.

Demand, or not

Oura says the decision was made "despite strong demand". Chief executive Tom Hale said: "We aim to deliver an extraordinary initial public offering for our employees and investors and we have the luxury of choosing our moment." The company added that its business had "further strengthened" since the process began.

Investing.com reported that the book was about four times covered. That figure sits awkwardly with a market-uncertainty explanation. A book that size normally supports a deal, unless the demand was concentrated at prices below the range. The same report says some investors balked at the valuation. It also says they worried about the large secondary share and pointed to weak comparables: Fitbit traded below its IPO price before Google bought it, and Peloton's pandemic surge later reversed. Those accounts are based on reports, and Oura has not confirmed them.

The reading that fits the evidence is a pricing dispute, not a closed market. Investors were willing to buy Oura shares. Whether they would pay $15bn, with early holders selling into the offering, is another matter.

What it says about listings

One postponement does not show a frozen market, and this one does not look like a verdict on Oura's business. It does show that buyers are discriminating on price and deal structure. A high-growth consumer-tech name with strong margins still could not clear its range when the sellers were the early backers.

The practical signal for other issuers is about structure. Deals that raise little primary capital and carry a big insider sale will draw more scrutiny. A lower range, a smaller secondary or a stronger quarter could each change the outcome.

What to watch: whether Oura returns with a revised range once it has another quarter of results, and how the next consumer-tech issuer prices against its marketed range. Oura's own third-quarter figures, when they arrive, will show whether the 90% growth guidance is holding.
