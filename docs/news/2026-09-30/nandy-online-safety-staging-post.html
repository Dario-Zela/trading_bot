Nandy: children's social media ban is only a 'staging post' in online safety drive

In one sentence
Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy called the planned children's social media ban a 'staging post', signalling wider online-safety rules that could reach platform design and business models.

The UK government's proposed ban on social media for children is not meant to be the last word on regulating tech platforms. Culture Secretary Lisa Nandy described it as a "staging post" in a longer online-safety drive, according to the Guardian's reporting. The phrase suggests ministers want to regulate more than who is allowed onto a platform.

What was said

The reported facts are thin. Nandy characterised the ban as a step on the way to something else rather than an endpoint. She also signalled further online-safety measures aimed at platforms themselves, beyond enforcing age limits. No detailed list of those measures has been published. Nothing in the reporting says what they are, when they would arrive, or whether they would need new primary legislation.

The vocabulary is still informative. An age ban is a rule about users: who may sign up, and what checks a platform must run. Talk of a "staging post" points toward rules about the product, such as how services are designed and how content is recommended. That reading is an inference from the wording, not a policy that has been announced.

The regime already in place

Platforms operating in Britain are already subject to the Online Safety Act 2023, which Ofcom enforces. Ofcom can fine a company up to £18m or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater. The children's safety duties under the Act have applied since mid-2025, including requirements for stronger age checks on services likely to be accessed by under-18s.

An outright ban would sit on top of that framework. Any further measures could either extend it or add to it. Either way, the compliance burden for large services would probably grow, and it would not be limited to the age-verification tooling they are already building.

Why platforms will pay attention

For companies such as Meta and TikTok, the UK is a large and wealthy user market, and a policy move here is often copied elsewhere. The concern for them is less the ban itself than an open-ended trajectory. A rule that can be met with an age gate is a bounded engineering and cost problem. A rule aimed at business models, such as engagement-driven feeds, is much harder to price.

The market effect is unclear, since no measure has yet been costed or scheduled. The signal to watch is whether "further measures" turn into named proposals.

What to watch

The next test is detail. The government will need to say what the ban covers, how it would be enforced, and whether Ofcom gets new powers or the existing Act is simply applied harder. Consultation documents or a legislative timetable would show whether "staging post" describes a real roadmap or a rhetorical flourish. Until then, platforms have to plan for the wider version.
