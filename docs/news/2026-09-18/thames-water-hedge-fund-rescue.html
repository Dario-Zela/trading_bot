Cross-party MPs urge government to break off Thames Water talks with US hedge funds

Coppermills Water Treatment Works in Walthamstow, one of Thames Water's largest sites and part of the infrastructure at the centre of the rescue dispute. — Wikimedia Commons
In one sentence
A cross-party Commons committee wants ministers to reject Elliott Management and Apollo's £10bn Thames Water rescue and force the utility into special administration instead.

Thames Water has spent the better part of two years lurching from one deadline to the next, kept alive by lender forbearance while its owners search for someone — anyone — willing to inject fresh capital into Britain's largest water utility. This week, a cross-party group of MPs told the government to stop looking to Wall Street and the City for the answer.

The Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Efra) Committee has recommended ministers walk away from talks over a £10 billion rescue plan put forward by Thames Water's senior creditors — a consortium trading under the name London & Valley Water, fronted by US hedge fund Elliott Management and private equity giant Apollo Global Management, alongside Silver Point Capital, Invesco and PIMCO. Together the group holds roughly £17 billion of the utility's debt pile, which now exceeds £20 billion.

"We do not believe this opaque consortium of 100 hedge funds and others has the interests of the public, the company or the environment at heart."

That is the committee's chair, Liberal Democrat MP Alistair Carmichael, and it is about as blunt as select committee language gets. The report goes further, warning that the creditors' priority is to "extract immediate value from Thames Water, not steer it to long-term success" — language that reads less like a policy critique than an indictment of the private-capital model itself.

A deal built on relief from fines

The London & Valley Water proposal is not simply a capital injection. In exchange for the £10 billion, the consortium has sought concessions from regulator Ofwat on fines for pollution incidents and service failures — the penalties designed to punish exactly the kind of underinvestment that pushed Thames Water into crisis in the first place. MPs argue that trade-off inverts the accountability the regulatory system is meant to enforce: creditors recoup value while the penalties meant to protect customers and rivers get waived.

A spokesperson for the consortium has defended the plan on different grounds, saying its members "stepped in to fund a significant revenue shortfall to ensure Thames Water's record capital investment programme can continue." That defence — we are the ones still paying to keep the taps running — is precisely the leverage that has kept Thames Water's creditors at the negotiating table with successive governments since a prior rescue led by KKR collapsed over exec pay and fines in 2024.

The Efra Committee isn't alone in doubting the current structure. Emma Reynolds, environment secretary until earlier this year, rejected an earlier version of the London & Valley Water plan in June, saying it did not go far enough to protect customers or the environment. That the creditor consortium has now had two proposals waved back suggests the sticking points — fine relief, governance, and who bears the cost of a company with £20 billion of debt and chronic underinvestment — have not moved much despite months of negotiation.

The alternative is a modified insolvency

What the committee wants instead is for Ofwat and the Treasury to walk away and let Thames Water fall into the Special Administration Regime (SAR) — a bespoke, court-run insolvency process built for utilities that cannot be allowed to simply stop operating. Under SAR, a government-appointed administrator would run the company while a new ownership and financing structure is worked out, with the state underwriting continuity of service.

The committee's report goes a step further than simply endorsing SAR as a fallback: it recommends changing the law so ministers can trigger the regime on performance grounds, rather than waiting for the company to actually run out of cash — currently expected around the end of this year. That is a meaningful ask, since Thames Water's creditors have previously signalled they would contest an early trigger in court; a separate legal challenge from Liberal Democrat MP Charlie Maynard, disputing the interim restructuring plan, is already awaiting a decision on permission to appeal to the Supreme Court.

Prime Minister Andy Burnham has spoken in general terms about taking greater "public control" of utilities, but his government has so far avoided a direct statement on Thames Water specifically — leaving the £10 billion question unresolved even as the company's own runway shortens. SAR is not costless for taxpayers: administration would put the government on the hook for running costs until a new structure is found, and Ofwat's own polling has found customers broadly receptive to that route despite the near-term expense.

Why the politics matter beyond Thames Water

The committee's language — "opaque," "extract immediate value," lacking the "expertise to turn around a vital public service" — is notable less for what it says about Thames Water than for what it signals about the political appetite for hedge funds and private equity as the default fixers of distressed infrastructure. Water bills have already risen sharply — Ofwat granted Thames Water a 35% increase to £588 a year after rejecting a bid for 53% — and cross-party MPs are now explicitly framing further concessions to creditors as a transfer from customers and rivers to distressed-debt specialists, not a solution to either.

If ministers follow the committee's recommendation and let talks lapse, it would be one of the starkest instances yet of a government choosing a public backstop over a private-capital rescue for critical infrastructure — a precedent that firms weighing similar bets on other stressed utilities, from energy networks to transport concessions, will be watching closely. The next marker is whether the government responds to the Efra report before Thames Water's cash position forces the decision anyway, likely within the next few months.
