Revolut weighs dual London/New York stock listing, CEO confirms

Revolut founder and CEO Nik Storonsky, who told French newspaper Les Echos the company is weighing listings on both the London Stock Exchange and Nasdaq. — Stephen McCarthy/Web Summit via Sportsfile, Wikimedia Commons (CC BY 2.0)
In one sentence
Revolut CEO Nik Storonsky says the $115bn fintech is weighing a dual London-New York listing, a reversal from his past dismissal of London's market depth.

Nik Storonsky has spent the better part of two years telling anyone who would listen that London's stock market cannot compete with New York. On Wednesday, in an interview with the French newspaper Les Echos, Revolut's founder and chief executive softened that stance just enough to matter: the $115 billion fintech, he said, is now weighing a dual listing on both the London Stock Exchange and New York's Nasdaq.

It is the first time Storonsky has put a London leg on the table at all. As recently as 2024 he dismissed the idea outright, arguing the UK market lacked the depth of buyers to support a float of Revolut's size. The reversal matters less as a concession to London boosters than as a signal of how mega-valued European fintechs are starting to think about going public — not as an either/or between home market and Wall Street, but as a sequencing question.

Why New York still wins the argument

Storonsky was characteristically blunt about where his preference lies. "It's a larger market," he told Les Echos. "It includes institutional investors, hedge funds, fund managers and a considerable number of individual investors." The calculus, as he framed it, is simple: "We have the choice between selling in a small market with few buyers, or in a gigantic market with a huge number of buyers who will compete fiercely for our shares. Therefore, yes, we prefer the United States."

That logic tracks the valuation trajectory. Revolut priced a secondary share sale in July at $115 billion — shares changing hands at $2,017 apiece, a jump of more than 50% from the $75 billion mark set less than a year earlier. The Financial Times has reported internal discussions of an eventual IPO valuation as high as $150 billion to $200 billion. At those numbers, a London listing alone would install Revolut among Britain's largest public companies, ahead of Barclays and NatWest by market capitalisation and within range of BP — a fact London Stock Exchange officials have not been shy about pointing out as they court the company.

Building the regulatory scaffolding for two markets

A dual listing requires more than an ambivalent CEO — it requires banking infrastructure on both sides of the Atlantic, and Revolut has spent 2026 assembling exactly that. The company secured a full UK banking licence in March, ending a wait that began with a restricted licence back in 2024, and has picked up additional charters in France, Lithuania, Australia, Colombia and Mexico. In the US it holds conditional approval from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency for a national bank charter — the missing piece that would let it operate as a full-fledged deposit-taking bank rather than a payments app when it eventually courts American public-market investors.

The underlying business has kept pace. Revenue rose 46% last year to $6 billion, with net profit reaching $1.7 billion, while the customer base grew by more than 16 million to 68.3 million. Those are the kind of growth-plus-profitability numbers that let a company dictate listing venue rather than beg for one — a leverage previous-generation fintechs, many of which went public loss-making or scaled back valuations before their US debuts, rarely had.

None of this means a float is imminent. Storonsky said as recently as April that an IPO was still roughly two years out, and has previously ruled out going public before 2028. The dual-listing idea, for now, is a stated preference rather than a filed prospectus, and market conditions — rates, risk appetite, the state of tech valuations generally — will do as much to set the timeline as anything happening inside Revolut's Canary Wharf headquarters.

What a dual listing would signal for the sector

What has shifted is the framing. A decade of European fintechs — Wise chief among them — treated a US listing as the default aspiration and a domestic one as a consolation prize, if they considered it at all. Wise itself flipped that script in 2025 by shifting its primary listing to New York while retaining London ties, a move Revolut's dual-track thinking now echoes from the other direction. If Revolut follows through, it would test whether a single company can genuinely serve two exchanges' worth of liquidity, disclosure regimes and index inclusion — Nasdaq-100 ambitions on one side, an FTSE 100 weighting on the other — without either market treating it as an afterthought.

The next marker to watch is not a listing date but a venue decision: whether Revolut treats London and New York as equal primary listings or structures one as a secondary shell, and whether UK regulators offer further sweeteners — on stamp duty, dual-class share rules, or index eligibility — to keep the London leg meaningful rather than symbolic.
